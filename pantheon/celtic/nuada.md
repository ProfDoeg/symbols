# Nuada: Research Dossier

> **Class:** In medieval Irish tradition, Núadu is a supernatural king of the **Túatha Dé Danann**, a race explicitly called *gods* in early Irish usage but frequently euhemerized by Christian scribes as pre-Milesian rulers, magicians, or *aes síde*. In Roman Britain, the cognate **Nodens/Nodons** is securely a **god**, addressed in Latin votive inscriptions as *deus* and identified with Mars. In medieval Welsh literature, **Nudd/Lludd** is represented principally as a legendary royal ancestor rather than explicitly called a god.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest securely datable evidence belonging to this name-complex is Roman-period Britain: Latin inscriptions to **Nodonti, Nodenti,** or **Nudente**, probably spanning the later second through fourth centuries CE. The best-dated early candidate is a curse tablet from Ratcliffe-on-Soar assigned to the late second or early third century, but its association with Nodens is partly reconstructed and less secure than the Lydney and Cockersand dedications. The monumental cult complex at Lydney Park is firmly late Roman, substantially constructed or rebuilt after approximately 364–367 CE. No securely identified pre-Roman image, Gaulish inscription, or Irish pagan-period inscription names Nuada.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

**[Secure Roman attestation]** Nodens was a Romano-British god invoked for health, restitution, and possibly hunting, maritime protection, or prosperity. At Lydney Park in Gloucestershire his sanctuary included a temple, baths, accommodation, mosaics, votive images, and inscriptions. Some dedications equate him with **Mars**, but the material culture—dogs, bodily votives, water imagery, fish, sea creatures, and provision for pilgrims—does not reduce neatly to a Roman war god.

**[Medieval Irish literary tradition]** Núadu Airgetlám is the first or an early king of the Túatha Dé Danann in Ireland. He loses his hand or arm to Sreng in the first battle of Mag Tuired, becomes ineligible for kingship because of bodily blemish, is replaced by Bres, receives a functional silver prosthesis from Dían Cécht with Crédne’s assistance, later has his natural limb restored by Míach, regains the kingship, admits Lug to Tara, temporarily yields Lug the royal seat, and is killed by Balor in the second battle.

**[Medieval Welsh regional reflex]** Nudd or Lludd Llaw Eraint, “Nudd/Lludd of the Silver Hand,” appears in Welsh genealogical and Arthurian material. In *Culhwch ac Olwen*, Lludd is father of Creiddylad and Nudd is father of Gwyn and Edern. *Lludd and Llefelys* makes Lludd son of Beli a legendary king of Britain. The equation of all these figures with one another is philologically plausible but must not erase their distinct textual histories.

### Names

- **Nodonti, Nodenti, Nudente** — Latin dative forms preserved in Romano-British inscriptions. The nominative **Nodons** or **Nodens** is scholarly reconstruction, although “Nodens” has become conventional.
- **Núadu, Núada** — Old and Middle Irish forms; modern Irish **Nuadha**.
- **Núadu Airgetlám / Nuada Airgetlám** — *airget* “silver” + *lám* “hand, arm”: “Nuada Silver-hand/arm.”
- **Nudd** — Middle Welsh cognate.
- **Lludd** — Welsh form generally explained as alteration of *Nudd*, probably through alliterative assimilation in combinations such as **Lludd Llaw Eraint**.
- **Llaw Eraint** — Welsh “Silver Hand.” Orthographies include *Llaw Ereint* and *Llaw Eraint*.
- **Nodent** — an Old Breton personal name sometimes included in the cognate group, though its vocalism creates difficulties.
- British post-Roman names written **Nudi** and **Nudinti** have also been compared, but personal-name survival does not by itself demonstrate continued worship.

### Etymology

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction; disputed]** The underlying stem is usually reconstructed as Proto-Celtic **\*Nowdont-** or **\*Nōdont-**. The phonological relationship among British *Nodonti*, Irish *Núadu*, and Welsh *Nudd* is widely accepted; its lexical meaning is not.

Proposals include:

1. **“Catcher, snarer, acquirer, hunter, fisherman.”** Tolkien compared Germanic forms including Gothic *niutan*, “to attain/enjoy,” and connected the proposal with Lydney’s fish and hunting imagery. It is attractive semantically but lacks an independently attested Celtic verbal base.
2. **Wealth or acquisition.** Rhys and others compared Lithuanian *naudà*, “property, profit,” and Germanic words for acquiring or enjoying. This produced interpretations such as “giver of wealth.” It remains conjectural.
3. **Mist or cloud.** A derivation from a reconstructed *\*snowdo-* has been proposed, partly by comparison with Welsh *nudd*, “mist.” The required loss of initial *s-* is difficult to demonstrate consistently, particularly for Irish.
4. **Need or compulsion.** Comparison with Proto-Indo-European *\*neh₂u-t-* and Celtic words for “need” has been suggested, but Ranko Matasović regards the formal derivation as difficult.
5. **“Giver of renewal.”** A modern Russian etymological proposal analyzes the name through elements meaning “new” and “give,” aligning it with healing. This is not a consensus derivation.

John Carey’s conclusion remains the safest: no proposed lexical etymology commands full confidence. The correspondence of the names is substantially stronger than any claimed meaning.

### Roman identifications

- **Mars Nodons / Mars Nodens:** directly epigraphic at Lydney and Cockersand. This is a historical Romano-British *interpretatio*, not proof that Irish Nuada was simply “the Celtic Mars.”
- **Nodens Silvianus:** the Lydney curse tablet’s syntax has been read as invoking Nodens with or alongside Silvanus. Hunting, woodland, and restitution associations may have encouraged the linkage.
- **Mercury or Neptune:** occasionally asserted in older or popular accounts, but no equally secure full inscription identifies Nodens with either. Maritime imagery can support a water-related function without proving a formal equation with Neptune.

There is no attested Greek identification and no Gaulish inscription naming Nodens. Caesar’s *Bellum Gallicum* VI, Lucan’s *Pharsalia* I and its scholia describe or name other Celtic gods and cult practices; they do **not** name Nuada/Nodens.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Roman epigraphy

**[Secure Roman material]** The Roman Inscriptions of Britain database records variants of the deity’s name at Lydney, Cockersand Moss, and in more uncertain material from Ratcliffe-on-Soar and London.

- **RIB 305, Lydney Park:** *D(eo) M(arti) Nodonti…*—“To the god Mars Nodons…” A votive dedication by Flavius Blandinus, conventionally interpreted as a military weapons instructor. Precise date uncertain, culturally late Roman.
- **RIB 306, Lydney Park:** a lead curse or restitution tablet:

  *De{u}o Nodenti Silvianus anulum perdidit … non illis permittas sanitatem donec perferat usque templum [No]dentis.*

  “To the god Nodens: Silvianus has lost a ring and has given half [its value] to Nodens. Among those called Senicianus, do not permit health until he carries it to the temple of Nodens.”

  The tablet is direct evidence that a petitioner expected Nodens to punish a suspected thief through illness and restore the property or its value. Its exact archaeological context was lost because it was found before systematic excavation.
- **RIB 307, Lydney:** *Deo Nudente M(arti)…*, accompanied by a dog image; another explicit Mars identification.
- **RIB 2448.3, Lydney mosaic:** dedicates the pavement to Nodens from collected offerings. Titus Flavius Senilis is called *praepositus*; Victorinus is an *interpres*. The older interpretation of *interpres* as a professional interpreter of dreams is plausible in an incubation shrine but not certain.
- **RIB 616, Cockersand Moss, Lancashire:** *Deo Marti Nodonti Aurelius…* on the base of a Mars statuette reportedly found in 1718. The object is lost, so the early transcription is the evidence.
- **RIB 617, Cockersand:** a fragmentary abbreviated dedication, sometimes restored to Nodons; less secure.
- **Ratcliffe-on-Soar tablet, JRS 53 (1963), p. 121:** late second/early third century. Nodens has been proposed in the reading or contextual interpretation, but the fragment is less explicit than the Lydney texts.
- **London tablet, Brit. 2018.1:** *Nodenti/Nudente* appears in commentary on a difficult list or curse tablet; interpretation remains provisional.
- **RIB 3500, Cramond:** Nodons has been discussed in the commentary, but it is not a straightforward complete divine dedication.

The epigraphy establishes a British cult; it does not establish the silver hand, Irish kingship myth, or the name of any consort.

### Irish corpus

#### *Lebor Gabála Érenn*

**[Later canonical learned systematization]** The *Book of the Taking of Ireland* is Christian Irish synthetic history, assembled through successive recensions rather than a pagan scripture. Its earliest major surviving witness is the Book of Leinster, copied in the later twelfth century, around 1150–1160; other witnesses range into the early modern period.

Núadu is king of the Túatha Dé Danann, rules before and after Bres, and falls in the final battle of Mag Tuired. Genealogical materials call him son of Echtach, son of Etarlám in some recensions. The text synchronizes Irish legendary invasions with biblical and classical chronology. Those synchronisms are medieval scholarship, not independent dating of an ancient cult.

#### *Cath Maige Tuired*

**[Medieval canonical narrative with earlier ingredients]** The extant text of the “Second Battle of Mag Tuired” survives in British Library Harley MS 5280, a sixteenth-century manuscript. Its language and compositional strata are earlier, commonly assigned broadly to the Middle Irish period, with portions conventionally traced to the ninth–twelfth centuries. Elizabeth Gray’s 1982 critical edition remains fundamental.

The text supplies most of Nuada’s familiar dossier:

- sword from Findias;
- loss of his hand to Sreng in the battle against the Fir Bolg;
- silver hand made by Dían Cécht with Crédne;
- disqualification from kingship and Bres’s succession;
- restoration of the natural hand by Míach;
- return to kingship;
- admission of Lug;
- exchange of seats with Lug for thirteen days;
- death with Macha at Balor’s hands.

The opening catalogue of the four cities and treasures is commonly considered a learned introductory stratum related to *Lebor Gabála*, not necessarily the oldest layer of the battle tale.

#### *Cath Maige Tuired Cunga*

**[Late medieval/early-modern elaboration]** The separate “First Battle” expands the combat with the Fir Bolg. Sreng cuts off Nuada’s right arm at the shoulder, taking part of his shield with it. The defeated Fir Bolg receive Connacht in some versions. Its manuscript transmission and narrative form are later than the core notices embedded in *Cath Maige Tuired* and *Lebor Gabála*.

#### *The Four Jewels of the Túatha Dé Danann*

**[Learned systematization]** This Middle Irish catalogue associates four cities, teachers, and treasures:

- Falias—Lia Fáil;
- Gorias—Lug’s spear;
- Findias—Nuada’s sword;
- Murias—the Dagda’s cauldron.

No one escapes Nuada’s sword once it is drawn, and no one can resist it. Modern identification of it with **Fragarach** or with a universal **Claíomh Solais**, “Sword of Light,” is not explicit in the early catalogue and is usually a modern harmonization.

#### Dindshenchas and genealogical material

**[Regional and learned variants]** Dindshenchas poems associate persons named Nuada with places and genealogies. Not every Nuada is necessarily the god: medieval Irish narrative also knows Nuadu Necht, Nuada Find Femin, Nuada son of Achi, and other legendary or pseudo-historical namesakes. Claims about children such as Gaible or descendants involved in Fenian genealogies require case-by-case discrimination.

#### *Tochmarc Étaíne*

**[Medieval mythological-cycle tradition]** Its principal recensions survive in the Yellow Book of Lecan and other manuscripts. It describes the Túatha Dé, the Dagda, Midir, Étaín, Óengus, and Bres-related mythic society. Nuada is not a principal actor in its central wooing plot. General statements that it supplies a major Nuada myth are therefore misleading.

#### *Táin Bó Cúailnge* and related *Táin* texts

**[Allusive material]** Names, weapons, comparisons, or genealogical figures called Nuada occur across the Ulster-cycle corpus, but no central episode of the *Táin* supplies the silver-hand kingship narrative. “Nuada’s Cainnel/Torch,” sometimes treated as his sword, belongs to allusive weapon nomenclature whose identification with the Findias sword is inferential.

#### *Acallam na Senórach*

**[Twelfth-century composition; later manuscripts]** The *Acallam* integrates the Túatha Dé and *síd* beings into Fenian landscape legend. It is important for the transformation of former gods into inhabitants or rulers of the otherworld, but Nuada Airgetlám has no comparably sustained role. Persons called Nuada in Fenian pedigrees should not automatically be equated with him.

### Welsh corpus

#### *Culhwch ac Olwen*

**[Medieval Welsh regional tradition]** The tale was probably redacted around the late eleventh or twelfth century; it survives incompletely in the fourteenth-century White Book of Rhydderch and completely in the later fourteenth-century Red Book of Hergest.

It names:

- **Gwyn ap Nudd**, a member of Arthur’s supernatural company;
- **Edern ap Nudd**;
- **Creiddylad ferch Lludd Llaw Eraint**, “Creiddylad daughter of Lludd Silver-Hand.”

Gwyn abducts Creiddylad from Gwythyr ap Greidawl. Arthur intervenes and orders Gwyn and Gwythyr to fight each May Day until Judgment Day. Lludd himself does not enter the scene; his relation is genealogical.

#### *Lludd and Llefelys*

**[Medieval learned and regional elaboration]** The earliest surviving version is interpolated into Welsh *Brut y Brenhinedd* manuscripts; the oldest witness, Llanstephan 1, dates approximately 1225–1250. Later copies occur in the White and Red Books.

Lludd son of Beli succeeds to Britain; his brother Llefelys rules France. Britain suffers three plagues: the Coraniaid, a scream that causes devastation, and the disappearance of provisions. Llefelys supplies remedies. The narrative has no silver-hand episode, and its association with Nuada depends on the name Lludd/Nudd and the separate epithet *Llaw Eraint*.

#### Four Branches and *Book of Taliesin*

There is no substantial, securely identifiable Nuada/Nudd narrative in the Four Branches or the *Book of Taliesin*. The absence matters: one should not reconstruct a pan-Welsh cult biography merely from later genealogical names.

### Materials that do not attest him

The **Gundestrup cauldron**, **Pillar of the Boatmen**, and **Coligny calendar** contain important Celtic religious imagery or language, but none securely names or depicts Nuada/Nodens. Identifying an anonymous Gundestrup figure as Nuada is modern speculation. The Gaulish corpora, including *Recueil des inscriptions gauloises*, furnish no accepted dedication to him.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Kingship and bodily integrity

**[Medieval Irish canonical]** Nuada embodies sacral or juridical kingship: after losing a hand he cannot remain king. The text says he was “not eligible” after mutilation; it does not set out a general legal tract on divine disability. Comparison with Irish ideas of an unblemished king is persuasive, but the myth itself is the direct evidence.

His abdication permits Bres’s reign and Fomorian domination. His restoration—whether because the silver hand functions perfectly or because Míach restores flesh—allows his return. The different narrative moments are not entirely harmonized: one passage gives him a silver prosthesis; another restores his original hand; he nevertheless remains “Silverhand” at death.

### Warfare

His irresistible sword, leadership in both Mag Tuired battles, and identification of British Nodens with Mars support a martial dimension. Yet the Roman evidence does not prove that Nodens was primarily a war god, and the Irish tales portray Nuada more often as king, host, and casualty than as the decisive champion.

### Healing

**[Secure Roman material plus medieval parallel]** Lydney’s baths, possible pilgrim accommodation, dog votives, anatomical arm, eye-medicine stamp, and curse asking the god to withhold health support healing or health-control. The Irish myth’s artificial and restored limbs provide a striking thematic parallel. It remains possible, however, that scholars have allowed the medieval silver arm to overdetermine interpretation of ambiguous Roman finds.

### Water, fishing, and maritime protection

Fish, anchors, sea monsters, tritons, and the sanctuary’s view over the Severn estuary give Nodens a genuine maritime or aquatic context. They do not prove that he was a “sea god” in the same exclusive sense as Neptune or Manannán.

### Hunting and prosperity

Silvanus, dogs, and Tolkien’s “catcher” etymology prompted a hunting interpretation. Dogs can equally signify healing, guardianship, companionship, or hunting. Prosperity and distributive kingship are more strongly developed in comparative interpretation than in explicit inscriptions.

### Iconography

No image can be identified with absolute certainty as the god’s portrait. Lydney finds include:

- bronze dogs and a dog depicted beside a dedication;
- a small hollow bronze arm, plausibly an anatomical votive;
- fish, dolphins, sea monsters, and ichthyocentaurs in mosaic;
- a chariot figure and other damaged metal images;
- a figure apparently striking or spearing a fish;
- pins, bracelets, coins, and other offerings.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The anonymous Tandragee idol, Janus-like stone heads, Gundestrup figures, and other “silver-handed” or one-armed objects are sometimes labeled Nuada online. None bears his name or a diagnostic silver hand; those identifications are unproved.

No ancient source assigns Nuada a sacred color, number, weekday, planet, tree, or festival. Such correspondences in modern occult tables are modern inventions unless independently sourced.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations

### Genealogical versions

- **[Medieval learned genealogy]** Some *Lebor Gabála* recensions make him **Núadu son of Echtach, son of Etarlám**.
- **[Welsh regional tradition]** Nudd is father of **Gwyn** and **Edern** in *Culhwch ac Olwen*.
- **[Welsh regional tradition]** Lludd Llaw Eraint is father of **Creiddylad**.
- **[Learned British pseudo-history]** Lludd is son of **Beli Mawr** and brother of **Llefelys** and, in the wider genealogical scheme, Caswallon.
- **[Possible conflation]** Irish Fenian genealogies sometimes make a Nuada ancestor of Tadg and therefore an ancestor of Fionn through Muirne. These notices may concern Nuada Airgetlám, Nuadu Necht, or another eponym; manuscripts and modern summaries do not agree.

No secure early Irish source gives Nuada a named consort. Claims that he is married to the Morrígan, Macha, Boann, or a generic “queen of sovereignty” are modern harmonizations unless tied to a particular late retelling.

### Documented divine or supernatural interactions

Each line records an actual textual conjunction, not mere membership in the same pantheon.

- **Sreng:** cuts off Nuada’s hand/arm in the first battle of Mag Tuired. *Cath Maige Tuired* and the expanded *Cath Maige Tuired Cunga*, medieval Irish. **[Canonical; expanded in later battle tale]**
- **Dían Cécht:** fits Nuada with an articulated silver hand. *Cath Maige Tuired*, surviving in Harley 5280. **[Canonical]**
- **Crédne:** assists Dían Cécht in making or fitting the silver hand. *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Canonical]**
- **Míach:** rejects the prosthesis as insufficient and restores Nuada’s natural hand over nine days by the charm “joint to joint and sinew to sinew.” *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Canonical]**
- **Bres:** succeeds Nuada because Nuada is blemished; after Bres’s oppressive reign and deposition, Nuada returns. *Cath Maige Tuired*; *Lebor Gabála*. **[Canonical/learned systematization]**
- **Lug:** Nuada admits him to Tara after learning that he possesses every art; later exchanges seats with him for thirteen days so Lug can direct resistance to the Fomoire. *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Canonical]**
- **Dagda:** joins Nuada, Lug, Ogma, Goibniu, and Dían Cécht in the war council at Grellach Dollaid. *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Canonical joint scene]**
- **Ogma:** participates with Nuada and Lug in the war council. *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Canonical joint scene]**
- **Goibniu:** summoned with Dían Cécht to the council convened by Lug and Nuada. *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Canonical joint scene]**
- **Balor:** kills Nuada and Macha in battle. *Cath Maige Tuired* §133/Gray’s corresponding passage; *Lebor Gabála*. **[Canonical]**
- **Macha daughter of Ernmas:** falls beside Nuada at Balor’s hands. No separate exchange between them is narrated. *Cath Maige Tuired*. **[Canonical joint death]**
- **Gwyn ap Nudd:** Welsh text identifies Gwyn as Nudd’s son; no meeting between father and son is narrated. *Culhwch ac Olwen*. **[Regional genealogical relation]**
- **Edern ap Nudd:** appears as Nudd’s son in Arthur’s company; no interaction with Nudd is narrated. *Culhwch ac Olwen*. **[Regional genealogical relation]**
- **Creiddylad:** identified as daughter of Lludd Llaw Eraint; Lludd does not participate in her abduction or the May Day combat. *Culhwch ac Olwen*. **[Regional genealogical relation]**
- **Llefelys:** Lludd consults his brother about Britain’s three plagues and follows his remedies. *Lludd and Llefelys*, thirteenth-century and later witnesses. **[Late medieval Welsh elaboration]**
- **Beli Mawr:** Lludd succeeds his father as king of Britain. *Lludd and Llefelys* and associated genealogical tradition. **[Learned pseudo-history]**

No primary text records Nuada meeting Manannán, Brigid, the Morrígan, Nechtan, Cernunnos, Týr, or the Fisher King. Comparisons with those figures are scholarly or modern mythographic constructions, not interactions.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Arrival and the sword of Findias

**[Learned medieval systematization]** The Túatha Dé dwell in the northern islands of the world and study magical arts in four cities. From Findias comes Nuada’s sword, taught or guarded by the poet Uiscias. Once drawn, it permits no escape or resistance.

The city-and-treasure scheme is highly organized and probably belongs to a learned redactional layer. It is not corroborated by an inscription or independent early ritual text. The sword is not named Fragarach in the passage.

### The First Battle of Mag Tuired

**[Canonical core; later expanded version]** The Túatha Dé arrive in Ireland and demand either kingship or a division of the island from the Fir Bolg. War follows. Nuada and the Fir Bolg champion Sreng meet; Sreng severs Nuada’s hand or right arm.

The Túatha Dé win, but Nuada’s mutilation disqualifies him. The expanded first-battle tale emphasizes fair inspection of armies, heroic combats, the arm falling with part of the shield, and a settlement in which surviving Fir Bolg receive territory. These details should not automatically be projected into the shorter *Cath Maige Tuired* notice.

### Abdication and Bres

**[Canonical]** The Túatha Dé and their women debate the kingship. Because Nuada is no longer physically whole, Bres—son of the Fomorian Elatha and the Túatha Dé woman Ériu—is selected. The political hope is that his mixed ancestry will secure alliance with the Fomoire.

Bres proves ungenerous and imposes degrading service and tribute. Coirpre’s satire destroys his legitimacy. Bres is required to relinquish the kingship after a fixed period, and Nuada resumes it.

The episode joins physical perfection to kingship but also makes hospitality and economic justice essential. Nuada’s mere bodily restoration does not defeat the Fomorians; political liberation requires Lug’s skills and coalition.

### The silver hand and Míach’s restoration

**[Canonical; internally layered]** Dían Cécht, assisted by Crédne, provides a silver hand “with the movement of any other hand.” Míach, Dían Cécht’s son, considers this inadequate. He joins the severed limb to Nuada and heals it over nine days.

The tale then shifts to conflict between Dían Cécht and Míach: the father kills the son out of professional jealousy. Nuada does not intervene in the surviving text. The narrative therefore preserves two solutions—the marvelous prosthesis and organic regeneration—without fully resolving why “Silverhand” remains his defining epithet.

### Lug’s admission and Nuada’s transfer of authority

**[Canonical]** At Nuada’s feast at Tara, Lug arrives claiming competence in every necessary art. The doorkeeper initially refuses each individual skill because the court already has a specialist. Lug asks whether the court has one person possessing all skills. Nuada admits him after tests.

Concluding that Lug may free the Túatha Dé from Fomorian bondage, Nuada exchanges seats with him for thirteen days. This is not necessarily a permanent abdication. It is a formal recognition of Lug’s superior capacity for the immediate crisis.

### Second Battle and death

**[Canonical]** Lug organizes the crafts, magic, medicine, law, and military power of the Túatha Dé. During the battle, Balor kills Nuada Silverhand and Macha. Lug then destroys Balor’s poisonous eye with a slingstone and turns its force against the Fomorian army.

Some modern retellings make Nuada duel Balor at length, sacrifice himself deliberately, or formally bequeath the crown to Lug. The surviving *Cath Maige Tuired* gives only the compressed statement that Nuada and Macha fell at Balor’s hands. The elaborate duel is modern narrative supplementation.

### Welsh plague-king narrative

**[Regional medieval elaboration]** In *Lludd and Llefelys*, Lludd inherits Britain and confronts three oppressions. His brother Llefelys diagnoses them from France:

1. the Coraniaid, who can hear every conversation;
2. a terrifying annual scream caused by battling dragons;
3. a magician who causes provisions to disappear.

Lludd destroys the Coraniaid with a poisonous mixture harmless to Britons, imprisons the dragons at Dinas Emrys, and defeats the magician by remaining awake. The tale connects Lludd with wise kingship and restoration but contains neither Nuada’s arm nor Mag Tuired.

### Gwyn, Creiddylad, and May Day

**[Welsh regional tradition]** Gwyn ap Nudd abducts Creiddylad, daughter of Lludd Silver-Hand, from Gwythyr. Arthur rescues her and decrees that the rivals fight each Calan Mai until Judgment Day, when the victor will receive her. Lludd is only genealogically present. Modern versions that make Nuada preside over the contest are unattested.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Lydney Park

**[Secure archaeological cult]** The sanctuary occupies an Iron Age hillfort above the Severn in Gloucestershire. Pre-Roman occupation is secure, but excavators did not establish a pre-Roman Nodens temple. The visible monumental sanctuary belongs mainly to the late third or fourth century, with a major building phase after approximately 364–367.

The complex included:

- an unusual Romano-Celtic temple;
- a mosaic-floored cella;
- baths;
- a long building interpreted as an *abaton*, dormitory, or pilgrim accommodation;
- a guesthouse or courtyard building;
- precinct works;
- workshops and activity connected with the surrounding mining settlement.

Coins and later occupation indicate activity into the late fourth or possibly fifth century, but the exact final date of organized worship is unknown.

### Personnel and finance

The mosaic says that Titus Flavius Senilis laid the pavement **from offerings**—*ex stipibus*—direct evidence that accumulated donations financed monumental work. Victorinus, *interpres*, supervised or interpreted; “dream interpreter” is a plausible but not compulsory translation. No complete priestly hierarchy survives.

### Healing and incubation

Bathing facilities, anatomical votives, dog images, accommodation, and the possibility of an interpreter of dreams support a healing pilgrimage center where incubation may have occurred. Actual instructions for sleeping in the sanctuary do not survive. “Dogs licked patients’ wounds” is comparative inference from Mediterranean healing cults, not a documented Lydney ritual.

### Vows, offerings, and curses

Votive formulae show worshippers fulfilling vows. The Silvianus tablet shows conditional donation and coercive prayer: Nodens is asked to deny health until a ring is returned. This was not necessarily marginal “black magic”; curse and restitution tablets formed part of ordinary provincial religious practice.

The proposed identification of the stolen ring with a gold ring from Silchester/The Vyne, inscribed with the name Senicianus, is ingenious but unproved. The objects have no secure shared archaeological context.

### Cockersand Moss

A Mars statuette with *Deo Marti Nodonti* indicates worship well north of Lydney. Because the object is lost and no sanctuary has been excavated, the scale and character of this cult cannot be reconstructed.

### Irish cult

There is no known Irish temple, altar, inscription, priesthood, festival, or votive deposit securely dedicated to Nuada. Tara and Mag Tuired are literary settings, not demonstrated Nuada cult centers. Claims of an ancient feast day on May 1 or Lughnasa derive from modern extrapolation, often through the Gwyn–Gwythyr contest or general Celtic calendrical festivals.

### Sacrifice and contested practices

No source documents human sacrifice, temple prostitution, ritual mutilation, or a dedicated warrior cult for Nuada/Nodens. Caesar and Lucan discuss alleged or literary Celtic human sacrifice in other contexts, but neither connects it to this deity. Applying those passages to Nuada would be guilt by pantheon rather than evidence.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Roman Britain, approximately second–fourth centuries

The name is first materially visible in Britain. Nodens receives Latin dedications and undergoes identification with Mars. His best-documented center combines local cult with Roman architecture, epigraphic formulae, mosaic art, monetary offerings, bathing, and curse-tablet practice.

Whether this cult developed from an Iron Age deity cannot presently be demonstrated. The Iron Age occupation beneath Lydney supplies continuity of place, not continuity of divine name.

### End of Roman rule, fifth–seventh centuries

The organized sanctuary declined after Roman administration and its monetary economy collapsed. There is no documented Christian edict specifically closing the temple and no account of a forced conversion directed against Nodens’s worshippers. Destruction by Christians, revival under Julian, and violent suppression have all been proposed in older writing, but the archaeological phases do not securely narrate those events.

Names cognate with Nodens persisted in Britain. Linguistic survival is compatible with cultural memory but cannot establish uninterrupted cult.

### Medieval Ireland, ninth–sixteenth centuries

Christian literati preserved and transformed Núadu within vernacular pseudo-history and mythological narrative. A god-like ruler became an ancestor-era king placed into a universal history beginning with Creation and the Flood. The narratives nevertheless retained supernatural craft, bodily restoration, magical weapons, and divine collectivities.

The surviving manuscript gap is crucial: the richest text is many centuries later than Roman Nodens and survives in a sixteenth-century copy. Its content may preserve much older material, but dating individual motifs to pagan antiquity requires argument, not assumption.

### Medieval Wales, eleventh–fourteenth centuries

Nudd/Lludd appears in Arthurian lists, supernatural genealogies, and British pseudo-history. The silver-hand epithet links him strongly with Irish Núadu. Yet Welsh tradition developed independent narratives: Gwyn’s otherworldly role and Lludd’s plague-removal story have no direct Irish counterpart.

### Early modern antiquarianism

Welsh and Irish manuscripts entered antiquarian circulation. Geoffrey of Monmouth’s Latin *Historia Regum Britanniae* had already transformed **Lud son of Heli** into a legendary British king and eponym of London; Welsh translators identified this Lud with Lludd son of Beli. Renaissance and later historians often treated such kings as literal national history.

### Nineteenth-century Celtic studies and revival

Comparative philology connected Nodens, Nuada, and Nudd. John Rhys interpreted the complex through Celtic religion, wealth, water, and solar or sky-god models characteristic of his era. Some of those broad “Celtic Zeus” reconstructions are no longer accepted.

Irish Revival writers popularized the mythology. Lady Gregory’s *Gods and Fighting Men* (1904) arranged Irish-language materials into accessible English narrative, presenting Nuada’s silver hand, Bres’s reign, Míach’s healing, Lug’s arrival, and the battles as a coherent cycle. Her work is a modern literary synthesis, not a translation of a single manuscript.

### Twentieth century to present

The Wheelers’ 1928–1929 excavation and 1932 report established Lydney as the archaeological center of Nodens studies. Tolkien’s appendix “The Name Nodens” provided a durable philological synthesis. Elizabeth Gray’s 1982 edition of *Cath Maige Tuired* made a more reliable Irish text and translation widely usable. Later reassessments emphasized that healing, maritime, hunting, and martial interpretations overlap but are not interchangeable.

Contemporary Celtic polytheists and some Druid practitioners worship or honor Nuada/Nodens. This is revival or reconstruction after a long documentary break, not demonstrable institutional continuity from Lydney or medieval Ireland.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Irish Literary Revival

Lady Gregory’s 1904 retelling remains one of the chief sources for modern popular Nuada. It smooths manuscript variants into connected prose and supplies dialogue and transitions. W. B. Yeats’s broader Celtic Revival milieu helped reposition the Túatha Dé as national and esoteric symbols, though Nuada is less central in Yeats’s own canon than figures such as the Sidhe, Lugh, or Cuchulain.

### Arthur Machen and H. P. Lovecraft

Arthur Machen used the form **Nodens** in the pseudo-antique Latin inscription concluding *The Great God Pan* (1894), drawing on antiquarian Celtic religion. H. P. Lovecraft then transformed Nodens into the “Lord of the Great Abyss,” an elder supernatural power opposed to Nyarlathotep, notably in *The Dream-Quest of Unknown Kadath*, written in 1926–1927 and published posthumously in 1943.

**[Modern invention]** Lovecraft’s elderly, chariot-riding cosmic Nodens is not an ancient Celtic description. Later “Cthulhu Mythos” taxonomies that classify Nodens as an Elder God are twentieth-century fiction.

### Tolkien

Tolkien’s role is documentary: he wrote the philological appendix to the Wheelers’ excavation report. Claims that the Lydney curse and the Silchester ring directly generated *The Lord of the Rings*, or that Celebrimbor is simply Nuada, remain speculative. Tolkien’s name **Celebrimbor**, “silver-fist/hand,” offers an evocative linguistic parallel, but direct derivation from Nuada has not been demonstrated by an authorial statement.

### Neopagan and Druid reception

Modern practitioners variously understand Nuada as:

- god of kingship and just rule;
- divine amputee and patron of prosthetics or disability;
- healer;
- warrior and protector;
- water or sea deity;
- guarantor of sovereignty and social order.

These readings usually combine Irish narrative with Lydney archaeology. No surviving ancient rite tells worshippers how to invoke him, so contemporary prayers, feast days, altar colors, tarot correspondences, and offerings are modern religious creation. Their modernity does not make them insincere; it does make them historically distinct.

Modern Druid institutions themselves are revival organizations: the Druid Order dates from the early twentieth century, OBOD from 1964, the British Druid Order from 1979, and Ár nDraíocht Féin from 1985. Individual use of Nuada within them varies, and no reliable census isolates his worshippers.

### Popular media

Nuada-inspired characters appear in comics, fantasy fiction, role-playing games, and electronic games. A particularly influential reinvention is **Prince Nuada Silverlance** in Guillermo del Toro’s *Hellboy II: The Golden Army* (2008), a militant elven prince bearing little beyond name, royalty, and silver imagery from the medieval figure. Marvel and other fantasy settings similarly recast him as a generic Celtic war or sky god.

**[Modern invention]** Descriptions of Nuada as a fixed “sun god,” “Atlantean king,” “lord of all Celtic gods,” or owner of Fragarach typically derive from modern occultism, comics, role-playing material, or unsourced internet synthesis.

### Political appropriation

Nuada participates in generalized Celtic nationalist and heritage imagery, especially through kingship and resistance to foreign oppressors. No major, sustained extremist cult focused specifically on him is documented in the sources consulted. The absence should be distinguished from broader far-right appropriation of European pagan symbols.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Nodens the same figure as Nuada and Nudd?

The name correspondence is strong and the shared silver-hand epithet between Irish and Welsh traditions is unusually specific. The historical model is nevertheless one of related cultural reflexes, not a recoverable single biography. Roman Nodens’s healing and aquatic cult cannot simply be copied into every medieval Nuada episode; conversely, no Roman inscription calls Nodens king of the Túatha Dé.

### Was Nodens Irish, British, or pan-Celtic?

Tolkien argued that the western British distribution and Irish evidence might indicate a Goidelic god introduced eastward. The absence of Gaulish attestations weighs against calling him securely pan-Celtic. Later scholarship is more cautious: the British and Irish names can descend from shared Insular Celtic inheritance without requiring a historically documented migration of the cult.

### What was his primary function?

Competing models emphasize:

- Mars and warfare;
- healing and incubation;
- water and fishing;
- hunting;
- prosperity and acquisition;
- kingship.

The evidence supports a multifunctional sanctuary or a god whose functions do not fit modern categories. Determining a single “original domain” exceeds the record.

### What exactly was lost: hand or arm?

Irish *lám* can encompass hand and arm. The shorter *Cath Maige Tuired* formulation says “hand”; the expanded First Battle has the right arm severed at the shoulder. “Silver Hand” and “Silver Arm” are therefore both defensible translations, but they reflect variant narrative precision.

### Did the silver prosthesis restore his kingship?

The text first emphasizes that Dían Cécht’s hand moves normally, then separately has Míach restore flesh. Nuada’s eventual return follows Bres’s fall, but the precise legal moment at which he becomes eligible is not formally stated. Retellings often make Míach’s operation the decisive constitutional cure; this is reasonable inference, not explicit legal commentary.

### Is Nuada’s sword the Sword of Light or Fragarach?

The Findias sword is explicit. Its names **Claíomh Solais** and **Fragarach** are not. Fragarach is primarily attached in later tradition to Manannán and Lugh. Treating all marvelous Irish swords as one artifact is modern system-building.

### Archaeological interpretation at Lydney

The Wheelers’ excavation was foundational but reflected early twentieth-century methods. Later work has questioned exact phasing, the “dream interpreter,” the purpose of the long building, and overconfident comparison with classical incubation sanctuaries. The healing interpretation remains probable rather than proven in every detail.

### The Ring hypothesis

The Lydney tablet curses Senicianus over a ring; a Roman gold ring found near Silchester bears Senicianus’s name. They could be connected, but distance, incomplete provenance, and the frequency of the name prevent certainty. Claims that this pair directly inspired Tolkien’s One Ring add another unproved step.

### Evidence state

The Lydney mosaics were already damaged by mining, stone removal, and early excavation. Many remains were reburied for protection. The Cockersand statuette is lost. Irish and Welsh texts survive in manuscripts centuries later than the social worlds they depict. Consequently, the deity has unusually rich comparative evidence but no continuous archive.
