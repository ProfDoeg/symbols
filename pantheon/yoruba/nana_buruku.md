# Nana Buruku (Nanã): Research Dossier

**Class:** primordial vodun or supreme creator in several Fon/Dahomean accounts; localized vodun and pilgrimage deity farther west; adopted or cognate òrìṣà in western Yoruba-speaking regions; female orixá/vodum in Brazilian Candomblé and Umbanda; comparatively rare Arará/Lucumí divinity in Cuba. Some West African traditions describe the being as female, some as androgynous, and some distinguish a masculine Nana Densu. These classifications are historically and regionally different, not interchangeable.

**Earliest secure attestation presently identified:** an official German colonial report of **4 October 1896**, quoted and contextualized by Pierre Verger, identifies Siadé/Siaré—now usually associated with the Adele/Adélé area near the Ghana–Togo frontier—as the seat of the powerful “idol” **Buruku**. Its ruler-priest was arrested during a German expedition; the report describes cult staffs, sacrificial implements, blood, and poultry feathers. This securely attests Buruku and a regional cult, but not yet the complete later figure “Nanã, primordial mother of the mud.” Alfred Burdon Ellis’s *The Eʋe-Speaking Peoples of the Slave Coast* (1890) is earlier general evidence for Ewe/Fon religion, but a verified occurrence of this divine name has not been established here. The first detailed, securely datable account of **Nana Buluku as the primordial parent of Mawu and Lisa** is Melville J. Herskovits’s fieldwork-based *Dahomey: An Ancient West African Kingdom* (1938). No precolonial inscription, manuscript, securely identified ancient image, or archaeological object bearing the name is presently known.

**Evidence labels used below:** **[EA]** securely attested early documentary evidence; **[OT]** recorded oral tradition; **[RV]** regional variant; **[LS]** later scholarly or priestly systematization; **[SY]** syncretistic identification; **[F]** folklore; **[MR]** modern religious retelling or reception; **[MI]** demonstrably modern invention or unsupported Internet-era claim.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

“Nana Buruku” is not one historically uniform being. The dossier’s central finding is a family of overlapping divine identities distributed from central Ghana and Togo through Benin and western Nigeria and subsequently remade in the Atlantic world.

### Names

- **Nàná Bùkùú / Nàná Buruku / Nàná Brukung**: spellings recorded by Pierre Verger across Benin, Togo, Ghana, and Yoruba-speaking districts. **[OT/RV]**
- **Buruku, Bùkùú, Brukung**: independent or shortened cult names, especially toward the western end of the distribution. **[EA/RV]**
- **Nana Buluku**: the standard ethnographic spelling for the primordial being in twentieth-century accounts of Dahomean/Fon cosmology. **[LS]**
- **Nanã Buruku, Nanã Buruquê, Nanã Borocô, Nanamburucu, Anamburucu**: Portuguese and liturgical variants in Brazil. **[RV/SY]**
- **Naná Burukú, Nana Baruke, Nanán Baruqué**: Spanish/Lucumí forms in Cuba and later Spanish-language manuals. **[RV/SY]**
- **Nana Densu**: a reportedly masculine Ewe/Mina vodun, sometimes made the husband of Mami Wata. It should not automatically be equated with every Nana Buluku. **[RV]**

Verger found that *nana/nàná* is a deferential designation for an old or respected person in Akan-speaking areas and can mean “mother” among Fon, Ewe, and Guang speakers. It is therefore more plausibly an honorific—“Mother,” “Elder,” “Grandmother”—than an ordinary personal name. The second element’s etymology remains unsettled. Derivation from Yoruba *burúkú* (“bad, evil, ill-fated”) is a popular folk etymology but linguistically and historically inadequate: **Bùkùú/Brukung** occurs as a divine and place-associated name outside a purely Yoruba setting, and Verger’s distributional evidence points toward a wider Guang–Adele–Mahi complex. Claims that the full name means “wicked grandmother,” “mother of evil,” “birth-mother,” or “the ancient one who creates” are **[F/MI]** unless supported by a specific language, tonal form, and historical source.

### Divine classifications

1. **Dahomean/Fon Nana Buluku:** primordial being prior to Mawu and Lisa; creator or first cause who retires after bringing forth the paired creators. Herskovits reported that she had recognized precedence but no clear separate cult in the communities he studied. **[OT/LS]**
2. **Adele/Guang/Atakpamé–Tchetti Brukung/Bùkùú:** powerful localized deity with priesthood, initiation, pilgrimage, staffs, sacrifices, dance, and a territorial sanctuary. **[EA/OT/RV]**
3. **Western Yoruba and Ana/Nago Nana:** an adopted or frontier òrìṣà, important at Mẹko and in districts adjoining Dahomey but not comparably central in eastern Yoruba religion. William Bascom explicitly says Nana Buruku was important at Mẹko and in Dahomey, but not among eastern Yoruba. **[OT/RV]**
4. **Brazilian Nanã:** an aged female orixá/vodum of still water, mud, death, seniority, and ancestral memory, bearing the *ibiri* and often treated as mother of Obaluaiê/Omolu. **[RV/SY]**
5. **Cuban Naná Burukú:** a comparatively rare, strongly Arará-associated divinity, sometimes absorbed under Obatalá or received through particular Arará/Lucumí lineages. **[RV/SY]**

Calling her simply “a Yoruba goddess” reverses much of the recoverable history. The evidence instead suggests a deity or cluster of deities circulating across an old multilingual frontier and entering Yoruba, Fon, Ewe, Guang, and Atlantic systems in different forms.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The documentary threshold

No ancient written corpus comparable to Greek hymns, Mesopotamian tablets, or Sanskrit scriptures exists for Nanã. The traditions were transmitted primarily through ritual practice, praise poetry, divination verses, initiation, song, and priestly memory. Dates below are therefore usually dates of documentation, not dates of origin.

### Principal evidence

#### 1896: the Siadé colonial report

**[EA]** Lieutenant Count Zech, then head of the German post at Kete-Krachi, reported an expedition against Siadé/Siaré on 4 October 1896. He described it as the former capital of the Adjuti/Atyuti country and seat of the widely renowned “idol Buruku.” Kings of Dagomba, Asante, Gonja, and neighboring polities reportedly sought its protection in war through embassies and gifts. The ruler-priest resisted German authority and was seized by surprise. Zech found iron-pointed cult objects with pear-shaped handles and a wooden staff marked by blood and poultry feathers. The report proves a politically significant Buruku sanctuary and sacrificial cult; it does **not** prove that this Buruku was already understood as the Fon creator and mother of Mawu-Lisa. Verger expressly entertained the possibility that western Brukung and eastern Nana Buruku were two cults whose names later became confused. [Verger’s transcription and analysis](https://terreirosdeportugal.pt/wp-content/uploads/2016/10/224166186-Orixas-Pierre-Verger-pdf-1.pdf)

#### 1934: J. C. Guinness’s Adele material

**[OT]** Verger summarizes an unpublished or difficult-to-access study by J. C. Guinness. Its migration legend places Brukung in a cavern beneath the Niger/Kwara with a wife, child, and servant Langa; after offerings were stolen and migrations followed, the cult reached the forest and ultimately Shiari. Verger treats this not as literal history but as possible encoded memory of east–west migration. The story notably makes Brukung male or at least gives Brukung a wife, sharply contradicting the later standardized image of a female grandmother.

#### 1938: Herskovits’s *Dahomey*

**[OT/LS]** Melville and Frances Herskovits’s Dahomey fieldwork supplies the influential cosmological scheme: before Nana Buluku produced Mawu and Lisa, nothing existed for the twins to administer; she assigned night and moon to female Mawu and day and sun to male Lisa, then withdrew. Herskovits stated that Nana Buluku’s precedence was acknowledged but found no distinct cult devoted to her. Later encyclopedias have often converted this localized ethnographic statement into a universal “West African creation myth,” obscuring its source and limits. [Bibliographic record for Herskovits’s *Dahomey*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Dahomey.html?id=l-FEAAAAMAAJ)

#### Ifá and cowrie-divination corpora

**[OT/RV]** Bascom’s *Ifa Divination: Communication between Gods and Men in West Africa* (1969) is more useful for locating Nana geographically than for supplying a universal Nanã cycle. He reports Nana Buruku/Buku as an important divinity at **Mẹko** and in Dahomey, while noting her absence from his comparison set of Ifẹ̀ verses. This is evidence against assuming that she was always a pan-Yoruba deity.

Bascom’s *Sixteen Cowries* (1980), Wande Abimbola’s published Ifá collections, and Epega and Neimark’s later English-language collections do not yield a stable, early, pan-Yoruba Nanã biography. Modern compilations sometimes attach her to particular odù, but without a recorded informant, place, date, and verse text such allocations remain lineage-specific **[RV]** or modern systematization **[LS/MR]**, not an ancient canon. Bascom’s distinction between Mẹko and Ifẹ̀ is especially important because it demonstrates internal Yoruba variation rather than an undifferentiated “Yoruba pantheon.”

#### Oríkì and ritual songs

**[OT]** Verger recorded praise lines at Ketu and Abeokuta which call her:

- owner of a staff;
- one whose red-spattered clothing resembles blood;
- the òrìṣà who makes Fon people speak Nago;
- a mother originally from the Bariba region;
- “still water that kills suddenly”;
- one who kills a goat without a knife.

These lines link staff, blood, multilingual frontier identity, dangerous still water, and ironless sacrifice. They are among the most compact primary oral witnesses to the cult. They do not mention primordial mud or the creation of humanity.

At Tchetti, Verger recorded Ana-language pilgrimage songs. One says, approximately, “They deceived me, saying it was not far; accompany me—it is not far”; another refers to inability to climb, returning home without speaking of what was seen, and accepting only what Inie grants. **[OT]** These are cultic performance texts, not narrative myths.

#### Brazilian and Cuban ethnography

- Roger Bastide’s *Le Candomblé de Bahia* (1958) describes the ritual system of Nagô Candomblé rather than furnishing an ancient Nanã scripture. [Digitized bibliographic copy](https://bdor.sibi.ufrj.br/handle/doc/361?locale=pt_BR)
- Pierre Verger’s *Notes sur le culte des orisa et vodun* (1957) and later *Orixás* compare West African and Bahian practices and preserve crucial observations, names, songs, pilgrimage traditions, and material culture. [Bibliographic record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark%3A/12148/cb31559277q)
- Lydia Cabrera’s *El Monte* (1954) records Cuban Lucumí, Arará, and Congo religious knowledge through named and unnamed practitioners. It is primary ethnographic testimony for mid-twentieth-century Cuba, not a transparent transcript of pre-slavery Africa. [Book record](https://books.google.ba/books?id=8cbXAAAAMAAJ)
- Natalia Bolívar’s *Los orishas en Cuba* records a Naná Burukú *pattakí* and modern Cuban ritual classifications, but these must be dated to the book and its informants rather than projected unchanged into Africa. [Digital copy](https://iworos.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/01/Los-Orisha-en-Cuba.pdf)
- Reginaldo Prandi’s *Mitologia dos Orixás* (2001) assembles 301 myths from earlier ethnographies and living Brazilian traditions. It is the best large concordance for Brazilian narratives, but a compilation rather than an ancient canon. [Publisher-indexed Google Books record](https://books.google.com.br/books?hl=pt-BR&id=Jp7aDwAAQBAJ)

Samuel Johnson’s *History of the Yorubas* (completed 1897; published 1921), Idowu’s *Olódùmarè* (1962), and the better-known Ifẹ̀/Oyo archaeological literature do not establish an ancient central cult of Nanã. The famous naturalistic Ifẹ̀ heads, broadly dated to the twelfth–fifteenth centuries, have no identifying inscription or securely demonstrated Nanã iconography. Assigning one to her would be **[MI]**.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### West African domains

The earliest securely documented western Buruku is a territorially powerful protector sought by rulers, especially in war. **[EA]** The later Tchetti/Atakpamé complex centers on pilgrimage, initiated elders, sacred staffs, an important tree, red ritual substances, and guarded knowledge. **[OT]**

The Fon cosmological Nana Buluku is more remote: prior cause and parent of Mawu-Lisa, then withdrawn. **[OT/LS]** She is not there primarily “goddess of swamps.”

### Brazilian domains

In Brazilian Candomblé, Nanã is characteristically:

- the oldest female orixá, addressed as grandmother;
- ruler of still, deep, or marshy water, mud, swamps, and flooded earth;
- associated with primordial matter from which human bodies can be modeled;
- receiver of bodies at death and therefore a mediator between generation, burial, ancestry, and renewed life;
- a grave, slow, dignified elder;
- mother or senior relation of Obaluaiê/Omolu and frequently of Oxumarê and Ewá.

These are **[RV/SY]** Afro-Brazilian developments, though some preserve recognizable West African elements: seniority, staff, slow dance, ironless killing, and association with the Sapata/Obaluaiê complex.

### The *ibiri*

The Brazilian emblem is the **ibiri**, a curved or bundled ritual scepter made from palm ribs or fiber, often decorated with cowries. Devotional interpretation makes it an instrument for controlling the dead, sweeping illness, or drawing primordial mud. Verger’s West African comparison is more cautious: western initiates carried large red-spattered staffs, and Brazilian possession dance imitates an aged person holding a staff. The specifically cowrie-covered Brazilian *ibiri* is not securely documented in the 1896 report.

### Iron taboo

Nanã’s offerings in Brazilian and related traditions are killed without an iron knife; descriptions mention strangling, twisting the neck, or cutting with a sharpened reed or bamboo implement. Verger’s oríkì explicitly says she kills a goat without a knife. **[OT/RV]**

Two explanations coexist:

- **[OT/etiological myth]** Nanã rejected Ogun’s claim that iron made him indispensable and demonstrated that sacrifice could be performed without his tools.
- **[LS/historical interpretation]** her cult represents or preserves a religious stratum symbolically anterior to the spread of ironworking.

The second is an attractive interpretation, not archaeological proof that the cult literally predates the Iron Age. Moreover, the 1896 Siadé sanctuary reportedly contained iron-pointed objects. That contradiction supports Verger’s warning that western Brukung and eastern/Brazilian Nanã may not be a single uniform cult.

### Colors, day, animals, and foods

Brazilian houses variously assign white and dark blue, lilac or purple, or blue-white patterned beads; Monday is shared with Obaluaiê in some houses, Saturday with water divinities in others. Verger records white beads striped with blue, the salutation **“Salúba!”**, goat and guinea-fowl offerings without knives, and okra dishes without palm oil. These are **[RV]**, not universal attributes. Purple, July 26, and a standardized list of “personality traits of Nanã’s children” became especially prominent in twentieth- and twenty-first-century Umbanda and popular manuals. **[LS/MR]**

No securely identified precolonial anthropomorphic portrait of Nana Buruku is known. Living iconography consists chiefly of consecrated containers, stones, water, staffs, beadwork, ritual dress, possession performance, and, more recently, devotional statues and illustrations.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Competing genealogies

1. **Fon/Dahomean cosmology:** Nana Buluku produces Mawu and Lisa, assigns their complementary domains, and retires. Mawu-Lisa then orders the world and produces or governs the subsequent vodun. **[OT/LS]**
2. **Abomey Sapata genealogy recorded by Verger:** Nàná Bùkùú is mother of the couple Kohosu and Nyohwe Ananu, parents of the Sapata deities. **[OT/RV]**
3. **Brazilian Candomblé:** Nanã is usually mother of Obaluaiê/Omolu; some traditions add Oxumarê and Ewá, and occasionally Ossaim or Iroko. Oxalá/Oxalufã may be consort or father of her children. **[RV]**
4. **Cuban Arará/Lucumí:** genealogies vary by lineage. Naná may be Obatalá-like or received under Obatalá, mother or senior of Asojano/Babalú-Ayé, or an independent Arará power. **[RV]**
5. **Guinness’s Brukung legend:** Brukung has a wife, a child, and the servant Langa. **[OT/RV]** This may describe another deity sharing the name.

### Documented divine interactions

The following are discrete interactions located in the surveyed published sources. “No interaction” means that co-membership in a pantheon has not been converted into a fictitious encounter.

- **Mawu — birth and assignment:** Nana Buluku brings forth female Mawu and assigns her night and the moon; Herskovits, *Dahomey* (1938). **[OT/LS]**
- **Lisa — birth and assignment:** Nana Buluku brings forth male Lisa and assigns him day and the sun; Herskovits (1938). **[OT/LS]**
- **Mawu-Lisa — withdrawal:** after producing the paired creator, Nana Buluku leaves ordering of the world to Mawu-Lisa; Herskovits (1938). **[OT/LS]**
- **Kohosu — maternity:** Nàná Bùkùú is his mother in an Abomey account collected by Verger in the twentieth century. **[OT/RV]**
- **Nyohwe Ananu — maternity:** Nàná is mother of the female member of the pair who generate the Sapata powers; Verger’s Abomey material. **[OT/RV]**
- **Sapata/Xapanã/Obaluaiê/Omolu — maternal relation:** west/east African cults sometimes merge Nanã with or make her mother of the disease-and-earth deity; Brazil standardizes her as Obaluaiê’s mother. Verger, mid-twentieth-century fieldwork; Prandi, 2001 compilation. **[RV/SY]**
- **Obaluaiê/Omolu — abandonment and rescue:** in a Brazilian myth Nanã abandons the sore-covered child; Yemanjá finds and raises or heals him. Prandi’s compilation (2001), drawing on Brazilian oral/ethnographic sources. **[RV]**
- **Oxumarê — maternity:** widely given in Brazilian Candomblé as Nanã’s child and temple companion, but not a universal Fon genealogy; Verger and later Brazilian sources. **[RV/SY]**
- **Ewá — maternity:** found in Brazilian classifications; source tradition is regional and later than the Fon Mawu-Lisa account. **[RV]**
- **Oxalá/Oxalufã — procreation:** Brazilian narrative makes Nanã bear a child by Oxalufã; indexed in Prandi (2001). **[RV]**
- **Oxalá/Obatalá — human creation:** Nanã supplies or retrieves mud with which Oxalá forms human bodies; modern Brazilian oral and liturgical accounts, systematized in twentieth-century literature. **[RV/LS]**
- **Oxalá — Egungun power:** a Brazilian story says Nanã controlled the dead; Oxalá disguised himself in her clothes, learned the secret, and transferred male authority over Egungun. This is an etiological gender-and-office myth, not early African history. **[RV/LS]**
- **Ogun — quarrel over iron:** Nanã refuses Ogun’s metal, kills sacrificial animals without a knife, and excludes iron from her rites; Brazilian myth collected in modern compilations, with an African oríkì paralleling the ironless killing. **[OT/RV]**
- **Oduduwa — temporal precedence:** a mythic-historical interpretation places Nanã/Yemowo and the old water-mud order before Oduduwa’s arrival with Ogun and iron. Verger presents this as a hypothesis suggested by oral tradition, not established chronology. **[OT/MR]**
- **Oranmiyan — primordial waters:** Verger notes that Oyo tradition attributes the encounter with primordial waters to Oranmiyan rather than Oduduwa; Nanã’s waters are connected interpretively, but a direct encounter is not securely narrated. **[RV; no direct interaction established]**
- **Yemanjá — fosterage rivalry/co-motherhood:** in the Brazilian Omolu cycle Yemanjá rescues the child abandoned by Nanã. **[RV]**
- **Ikú (Death) — authority or concealment:** some contemporary devotional retellings say Nanã concealed, restrained, or houses Death. A securely traceable early text has not been located; treat as **[MR]**.
- **Mami Wata — marriage to Nana Densu:** reported for an Ewe/Mina masculine Nana variant. This concerns Nana Densu and cannot automatically be transferred to female Nanã Buruku. **[RV]**
- **Dan/Aido Hwedo — alleged co-creation:** some modern summaries associate Nana Buluku with the cosmic serpent in producing Mawu-Lisa. In Herskovits’s better-attested scheme, Aido Hwedo belongs principally to the subsequent ordering of the world under Mawu, not clearly to Nana’s act of parturition. **[LS/MR; disputed]**
- **Orunmila/Ifá — no secure major encounter:** Nana is assigned to local divination configurations at Mẹko and in later manuals, but the surveyed Bascom and Abimbola publications do not establish a pan-Yoruba narrative encounter. **[Absence of evidence]**
- **Shango, Oshun, Eshu, Oya, and other widely worshipped òrìṣà — no specific early interaction established:** modern charts place them in one pantheon, but that does not document an episode. **[Absence of evidence]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Nana Buluku and the birth of Mawu-Lisa

**[OT/LS; documented 1938]** At the beginning, Nana Buluku exists before the ordered cosmos. She brings forth Mawu and Lisa, differentiated as female/moon/night/coolness and male/sun/day/heat. She assigns their spheres and withdraws. Mawu-Lisa becomes the immediate creator or organizer.

The myth’s historical significance lies partly in its layered supremacy: Nana is prior in generation, while Mawu-Lisa is active in governance. Later summaries often say Nana alone “created the universe,” whereas Herskovits distinguishes origination from ordering. The account belongs specifically to recorded Dahomean cosmology and should not be presented as an ancient pan-Yoruba creation story.

### The primordial mud and creation of human beings

**[RV/LS; Brazilian]** Oxalá is charged with making human bodies but lacks suitable material. Nanã, mistress of the deep, still, muddy water, retrieves or provides clay. Human beings are molded from this mud, live through divine breath or power, and return to Nanã’s earth-and-water matrix at death.

The story integrates Nanã into Yoruba-derived creation theology by making her the material complement to Oxalá’s formative agency. Its widespread modern presentation as an immemorial Yoruba myth exceeds the evidence. Verger’s recorded African praise poetry stresses still water and ironless sacrifice, while the fully elaborated “mud of humanity” cycle is best documented in Brazil.

### Nanã against Ogun

**[RV; twentieth-century documentation]** The òrìṣà debate which power is indispensable. Ogun’s iron tools appear to make him supreme. Nanã refuses the claim, excludes iron from her domain, and shows that she can perform sacrifice without a metal blade. Hence animals offered to her are killed without iron, often by twisting the neck or using nonmetal implements.

The myth explains an actual ritual prohibition and may encode the encounter between cult systems differently related to iron technology. Yet “Nanã historically predates the Iron Age” remains a scholarly or devotional inference. The iron-pointed objects found at Siadé in 1896 prevent treating iron avoidance as universal to every Buruku cult.

### Nanã, Omolu, and Yemanjá

**[RV; Brazilian]** Nanã gives birth to a child covered in sores or bodily deformity and abandons him near water. Yemanjá finds, cleans, nourishes, and raises him; he becomes Omolu/Obaluaiê, lord of epidemic disease, healing, earth, and the dead. Variants alter Nanã’s motive, the child’s condition, and Yemanjá’s role. Some traditions mute the abandonment and emphasize Nanã’s enduring closeness to her son.

This cycle binds Mahi/Jeje-derived Sapata traditions to the Nagô/Yoruba-derived orixá system. It should not be retrojected into Herskovits’s Nana–Mawu-Lisa genealogy.

### Nanã and the power of the dead

**[RV/LS; Brazilian]** Nanã possesses knowledge for summoning or commanding Egungun. Oxalá spies on or deceives her, dresses as a woman, and induces the dead to obey him or male ritual authorities. The tale explains the exclusion of women from certain Egungun offices. Its social function is clearer than its antiquity: it legitimizes a gendered ritual institution by narrating transfer of power from an elder female owner.

### Brukung’s migration

**[OT/RV; recorded 1934]** Brukung lived beneath the Niger/Kwara with a wife, child, and servant. A human devotee made offerings at the riverbank; fishermen stole them, precipitating withdrawal and migration through forest settlements toward Shiari. The story differs in gender, family, landscape, and structure from Nana’s Brazilian myths. Verger suggests it may encode migration, but this is **[modern scholarly reconstruction]**, not demonstrated history.

## 6. Cult, sanctuaries, and ritual practice

### West Africa

Verger mapped Nana/Bùkùú/Brukung traditions across Dassa-Zoumé, Abomey, Savè, Tchetti, Bantè, Kpési, Atakpamé, Adele, and areas toward Abeokuta, Ketu, Mẹko, and Porto-Novo. His crucial conclusion was not simple unity but differentiation: toward the east, Nana could merge with Sapata/Obaluaiê; farther west, Brukung remained an independent cult.

At Atakpamé, devotees said the true deity remained at Shiari and local sanctuaries held only representations. Pilgrimage took place on a three-year cycle and required three journeys. The *Olíbùkùú*, priest of Bùkùú, led pilgrims; the round trip could last approximately three months. Initiatory restrictions extended over six years and three months. Only specified families held participation rights. Death on pilgrimage was interpreted as divine punishment and announced silently by returning the dead person’s staff. **[OT, observed or recorded by Verger]**

At Tchetti, Verger personally observed a vigil followed by dancing beneath a *Ficus*. Elderly initiates, heads shaved and marked with red and white substances, wore cloth, necklaces, and bracelets and carried red-spattered staffs. Their slow, careful, side-stepping dance represented the exhaustion of pilgrimage. Drums, percussion bells, maize beer, millet beer, song, and secrecy structured the event.

The 1896 report’s blood and feathers demonstrate animal sacrifice at Siadé. It does not demonstrate human sacrifice to Buruku. Dahomey’s royal Annual Customs did include historically documented and frequently exaggerated human killing, but no reliable source located here assigns such sacrifice specifically to Nana Buluku. General Dahomean violence must not be silently transferred to every vodun.

### Brazil

Nanã is worshipped in Candomblé Jeje as a vodum and in Ketu/Nagô houses as an orixá. Her ritual object is installed in an *ibá* or other consecrated receptacle; details are house-specific and much initiatory material is not public. Possession dances represent an elderly woman’s slow, stooped, dignified movement, often with hands arranged as if grasping a staff. Offerings reported by Verger include goat and guinea fowl killed without knives, along with okra-based food without palm oil.

Her sanctuary frequently forms part of a ritual family grouping with Obaluaiê/Omolu and Oxumarê. This family cluster is historically important because it reveals the incorporation of Mahi/Jeje powers into Ketu/Nagô taxonomies rather than a pristine, bounded “Yoruba pantheon.”

The formation of Jeje Candomblé in Bahia is historically tied to enslaved Gbe-speaking peoples and the reconstitution of vodun communities under slavery. Luis Nicolau Parés shows that Jeje cult institutions were foundational—not marginal additions—to Bahian Candomblé. [Parés, *A formação do Candomblé*](https://books.scielo.org/id/4qtk5)

### Cuba

In Cuba the cult survived principally through Arará as well as some Lucumí houses. Naná Burukú is rarer than Obatalá, Yemayá, Oshún, or Changó. Some communities treat her as an independent power; others receive her “through” Obatalá or Yemayá, or describe her as absorbed into Obatalá. David H. Brown’s historical approach to Afro-Cuban ritual innovation is useful here: apparent identity is often the result of institutional consolidation, not proof that two African gods were originally one.

Mid- and late-twentieth-century Cuban descriptions assign white-and-blue or multicolored necklaces, nonmetal sacrifice, reed implements, and Catholic correspondences. Verger records Cuban identifications with Our Lady of Mount Carmel or Saint Teresa, whereas modern Internet summaries often substitute Saint Anne. The variation must be retained.

A 1970s study of Cuban “Africa” notes that in Matanzas, Arará practitioners continued making offerings to Shakpata and Naná Burukú in discreet household cult centers even during the revolutionary period. [Historical study of Cuban African religious memory](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/13226/1/537822.pdf)

### Priesthood, temples, and archaeology

No securely excavated ancient “Temple of Nana” or inscribed foundation has been identified. The strongest material record consists of late nineteenth-century colonial descriptions, twentieth-century field photographs and collected objects, living sanctuaries, ritual staffs, receptacles, beads, and performance.

Claims connecting Nanã directly with the archaeological Ifẹ̀ heads are unsupported. Likewise, the absence of her name from Samuel Johnson’s central Yoruba political history is not proof that no local western Yoruba cult existed; it does show that she was not a conspicuous figure in the Oyo-centered historical synthesis Johnson inherited and constructed.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the nineteenth century: reconstructable background

The wide geographic distribution and existence of pilgrimage networks imply a history older than colonial documentation, but its depth cannot be measured securely. Oral migration accounts connect Niger/Kwara, Bariba, Ana, Adele, and Guang zones. Verger hypothesized east–west cult movements and possible fusion of two similar names. **[MR]** Archaeology has not independently dated those movements.

### 1890s–1930s: colonial documentation

The 1896 German expedition supplies the earliest secure named cult event. Colonial seizure of the Siadé chief was simultaneously political suppression and documentation. Guinness’s 1934 work and Herskovits’s 1938 *Dahomey* then captured two very different forms: territorial Brukung and remote primordial Nana Buluku.

### Atlantic transformation under slavery

Enslaved Fon, Mahi, Ewe, Mina, Nago, and Yoruba-speaking people carried cult knowledge to Brazil and Cuba. The evidence does not support a simple one-ship, one-tribe transfer. In Bahia, Jeje vodun institutions and Ketu/Nagô òrìṣà institutions interacted over generations. Nanã became the aged mud-and-death mother and joined the Omolu–Oxumarê cluster. In Cuba, preservation was more restricted and often Arará, with later accommodation to Lucumí initiation structures.

### Nineteenth- and twentieth-century suppression

Brazilian Candomblé communities endured denunciation, police entry, confiscation of sacred objects, medical pathologization, and criminal allegations. Scholarship documents accusations against terreiros in Salvador from 1863 onward and especially intense police/psychiatric persecution in the 1920s–30s. These records concern Candomblé generally; a documented Nanã-specific raid has not been established. [Study of Bahian police and psychiatric repression](https://rbhciencia.emnuvens.com.br/revista/article/view/1055)

Cuban Afro-religions likewise faced slavery-era Catholic domination, republican policing and racialization, and revolutionary ambivalence. Continuity often depended on private houses rather than public temples.

### Late twentieth century to present

Printed ethnographies by Cabrera, Bastide, Verger, Thompson, and others became internal as well as external religious authorities. Prandi’s mythography now circulates among practitioners and on websites, sometimes displacing the local attribution of a story. Nanã’s image has consequently become more standardized: purple-clad grandmother, *ibiri*, swamp, mud, death, Saint Anne, July 26.

Umbanda further reinterprets Nanã through Kardecist ideas of reincarnation, spiritual evolution, “vibrations,” and lines of divine energy. Renato Ortiz’s critique in *A morte branca do feiticeiro negro* treats Umbanda’s formation as social transformation and “whitening,” not the simple survival of an unchanged African religion.

Brazilian Umbanda and Batuque spread to Uruguay and Argentina through initiation networks from Rio Grande do Sul from the 1960s–80s onward. Alejandro Frigerio and María Julia Carozzi show that “transnationalization” involved institutional adaptation, public controversy, and new national identities. Evidence for Nanã specifically is less abundant than evidence for the systems carrying her; therefore claims of a large independent Argentine Nanã cult cannot be made. [Frigerio’s study of transnational Umbanda and Batuque](https://repositoriosdigitales.sicyt.gob.ar/vufind/Record/CONICETDig_b71b9960f459e3c4efda195ee7d5852b)

Living Candomblé and Umbanda communities remain targets of religious racism and attacks on sacred spaces. The Organization of American States’ 2025 review noted reports of aggression against practitioners and invasions of Afro-Brazilian places of worship. [IACHR 2025 annual report](https://www.oas.org/en/iachr/docs/annual/2025/Chapters/IA2025_4A_EN.pdf)

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Catholic identifications

- **Saint Anne/Sant’Ana:** dominant in much of Brazil because both figures are construed as venerable grandmothers. July 26 consequently became a common festival date. **[SY]**
- **Our Lady of Mount Carmel and Saint Teresa:** reported by Verger for Cuba. **[SY/RV]**
- Treating any Catholic saint as Nanã’s “secret true identity” is theological interpretation, not historical fact.

### Literature and visual culture

Nanã appears in Brazilian poetry, popular song, theatre, children’s books, devotional art, and educational treatments of the orixás. Modern illustrations usually portray an elderly Black woman in purple, lilac, white, or blue carrying an *ibiri*. That stable anthropomorphic image is largely modern; older cult presence was often embodied in objects, natural sites, and possessed initiates rather than portraiture.

Afro-Cuban modernism, including Wifredo Lam’s hybrid sacred imagery, materially shaped global expectations of Afro-Atlantic divinity, but no famous Lam figure should be identified as Naná Burukú without a title or artist-supported attribution. Lam himself resisted reducing individual figures to a fixed symbolic code. [Wifredo Lam Foundation](https://www.wifredolam.org/)

### Esotericism, New Age, and neopaganism

Nanã is largely absent from Renaissance Hermetism, the Theosophical canon, and the original Golden Dawn system. Online equivalences with:

- Tiamat,
- Sophia,
- the Celtic Danu,
- Hindu Lakshmi or Durga,
- the biblical “Deep,”
- a universal “Dark Mother,”
- astrology’s Saturn or Pluto,

are **[MI]** unless explicitly presented as an individual practitioner’s modern syncretism. No historical transmission linking Nana Buluku to ancient Mesopotamia, Vedic India, medieval Kabbalah, or Celtic religion has been demonstrated. Similarity of motherhood, water, old age, or a syllable *na/na-na* is not evidence of common origin.

Contemporary “goddess spirituality” and neopagan pages often detach Nanã from initiation, lineage, African languages, sacrifice, and community and recast her as an archetype of menopause, shadow work, grief, ecological mud, or ancestral DNA. These are datable modern receptions, not recoveries of an ancient universal cult.

### Commercial use

Statues, candles, perfumes, colored guides, oracle cards, “offerings kits,” and personality readings are sold internationally. Standardized purple coloring and lists such as “Nanã people are slow, wise, resentful, maternal” partly descend from twentieth-century ethnographic “archetype” writing and partly from commercial astrology-like packaging. They should not be mistaken for the earliest corpus.

## 9. Historiography and contested material

### One goddess or several?

This is the major historical question. Verger’s field evidence led him to propose that western Brukung and eastern Nana Buruku might be distinct beings whose names and cults converged. Evidence favoring differentiation includes:

- western Brukung’s pear-handled cult objects and territorial-political sanctuary;
- the male Brukung with wife and child in the 1934 legend;
- iron objects at Siadé;
- absence in the west of the close Sapata/Obaluaiê fusion found farther east.

Evidence favoring historical relationship includes:

- overlapping names;
- pilgrimage claims linking eastern sanctuaries to Shiari;
- shared staffs and slow pilgrimage dance;
- traditions of movement across Ana, Adele, Bariba, Mahi, and Yoruba areas.

The responsible conclusion is a network of related or assimilated cults, not proven primordial identity.

### Yoruba or Dahomean?

Calling Nanã exclusively Yoruba or exclusively Fon is too simple. Bascom’s Mẹko evidence and Verger’s Ketu/Abeokuta oríkì establish Yoruba-language reception. Herskovits establishes a Dahomean creator figure. Verger’s broader survey points farther west toward Adele/Guang centers. “Adopted into Yoruba religion from Dahomey” is plausible as a broad historical summary, but the frontier was multilingual and bidirectional; it was not a one-time official incorporation into a fixed pantheon.

### Etymology

The respectful title *nana* is comparatively well grounded. The equation of *Buruku* with ordinary Yoruba *burúkú* (“bad”) is disputed and likely folk etymology when applied to the entire transregional name. A definitive historical etymology for Bùkùú/Brukung remains unestablished.

### “Older than iron”

The ritual avoidance of iron is documented; literal prehistoric antiquity is not. The myth contrasts Nanã with Ogun and can be interpreted as memory of different technological or political formations. But cultic symbolism is not a radiocarbon date.

### Human sacrifice

Animal sacrifice is directly documented. Dahomean royal human sacrifice is historically documented in other contexts, although colonial observers often sensationalized numbers and used them politically. No reliable evidence located in this survey identifies human sacrifice as a distinctive rite of Nana Buluku/Nanã. Claims that “the cult demanded virgins” or sacrificed children are **[MI]** absent a primary source.

### Temple prostitution

No evidence was found for institutional “temple prostitution” in this cult. Applying that old comparativist category here would be unsupported.

### Ifá antiquity

The absence of Nanã from a published Ifẹ̀ verse sample does not prove absence from all Ifá. It does demonstrate that modern claims assigning her a universal, fixed place in every odù corpus need source-specific verification. Ifá is an immense, locally transmitted corpus, not a single closed book.

### Archaeology and imagery

Neither Ifẹ̀ heads nor generic prehistoric female figures can be identified as Nanã without inscriptions, continuous provenance, or specific ritual testimony. Such identifications are modern projection.

### Political use and suppression

The 1896 arrest of Siadé’s ruler-priest shows colonial power directly confronting a cult center whose religious prestige underwrote political autonomy. In the Atlantic, legal repression and racialized medicine targeted the religious systems carrying Nanã. In the present, attacks are more often directed at Candomblé/Umbanda as a whole than at Nanã specifically.

### State of the archive

The record is dispersed among:

- German colonial reports;
- ethnographic notebooks, photographs, and objects;
- Herskovits papers and publications;
- Lydia Cabrera’s papers at the University of Miami;
- oral archives held within priestly houses;
- recordings, including Arará songs for Nana;
- living shrines in West Africa, Brazil, and Cuba;
- museum collections whose sacred objects were sometimes seized under coercive conditions.

Access to initiatory knowledge is deliberately restricted. A “complete mythology” assembled only from public books will necessarily be incomplete.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before 1800:** Cult histories and pilgrimage traditions claim older migrations among Niger/Kwara, Bariba, Ana, Mahi, Adele, Guang, and neighboring populations. **[OT; undatable]**
- **Atlantic slavery era, sixteenth–nineteenth centuries:** Gbe- and Yoruba-speaking captives transport vodun/òrìṣà institutions to Brazil and Cuba. No single dated embarkation of Nanã’s cult is known.
- **1890:** A. B. Ellis publishes *The Eʋe-Speaking Peoples of the Slave Coast*, an early colonial synthesis of regional religion; a securely verified Nana/Buruku passage remains to be established.
- **4 October 1896:** German forces seize the ruler-priest at Siadé/Siaré; Count Zech reports Buruku’s politically influential sanctuary and ritual objects. **[EA]**
- **1897/1921:** Samuel Johnson completes, and his editors later publish, *The History of the Yorubas*. It does not establish Nanã as a central pan-Yoruba divinity.
- **Early twentieth century:** Jeje, Ketu/Nagô, Arará, and Lucumí houses continue reconstructing and combining cult lineages in Bahia, Maranhão, Rio de Janeiro, Havana, and Matanzas.
- **1920s–30s:** Bahian police and psychiatric institutions intensify repression of Candomblé; Nanã’s communities are exposed as part of that wider religious field.
- **1934:** J. C. Guinness records the Brukung migration legend in or near the Adele region. **[OT]**
- **1938:** Herskovits publishes the foundational detailed account of Nana Buluku as predecessor and parent of Mawu-Lisa. **[OT/LS]**
- **1954:** Lydia Cabrera publishes *El Monte*, a foundational source for Cuban Afro-religious practice.
- **1957:** Pierre Verger publishes *Notes sur le culte des orisa et vodun*, comparing Bahia and the former Slave Coast.
- **1958:** Roger Bastide publishes *Le Candomblé de Bahia*.
- **1961:** A medical/ethnological article titled “Nana Buruku: a contribution to the cult of a god in Upper Guinea” appears, reflecting mid-century scholarly recognition of the cult’s breadth. [Bibliographic record](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/13789025/)
- **1969:** William Bascom publishes *Ifa Divination*, distinguishing Nana’s importance at Mẹko/Dahomey from eastern Yoruba practice.
- **1970s:** Arará devotees in Matanzas are documented continuing household offerings to Shakpata and Naná Burukú.
- **1980:** Bascom publishes *Sixteen Cowries*, expanding the comparative record of Yoruba and diaspora divination.
- **Late twentieth century:** Verger’s *Orixás* popularizes his Africa–Brazil comparisons; Umbanda and Batuque expand from Brazil into Uruguay and Argentina.
- **2001:** Reginaldo Prandi publishes *Mitologia dos Orixás*, giving the Brazilian Nanã narratives an influential printed concordance.
- **2006:** Luis Nicolau Parés publishes his historical reconstruction of Jeje Candomblé formation in Bahia.
- **2008:** The *Encyclopedia of African Religion* reiterates the modern standardized account of androgynous creator Nana Buluku and Mawu-Lisa. [Reference entry](https://doi.org/10.4135/9781412964623.n441)
- **2020s:** Nanã remains a living divinity in African and Atlantic communities; online devotional systems greatly expand her universal-mother and psychological imagery. Afro-Brazilian worshippers simultaneously face continuing religious-racist attacks.

## 11. Sources and evidentiary assessment

The strongest source chain is:

1. the 1896 colonial report for a material and political Buruku cult;
2. Herskovits for the 1930s Dahomean cosmology;
3. Verger for geographically comparative fieldwork, oríkì, pilgrimage, ritual, and Africa–Brazil comparison;
4. Cabrera and later Cuban ethnography for Arará/Lucumí survival;
5. Bastide and Parés for Bahian institutional development;
6. Bascom and Abimbola for the structure and regionality of Ifá evidence;
7. Prandi for a clearly modern, source-assembled Brazilian myth corpus.

The major negative findings are equally important:

- no ancient inscription or manuscript names her;
- no Ifẹ̀ head can be securely identified as her;
- no universal genealogy exists;
- the swamp-and-primordial-mud identity is most fully documented in Brazil, not in the earliest Siadé evidence;
- no reliable source ties her specifically to human sacrifice or temple prostitution;
- no historical evidence equates her with Tiamat, Danu, Sophia, Lakshmi, or other distant goddesses;
- “oldest orixá” is a meaningful Brazilian theological rank, not a securely datable statement about prehistoric Yoruba religion.
