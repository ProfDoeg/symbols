# Yemoja (Yemayá, Iemanjá): Research Dossier

**Class:** Òrìṣà—female divine power/deity—in Yoruba religion; *oricha* in Cuban Lucumí/Regla de Ocha; *orixá* in Brazilian Candomblé, Umbanda, and Batuque. English “goddess” is serviceable but less exact than these living traditions’ own categories.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest explicit documentary reference located is a Church Missionary Society journal entry dated **13 August 1855**, mentioning **Akere, a priestess of Yemoja**, in the Ẹ̀gbá/Abeokuta mission field. It survives as archival missionary testimony rather than a precolonial inscription or indigenous manuscript. Richard Burton’s published description of Abeokuta followed in 1863; the earliest located extended printed mythology is A. B. Ellis’s *The Yoruba-Speaking Peoples of the Slave Coast of West Africa* (1894). No securely identified ancient inscription, excavated image, Ifẹ̀ head, or other archaeological object can presently be assigned specifically to Yemoja.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** nineteenth-century observation, archival record, or early transcription.
- **[LE] Later ethnographic or canonical elaboration:** principally twentieth-century transcriptions of oral, liturgical, or divinatory material.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** restricted to a city, lineage, nation, or diaspora tradition.
- **[LS] Learned/priestly systematization:** an ordered theology assembled from living but nonuniform traditions.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** historically produced equation or ritual juxtaposition.
- **[F] Folklore/oral tradition:** documented as tradition but not independently dateable to the events narrated.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an academic inference rather than an indigenous statement.
- **[MI] Modern invention or creative retelling:** demonstrably recent attribution, image, taxonomy, or narrative.

The date of a transcription dates the evidence, not necessarily the oral tradition performed for the recorder.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

**[EA/LE]** Yemoja is a Yoruba water òrìṣà whose best-documented Nigerian center is the **Ògùn River at Abeokuta**, especially among Ẹ̀gbá communities. She is associated with motherhood, fertility, children, fish, river abundance, protection, and the dangerous as well as nurturing power of water. In Cuba and Brazil she became predominantly maritime—**Yemayá/Iemanjá, owner or queen of the sea**—while Nigeria’s older documented distinction normally leaves the deep sea to Olókun. This is a historical Atlantic transformation, not proof that one side has preserved a single “original” theology.

### Names

- **Yemọja / Yemoja / Yẹmoja** (modern Yoruba orthographies; often written **Yemaja** in nineteenth-century English): the usual explanation is an elision of **Yèyé ọmọ ẹja**, “mother whose children are fish” or “mother of fish.” Ellis gives “Yemaja” and parses *yeye*, mother, plus *eja*, fish. The fuller modern parsing includes *ọmọ*, child. **[EA/LS]**
- **Yemayá, Yemallá, Yemaya**: Cuban Lucumí and Spanish spellings. The final stress and *y* reflect Hispanic transmission. **[RV]**
- **Iemanjá, Yemanjá, Janaína, Dona Janaína, Rainha do Mar, Mãe d’Água**: Brazilian forms and titles. *Janaína* is strongly associated with Brazilian popular religion, literature, and music; it should not be projected into ancient Yorubaland. **[RV/LE]**
- **Yemonja, Yemanja, Emanje**: spellings recorded elsewhere in the Atlantic world. **[RV]**
- **Yewajọbí**, glossed in modern scholarship as “mother of all the gods/all living things,” and titles emphasizing immense breasts, fecundity, or universal maternity occur in praise poetry and modern theological presentations. The precise wording and distribution require attribution to the particular *oríkì* performer or published collection; they are not a fixed pan-Yoruba litany. **[LE/LS]**
- **Awoyó**, **Asèsú/Asesú**, **Okuté/Okutí**, **Mayalewo**, **Achabá**, **Konlá**, and others are Cuban *caminos*—ritual “roads” or manifestations—rather than securely attested prehistoric epithets. Lydia Cabrera’s collected Cuban accounts and later Regla de Ocha manuals systematize them differently. Claims that there are exactly seven, twenty-one, or another fixed number are lineage-dependent. **[RV/LS]**

### Etymological caution

**[EA/MSR]** “Mother whose children are fish” is early and linguistically plausible, but popular presentations often treat the segmentation as if it proved every later claim that Yemoja literally generated all fish, all humanity, or every òrìṣà. Etymology does not establish a myth’s date. Alternative modern readings such as *Yèyé mo já* or “mother who knows suffering” are devotional reinterpretations unless accompanied by historical linguistic evidence.

### Distinctions and equations

- **Yemoja ≠ Yemòwó:** Yemòwó is documented as a consort of Ọbàtálá in important Yoruba traditions. Similarity of names has encouraged conflation, but equivalence is not established. **[RV/contested]**
- **Yemoja ≠ Olókun:** Nigerian and Edo/Yoruba materials usually distinguish the river mother from the owner of the sea/deep water. Cuban and Brazilian systems bring their functions closer, sometimes treating Olókun as the abyssal or masked depth beneath Yemayá’s accessible ocean. Complete identity is lineage-specific, not universal. **[RV/SI]**
- **Yemoja ≠ Mami Wata:** both may be represented through aquatic female imagery, and practitioners sometimes associate them, but Mami Wata designates a broad, internally diverse complex of water spirits. Treating Mami Wata as simply “another name for Yemoja” erases separate histories. **[SI/contested]**
- **Yemayá–Our Lady of Regla:** a Cuban historical conjunction, not an African identity. **[SI]**
- **Iemanjá–Nossa Senhora dos Navegantes / Nossa Senhora da Conceição / other Marian figures:** Brazilian correspondences vary by region and ritual nation. No single Catholic equivalent governs all Candomblé or Umbanda practice. **[SI/RV]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Documentary beginning

**[EA] 1855.** A CMS journal dated 13 August records a priestess, Akere, of “Yemoja.” This is the earliest located explicit reference. Its value is narrow but important: by 1855 Yemoja possessed a recognized priestly office in the Abeokuta region. It does **not** supply a complete mythology, prove the cult’s date of foundation, or identify Yemoja with the sea.

**[EA] 1863.** Richard F. Burton’s *Abeokuta and the Camaroons Mountains* documents the city and its religious environment. Burton is a hostile and highly mediated colonial observer; his evidence must be separated from his racial and religious commentary.

**[EA] 1894.** A. B. Ellis’s *The Yoruba-Speaking Peoples* gives the first located extended printed account: Yemoja is daughter of Ọbàtálá and Odùduwà, sister-wife of Aganju, mother of Orungan, goddess of streams, and source of numerous deities after Orungan’s assault or pursuit. Ellis also describes her as a yellow female figure wearing blue beads and white cloth. This is an invaluable dated transcription, but the coherent “family of gods” may reflect Ellis’s comparative, genealogizing method as much as a universally accepted Yoruba canon.

**[EA/LE] 1897/1921.** Samuel Johnson completed *The History of the Yorubas* in 1897; after the manuscript was lost in transmission, his brother Obadiah reconstructed it from notes, publishing it in 1921. Johnson remains indispensable for Oyo and nineteenth-century Yoruba historical traditions, but neither its genealogies nor its religious notices are transparent survivals from antiquity.

**[EA] 1907–1913.** Leo Frobenius’s fieldwork and *The Voice of Africa* preserve stories, observations, and objects from Yorubaland, while also notoriously forcing material into speculative “Atlantis” and Mediterranean-origin frameworks. His documentation and his diffusionism must be judged separately.

### Oral and divinatory corpora

**[LE] Ifá.** The *Odù Ifá* are not a single closed scripture with one ancient manuscript. They comprise 256 divinatory signs, each carrying many performable *ẹsẹ Ifá*. Wande Abimbola’s *Ifá: An Exposition of Ifá Literary Corpus* (1976), William Bascom’s *Ifa Divination* (1969), and later editions give transcribed Yoruba performances and translations. Epega and Neimark’s *The Sacred Ifa Oracle* (1995) is influential in English-language practice but is a late compilation, not an ancient codex. The date of a collected verse is the date of recording; a verse may be older, locally composed, altered in performance, or recently learned.

**[LE] Sixteen cowries.** Bascom’s *Sixteen Cowries* (1980) publishes Yoruba texts and translations collected principally from the diviner Salako at Iléṣà and comparative material from Cuba. Yemoja appears in sacrifice, marriage, taboo, and river narratives, including the account of Yemoja and Okere. Cowry divination overlaps with Ifá but is not merely a defective copy of it.

**[LE/RV] Oríkì.** Praise poetry names Yemoja through water, fish, breasts, children, protection, wealth, and overwhelming force. An *oríkì* is a performed, expandable genre, not a single hymn. Assertions that a line is “the ancient hymn to Yemoja” are unestablished without performer, place, collector, and date.

### African and Atlantic ethnographic corpus

- Bolaji Idowu, *Olódùmarè: God in Yoruba Belief* (1962): a Yoruba Christian theologian’s influential systematization. **[LS]**
- Bascom, *Ifa Divination* (1969) and *Sixteen Cowries* (1980): rigorously transcribed oral/divinatory texts, though collected in the twentieth century. **[LE]**
- Pierre Verger, *Notes sur le culte des orisa et vodun* (1957) and *Orixás* (1981): comparative West African–Bahian documentation, especially valuable for names, ritual, and photography. **[LE/MSR]**
- Lydia Cabrera, *El Monte* (1954) and *Yemayá y Ochún* (1974): Cuban testimony, songs, ritual vocabulary, and narratives; indispensable but not a verbatim survival of pre-slavery Africa. **[RV/LE]**
- Fernando Ortiz’s Afro-Cuban studies: foundational evidence for cabildos, music, and “transculturation,” written through changing early twentieth-century frameworks. **[LE/MSR]**
- Roger Bastide, *Le Candomblé de Bahia* (1958): analysis of Brazilian ritual nations and syncretism. **[LE/MSR]**
- Reginaldo Prandi, *Mitologia dos Orixás* (2001): large modern synthesis of narratives from Africa and Brazil, useful only when its individual source attributions are retained. **[LS]**
- J. Lorand Matory, Robert Farris Thompson, Henry and Margaret Drewal, John Pemberton III, Andrew Apter, and J. D. Y. Peel: studies of Atlantic circulation, visuality, performance, power, and Christian encounter. **[MSR]**

### Material evidence and archaeology

**[MSR/negative finding]** Excavations and artworks from Ilé-Ifẹ̀ and Oyo establish sophisticated Yoruba urbanism, kingship, ritual art, and long historical depth. The naturalistic Ifẹ̀ heads, commonly dated approximately twelfth–fifteenth centuries, are not securely portraits or icons of Yemoja. No inscription names her; no excavated object has an uncontested Yemoja provenance. Using an Ifẹ̀ head as “an ancient statue of Yemoja” is modern illustrative attribution.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Nigeria

**[EA/LE]** Yemoja is primarily associated with the Ògùn River and with streams, fertility, childbirth, children, fish, cleansing, and ordeals or oaths involving water. Her water both sustains and overwhelms. She may grant children and prosperity but can punish oath-breaking or ritual neglect.

**[EA]** Ellis’s 1894 image description—female, yellow-colored, blue beads, white cloth—is the earliest located specific printed iconographic notice. It does not establish a timeless canon.

**[LE/RV]** Abeokuta and Ibadan festivals employ pots, calabashes, beaded regalia, processional images, cloth, drumming, and river offerings. A documented twentieth-century Abeokuta arrangement included an urban shrine and another near the Ògùn River. Sculptures may emphasize maternity through large breasts or a child, but there is no single universally binding Yoruba form.

### Cuba

**[RV/LS]** Yemayá governs salt water, the sea’s surface, maternity, household protection, and royal composure; Olókun commonly represents inaccessible oceanic depth. Blue and white, sevenfold patterns, shells, boats, anchors, fans, moons, fish, and silver-colored tools are widespread Cuban attributes. Specific colors and implements vary by *camino* and house.

**[RV]** Offerings reported in Lucumí practice include molasses, fruit, cooked dishes, fish, ducks or other animals where the lineage prescribes sacrifice, and blue-and-white devotional objects. Public “recipes” detached from divination or priestly authority are modern popularizations, not a universal liturgy.

### Brazil

**[RV]** Iemanjá is *Rainha do Mar*, a maternal yet potentially lethal sea power. White and shades of blue predominate in public imagery; boats, stars, shells, mirrors, combs, perfume, flowers, fish, crowns, and flowing dresses are common. Candomblé nations differ, while Umbanda has helped circulate an accessible white- or blue-robed “queen” beyond initiated communities.

### The mermaid

**[SI/MI]** The fish-tailed, long-haired, comb-and-mirror Iemanjá is not securely documented as ancient Yoruba iconography. It arose through Atlantic visual traffic involving European mermaids and ship figureheads, Brazilian *sereia* and Iara imagery, Marian images, and the broader Mami Wata complex.

Henry Drewal traced an especially influential Mami Wata image to an 1880s Hamburg circus poster of the snake-charmer Maladamatjaute/Nala Damajanti, reprinted in Bombay in 1955 and exported to West Africa. That snake-charmer history is not the sole source of every Yemoja mermaid, but it demonstrates why “traditional-looking” water-spirit images may have precise modern, transcontinental genealogies. The fair-skinned, straight-haired mermaid sold today as “ancient Yemaya” is therefore usually **modern Atlantic iconography**, not evidence for precolonial form.

### Calendrical and numerical attributes

- **Seven:** prominent in Cuban Yemayá symbolism and later global practice. **[RV/LS]**
- **2 February:** Rio Vermelho and much of southern South America; historically linked to Brazilian maritime/Marian calendars, not an ancient Yoruba birthday. **[RV/SI]**
- **8 September:** Our Lady of Regla’s Cuban feast and therefore a major Yemayá/Regla devotional date. **[SI]**
- **New Year’s Eve:** Copacabana and other Brazilian beaches; a twentieth-century Umbanda/Candomblé-inflected public custom. **[RV/MI]**

No ancient evidence establishes a universal Yemoja weekday, planet, tarot card, chakra, crystal, or zodiac sign. Such tables belong to modern occult correspondence systems. **[MI]**

## 4. Genealogy and divine relations

There is no single canonical family tree.

### Competing genealogies

1. **Ellis’s 1894 genealogy [EA]:** Ọbàtálá and Odùduwà produce Aganju and Yemoja; the siblings marry and produce Orungan. Orungan assaults or pursues Yemoja; from her ruptured body emerge fifteen or sixteen divine powers.
2. **Later theological expansion [LE/LS]:** Yemoja is “mother of the òrìṣà,” sometimes literally, sometimes honorifically as primordial maternity.
3. **Regional Oyo traditions [RV]:** Yemoja can be Ṣàngó’s mother, wife, or nurturing counterpart. These contradictions encode different local and political relationships; they should not be combined into a single marriage-and-maternity story.
4. **Cuban Lucumí [RV]:** Yemayá is a royal oceanic mother with numerous *caminos*. Her consorts or children vary by house and narrative; Ogun, Orisha Oko, Olókun, Ṣàngó, and others enter different configurations.
5. **Brazilian Candomblé/Umbanda [RV]:** Iemanjá is often universalized as mother of the orixás, sometimes paired with Oxalá, but nation and terreiro traditions differ.

### Documented god–god interactions

Each line records an interaction, not mere pantheon co-membership.

- **Ọbàtálá — parent of Yemoja with Odùduwà.** Ellis, *Yoruba-Speaking Peoples* (1894). **[EA; colonial transcription]**
- **Odùduwà — parent of Yemoja with Ọbàtálá.** Ellis (1894). Gender assignments differ sharply across Yoruba traditions. **[EA/RV]**
- **Aganju — brother and husband; father with her of Orungan.** Ellis (1894); repeated by Lucas and Idowu. Not universally corroborated, and “Aganju” also names a remembered Oyo ruler in other traditions. **[EA/contested]**
- **Orungan — son who rapes or attempts to rape Yemoja and pursues her, precipitating her fall and the divine births.** Ellis (1894); later Lucas/Idowu retellings differ over whether there was one completed assault, an attempt, or repeated assault. **[EA/LE]**
- **Olókun — emerges from Yemoja’s body in Ellis’s version; elsewhere distinct sea sovereign, parent, counterpart, spouse, or abyssal complement.** Ellis (1894); twentieth-century Yoruba, Cuban, and Brazilian ethnography. **[EA/RV]**
- **Olósa — lagoon deity born from Yemoja in Ellis’s list.** Ellis (1894). **[EA]**
- **Ṣàngó — born from Yemoja in Ellis; mother/son or wife/husband in competing Oyo-region traditions; Cuban practice frequently emphasizes mutual closeness and joint eating in some houses.** Ellis (1894); regional oral and Lucumí sources recorded in the twentieth century. **[EA/RV]**
- **Ògún — born from Yemoja in Ellis; husband or companion of Yemayá Okuté in Cuban traditions.** Ellis (1894); Cabrera and later Lucumí documentation. **[EA/RV]**
- **Ọ̀ṣun — born from Yemoja in Ellis; sister, daughter, or fellow water power in later traditions. Cabrera’s *Yemayá y Ochún* documents their Cuban comparison without making every version genealogically identical.** **[EA/RV]**
- **Ọya — born from Yemoja in Ellis; elsewhere Ṣàngó’s wife and Niger/market/storm power without a necessary maternal link to Yemoja.** **[EA/RV]**
- **Ọbà — born from Yemoja in Ellis; otherwise an independent river òrìṣà and wife of Ṣàngó.** **[EA/RV]**
- **Dàda — vegetable/corn or royal-child power born from Yemoja.** Ellis (1894). **[EA]**
- **Òrìṣà Oko — agricultural deity born from Yemoja in Ellis; consort in some Cuban narratives.** Ellis (1894); twentieth-century Cuban sources. **[EA/RV]**
- **Ọ̀ṣọ́ọ̀sì — hunter deity born from Yemoja.** Ellis (1894). **[EA]**
- **Òkè — mountain deity born from Yemoja.** Ellis (1894). **[EA]**
- **Ajé Ṣaluga — wealth deity born from Yemoja.** Ellis (1894). **[EA]**
- **Ṣọ̀pọ̀na/Ọbalúayé — smallpox deity born from Yemoja.** Ellis (1894). **[EA]**
- **Ọ̀rún (Sun) and Oṣù (Moon) — born from Yemoja in Ellis’s enumeration.** Ellis (1894). **[EA]**
- **Okere — husband or male antagonist whose taboo-breaking or insult causes Yemoja’s water to burst outward.** A narrative recorded at Ìsẹ́yìn and published by Bascom in *Sixteen Cowries* (1980). **[LE/RV]**
- **Èṣù — ritual messenger who appears in divination and sacrifice contexts involving Yemoja, but no single universal Yemoja–Èṣù narrative was established in the consulted evidence.** **[LE; absence noted]**
- **Ọ̀rúnmìlà — divination links worshippers to prescribed offerings involving Yemoja; no universal marriage or combat cycle is established.** Bascom and Abimbola collections. **[LE; limited interaction]**
- **Oxalá — spouse or cosmogonic counterpart in some Brazilian popular and Umbanda/Candomblé systematizations.** Twentieth-century Brazilian literature. **[RV/LS]**
- **Ibeji — Yemoja is sometimes invoked as nurturer or protector of twins, but a universally attested myth in which she ends twin infanticide was not located.** **[F/contested]**

Claims of meetings with Greek, Egyptian, Norse, Hindu, or other pantheons are modern comparative or fictional constructions unless a specific recent work is named. **[MI]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. Orungan’s assault and the birth of the divine powers

**[EA]** Ellis’s 1894 account makes Yemoja the daughter of Ọbàtálá and Odùduwà, wife of her brother Aganju, and mother of Orungan. Orungan violates or attempts to violate her and later pursues her. She falls; her body swells or bursts; water issues out and a company of divine beings emerges—Olókun, Olósa, Ṣàngó, Ọya, Ọ̀ṣun, Ọbà, Ògún, Dàda, Òrìṣà Oko, Ọ̀ṣọ́ọ̀sì, Òkè, Ajé Ṣaluga, Ṣọ̀pọ̀na, Sun, and Moon, with order and membership varying.

**[LE]** Lucas and Idowu repeat and rationalize the narrative. Some versions have water flow from Yemoja’s breasts or body to form lagoon, lakes, and rivers; some give fifteen offspring, others sixteen. Modern summaries frequently combine every detail.

**Assessment:** The first secure text is late nineteenth century. It is false to call this “an ancient Yoruba creation tablet.” The incest, rape, bodily rupture, and cosmic maternity are explicit parts of the documented story and should not be sanitized; equally, the episode is not the creed of every Yemoja devotee. Some Yoruba-oriented critics argue that Ellis or earlier missionary catechetical literature imposed a Greek-style genealogy and that the story lacks wide local confirmation. That criticism is significant, but without a full provenance for Ellis’s informants it cannot prove wholesale fabrication. **[contested]**

### B. Yemoja and Okere: the bursting waters

**[LE/RV]** In the narrative recorded at Ìsẹ́yìn and published by Bascom, Yemoja’s relationship with Okere depends on a taboo or prohibition—commonly that he must not ridicule a feature of her body or reveal a secret. He violates it during conflict or intoxication. Yemoja leaves; her water vessel, body, or restrained waters break loose, producing a flood or river. Okere attempts to stop or pursue her.

This story explains the movement or origin of water and Yemoja’s withdrawal from an abusive or dishonorable union. It is distinct from the Orungan cycle, though modern retellings often fuse their fleeing, bursting, and river-creation motifs.

### C. Mother of fish and mother of the òrìṣà

**[EA/LE]** The name and early mythology support maternity and fish. “Mother of all òrìṣà” can mean genealogical mother in the Ellis cycle or supreme maternal patron in worship. It is not a consistent biological genealogy. In Atlantic religions the image expanded toward universal motherhood—children carried in amniotic water, humanity sustained by the ocean, and enslaved descendants accompanied across the Atlantic. The last motif is a powerful diaspora theology, but no slavery-era text has been located that records Yemoja literally following slave ships. **[RV/F]**

### D. Migration from river to sea

**[MSR]** This is a historical development expressed mythically rather than a single old narrative. Enslaved and free Africans from multiple regions rebuilt cults in plantation and port societies. River divinities entered coastal ritual ecologies; separate African water powers could be grouped, and the ocean became both grave, route of displacement, and source of life. In Cuba and Brazil Yemoja’s maternal authority increasingly attached to salt water, while Olókun retained, lost, or shared maritime functions according to lineage.

### E. The accepting or rejecting sea

**[RV/F]** At Brazilian and southern-cone beach rites, gifts that drift outward or sink may be interpreted as accepted, while gifts returned by the surf may be read as rejected. This is living ritual interpretation, not a rule attested for ancient Yorubaland. Environmental campaigns increasingly replace plastic, mirrors, perfume bottles, and nonbiodegradable boats with flowers or degradable materials. **[modern adaptation]**

## 6. Cult, shrines, temples, and ritual practice

### Yorubaland

**[EA/LE]** Abeokuta, founded as an Ẹ̀gbá refuge in the nineteenth century, is the best-documented center. Yemoja’s shrine network includes urban sacred space and the Ògùn riverbank. A **Baálẹ̀ Yemoja** or chief priest and other ritual specialists supervise offerings, prayer, possession, music, and procession. Contemporary documentation shows calabashes, kola nuts, prepared foods, river water, drumming, dance, and communal petitions.

**[LE/RV]** Ibadan also maintains Yemoja festival traditions and processional images. Local priesthood, calendrical timing, and image form cannot automatically be transferred to Abeokuta or all Yorubaland.

**[EA/LE]** Offerings may include food, cloth, kola, fish, fowl, goats, or other prescribed animals. Animal sacrifice is documented within broader Yoruba and Atlantic ritual systems, but details depend on divination and lineage.

**Human sacrifice:** Human sacrifice and judicial/funerary killing are historically documented in some nineteenth-century Yoruba polities, including Ondo. The consulted evidence does **not** securely establish a distinct institutional practice of human sacrifice to Yemoja. General evidence for Yoruba sacrifice cannot be reassigned to her without a source. **[negative finding]**

**Temple prostitution:** No credible evidence located supports an institution properly described as “temple prostitution” in Yemoja’s cult. Sexual symbolism, priestly marriage metaphors, or spirit possession are not evidence of commercial sacred sex. **[negative finding]**

### Cuba: Yemayá and Regla

**[SI/RV]** Regla, across Havana Bay, acquired a shrine to the Black Madonna **Nuestra Señora de Regla** after land was donated for a hermitage in 1687; the first structure was destroyed by a 1692 storm, and a Spanish image was installed in the rebuilt shrine in the 1690s. The Virgin became patroness of Regla in 1714. Her dark image, maritime patronage, port location, and feast on 8 September made the shrine an especially productive setting for identification with Yemayá.

The equation was not instantaneous in 1687 and should not be narrated as a planned disguise invented on one date. It developed through cabildos, Lucumí houses, Black Catholic participation, maritime labor, and overlapping devotion. Devotees may understand Virgin and oricha as equivalent, mutually present, strategically paired, or distinct; “syncretism” does not describe all positions.

**[RV]** Regla de Ocha initiates receive consecrated objects and learn house-specific songs, sacrifices, taboos, dances, and *caminos*. Public blue-and-white statues are only an outer layer of a cult whose central objects and knowledge are often restricted.

### Brazil: Candomblé, Umbanda, and public festivals

**[RV]** Candomblé places Iemanjá within initiated terreiro systems of possession, sacrifice, song, dance, divination, and reciprocal obligation. Umbanda, institutionalizing during the twentieth century, helped make her a national, publicly approachable sea mother represented in white or blue robes. Roger Bastide, Pierre Verger, Renato Ortiz, Matory, and Prandi document the differences and exchanges; neither religion can be reduced to “African paganism hidden under Catholic saints.”

#### Rio Vermelho, Salvador

**[RV; documented twentieth-century festival]** The fishermen’s offering began around **1923–1924**, according to later institutional memory, during poor catches. No contemporary record securely fixes the first year; the 1923 centenary narrative and the scholarly caution favoring 1924 should both be retained. The gift basket is carried from the fishermen’s colony into the sea on 2 February.

The festival grew from fishermen’s and terreiro practice, interacted with the neighboring feast of Sant’Ana, and eventually displaced the Marian festival’s local prominence. It became explicitly known and institutionalized as the Festa de Iemanjá during the mid-twentieth century. Salvador registered it as cultural heritage in 2020. Claims that it is wholly untouched by Catholic history are too strong; its present public focus on an orixá is nevertheless unusual.

#### Copacabana

**[RV/modern public ritual]** Umbanda groups began or expanded public New Year observances on Rio beaches in the mid-twentieth century. White clothing, flowers, candles, seven waves, and gifts to Iemanjá became entangled with the mass Réveillon at Copacabana, especially by the 1960s–1970s. Municipal spectacle, tourism, fireworks, popular custom, and religion now coexist. Wearing white or jumping waves does not necessarily identify a person as an initiate.

### Uruguay and Argentina

**[RV]** Afro-Brazilian Umbanda and Batuque expanded strongly into Uruguay and Argentina during the twentieth century, especially from the 1960s onward. On **2 February**, Montevideo’s **Playa Ramírez** becomes Uruguay’s principal public Iemanjá site; municipal documentation recognizes the festival. Devotees launch flowers, fruit, boats, candles, and petitions into the Río de la Plata.

**[RV]** Argentina has public ceremonies at Mar del Plata, Quilmes, and other shores. A nationally recognized Mar del Plata observance had reached its thirty-fifth iteration by 2019. Frigerio and Carozzi document how Umbanda could operate as a bridge between popular Catholicism and Batuque, and how Afro-American religions negotiated stigma, media accusations, regulation, and public visibility.

**Suppression and stigma:** Colonial and missionary Christianity characterized òrìṣà worship as “fetishism” or demonic error; African and diaspora practitioners also faced police regulation, seizure of ritual objects, and public-health or cruelty accusations. Cuba’s post-1959 state restricted public religious life, especially in the officially atheist period, while permitting uneven domestic and folkloric expression; constitutional atheism was removed in 1992. Brazil’s terreiros endured raids and object confiscation into the twentieth century. In Argentina and Uruguay, practitioners report racialized “sect” and “black magic” stereotypes. These are documented histories of regulation and stigma; a single continuous international ban on Yemoja worship did not exist.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1855: inferable but undocumented duration

**[MSR]** The cult necessarily predates its recorded priestess, but no defensible foundation year exists. Linguistic, ritual, and settlement history suggest a local water cult incorporated into evolving Ẹ̀gbá and broader Yoruba networks. Archaeology currently cannot date Yemoja by name.

### 1855–1894: mission encounter and colonial textualization

**[EA]** The Akere notice establishes an organized priesthood. Missionaries and travelers recorded indigenous religion while working to replace it; their archives preserve information and simultaneously distort it. Ellis transformed scattered local materials into a comparative pantheon and family tree.

### 1894–1930s: ethnographic canon and Atlantic consolidation

**[EA/LE]** Ellis, Johnson, Dennett, Frobenius, and Lucas circulated an increasingly standardized “Yoruba mythology.” In Cuba and Brazil, Lucumí and Candomblé institutions consolidated regional Yemoja forms. Regla’s Black Madonna and maritime cult became inseparable in public Cuban perception, although not doctrinally identical.

### 1920s–1950s: public sea mother

**[RV]** Rio Vermelho’s fishermen’s offering began around 1923–24. Brazilian writers, radio, urban Umbanda, and visual artists moved Iemanjá from terreiro-centered worship into national culture. Lydia Cabrera’s *El Monte* (1954), Verger’s 1957 study, and Bastide’s 1958 monograph fixed enormous bodies of living practice in print.

### 1960s–1980s: scholarship and transnational expansion

**[LE/MSR]** Idowu, Bascom, Abimbola, Thompson, Drewal, and others shifted study toward Yoruba-language theology, oral performance, art history, and Atlantic circulation. Umbanda and Batuque expanded in Argentina and Uruguay. Copacabana’s Iemanjá-inflected Réveillon became a mass civic event.

### 1990s–present: revival, heritage, and global circulation

**[RV/MI]** Nigerian festivals continue or revive through local priesthoods and cultural tourism. Cuban religious practice expanded publicly after the relaxation of state atheism. Brazilian and southern-cone festivals gained municipal recognition while facing evangelical opposition and environmental criticism. Internet religion, diaspora initiations, feminist spirituality, games, and commercial images now circulate Yemoja independently of lineage.

Continuity is therefore real but not uniform: living priesthoods preserve inherited practices while each Atlantic society has also produced major innovations.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and music

**[modern reception]** Jorge Amado’s fiction, especially *Mar Morto* (1936), helped make Iemanjá/Janaína the sovereign of Bahia’s fishermen in national and international imagination. Dorival Caymmi’s maritime songs—among them “É Doce Morrer no Mar” (recorded in the early 1940s) and “Dois de Fevereiro” (1957)—gave the sea mother a canonical Brazilian sound. Caymmi’s work did not invent the cult, but it powerfully selected and nationalized a Bahian image. Clara Nunes, Vinícius de Moraes, Baden Powell, Gilberto Gil, Caetano Veloso, Celia Cruz, and many others subsequently invoked Yemayá/Iemanjá.

### Visual art

**[modern reception]** Carybé’s drawings and sculptures, Verger’s photographs, festival monuments, plaster mermaids, and commercial devotional prints helped establish the crowned, flowing-haired sea queen. Her racial presentation ranges from dark-skinned African mother to white or light-skinned Europeanized mermaid. The latter is evidence of Brazilian and transatlantic visual politics, not proof that devotees abandoned an “authentic” single image.

### Occultism, neopaganism, and New Age systems

**[MI]** Late twentieth- and twenty-first-century books and websites relocate Yemaya into Wiccan “goddess” calendars, Jungian mother-archetype lists, tarot, chakra, astrology, crystal healing, and generic “divine feminine” practice. These may constitute sincere new religions, but none is an ancient Yoruba correspondence system. Fixed claims such as “Yemaya rules Cancer, the Moon, the sacral chakra, aquamarine, and Saturday” must be dated to their modern authors.

African-diaspora initiatory traditions should not be labeled “neopagan” merely because outsiders group all polytheisms together. Lucumí, Candomblé, Batuque, Umbanda, and contemporary Yoruba religion possess distinct histories, authority structures, and debates over initiation and ancestry.

### Film, fiction, comics, and games

- **2019:** *SMITE* introduced Yemoja as a playable “Goddess of Rivers,” combining river identity, healing, water barriers, and fantasy combat. Its character lore is explicitly a modern game narrative. **[MI]**
- **2021:** Natasha Bowen’s *Skin of the Sea* made Yemoja creator of mermaid-like Mami Wata in a young-adult fantasy. That genealogy belongs to Bowen’s novel, not to a demonstrated old Yoruba source. **[MI]**
- Contemporary Afrofuturist and Black-diaspora art uses Yemoja as maternal ocean, Middle Passage witness, ecological guardian, queer/feminine power, and emblem of cultural survival. These are datable interpretations with roots in older symbolism but are not unchanged antiquity. **[modern reception]**

### Politics and commerce

**[modern reception]** Iemanjá functions as an emblem of Bahia, Afro-Brazilian heritage, Black resistance, tourism, and Brazilian national culture. Municipal sponsorship may protect festivals while converting worship into spectacle. Commercial mermaid statues, perfumes, jewelry, candles, and “Yemaya spell kits” often mix Cuban, Brazilian, Nigerian, Mami Wata, and New Age elements without provenance.

No documented organized extremist movement centered on Yemoja was located. Religious-nationalist and anti-racist uses exist, but describing them as extremist without specific evidence would be unwarranted. **[negative finding]**

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debates

### How old is the recoverable record?

The central difficulty is the difference between **cultic antiquity** and **documentary antiquity**. Yemoja was plainly not invented in 1855, yet no text permits an exact earlier date. Scholarship that calls a modern transcription “a 5,000-year-old myth” confuses oral possibility with demonstrated transmission.

### Did Ellis preserve or manufacture the pantheon?

Ellis preserves the earliest extended version of the Orungan myth, but his tidy divine genealogy resembles contemporary comparative mythology. Later authors copied him, creating apparent independent confirmation. The appropriate conclusion is neither “ancient fact” nor “Ellis invented everything”: it is a late nineteenth-century attestation of uncertain local range and uncertain prehistory.

### River or sea?

“River in Africa, sea in the Americas” is broadly accurate but too absolute. Yoruba water powers overlap, coastal Yoruba knew the sea, and modern Nigerian Yemoja discourse is affected by Atlantic return. Still, the documentary center of the Nigerian cult is the Ògùn River, whereas Cuban and Brazilian worship made oceanic sovereignty defining. **[MSR]**

### Mother of all òrìṣà?

Ellis gives a literal birth list; many other systems do not. “Mother of the òrìṣà” may be praise, metaphysical motherhood, or genealogy. Scholars should not make a metaphorical title resolve conflicting local pedigrees.

### Syncretism: concealment or creation?

Older explanations held that enslaved Africans “hid” deities behind saints. Concealment occurred, but Bastide, Matory, Peel, and material-religion scholarship show more: Catholics, Africans, and descendants created durable relationships through images, calendars, brotherhoods, ports, kinship, and ritual exchange. The Virgin of Regla is therefore neither merely a mask nor timelessly Yemayá.

### Mami Wata and the mermaid

Drewal emphasizes creative African appropriation of European and Asian images; critics warn that “Mami Wata” can flatten numerous local water beings. Both points matter. A circulating chromolithograph can transform iconography without creating the underlying experience of sacred water from nothing. Yemoja’s mermaid form is related to this visual field but cannot be wholly reduced to the Hamburg snake-charmer print.

### Ifá as “scripture”

Abimbola demonstrated the literary sophistication of *ẹsẹ Ifá*, while Bascom documented performance and variation. Printed compilations are selections from an open oral corpus. Epega–Neimark or internet “complete Odù” collections should not be mistaken for a universally authorized, closed Bible.

### Source losses and archives

Relevant evidence is dispersed across CMS archives, Nigerian repositories, university sound and field-note collections, museums, Cuban private houses and ethnographic papers, Brazilian terreiro archives, and oral custody. Colonial collecting removed sacred objects and stripped many of provenance. Ritual secrecy also limits the public corpus. Absence from print can mean local restriction, loss, collector neglect, or genuine absence; it cannot automatically prove antiquity.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before the nineteenth century:** Yemoja’s cult presumably existed, but no securely named archaeological or written attestation has been identified. **[MSR]**
- **1843/1852:** Samuel Ajayi Crowther publishes foundational Yoruba vocabularies; these establish early Yoruba literacy but do not, in the accessible catalog evidence, securely document Yemoja.  
- **13 August 1855:** CMS journal mentions Akere, priestess of Yemoja, in the Abeokuta mission field. **[EA]**
- **1863:** Burton publishes *Abeokuta and the Camaroons Mountains*. **[EA]**
- **1880s:** Hamburg snake-charmer poster later central to Mami Wata visual history is produced; relevant to later aquatic iconography, not proof of Yemoja’s origin. **[SI]**
- **1894:** Ellis publishes the earliest located extended Yemoja description and Orungan genealogy. **[EA]**
- **1897:** Samuel Johnson completes the manuscript underlying *History of the Yorubas*.  
- **1907–1913:** Frobenius’s Yoruba fieldwork and publications circulate Yoruba art and mythology internationally.
- **1921:** Obadiah Johnson publishes Samuel Johnson’s reconstructed *History*.  
- **c. 1923–1924:** fishermen’s offering at Rio Vermelho begins; exact first year disputed. **[RV]**
- **1936:** Jorge Amado publishes *Mar Morto*. **[modern reception]**
- **1940s:** Caymmi’s sea songs bring Iemanjá into Brazilian mass media.
- **1950:** official narratives place the public naming/consolidation of Rio Vermelho’s Festa de Iemanjá around mid-century; local accounts differ.
- **1954:** Lydia Cabrera publishes *El Monte*.  
- **1955:** Bombay reprint of the nineteenth-century snake-charmer image circulates widely in West Africa. **[SI]**
- **1957:** Verger publishes *Notes sur le culte des orisa et vodun*; Caymmi records “Dois de Fevereiro.”
- **1958:** Bastide publishes *Le Candomblé de Bahia*.  
- **1962:** Idowu publishes *Olódùmarè*.  
- **1960s–1970s:** Afro-Brazilian religions expand in Argentina and Uruguay; Copacabana’s Iemanjá-linked New Year practices become mass public custom. **[RV]**
- **1969:** Bascom publishes *Ifa Divination*.  
- **1974:** Cabrera publishes *Yemayá y Ochún*.  
- **1976:** Abimbola publishes *Ifá: An Exposition of Ifá Literary Corpus*.  
- **1980:** Bascom publishes *Sixteen Cowries*.  
- **1981:** Verger publishes *Orixás*.  
- **1980s–1990s:** Iemanjá beach ceremonies gain increasing public visibility in Argentina and Uruguay amid stigma and regulation.
- **1992:** Cuba removes the constitutional characterization of the state as atheist, part of the broader reopening of public religious life.
- **2001:** Prandi publishes *Mitologia dos Orixás*.  
- **2008:** Drewal’s major Mami Wata exhibition/catalogue consolidates the transcontinental iconographic history.
- **2013:** Otero and Falola edit *Yemoja: Gender, Sexuality, and Creativity in the Latina/o and Afro-Atlantic Diasporas*.  
- **2019:** *SMITE* releases Yemoja as a playable character; Argentina’s government publicizes the thirty-fifth Mar del Plata ceremony.
- **2020:** Salvador registers the Festa de Iemanjá as municipal cultural heritage.
- **2021:** Bowen publishes *Skin of the Sea*.
- **2023:** Bahia marks the conventionally calculated centenary of Rio Vermelho’s festival, while researchers note the absence of decisive proof for 1923 rather than 1924.
- **Present:** active worship continues in Nigeria, Cuba, Brazil, Uruguay, Argentina, the United States, Europe, and transnational initiatory networks; mermaid imagery, ecological reform, digital devotion, and lineage-based practice coexist.

## 11. Sources and conclusions on evidentiary status

The most defensible reconstruction is of a documented **nineteenth-century Ẹ̀gbá/Yoruba river òrìṣà with priesthood, maternity, fish, fertility, and dangerous water powers**, whose printed family mythology begins with Ellis in 1894. Twentieth-century oral-text collections enormously expand the record but do not supply ancient manuscript dates. Through slavery, ports, cabildos, terreiros, migration, and modern mass culture, the river mother became Cuba’s Yemayá of Regla and Brazil’s Iemanjá, Queen of the Sea. Her mermaid body and many supposedly timeless correspondences are demonstrably Atlantic and modern; her cult itself is not thereby modern or derivative.

No evidence located supports attaching the Ifẹ̀ heads, temple prostitution, a distinct Yemoja human-sacrifice institution, a closed ancient “Book of Ifá,” or modern zodiac/chakra correspondences to the early cult. Conversely, lack of pre-1855 writing does not imply lack of earlier worship. The proper finding is documentary silence before the colonial archive, not nonexistence.
