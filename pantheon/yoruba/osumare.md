# Osumare (Oxumaré): Research Dossier

> **Class:** Òrìṣà—divine power/deity—in Yoruba tradition; orixá in Brazilian Candomblé and some Umbanda; closely identified with, but historically not automatically identical to, the Mahi-Fon vodun Dan/Ayido-Hwedo.  
> **Earliest secure attestation as a divine being:** A. B. Ellis, *The Yoruba-Speaking Peoples of the Slave Coast of West Africa* (London, 1894), p. 81: “Oshumare” is explicitly called the “Rainbow-god” and “Great Snake of the Underneath.” Earlier Yoruba dictionaries document the word for “rainbow,” but the consulted evidence does not securely establish that their entries classified Òṣùmàrè as a deity.  
> **Evidentiary warning:** Òṣùmàrè belonged to a predominantly oral and locally variable religious field. Most detailed myths were written down only in the twentieth century. Their age as oral compositions is usually indeterminable; the date of collection must not be mistaken for the date of origin.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Òṣùmàrè is the Yoruba rainbow and the divine being manifested in or through it. The securely attested nineteenth-century description also makes Òṣùmàrè a subterranean cosmic serpent whose periodic emergence produces the rainbow. Twentieth-century Yoruba and Afro-Brazilian records expand the figure into the power of circulation, motion, continuity, prosperity, growth, rain, and the connection between earth and sky. Brazilian Candomblé especially developed Oxumaré as a serpent-bearing, visibly embodied orixá with defined colors, implements, foods, dances, feast days, and initiatory lineages.

### Names

- **Yoruba:** **Òṣùmàrè**, with common unmarked spellings *Osumare, Oshumare, Oshumaré,* and sometimes *Osunmare*. The ordinary Yoruba noun *òṣùmàrè* means “rainbow”; it may also denote a python in dictionaries reflecting the religious association.
- **Brazilian Portuguese:** **Oxumaré**, historically also *Oxumarê*. Portuguese **x** represents the Yoruba **ṣ** /ʃ/.
- **Cuban/Lucumí Spanish:** *Ochumaré, Oshumare, Osumaré,* and Cabrera’s *osúmaremi*. Lydia Cabrera recorded *ochumaré* primarily among Cuban words for the rainbow, not as evidence of a widespread independent Lucumí cult.
- **Mahi-Fon/Gbe comparison:** **Dan/Dã**, **Da Ayido Hwedo**, **Aido-Hwedo/Ayido-Hwedo**. In Brazilian Jeje houses, names such as **Bessém/Besén** occur. These are historically connected serpent and rainbow powers, but “Dan = Òṣùmàrè” is itself a product of contact, translation, and diasporic liturgical organization, not a license to merge every Dan, Dangbe, Damballah, Ayida, and Òṣùmàrè tradition.
- **Angola/Congo-derived Brazilian houses:** **Angorô/Hongolo** and **Angoroméia** may be placed in correspondence with Oxumaré. These are Bantu-derived rainbow-serpent powers, not Yoruba names.

### Etymology

**[Secure linguistic fact]** Modern dictionaries derive the divine name from the ordinary noun *òṣùmàrè*, “rainbow.” That explains the Portuguese form *Oxumaré* but not the deeper morphology of the Yoruba word.

**[Early colonial reconstruction; disputed]** Ellis in 1894 proposed *ṣù*, “gather into dark clouds,” plus an uncertain *màrè/máyè*. His analysis depended on the limited nineteenth-century orthography and comparative method and is not an established modern etymology. Ellis elsewhere speculated that the ending might involve *omi* (“water”) and *ayé* (“life/world”), illustrating his uncertainty rather than confirming a derivation.

**[Modern speculative analyses]** Internet explanations dividing the word into several verbal elements, or linking its final element to *Olódùmarè*, are not supported by a demonstrated historical-linguistic derivation. They should be labeled devotional or folk etymologies unless tied to specialist Yoruba linguistics.

### Titles and praise language

Pierre Verger published an oríkì in which Òṣùmàrè:

- remains in heaven;
- controls the rain falling on earth;
- enters the forest “breathing like the wind”;
- is invoked as “Father” to grant growth and long life.

Verger also records the Ifá-description “child of the owner of the brilliantly colored stole.” These are twentieth-century transcriptions of oral texts; they are primary evidence for the traditions Verger encountered, not datable archaic hymns.

Brazilian liturgical speech uses the salutation **Àróbò bó yìí!**, commonly written *Arroboboi*, *Aroboboi,* or *Aoboboí*. Exact segmentation and translation vary in practitioner literature; confident internet translations such as “hail the rainbow lord” should not be treated as philologically established.

### Classification and gender

- **Yoruba evidence:** principally an òrìṣà and rainbow-serpent power. Ellis uses masculine pronouns but records no alternating-sex doctrine.
- **Verger’s Yoruba/Brazil comparative account:** “at the same time male and female,” signified by red and blue around the rainbow.
- **Brazilian Candomblé:** commonly masculine in address and public costume but possessing both male and female principles. Some houses distinguish a feminine quality or “road,” sometimes called Frekuén/Frequém.
- **Modern “six months male, six months female” claim:** widespread in Brazilian educational and popular literature, but no early Yoruba text located in this research states that calendar formula. It should be classified as a **regional modern systematization**, not an established ancient doctrine.
- **Modern queer interpretation:** Oxumaré is increasingly read as gender-fluid, nonbinary, or trans-symbolic. This is a meaningful contemporary reception grounded in older dual-gender descriptions, but modern identity categories cannot simply be projected onto nineteenth-century Yoruba evidence.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

**1843/1852—lexical evidence, not secure cult evidence.** Samuel Ajayi Crowther’s *Vocabulary of the Yoruba Language*, first issued in 1843 and enlarged in 1852, is the earliest major printed Yoruba lexicon. Later dictionaries based on it establish the nineteenth-century written history of Yoruba rainbow vocabulary. The available searchable record did not securely show that Crowther identified Òṣùmàrè as an òrìṣà. It therefore cannot presently displace Ellis as the first explicit divine attestation.

**1894—first secure explicit description.** Ellis lists “Oshumare” as seventeenth among the “Minor Gods”:

> “Oshumare is the Rainbow-god, the Great Snake of the Underneath, who comes up at times above the edge of the earth to drink water from the sky.”

Ellis adds that a python called *ere* is the god’s sacred messenger and compares the being to the Ewe Anyiewo. This passage securely attests, by 1894:

1. divine status;
2. rainbow identity;
3. subterranean serpent cosmology;
4. a sacred-python relationship;
5. comparison with neighboring Gbe-speaking religion.

It does **not** attest Nanã as mother, alternating gender, service to Ṣàngó, Brazilian colors, St Bartholomew, an ouroboros image, or the detailed diviner narrative.

Ellis was a colonial officer and comparative ethnographer, not an initiated Yoruba ritual authority. His report remains primary historical evidence, but his evolutionary classifications, etymologies, and pan-ethnic equivalences require criticism.

### What archaeology does—and does not—show

Ile-Ife archaeology documents an extraordinary urban and artistic civilization, especially from approximately the twelfth through fifteenth centuries. Oyo archaeology documents political and material histories of the Oyo world. No excavated object, Ife head, inscription, or securely provenanced medieval image located in the consulted archaeological literature is identified by contemporary inscription or archaeological context as Òṣùmàrè.

Serpents occur broadly in Yoruba royal, chthonic, medicinal, and religious symbolism. Assigning every serpent image to Òṣùmàrè would be circular. The Ife naturalistic heads provide no evidence for the deity. Thus the archaeological record presently supplies cultural context, not a first attestation.

### Ifá corpus

Ifá is an oral, performative corpus: 256 odù headings contain numerous locally and individually transmitted ẹsẹ̀. A book titled “the Odu Ifá” is not a single ancient fixed manuscript. Wande Abimbola’s 1976 exposition is fundamental for the corpus’s form and transmission, but it does not make every verse later printed under an odù universally canonical.

Published or currently circulating Òṣùmàrè verses include:

- **Òwónrín Méjì:** Òṣùmàrè Ẹ̀gọ́ is a babaláwo who divines for Ìrókò when enemies obstruct Ìrókò’s greatness.
- **A verse assigned in modern teaching to Òtúrá-Ìrẹtẹ̀:** Òṣùmàrè Ẹ̀gọ́ divines for Olókun Seniade, performs sacrifice, and receives prosperity.
- **Diviner narrative recorded by Verger:** Òṣùmàrè, formerly poor and poorly rewarded by the ruler of Ife, heals Olókun’s child, receives wealth, is rewarded again by Olofin, then heals Olódùmarè’s eyes and is retained in heaven.

These verses are **attested twentieth-/twenty-first-century records of oral Ifá traditions**. Their underlying narratives may be older, but no manuscript or inscription supplies an ancient composition date. The exact texts could not be verified in the accessible previews of Bascom, Abimbola, or Epega–Neimark; accordingly, claims that a particular printed collection contains a particular Òṣùmàrè verse need page-level confirmation.

William Bascom’s *Ifa Divination* and *Sixteen Cowries*, Abimbola’s *Ifá*, and Epega and Neimark’s *The Sacred Ifa Oracle* are differently constituted collections. They must not be treated as interchangeable recensions.

### Oríkì and ethnographic corpus

- **Ellis, 1894:** earliest explicit Yoruba description.
- **R. E. Dennett, *Nigerian Studies* (1910):** mentions the snakes/rainbows Oshumare and Ere in a speculative reconstruction of Yoruba color and wealth symbolism.
- **Samuel Johnson, *The History of the Yorubas* (completed 1897; published 1921):** indispensable for Yoruba political and religious history, but it does not provide a comparably full Òṣùmàrè dossier.
- **Frobenius’s Yoruba expeditions, 1910–12:** important to early Ife archaeology and colonial-era collection history, but no securely identified ancient Òṣùmàrè image emerges from that work.
- **Bolaji Idowu, *Olódùmarè: God in Yoruba Belief* (1962):** theological systematization of Yoruba religion; useful but shaped by comparative theology and the effort to present a coherent pan-Yoruba system.
- **Verger, *Notes sur le culte des orisa et vodun* (1957) and later *Orixás*:** central comparative documentation from West Africa and Bahia, including myth, oríkì, ritual equipment, food, dance, and the Dan comparison.
- **Bascom and Abimbola:** critical for understanding why Ifá verses are plural, performed, and recension-specific.
- **Prandi, *Mitologia dos Orixás* (2001):** a literary compilation of 301 African, Brazilian, and Cuban narratives. Prandi deliberately gathered dispersed oral and written versions; his arrangement is a modern documentary synthesis, not an ancient canon.

### Cuban record

Cabrera’s *El Monte* (1954) records *osúmaremi/ochumaré* among Lucumí terms for “rainbow.” This is solid evidence that the name survived in Cuban ritual language.

Natalia Bolívar Aróstegui’s *Los orishas en Cuba* (1990) reports that the cult existed in nineteenth-century Cuba but diminished because ritual specialists and “secrets” were lost. It says Ochumare survives through **Yemayá Ochumare**—“the crown of Yemayá”—and within some Ifá knowledge.

Consequently, “absent from Cuba” is too absolute. The better finding is:

- lexical and mythic survival: attested;
- a widespread independent Lucumí initiation cult comparable to Brazilian Oxumaré: not attested;
- limited, lineage-specific, Arará, Ifá, or Yemayá-associated survival: attested or reported;
- recent importation from Brazil or Nigerian Iṣẹ̀ṣe into some Cuban-derived communities: modern transnational development.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**[Secure by 1894]** Rainbow, serpent, the earth’s underside, heavenly water, and the python as messenger.

**[Twentieth-century Yoruba documentation]** Rain, wind, growth, longevity, wealth, activity, continuity, and “everything elongated.” Verger reports control of the umbilical cord: placenta and cord are buried beneath a palm whose survival is connected with the child’s health.

**[Dahomean comparison]** Dan supplies wealth and movement and supports or encircles the world. Blue *segi* beads are called *danni*, “serpent excrement,” and are imagined as precious matter found underground.

**[Brazilian elaboration]** Cycles, transformation, renewal, prosperity, fertility, the movement of wealth, ascent and descent, and the link between *ayé* and *ọ̀run*.

### Form and iconography

- rainbow;
- large snake or python;
- serpent encircling the world;
- serpent biting its tail;
- anthropomorphic dancer bearing a forged iron snake;
- multicolored clothing or rainbow-colored bands;
- in Bahia, yellow-and-green bead necklaces according to Verger;
- **brajá**, long cowrie strands arranged like serpent scales;
- **ebiri**, a palm-rib implement also associated with Nanã/Obaluaiê complexes;
- iron serpent or paired serpents.

During possession-dance, Verger observed initiates alternately point upward and downward, enacting the connection between heaven and earth. The iconographic record is principally ethnographic and modern; there is no securely labeled medieval Yoruba Òṣùmàrè statue.

The ouroboros comparison appears in twentieth-century ethnography. It is descriptively apt where a serpent bites its tail, but claims that Yoruba tradition borrowed directly from Egyptian, Greek, alchemical, or Theosophical ouroboros symbolism are unproved.

### Colors, day, materials, and offerings

Verger records for Bahia:

- yellow and green glass beads;
- Tuesday;
- ducks;
- dishes combining beans, maize, shrimp, and palm oil;
- cowries, palm fibers, and forged iron.

Other houses use rainbow colors, blue and red, green and yellow, or combinations determined by lineage. Claims that one palette is universally Yoruba are untenable.

A 2018 ethnographic account documents the sacrificial offering of a goat to Oxumaré in Bahia, with the meat shared by the community. This is direct evidence for modern animal sacrifice. No secure evidence was located for human sacrifice to Òṣùmàrè in Yoruba, Brazilian, or Cuban practice. Ellis’s neighboring paragraph reports a human victim for the mountain deity Oke; it must not be transferred to Òṣùmàrè.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

No single genealogy is universally authoritative.

### Genealogical systems

**[Early attestation]** Ellis gives no parents, spouse, siblings, or children.

**[Mahi-Fon/Brazilian complex]** Oxumaré is often made a child of **Nanã Buruku** and sibling of **Obaluaiê/Omolu** and sometimes **Ewá** or **Ìrókò**. This family groups deities with strong Mahi, Fon, Jeje, earth, disease, serpent, and antiquity associations. It is best understood as a regional and diasporic theological complex, not a genealogy demonstrated for all Yoruba communities.

**[Brazilian literary version]** Prandi’s 2004 children’s retelling calls Oxumaré the son of Nanã, “the oldest of women.” That is a modern literary adaptation based on his 2001 compilation.

**[Dan relation]** Verger says Òṣùmàrè, like Nanã and Obaluaiê, was said to originate in Mahi country and is called Dan there. Peel likewise describes Aido-Hwedo/Dan as a Mahi rainbow serpent called Osumare by Yoruba speakers. This supports historical borrowing or deep regional interaction, but it does not determine one simple direction of origin.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The subterranean serpent drinks from heaven

**[Secure early attestation: 1894]** Òṣùmàrè is the Great Snake beneath the earth. At intervals he raises himself over the earth’s rim to drink water from the sky; this visible emergence is the rainbow. The python *ere* is his messenger.

This concise cosmology is the earliest documented narrative and should be treated as the early recoverable core: rainbow, cosmic serpent, below-earth location, water, and sacred python.

### The serpent holds the world together

**[Twentieth-century Yoruba/Dahomean synthesis]** Òṣùmàrè coils around the earth to prevent its disintegration. If the serpent weakened, the world would end; offerings maintain its strength.

The fuller Dan/Ayido-Hwedo myth says the serpent carried the creator Mawu-Lisa during formation of the world; its winding movement formed terrain, while mineral wealth came from its excretions. When the completed earth became too heavy, the serpent coiled beneath or around it in the sea. Movement produces earthquakes or celestial motion.

This myth is securely documented for Dahomean religion by Herskovits in the 1930s. Its wholesale attribution to ancient pan-Yoruba religion is unwarranted. Verger’s account demonstrates a twentieth-century Yoruba–Mahi identification but also says Dan was more important among Mahi and Fon than Òṣùmàrè was among Yoruba.

A crucial distinction is required between:

- **Dangbe**, the royal/sacred python cult of Hueda and Ouidah;
- **Dan**, a broad serpent/wealth complex;
- **Ayido-Hwedo**, the rainbow/cosmic-serpent manifestation;
- **Yoruba Òṣùmàrè**;
- Haitian **Dambala Wedo and Ayida Wedo**.

They interact historically but are not timeless synonyms.

### The poor diviner becomes wealthy

**[Attested twentieth-century Ifá tradition]** Òṣùmàrè is a gifted but impoverished babaláwo. Olofin consults him every four days and pays him so little that he remains in poverty. Olókun summons him to cure a child who cannot stand and rolls in hot ashes during attacks. Òṣùmàrè succeeds and receives abundant gifts and blue clothing. Olofin, embarrassed by his avarice, gives further wealth and a red garment. Finally, Òṣùmàrè cures Olódùmarè’s eyes; the supreme being keeps him in heaven. His occasional descent brings human prosperity.

The story explains:

- rainbow color through blue and red garments;
- wealth after poverty;
- the four-day ritual week;
- residence in heaven;
- the orixá’s periodic visibility;
- his powers as healer and diviner.

Verger’s summary is the fullest accessible documentary source. The story’s exact odù and pre-collection history remain uncertain.

### Returning water to Ṣàngó

**[Later explanatory myth]** As Ṣàngó’s servant, Òṣùmàrè lifts fallen rainwater back to the clouds. The rainbow thereby becomes a visible water-transporting arc. Verger questioned the formulation as resembling a Western primary-school explanation of the water cycle. His reservation is important: an indigenous divine association may have been recast under colonial scientific pedagogy.

### Alternating male and female form

**[Brazilian regional elaboration]** Oxumaré is both male and female, sometimes said to change from one to the other for six-month periods. Earlier sources support duality or simultaneous male/female nature more securely than a literal semiannual transformation. The calendarized version should therefore be cited as modern Brazilian religious folklore/systematization.

### Oxumaré stops the destructive rain

**[Modern literary retelling]** In Prandi’s 2004 children’s book, rain floods the world and spreads disease. Oxumaré, son of Nanã, cuts the sky with a silver dagger, halting the rain and becoming visible as the rainbow. This is explicitly a modern literary adaptation based on the author’s myth compilation, not an independently dated ancient text.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### West Africa

The documentary evidence suggests a real but relatively localized Yoruba cult, especially in zones of interaction with Mahi-Fon traditions. It does not support the image of Òṣùmàrè as one of the universally dominant civic deities of every Yoruba city.

Ellis calls the being a “minor god.” Verger describes Òṣùmàrè as universally recognizable in the rainbow but contrasts the cult’s Yoruba importance with Dan’s greater prominence among Mahi and Fon. Contemporary practitioner testimony likewise reports surviving families and priests, notably in and around Ketu/Benin, but limited popularity elsewhere.

No securely documented monumental Yoruba temple with a known foundation date was located. Nor were separate archaeological priestly quarters, votive inscriptions, or theophoric-name series securely attributable to Òṣùmàrè. The absence matters: most knowledge comes from oral verse, ritual testimony, and comparative ethnography.

### Brazil

Oxumaré has a much more visible institutional existence in Bahia than the available Yoruba urban record would suggest.

The **Ilé Òṣùmàrè Aràká Àṣẹ Ògòdó**, commonly Casa de Oxumarê, traces its ancestry to Manoel Joaquim Ricardo, Bàbá Tàlábí, and to an Ajunsun/Sakpata-oriented calundu at Obitedó in Cachoeira. Institutional narratives place its beginnings around the turn of the nineteenth century, although different accounts distinguish ancestral foundation, transfer to Salvador, and establishment at the present site.

The house reports more than a thousand descendant communities. It was:

- recognized as Afro-Brazilian cultural territory by Fundação Cultural Palmares in 2002;
- protected by Bahia’s IPAC in 2004;
- recognized by IPHAN as Brazilian cultural heritage in 2014.

Its name does not mean that every rite there is exclusively directed to Oxumaré; a Candomblé terreiro houses an extensive pantheon and lineage.

### Ritual

Verger’s Bahia documentation includes:

- Tuesday observance;
- yellow-and-green beads;
- *brajá* cowrie strands;
- *ebiri*;
- iron serpent;
- dances pointing between heaven and earth;
- the salutation *Aoboboí*;
- duck and prepared-food offerings;
- pilgrimage or bathing at a rainbow-bearing waterfall near São Bartolomeu on 24 August.

Later houses vary in animals, foods, colors, rhythms, and secrecy. Public manuals routinely flatten these differences.

Animal sacrifice remains documented. It produces *axé* and food shared among deity and community; contemporary legal controversies over the practice must be understood in the history of racial and religious discrimination. Human sacrifice specifically to Oxumaré is not documented in the sources examined.

### Cuba

Ochumare’s name and cosmological idea survived, but the independent cult contracted. Reported causes include loss of priests and ritual knowledge. A “Yemayá Ochumare” path, Ifá references, and Arará correspondences preserve traces. Claims that ordinary Lucumí houses regularly crown or initiate Ochumare should be treated as lineage-specific or recent unless a documented genealogy is produced.

### Umbanda and Batuque

Oxumaré is present unevenly in Umbanda. Some houses treat the name as an orixá force of renewal; others omit it or absorb it under Oxum, Yemanjá, or a “rainbow line.” Correspondences with Angorô, Bessém, Gabriel, St Bartholomew, or even the Devil reveal locally authored classificatory systems rather than a single Umbanda canon.

Brazilian Batuque and the Batuque–Umbanda religious field transmitted to Argentina incorporate orixá devotion through particular Rio Grande do Sul lineages. Frigerio and Carozzi document the Argentine expansion and adaptation of this broader field, but the consulted material does not establish a distinct, numerically significant Argentine Oxumaré cult. Absence of deity-specific evidence should not be replaced with inference from the presence of Batuque generally.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the nineteenth century

The rainbow word, oral cosmology, and cult are certainly older than Ellis’s book, but no dated inscription, manuscript, or securely identified archaeological image establishes how much older. Claims of a cult dating to “6000 BCE,” primordial Egypt, or prehistoric Ife are modern pseudohistory.

The Dan and sacred-python complexes of the Slave Coast were described by European travelers earlier than the Yoruba Òṣùmàrè documentation. Yet those reports often concern Dangbe at Ouidah, not necessarily Ayido-Hwedo or Òṣùmàrè. Earlier documentation of a neighboring serpent cult does not prove direct ancestry.

### Nineteenth century

- Yoruba religious vocabulary enters missionary dictionaries.
- Atlantic slavery and post-slave-trade movements transport Yoruba, Mahi, Fon, Ewe, and Central African traditions to Brazil and Cuba.
- Bahian calundus and emerging Candomblé houses combine persons and liturgies from multiple “nations.”
- Ellis publishes the first unambiguous description of Oshumare in 1894.

Missionary journals preserve exceptionally detailed information about some Yoruba cults but little securely identified Òṣùmàrè material. This imbalance suggests either localization, observer selectivity, or both.

### 1900–1950

Dennett and later ethnographers place Òṣùmàrè within broader Yoruba symbolic systems. In Bahia, Oxumaré becomes institutionally prominent through named houses, initiatory descent, public festivals, and material iconography.

Candomblé nevertheless operates under police surveillance and criminalizing or discretionary regulation. Raids, confiscation of sacred objects, and requirements for police authorization affected Afro-Brazilian religions broadly. No evidence located shows a state campaign aimed uniquely at Oxumaré.

Cuban evidence suggests contraction rather than equivalent expansion: the rainbow name survives, but specialists of an independent cult diminish.

### 1950–1980

Cabrera, Bastide, Verger, Bascom, Idowu, Abimbola, Herskovits, and others transform oral and initiated knowledge into print. This both preserves and standardizes it.

Verger’s transatlantic movement is particularly consequential: observations from Benin, Nigeria, and Bahia are placed side by side. The resulting comparison made the Òṣùmàrè–Dan relationship widely legible but also encouraged later readers to convert an explicitly historical comparison into a timeless identity.

Umbanda literature increasingly maps orixás into Spiritist, Catholic, esoteric, chromatic, and vibrational hierarchies. Oxumaré’s meanings of motion, transformation, and polarity lend themselves readily to such schemes.

### 1980–2000

Brazilian re-Africanization movements challenge obligatory Catholic syncretism, while heritage activism secures recognition for terreiros. Transnational travel among Brazil, Cuba, Nigeria, Benin, Argentina, Europe, and the United States creates new ritual transfers.

The cult’s limited Cuban survival becomes a site of contemporary controversy: practitioners dispute whether newly offered “Oshumare” consecrations preserve a lost lineage, derive from Arará practice, or are recent Brazilian/Nigerian imports.

### 2000–present

Prandi’s mythology compilation and children’s books popularize Oxumaré nationally. Museums collect serpent implements, costume, and sound recordings. The Museu da Língua Portuguesa presents Òṣùmàrè as a Yoruba lexical and cultural inheritance in Brazilian Portuguese.

Casa de Oxumarê’s heritage recognition in 2014 materially anchors the cult in Brazilian public history. The house has also participated in opposition to evangelical intimidation and anti-Candomblé violence.

Contemporary queer, trans, and decolonial writers increasingly use Oxumaré’s dual gender as a resource against rigid binaries. This is a modern interpretive history—not proof that precolonial Yoruba society formulated present-day LGBTQ identities through the deity.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and visual culture

Prandi’s *Oxumarê, o Arco-Íris* (2004), illustrated by Pedro Rafael, is the most clearly identifiable modern literary reinvention. It turns collected mythic motifs into a continuous children’s narrative.

Brazilian artists, including Carybé and terreiro-trained metalworkers, have helped stabilize the modern visual grammar of an anthropomorphic, richly dressed Oxumaré carrying a serpent. Museum serpent pairs and forged tools preserve actual cult objects, but their meanings depend on ritual context.

Musician Yilian Cañizares founded a group named Ochumare, using the Cuban-Yoruba word for “rainbow.” Such cultural usage shows lexical survival without proving an independent Cuban cult.

### Gender and sexuality

Modern scholarship and activism describe Oxumaré as simultaneously male and female, and sometimes as an emblem of gender fluidity. Recent ethnography records paired male-and-female sacrificial animals in some Brazilian rites. This is stronger evidence for ritual duality than the popular “six months each” formula.

Claims that Oxumaré is universally “patron of bisexuals,” “the transgender orixá,” or guarantees particular sexual behavior in devotees are modern essentializations. They are not supported by Ellis, early lexicons, or a universal Candomblé doctrine.

### Occult and neopagan appropriation

No evidence was found that Renaissance Hermeticists, the Theosophical Society, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, or an early European grimoire tradition knew Òṣùmàrè by name. Websites presenting Ochumare as an ancient “cosmic channel among the goddesses,” a Wiccan goddess, a chakra, or an alchemical current are modern appropriations.

The serpent biting its tail has encouraged comparison with the ouroboros and Kundalini. Similarity is not evidence of historical transmission. Likewise, Damballah, Quetzalcoatl, the Australian Rainbow Serpent, Nāga traditions, and Òṣùmàrè should not be declared manifestations of one prehistoric worldwide cult without historical evidence.

### Political and commercial reception

Oxumaré functions in Brazilian heritage, anti-racist education, Black cultural affirmation, festivals, jewelry, fashion, music, tourism, and commercial spirituality. Educational circulation expanded after laws requiring Afro-Brazilian history and culture in schools.

Commercial lists fixing personality types, lucky numbers, crystals, chakras, perfumes, and universal offerings usually derive from late twentieth- or twenty-first-century New Age and mass-market systems. Unless a lineage and dated source are supplied, these are modern inventions.

No documented modern extremist appropriation specific to Oxumaré was located. The relevant political history is instead racist and evangelical hostility toward Afro-Brazilian religion, including attacks on terreiros and disputes over ritual slaughter.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Yoruba or Mahi-Fon origin?

Verger states that Òṣùmàrè’s origin, like Nanã and Obaluaiê’s, “would be” Mahi and that Dan is more important among Mahi and Fon. Peel describes Dan/Aido-Hwedo as originating in Mahi country and being called Osumare by Yoruba speakers.

The evidence supports:

- intensive Yoruba–Mahi–Fon contact;
- cognate or translated rainbow-serpent concepts;
- a stronger and more elaborated Dan complex in the documented Mahi-Fon record;
- Brazilian grouping of Oxumaré with Nanã and Obaluaiê.

It does **not** securely establish the exact date, route, or one-way direction by which the cult entered Yoruba-speaking communities. The ordinary Yoruba word for rainbow and Ellis’s Yoruba evidence caution against reducing Òṣùmàrè to a mere modern mistranslation of Dan.

### The “pan-Yoruba pantheon” problem

Peel emphasizes that actual Yoruba religion historically consisted of local cult complexes, whereas modern books construct standardized pan-Yoruba pantheons. Òṣùmàrè demonstrates this problem vividly: universal recognition of a rainbow word is not the same as universal priesthood, shrine, ritual, or genealogy.

### Gender dispute

The sequence of evidence is:

1. Ellis, 1894: masculine god; no dual-gender statement.
2. twentieth-century comparative ethnography: male and female simultaneously.
3. Brazilian houses: masculine public address plus male/female qualities and paired ritual forms.
4. popular literature: six-month alternation.
5. contemporary scholarship: gender-fluid/decolonial interpretation.

This makes duality well attested in modern tradition, but the precise alternating calendar and its antiquity remain unestablished.

### Textual dating

Ifá verses are preserved through trained performance rather than a single closed scripture. Attribution to an odù does not automatically date a verse. Bascom, Abimbola, Verger, Epega–Neimark, and present-day priests recorded different selections and recensions. “It is in Ifá” must therefore be followed by collector, informant or lineage where known, publication, page, language, and date.

### Discredited or unsupported claims

The following remain unsupported:

- an identifiable Òṣùmàrè cult in the medieval Ife heads;
- a prehistoric global rainbow-serpent religion;
- direct Egyptian or Hermetic origin;
- universal Yoruba worship;
- universal six-month sex alternation;
- a single canonical genealogy;
- a major independent Cuban cult continuously maintained everywhere;
- human sacrifice to Òṣùmàrè;
- ancient Wiccan, Theosophical, Golden Dawn, or chakra doctrine;
- automatic equivalence of Òṣùmàrè, Dangbe, Dan, Damballah, Ayida Wedo, Angorô, and every rainbow serpent.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Documentary event |
|---|---|
| Before 1800 | Oral rainbow-serpent traditions likely existed, but no securely dated Òṣùmàrè inscription, image, or text is presently identified. |
| Late 18th–early 19th c. | Institutional memory of Casa de Oxumarê traces its ancestry to Bàbá Tàlábí and an Ajunsun-related calundu in Bahia; exact foundational stages remain debated. |
| 1843/1852 | Crowther publishes and enlarges the first major Yoruba vocabulary; establishes early written lexicographic context, but an explicit divine entry for Òṣùmàrè was not verified here. |
| 1894 | Ellis explicitly prints Oshumare as the Yoruba Rainbow-god, subterranean Great Snake, and lord of a sacred python messenger. |
| 1910 | Dennett mentions Oshumare/Ere as snake-rainbow powers in a speculative Yoruba symbolic system. |
| 1921 | Samuel Johnson’s *History of the Yorubas* appears posthumously, supplying broad historical context but no comparably detailed Òṣùmàrè cult history. |
| 1930s | Herskovits records the Dahomean Dan/Aido-Hwedo cosmic-serpent complex; Afro-Brazilian religion becomes a major ethnographic subject. |
| 1936 | Herskovits and Herskovits document Aido Wedo in Suriname and explicitly connect the name with Dahomey and Haiti. |
| 1954 | Cabrera’s *El Monte* records Cuban *osúmaremi/ochumaré* as rainbow vocabulary. |

| 1958 | Bastide publishes *Le Candomblé de Bahia*, systematizing the Nagô liturgical field. |
| 1962 | Idowu publishes *Olódùmarè*, a major theological reconstruction of Yoruba belief. |
| 1969/1976 | Abimbola’s dissertation and book define the literary and performative structure of the Ifá corpus. |
| 1984 | Robert Farris Thompson’s *Flash of the Spirit* popularizes Dan/Ayido-Hwedo and Afro-Atlantic aesthetic continuities. |
| 1988 | Associação Cultural e Religiosa São Salvador is formally organized at Casa de Oxumarê. |
| 1990 | Bolívar Aróstegui reports Ochumare’s nineteenth-century Cuban presence, later decline, and survival through Yemayá Ochumare and Ifá. |
| 2001 | Prandi publishes *Mitologia dos Orixás*, a major modern compilation rather than a fixed ancient canon. |
| 2002 | Casa de Oxumarê recognized by Fundação Cultural Palmares as Afro-Brazilian cultural territory. |

| 2018–19 | Brazil’s litigation over religious animal sacrifice culminates in constitutional protection, directly relevant to documented Oxumaré offerings. |
| 2020s | Oxumaré becomes increasingly visible in heritage programs, digital Iṣẹ̀ṣe, transnational initiation networks, and scholarship on gender fluidity; online material also proliferates modern inventions. |

## 11. Sources and assessment

The most important primary or near-primary documentary witnesses are Ellis’s 1894 description, recorded Ifá verses and oríkì, Cabrera’s Cuban testimony, Verger’s field documentation, Dahomean material recorded by Herskovits, ritual objects in museum collections, and the living institutional archives of Casa de Oxumarê.

The central methodological result is that the familiar modern portrait is stratified:

- **Earliest recoverable Yoruba core:** divine rainbow, subterranean serpent, water, sacred python.
- **Documented twentieth-century Yoruba elaboration:** rain, motion, length, umbilical cord, prosperity, oríkì, diviner-healer myths.
- **Mahi-Fon comparison or influence:** Dan/Ayido-Hwedo, world-supporting serpent, wealth beads, cosmic movement.
- **Brazilian canonical development:** Nanã family, highly visible serpent implements, defined beads and foods, possession-dance, St Bartholomew, institutional houses, simultaneous male/female character.
- **Cuban residue:** rainbow vocabulary, Yemayá Ochumare, limited Ifá and Arará preservation rather than a broadly independent cult.
- **Modern invention or reception:** exact six-month gender calendar where unsourced, universal occult correspondences, Wiccan or chakra classifications, archaeological attribution without inscriptions, and indiscriminate equation of all rainbow serpents.
