# Iroko: Research Dossier

**Class:** In Yoruba tradition, Ìrókò is variously classified as a sacred tree, the indwelling spirit of that tree, an òrìṣà, or a locus inhabited by a plurality of spirits. These classifications coexist and should not be harmonized. In Cuban Lucumí practice, Iroko is principally the sacred power or divine inhabitant of the ceiba. In Brazilian Ketu Candomblé, Iroko is an orixá associated with a consecrated tree; Dahomean/Fon Loko is a vodun associated with trees; and Angola-Congo Tempo/Kitembo is an inkice. Their Brazilian equation is a historical, cross-“nation” correspondence, not proof that they were originally one deity.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Samuel Ajayi Crowther’s *Vocabulary of the Yoruba Language* was first published in 1843 and enlarged in 1852, giving early written evidence for the word, though not a developed divine biography. The earliest presently verifiable printed description explicitly joining Yoruba worship, sacrifice, and “Iroko” appears in mid-nineteenth-century Anglican missionary literature: “The Yorubas worship also the African oak (Iroko),” which reports that a woodcutter offered fowls before cutting it. The first easily inspectable full ethnographic description is A. B. Ellis’s *The Yoruba-Speaking Peoples of the Slave Coast* (1894), followed by Samuel Johnson’s manuscript history, completed in 1897 and published posthumously in 1921. These sources are colonial and missionary records, but they establish that the cultic complex predates twentieth-century systematization. No securely identified ancient inscription, excavated image, or datable precolonial manuscript naming Ìrókò is presently known.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Yoruba

- **Ìrókò / Iroko / Iroco / Roko.** Yoruba tree-name and, according to context, the tree’s indwelling power. The timber term entered English as *iroko*. Modern botanical usage principally identifies *Milicia excelsa* and, commercially, *M. regia*. Older literature uses *Chlorophora excelsa* and misleading English names such as “African oak” or “African teak.”
- **Olúwéré / Oluwere.** A named spirit said in several Yoruba accounts to inhabit or own an iroko tree. In the Olúrónbí legend it is often Olúwéré, rather than an abstract “god Iroko,” who receives the woman’s vow. This is a regional/folkloric name, not demonstrably a universal epithet.
- **Ìrókò-ìgbó**, “Iroko of the forest” or “Iroko of Ìgbò,” occurs in an ẹsẹ Ifá published by Wande Abimbola. The ambiguity matters: *ìgbó* can denote forest, but Abimbola’s translation understands it as the place-name Ìgbò.
- **Igi Olórun**, “tree of the Lord/Owner of heaven,” and claims that Iroko is “king” or “commander” of sacred trees are found in later ritual and popular descriptions. Their antiquity has not been established.
- **Olú-Ìrókò**, “lord/chief of Iroko,” is used for priestly or custodial offices in some modern communities; it is not evidence of a centralized pan-Yoruba priesthood.

No convincing published linguistic derivation of *ìrókò* into smaller Yoruba morphemes was found. The secure statement is lexical: it is the Yoruba tree-name. Claims that the word inherently means “time,” “ancestral time,” “world axis,” or “first tree” are theological interpretations, mainly prominent in Brazil and recent transnational teaching, not demonstrated etymologies.

### Fon, Jeje, and related forms

**Loko, Loco, roko, irokò** name the tree and/or its vodun in the Bight of Benin. In Fon religious terminology Loko can enter compounds naming **atimɛ-vodun**, tree-vodun. Modern ethnobotanical research in Benin found multiple local classifications: 10.4 percent of respondents identified iroko specifically with Loko; 2.3 percent with Hêviosso; 47.4 percent said it might house any divinity. This is strong evidence against a single universal equation “iroko tree = only Loko.”

Brazilian Jeje terminology includes **Loko** and **Atinmé-vodun**, approximately “vodun in/within the tree.” Initiates may be called **Lokosi**; **Lokonon** can designate a cult functionary or name formed from Loko. These are Fon/Jeje categories, not Yoruba titles.

### Diasporic correspondences

- **Cuba:** Iroko/Iroke is a Lucumí name for the sacred power of the **ceiba**, *Ceiba pentandra*. Lydia Cabrera records “Arabba, Iroko, Elúwere, Asaba, Iggi-Arabbá, Iggi-Olorun” as overlapping informants’ names and titles. The ceiba is therefore a diasporic botanical substitution and a meeting place of several West and Central African religious histories, not literally the African *Milicia*.
- **Brazilian Ketu:** **Iroko/Iroco** is an orixá installed in or represented by a consecrated tree, commonly a gameleira or other locally available large tree.
- **Brazilian Jeje:** **Loko** is a vodun.
- **Brazilian Angola-Congo:** **Tempo, Kitembo/Kindembu** is an inkice, often represented by a pole, mast, flag, or sacred tree. The Iroko–Loko–Tempo alignment is a Brazilian syncretistic taxonomy produced through proximity among Candomblé “nations.” It is correspondence, not identity.
- Associations with **Saint Francis of Assisi**, **Saint Lawrence**, or the **Holy Spirit** vary by house and are late Brazilian Catholic correspondences. No universal pairing exists.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Documentary sequence

**1843/1852 — securely attested lexical evidence.** Crowther’s Yoruba vocabulary fixes an early written form of the name within the first generation of Yoruba print. It does not supply the modern theology of a male “orisha of time.”

**Mid-nineteenth century — securely attested cult report.** Anglican missionary reporting describes worship of the “African oak (Iroko)” and a fowl sacrifice required before felling. The report is valuable as an early observation but uses the Christian polemical vocabulary of “tree worship.”

**1894 — securely attested ethnography.** Ellis distinguishes Iroko from the silk-cotton tree, although botanical confusion enters his discussion. He says the Iroko is inhabited by a spirit “not very powerful or malicious”; a woodcutter could protect himself by invoking his own head-spirit and rubbing palm oil on his forehead. This differs markedly from later descriptions of Iroko as an inexorable cosmic governor.

**1897/1921 — securely attested Yoruba-authored historical compilation.** Samuel Johnson associates large iroko trees with the woodland company of **àbíkú**, children believed repeatedly to die and return. His evidence supports an association with dangerous forest spirits and recurrent infant death, but does not present a full Iroko genealogy or temple hierarchy.

**Early twentieth century — colonial ethnography.** Leo Frobenius recorded extensive Yoruba religion and art, but no securely excavated “Iroko image” comparable to the Ife heads can be extracted from his finds. The famous Ife heads, now dated broadly to the twelfth–fifteenth centuries, document the sophistication of Ife art and kingship, not Iroko specifically.

**1958 — dictionary evidence.** R. C. Abraham’s *Dictionary of Modern Yoruba* defines *iroko* as inhabited by a mischievous supernatural being and notes white cloth and sacrifice at its base. This records living usage, not an ancient textual canon.

**1950s–1970s — Ifá corpus publications.** William Bascom, Wande Abimbola, E. M. Lijadu, and later Epega and Neimark published selections from an orally transmitted, lineage-variable corpus. These editions do not constitute one closed scripture. A verse published by Abimbola says divination was performed for Ìrókò-Ìgbò when surrounded by enemies. Iroko sacrifices; Èṣù summons farmers to clear the forest, but stops them at Iroko because the diviners declared it an extraordinary tree. This is the clearest published Ifá narrative directly centered on Iroko.

Bascom’s *Ifa Divination* (1969) and *Sixteen Cowries* (1980) document divinatory institutions and narratives but should not be cited as though they contain a systematic “Book of Iroko.” Epega and Neimark’s *The Sacred Ifa Oracle* is a modern compilation and translation; its standardized presentation is later systematization. Online assertions assigning Iroko a fixed role in every one of the sixteen principal odù are modern teaching claims unless an identifiable verse and reciter are supplied.

**1954 onward — Cuba.** Lydia Cabrera’s *El Monte* records Cuban oral testimony about the ceiba/Iroko, including songs, offerings, spirits of the dead, destructive power, and narratives about attempted tree-felling. Cabrera deliberately preserves conflicting voices; her book is ethnography and literary montage, not a uniform Lucumí canon.

**1950s–1990s — Brazil.** Roger Bastide, Pierre Verger, Robert Farris Thompson, J. Lorand Matory, and Reginaldo Prandi document or interpret Candomblé formations. Verger explicitly treats Iroko and Loko together but admits he could establish relatively little about some Fon Loko cults. Prandi’s collected myths are invaluable records of late twentieth-century oral narration, not automatically evidence that every story is ancient or African.

### Oral forms

The relevant corpus also includes **oríkì**, songs, prayers, proverbs, children’s songs, ritual prescriptions, and local histories. These remain performance texts: wording and attribution vary by town, lineage, priest, and occasion. A text first transcribed in the twentieth century may preserve older material, but oral antiquity cannot be dated merely from traditional attribution.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Secure and recurrent attributes

Iroko is first of all a **large, long-lived tree and a dangerous sacred locus**. Recurrent practices include preserving the tree during clearing, tying white cloth around it, depositing kola, palm oil, food, money, pottery, or animal offerings at its base, and seeking permission before cutting. Beninese survey evidence documents pottery, iron, calabashes, cloth, money, stones, glass, convent structures, and sacrificial deposits used to signify divinities embodied in iroko.

The tree may house **ìwin** or other forest beings, **àbíkú**, ancestors, or a named spirit such as Olúwéré. These propositions are not mutually interchangeable: one locality may regard the tree itself as divine, another as a shrine or vessel, and another as a dangerous residence.

Its height and vertical form readily support later interpretations as a connection between earth and sky. “Axis mundi” is a useful scholarly comparison, but is not itself an early Yoruba title.

### Time

The now-popular identity “Iroko, orixá of Time” is strongest in Brazilian Candomblé and modern transnational literature. Longevity, duration, fulfilled promises, and generational continuity follow intelligibly from the ancient tree, but the fully personified cosmic office—recording divine decisions, governing karma, determining beginnings and endings—is **late learned or popular systematization**. “Karma” is especially diagnostic of modern cross-religious vocabulary.

### Form and representation

There is no securely authenticated precolonial anthropomorphic statue identifiable solely as Iroko. The primary icon is the **living consecrated tree**, sometimes marked with white cloth or ritual objects. Cuban Iroko is the ceiba; Brazilian substitutions include gameleira species; Dahomean Loko may inhabit *Milicia*, ceiba, oil palm, papaya, and other trees. Consequently botanical species alone cannot identify the divine occupant.

Claims assigning universal colors, weekdays, numbers, metals, weapons, or elaborate regalia are house-specific or modern handbook material. White cloth is comparatively well documented; Tuesday, green/brown/white, and fixed salutations are Brazilian ritual conventions rather than demonstrated pan-Yoruba antiquities.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

No securely early Yoruba source supplies Iroko with a stable parentage, spouse, siblings, or offspring. Modern charts that make Iroko a primordial male child of Olódùmarè are theological constructions unless tied to a local recitation. Fon/Brazilian accounts sometimes call Loko the firstborn of **Mawu-Lisa**, while other systems give primacy to Sakpata; this is a regional hierarchy, not Iroko’s Yoruba genealogy.

### Documented divine interactions

- **Èṣù — protection after sacrifice:** Èṣù calls farmers to cut down the trees hostile to Ìrókò-Ìgbò, then orders them to spare Iroko as an extraordinary tree. Source: ẹsẹ Ifá published by Wande Abimbola, *An Exposition of Ifá Literary Corpus* (1976); **recorded Ifá corpus / later published attestation**.
- **Èṣù — rescue of Olúrónbí’s child:** In one modern Ifá-style variant, Èṣù substitutes a goat’s head for the promised child and invokes a taboo against Iroko collecting twice. The older, better-known folktale often lacks this rescue. Source: late twentieth-/twenty-first-century retellings; **regional variant / modern retelling**.
- **Ọ̀rúnmìlà — marriage to Irinmọ̀dọ̀:** In Abimbola’s published “Nature” verses, Ifá makes Irinmọ̀dọ̀ queen of the trees and Ọ̀rúnmìlà marries her. The text concerns a tree sovereign but does not explicitly establish Irinmọ̀dọ̀ as identical with Iroko; **recorded Ifá material; identity unproven**.
- **Oyá — female presence at Iroko’s foot:** Cabrera records a Cuban informant’s statement that a delicate “sister of Oyá,” represented by two curved mother-of-pearl shells in a pot, lives at Iroko’s foot and receives sacrifice on a table. Source: *El Monte* (1954); **Cuban regional testimony**.
- **Aggayú — sacrifice beneath a ceiba:** Cabrera records a Matanzas plantation tradition that a bullock was sacrificed beneath the Socorro ceiba on 16 November for Aggayú, while the same tree was called Iroko. This is a shared ritual site, not proof that Aggayú and Iroko are identical. Source: *El Monte*; **Cuban testimony**.
- **Hêviosso — possible inhabitation of iroko:** A modern Beninese survey found a small minority identifying an iroko tree with Hêviosso. **Regional ethnographic variant**, not genealogy.
- **Mawu and Lisa — Loko as firstborn:** Reported in some Fon/Jeje theological ordering; rival schemes assign primacy elsewhere. **Learned/regional systematization**.
- **Tempo/Kitembo — correspondence:** Brazilian houses compare or align Iroko with the Angola-Congo inkice Tempo because both are linked with trees, duration, and outdoor standards. **Syncretistic identification**, not a narrative meeting.
- **Loko — correspondence:** Ketu Iroko and Jeje Loko are frequently correlated in Brazil and by Verger. **Syncretistic identification**; their similar names and Bight-of-Benin tree complex suggest historical relationship, but do not erase their distinct liturgies.
- **Olódùmarè/Olorun — claimed celestial authority:** Later descriptions call Iroko *Igi Olórun* or the tree by which other orixás descended. No early source located documents a discrete meeting between the two. **Late elaboration; interaction unestablished**.

No securely sourced interaction with Ṣàngó, Ọ̀ṣun, Yemọja, Ògún, Ọbàtálá, or Ọya in an early Yoruba narrative was located. Shared membership in modern orixá lists is not an interaction.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Olúrónbí and the promised child

This is the principal narrative. Women petition a sacred iroko/Olúwéré for children, promising sheep, goats, food, cloth, or other gifts. The childless Olúrónbí boasts or vows that, if granted a child, she will surrender that child. She gives birth—often to a daughter named Béporé or Olúrónkẹ́—but attempts to substitute ordinary offerings. Iroko recalls the exact promise in song and takes or demands the child.

The story’s common song contrasts others’ promises of animals with Olúrónbí’s promise of “her child, beautiful as palm oil,” concluding with Iroko as collector of the child. Its functions are transparent within the narrative: vows must be fulfilled; reckless speech has consequences; extraordinary demands require extraordinary payment.

Variants differ radically:

1. **Tragic folklore:** the child disappears into the tree or dies; Olúrónbí’s grief remains the ending.
2. **Èṣù rescue:** Èṣù substitutes a goat and saves the child.
3. **Rationalizing modern children’s version:** priests deny ever harming humans; a doll or other substitute resolves the problem.
4. **Modern literary horror:** the “Iroko-man” possesses or kills, elaborating the tree spirit into a monster.

The legend is evidence for cultural memory of a **promised human offering**, not by itself proof that children were historically sacrificed to Iroko. No archaeological assemblage, court record, named eyewitness, or securely dated ritual manual establishing an institutional Iroko human-sacrifice rite was located. General evidence for human sacrifice in nineteenth-century Yoruba and Dahomean states cannot be transferred automatically to this cult.

### Iroko amid enemies

In Abimbola’s Ifá verse, Iroko is surrounded by hostile trees. Divination prescribes sacrifice. After Iroko complies, Èṣù directs farmers to clear the forest. Every hostile tree falls, but Iroko is spared. The proverb-like conclusion says a forest cannot become so dense that Iroko is unrecognizable. This etiologizes Iroko’s conspicuous survival in cleared landscapes and presents sacrifice as the mechanism that reverses danger.

### The dangerous felling of Iroko

Ellis’s version makes protection relatively simple: invoke one’s own head and apply palm oil. Later folklore makes the consequences graver—madness, death, family calamity, or a spirit continuing to sound within worked timber.

Cabrera records a Cuban localization at the Socorro sugar estate. A company seeks to fell an ancient ceiba revered as Iroko. Local workers refuse. The outsider who accepts is stung in the eye by a wasp, becomes blind and mad, and dies; the company abandons the attempt. This is **twentieth-century Cuban oral testimony**, incorporating plantation memory and resistance to foreign capitalist ownership. It is not an African myth merely transplanted unchanged.

### Descent through the first tree

Modern Brazilian accounts say Iroko was the first tree planted and the route by which the orixás descended from Ọ̀run to Ayé. This is influential contemporary theology but was not located in the nineteenth-century record or the identified Abimbola verse. It should be labeled **late canonical elaboration within some Candomblé communities**, not securely ancient Yoruba material.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

Iroko’s characteristic shrine is not a monumental temple but a **living tree and the ground beneath it**. Its destruction, transplantation, and biological death complicate conventional archaeological recognition. Deposits decay, while cloth, food, palm oil, and animal remains are rarely diagnostic without inscriptions.

Documented offerings include kola nuts, palm oil, cooked food, grain, money, pottery, cloth, fowl, goats, sheep, and other animals. White cloth may encircle the trunk. Prayer and sacrifice precede felling or harvesting. The foot of the tree is a place for petitions, vows, fertility requests, ancestral observance, and deposits prescribed by divination.

No pan-Yoruba centralized Iroko priesthood, annual calendar, or state temple network is documented. Custodianship is local and may belong to priests, lineage elders, herbalists, shrine families, or the owner of the land. The relative absence of portable images and the tree’s own sufficiency as shrine help explain Iroko’s limited representation in museum collections.

In Cuba, the ceiba is approached across Lucumí, Palo, Abakuá, and popular Catholic contexts. Soil, leaves, bark, shade, and the tree’s base may have ritual uses, but rules differ by lineage. Cabrera records birds and other offerings; she also emphasizes that the ceiba is associated with the dead and multiple powers, so “Iroko” does not exhaust its meanings.

In Ketu Candomblé, Iroko is commonly located outdoors in a consecrated tree dressed with an **ojá**. In Jeje houses Loko may be an atimé-vodun. In Angola houses Tempo commonly has a flag-bearing outdoor installation. These spatial similarities encouraged correspondence while preserving distinct initiatory languages.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before the nineteenth century

The linguistic distribution of *iroko/loko/roko* and the mature, locally differentiated practices encountered by early writers strongly imply precolonial antiquity. Exact beginnings cannot be dated. Neither Ile-Ife/Oyo archaeology nor the Ife heads presently furnish a named Iroko attestation.

### 1840s–1890s: conversion-era documentation

Yoruba literacy, missionary lexicography, and travel ethnography brought the name into print. Christian observers classified sacrifice to a tree as “idolatry” or “superstition.” Their reports preserve valuable details while filtering them through conversionist categories.

### 1890s–1930s: ethnographic stabilization

Ellis, Johnson, and Frobenius helped turn localized practices into printed descriptions of “Yoruba religion.” Johnson’s àbíkú association is especially important; later universal portraits of Iroko as “time” should not displace it.

### Atlantic transformation

Enslaved Yoruba/Nagô/Lucumí, Aja-Fon/Jeje, and Central African peoples encountered American species and one another’s ritual systems. *Milicia excelsa* was unavailable in Cuba and initially absent from Brazil, so ceiba and gameleira assumed shrine functions. This is creative religious continuity through substitution, not botanical error.

In Brazil, “nation” formation brought Iroko, Loko, and Tempo into proximity. Scholarship on Candomblé increasingly rejects a purely Yoruba origin model: Jeje vodun institutions were formative from the eighteenth century onward. In Cuba, Cabrera’s evidence shows Iroko-ceiba embedded in a religious field broader than Regla de Ocha alone.

### Suppression and survival

Missionary Christianity and Islam discouraged sacrifice and divine-tree cults, while colonial forestry and commercial logging threatened sacred trees. Nevertheless, trees often survived clearing precisely because cutting them was taboo.

Benin’s Marxist-Leninist government under Mathieu Kérékou restricted Vodun ceremonies during the 1970s. After democratization, traditional religion regained public recognition; a national Vodun holiday has been observed on 10 January since 1996. This general political history affected Loko’s religious environment, though a Loko-specific suppression decree has not been identified.

### Present

Iroko, Loko, and Tempo remain living religious powers in West Africa and the diaspora. Their worship is decentralized and reliable population figures do not exist. Sacred status has also become a practical mechanism of biodiversity conservation: surveys show that religious protection preserves mature iroko and sacred groves amid agricultural and logging pressure.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

Contemporary books, websites, and social media increasingly portray Iroko as a universally male, primordial “Orisha of Time,” lord of karma, oaths, destiny, patience, ecological consciousness, and the Akashic record. Some themes develop genuine ritual associations—longevity, promises, ancestors—but the totalized portfolio is **modern synthesis**.

Lists assigning fixed weekday, number, planet, tarot card, chakra, crystal, zodiacal sign, or universal color are not supported by the early record. “Iroko was present silently at every divine council writing down all decisions” likewise appears in modern Brazilian popular theology rather than documented early Yoruba narrative.

Occult and neopagan writing frequently treats orixás as freely invocable archetypes. That approach is historically distinct from lineage-based Ifá, Orisha, Lucumí, Vodun, and Candomblé initiation. No substantial Renaissance hermetic, Golden Dawn, or classical European grimoire tradition of Iroko exists; claims otherwise are modern invention.

In literature, the Olúrónbí story has been adapted for children, poetry, drama, and horror. These adaptations often remove the promised child’s death, introduce ethical priests, or turn Iroko into a humanoid monster. They document modern reception, not recovered ancient versions.

Argentine Umbanda and Batuque grew through transnational movement from Brazil and Uruguay, as studied by Alejandro Frigerio and María Julia Carozzi. The available scholarship establishes this wider religious circulation but does not demonstrate that Iroko has an independent, large Argentine cult. Silence should not be converted into a claim.

## 9. Historiography and Contested Material

The field’s main methodological problem is the conversion of a **tree–spirit–shrine complex** into a standardized divine person. Nineteenth-century observers sometimes minimized Iroko as a minor indwelling spirit; twentieth-century theologians and diaspora writers elevated Iroko into a primordial cosmic orixá. Both descriptions may represent real communities, but they belong to different historical contexts.

A second dispute concerns **Iroko = Loko = Tempo**. Similar tree symbolism, neighboring vocabulary, migration, and Brazilian ritual dialogue justify comparison. They do not justify claiming that a Yoruba òrìṣà, Fon vodun, and Kongo-Angola inkice were eternally identical. Luis Nicolau Parés’s history of Jeje religion and Candomblé formation makes the distinct institutional histories especially important.

A third concerns **species identification**. Early writers confused *Milicia excelsa*, ceiba/silk-cotton, and other large trees. Diasporic practice can intentionally transfer sacred functions to a new species. Botanical mismatch therefore neither disproves continuity nor permits one to call every ceiba an African iroko.

A fourth concerns **human sacrifice**. The Olúrónbí legend plainly imagines surrender of a child and must not be sanitized. Yet a legend, even a grim one, is not an eyewitness record of customary execution. Modern assertions that Iroko historically received human victims generally recycle the tale or extrapolate from sacrifices elsewhere. The responsible finding is: promised human sacrifice is strongly attested in folklore; actual institutional performance remains unestablished.

A fifth concerns **Ifá as a fixed book**. Bascom and Abimbola demonstrate that verses are numerous, orally transmitted, and selected differently by specialists. Epega/Neimark and internet “Odù encyclopedias” impose greater textual uniformity. Claims about Iroko require a quoted verse, reciter or edition, and collection date—not merely “Ifá says.”

Finally, neither Ile-Ife archaeology nor famous Ife naturalistic heads provide direct evidence for Iroko. Invoking them as proof of an ancient Iroko cult is an evidentiary category error.

## 10. Chronology

- **Pre-nineteenth century:** Iroko/Loko vocabulary and cult practice almost certainly exist in the Bight of Benin, but no securely dated inscription or manuscript is known.
- **1843:** First edition of Crowther’s Yoruba vocabulary; early written lexical attestation.
- **1852:** Expanded Crowther/Vidal vocabulary published in London.
- **Mid-nineteenth century:** Anglican missionary report states that Yoruba worship Iroko and sacrifice fowls before felling it.
- **1894:** Ellis publishes a description of Iroko’s indwelling spirit and protective felling rite.
- **1897:** Samuel Johnson completes *The History of the Yorubas*.
- **1921:** Johnson’s work appears posthumously, associating large iroko trees with àbíkú companies.
- **1946:** French ethnobotanical reporting records offerings and wish-sacrifices at iroko elsewhere in West Africa, demonstrating a broader regional sacred-tree complex.
- **1954:** Cabrera publishes *El Monte*, the foundational extended Cuban record of ceiba/Iroko traditions.
- **1958:** Abraham’s Yoruba dictionary records supernatural inhabitation, white cloth, and sacrifice.
- **1969:** Bascom publishes *Ifa Divination*.
- **1976:** Abimbola publishes *An Exposition of Ifá Literary Corpus*, including the verse of Ìrókò-Ìgbò and Èṣù.
- **1970s:** Kérékou’s government restricts Vodun public practice in Benin.
- **1980:** Bascom publishes *Sixteen Cowries*.
- **1980s:** Verger’s writings consolidate comparative treatment of Yoruba orixás and Fon voduns in Africa and Bahia while acknowledging gaps concerning Loko.
- **1990:** Benin’s National Conference inaugurates political liberalization and renewed public recognition of traditional religion.
- **1996:** 10 January becomes Benin’s national Vodun holiday.
- **1990s–present:** Prandi and other scholars publish Brazilian myth collections; Iroko’s “time” theology becomes highly visible in popular Candomblé literature.
- **2000s–present:** Ethnobotanical studies document continuing sacrifice, sacred-tree protection, local variation, and conservation effects in Benin and Nigeria.
- **2020s:** Online transnational religion increasingly standardizes Iroko as a cosmic god of time, karma, and oaths; much of this expanded portfolio remains modern systematization.
