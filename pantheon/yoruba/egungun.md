# The Egungun: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Primarily a **collective of ancestral spirits and their ritually embodied masked manifestations**, together with the cult institutions and masquerade genres through which those ancestors appear. Egúngún is not consistently classified as one god or òrìṣà. Particular traditions may personify Egúngún, call individual manifestations *bàbá* (“father/ancestor”), or place the collective in genealogical relations with Ọya and Ṣàngó; Brazilian and modern transnational systems sometimes speak of “Egungun” more like a named divine being.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** No precolonial inscription, manuscript, or securely identified archaeological image names Egúngún. The earliest presently verifiable substantial written description is A. B. Ellis’s *The Yoruba-Speaking Peoples of the Slave Coast of West Africa* (1894), based on late-nineteenth-century coastal Yorubaland observation and testimony. Samuel Johnson recorded Ọ̀yọ́ traditions in a manuscript completed before his death in 1901, but *The History of the Yorubas* appeared posthumously in 1921. Claims assigning the institution to Ṣàngó’s reign or Aláàfin Ofinran, approximately the sixteenth century, are later-recorded oral chronologies, not contemporary attestations.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

**[Secure ethnographic attestation, late nineteenth century onward]** Egúngún designates both the returning dead and the completely concealed figure through whom the dead become publicly present. Ellis described an annual “All-Souls” observance in which disguised men represented departed relatives, while also recording the operative belief that the apparition was the dead person rather than merely an actor. Johnson similarly called the festival an invocation and worship of the dead in which masked persons “personate” departed relatives. Later Yoruba scholarship more carefully describes an ontological transformation: once properly clothed, empowered, voiced, and performed, the *elégúngún* or masker is not publicly treated as an ordinary man pretending to be a spirit.

Egúngún may denote:

1. the collective ancestral dead;
2. an individual returned ancestor;
3. the complete masked and costumed manifestation;
4. the lineage or cult society maintaining such manifestations;
5. the wider masquerade institution;
6. loosely, especially outside specialist usage, almost any Yoruba masquerade.

That last extension is historically misleading. Ọ̀yọ́ specialists and Oludare Ọlajubu and J. R. O. Ojo restrict Egúngún properly so called to the Ọ̀yọ́ ancestral masquerade complex, which later spread alongside—but did not erase—Gẹ̀lẹ̀dé, Agẹmọ, Ẹpa, and other regional forms.

### Names and forms

- **Egúngún** — standard modern Yoruba spelling. Tone marks distinguish it from superficially similar forms.
- **Eégún / Eegun** — common contracted form; used for an ancestor or the ancestral dead.
- **Egún / Egun** — widespread orthographic form in Cuba and Brazil. In Lucumí usage, *egún* usually means one’s dead or ancestors and does not necessarily imply a masked Egúngún society.
- **Égum / Egum / Egungum** — Brazilian Portuguese spellings.
- **Bàbá Egúngún / Babá Egun** — “Father Egúngún,” an honorific for senior or individually named manifestations, especially prominent in Bahia.
- **Ará Ọ̀run** — “inhabitant/person of heaven,” a frequent descriptive title for the returning dead.
- **Egúngún àgbà** — senior or ancient ancestor manifestation.
- **Elégúngún** — the person bearing or associated with an Egúngún manifestation; analytical translations such as “masker” must not be mistaken for the ritual identity publicly asserted.
- **Alápìnnì / Alápinni** — not a name for Egúngún but the senior Ọ̀yọ́ cult office.
- **Ojé** — initiated male cult member or priest; in Bahia, *ojé* is a principal sacerdotal title.
- **Ìgbàlẹ̀ / igbale** — the sacred grove, room, or enclosure associated with the ancestors; in Brazilian usage it can denote the consecrated area of the dead.
- **Ẹ̀kú / eku** — a term for the ritual covering or ensemble in Nigerian descriptions.
- **Òpá / opá** — in Itaparica usage, the liturgical vestment or material manifestation of an ancestral *bàbá*.
- **Ìṣán / ixan** — the staff carried by attendants in Bahia to direct the manifestation and prevent dangerous contact.

### Etymology

**[Disputed]** An older proposal, found in early religious literature, connects *Egúngún/Eégún* with Yoruba *egungun*, “bone” or “skeleton,” by interpreting the ancestor as the remains or essence of the dead. This is attractive semantically but tone and vowel distinctions make unmarked colonial spellings unreliable linguistic evidence.

**[Learned/priestly etymology]** A modern explanation associated with an Ifá verse in Ọ̀wọ́nrínṣẹ́ parses the word through *gún* (“arrive/land” and “set straight”), producing “those who arrive to put the world in order.” This is best treated as an exegetical or folk etymology, not an established historical derivation.

**[Unestablished]** Claims that the word literally means “powers concealed” occur in modern performance and theological writing. That is a meaningful interpretation of ritual concealment, but it has not been demonstrated as the word’s historical linguistic derivation.

### Classification across traditions

- **Ọ̀yọ́-Yoruba:** returning lineage and civic ancestors, ritually manifested; an ancestral institution rather than ordinarily one òrìṣà.
- **Other Yoruba regions:** an imported or localized ancestral-mask complex existing beside indigenous masquerade systems.
- **Bahian Candomblé-Egúngún:** distinguished ancestral *bàbá*, often eminent male founders and priests, materialized beneath consecrated garments and served by an organized cult.
- **Cuban Lucumí:** *egún* ordinarily means the dead/ancestors. The full West African hereditary masquerade institution did not cross intact in most historical Lucumí houses. Recent all-male Egúngún societies in Havana, Baracoa, and elsewhere are documented transatlantic reconstructions or innovations, not proof of uninterrupted Cuban transmission.
- **Umbanda and Batuque:** the dead, spirits, or ancestors are important, but *egum* may carry more negative connotations—an unquiet or ritually dangerous dead spirit—than in an Ọ̀yọ́ festival context. It should not be equated automatically with Umbanda *pretos velhos*, *caboclos*, or every possessing spirit.

No sound evidence identifies Egúngún with a Greek, Roman, Christian, or Islamic being. Comparisons with All Souls’ observances, ghosts, saints, or angels are analogies or syncretic interpretations, not historical equivalences.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The evidentiary problem

Yoruba religious knowledge was transmitted primarily through oral poetry, ritual instruction, divination verses, performance, textiles, and lineage custody. The survival of an institution before European description is therefore probable but cannot be dated simply by assigning an oral story to the reign in which it is set.

No known Ilé-Ifẹ̀ terracotta, copper-alloy head, pavement, or Old Ọ̀yọ́ excavation bears a readable label identifying it as Egúngún. The famous Ifẹ̀ heads and the general antiquity of Yoruba figural art establish neither the age nor the exact form of the Egúngún institution. Assigning an unprovenanced ancient mask or Ifẹ̀ head to Egúngún solely because later Egúngún may carry carved headpieces is retrospective speculation.

### Documentary sequence

#### A. B. Ellis, 1894

**[Secure early written attestation]** Ellis devotes a chapter to “Egungun, Oro, Abiku, and Various Superstitions.” He records:

- an annual commemoration of the dead;
- masked men representing deceased relatives;
- altered or artificial voices;
- visits to compounds;
- offerings and requests for protection;
- the belief that touching Egúngún could cause death;
- the social use of Egúngún to expose or discipline misconduct.

Ellis wrote as a British colonial officer using evolutionary categories now rejected. His statements are valuable as dated testimony, not as transparent access to timeless Yoruba religion.

#### Samuel Johnson, manuscript before 1901; publication 1921

**[Early Yoruba-authored historical synthesis]** Johnson describes belief in continuing existence after death, invocation of spirits during Egúngún festivals, vigils in the grove, prayer to deceased parents, and the cult hierarchy. He preserves an influential Ọ̀yọ́ historical tradition: Egúngún was introduced from the Nupe or Tápà country during Aláàfin Ofinran’s era by immigrants or cult officials, among them the first Alápìnnì and subordinate priests.

Because Johnson reconstructed early dynastic history from oral accounts centuries later, his narrative documents a nineteenth-century Ọ̀yọ́ memory of origins, not a securely dated sixteenth-century event.

#### Leo Frobenius, 1910–1912 fieldwork and later publications

**[Early-twentieth-century colonial ethnography]** Frobenius collected Yoruba art and traditions, but his diffusionist reconstructions—especially fantasies of Mediterranean or “Atlantis” origins—are not reliable explanations of Egúngún. Objects he and contemporaries removed are evidence of early-twentieth-century collecting and ritual arts, not proof of his civilizational theories.

#### Ifá and cowry-divination corpora

**[Oral corpora recorded mainly in the twentieth century]** Relevant verses occur in several Odù, including traditions associated with Ọ̀wọ́nrínṣẹ́, Ọ̀bàrà Ọ̀sẹ́, and Ìwòrì-related configurations. S. O. Babayemi reports several origin accounts and stresses that different Odù explain different conventions rather than one universally canonical beginning.

William Bascom’s *Ifa Divination* (1969) and *Sixteen Cowries* (1980), Wande Abimbola’s editions and studies, and later compilations by Epega and Neimark preserve major textual bodies. However:

- the Ifá corpus has no single ancient manuscript recension;
- verses vary by diviner, town, lineage, and occasion;
- the date a verse was recorded is not the date it originated;
- Epega and Neimark’s English compilation is a modern selection and arrangement, not an ancient Yoruba “Bible”;
- the absence of Egúngún from a particular published collection does not establish its absence from living Ifá traditions.

#### Oríkì, ìwì, songs, and oral histories

**[Continuously variable oral primary evidence]** Egúngún appearances are accompanied by:

- *oríkì*—praise names and lineage histories;
- *ìwì*—masquerade poetry or declamation;
- prayers and invocations;
- drum speech;
- satirical songs;
- genealogical recitations.

These texts identify a manifestation, recall ancestors, praise patrons, disclose wrongdoing, and negotiate local history. Their wording is performance-dependent. Undated internet versions titled “Oríkì Egúngún” should not be treated as fixed ancient scripture unless their performer, locality, collection date, and publication history are known.

### Material corpus

The securely catalogued museum corpus is overwhelmingly nineteenth- or twentieth-century. Examples include:

- a British Museum Abeokuta-style cap mask, acquired through the Wellcome collection and associated with miniature *ọ̀ṣé Ṣàngó* motifs;
- a British Museum mask attributed to the Adugbologe workshop, whose founder died shortly before 1914;
- masks collected around Iyin/Ado-Ekiti in 1959;
- a carved Ọ̀yọ́ Egúngún-society headdress acquired in 1964;
- twentieth-century multilayered cloth ensembles in Cleveland, North Carolina, and the Smithsonian.

These demonstrate regional variation. A full cloth tower with flying lappets is not the only valid type, nor is every wooden Yoruba mask Egúngún.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and functions

**[Secure ethnographic attestation]** Egúngún’s principal domain is reciprocal traffic between *ayé*, the visible world, and *ọ̀run*, the realm of the dead and divine powers. Manifestations:

- renew relations between living descendants and ancestors;
- bless households, children, fertility, health, and prosperity;
- receive food, drink, cloth, praise, and animal offerings;
- validate genealogies and inherited authority;
- commemorate founders and eminent dead;
- chastise misconduct;
- expose theft, adultery, debt, insult, or neglect through public song and satire;
- cleanse a town and avert danger;
- provide entertainment, virtuoso dance, and competitive display;
- display a family’s wealth through imported and locally woven cloth;
- participate in royal and civic politics.

The ancestors are therefore protective and dangerous. Their power is not reducible to benevolent “family memory.”

### The ensemble

A typical cloth Egúngún ensemble completely hides the bearer’s identity. Components vary, but may include:

- a close inner layer protecting and concealing the bearer;
- a netted or screened face;
- multiple textile panels or lappets;
- embroidered emblems;
- cowries, beads, sequins, mirrors, bells, leather packets, and medicines;
- imported velvet, damask, brocade, printed cotton, and locally dyed or woven cloth;
- a carved cap mask or superstructure in some regions;
- animal, human, occupational, Islamic, Christian, royal, or modern commercial imagery.

**[Secure modern object evidence]** Technical study of Smithsonian ensembles shows that costumes are cumulative objects. Families add, replace, and recycle panels over many years. A costume therefore has no necessary single creation date: individual fabrics can predate or postdate the basic construction.

### Motion, voice, and presence

Whirling sends the panels outward, making the hidden body appear enlarged and generating wind. Some communities construe this as a blessing; spectators may seek contact with the flying cloth. Elsewhere direct contact is dangerous and controlled.

The voice is altered—rasping, high, guttural, nasal, or otherwise nonordinary. Drums may converse with, energize, identify, or praise the manifestation. The combined concealment, transformed voice, medicines, music, and authorized performance constitutes ancestral presence; the costume alone in a museum is an incomplete remnant.

### Contact taboo: the evidence separated

- **Ellis, 1894 [secure early report]:** touching Egúngún, even accidentally, was popularly believed to cause death.
- **Later encyclopedic claim [poorly localized]:** both the wearer and touched person were formerly killed. This requires caution; it is often repeated without a checkable court record or locality.
- **Ọ̀yọ́ and other Yoruba performances [regional variation]:** flying panels can deliberately brush spectators as blessing.
- **Bahia [secure ethnographic attestation]:** *ojés* use the *ixan* staff to keep the living away from the dangerous ancestral garment and direct the *bàbá*.
- **Practical safety:** attendants also clear space because a fully covered, rapidly spinning performer has restricted vision.

Thus “the costume must never touch the living” is not a universal rule.

### Colors, numbers, animals, days

There is no universal Egúngún color, animal, weekday, celestial body, or number comparable to modern standardized òrìṣà charts.

- Multicolor and textile abundance are characteristic of many cloth ensembles.
- Red, *bàtá* drums, and Ṣàngó emblems appear especially in Ọ̀yọ́-related forms, but do not make all Egúngún manifestations Ṣàngó.
- Nine becomes prominent through Ọya/Iansã traditions and especially Brazilian and Cuban systematization. It is not demonstrably the universal ancient number of every Egúngún.
- Individual manifestations can have specific colors, emblems, foods, taboos, and praise names determined by lineage.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

Because Egúngún is normally a class of dead ancestors, a single divine genealogy is structurally inappropriate. Every lineage ancestor has an ordinary descent relation, while the ancestral collective is continually replenished by human death and ritual transformation.

Three competing origin models must be kept distinct:

1. **Ancestral model [secure ethnographic core]:** deceased lineage members become ancestors and may return as Egúngún.
2. **Ọya maternity model [regional mythic systematization]:** Ọya bears nine children; the last, Egúngún, has a strange voice or concealed/disfigured face and becomes the ancestor-founder.
3. **Ṣàngó institutional model [Ọ̀yọ́/Brazilian tradition]:** Ṣàngó founds, authorizes, controls, or royalizes the Egúngún institution.
4. **Nupe-historical model [later-recorded Ọ̀yọ́ oral history]:** cult officials or practices enter Ọ̀yọ́ from Tápà/Nupe country.

They answer different questions—cosmic parentage, ritual authorization, political incorporation, or historical diffusion—and should not be combined as one biography.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. Ọya’s nine children and the masked last-born

**[Regional myth; twentieth-century recording]** Ọya is barren and consults divination. In Babayemi’s Ọ̀yọ́ version, she uses the queen ant or an ant-associated fertility preparation and must deliver within an anthill. Eight children are sound; soldier ants disfigure the ninth child’s face. He covers it with a mask, and his abnormal speech makes observers recognize an otherworldly being. The child becomes associated with Egúngún.

Prandi’s Brazilian compilation gives a harsher variant: a diviner tells barren Ọya the conditions under which she will conceive; Ṣàngó fathers nine children; eight are mute, while the ninth speaks in a strange, deep, cavernous voice. This child is Egungum, ancestor-founder of families.

These are not one stable myth. One explains the mask through facial disfigurement; another explains the voice and the name Iansã as mother of nine. The Brazilian version reflects Candomblé transmission and Prandi’s editorial retelling of oral testimony.

### B. Women once possessed the masquerade secret

**[Widespread but variably recorded mythic motif]** Several Yoruba interpretations say women first possessed Egúngún or discovered the ritual power, after which men seized or institutionalized it. The motif explains the simultaneous centrality and exclusion of women: women contribute cloth, songs, food, genealogy, mourning, and audience response, but men normally wear the principal ancestor costume and control restricted ritual knowledge.

Because versions differ on which woman, which men, and which supernatural power transferred authority, “women originally owned Egúngún” should be cited to a local verse or informant rather than proclaimed as a single canonical event. Modern gender scholarship also reads the myth politically: exclusion is reproduced through institutions, even where ideology remembers female precedence.

### C. The dead children restored and veiled

**[Recorded folklore/Ifá-associated origin narrative]** A father’s children disobey him by eating a prohibited yam. They become thirsty, drink from a stream, and die. Ifá restores them after seven days, but their returned faces are too terrible to expose. They enter town veiled in beautiful costumes and become Egúngún.

This story provides an etiology for concealment and return from death. Abdul-Rasheed Na’allah cites it while reviewing the instability of origin narratives. Its first composition cannot be dated.

### D. The Tápà/Nupe origin

**[Later-recorded historical tradition; disputed reconstruction]** Johnson reports that Egúngún entered Ọ̀yọ́ from Nupe/Tápà territory during Aláàfin Ofinran’s reign, accompanied by the Alápìnnì and other priests. Later scholars propose:

- Nupe invention and Yoruba borrowing;
- Yoruba origin followed by adoption or “Nupe-ization”;
- a shared frontier complex created through long Ọ̀yọ́-Nupe warfare, migration, marriage, and ritual exchange;
- Old Ọ̀yọ́ codification of a broader ancestor-masking substrate.

Na’allah supports a Nupe source partly through Dadakuada poetry and Muslim Nupe memories. Kalilu’s costume comparison narrows the recognizable form to Old Ọ̀yọ́ and its Nupe frontier. Critics note that oral claims are reciprocal and late, and no sixteenth-century document settles direction of transmission. The most defensible conclusion is that the historically recognizable Egúngún complex was strongly shaped on the Old Ọ̀yọ́–Nupe frontier; exclusive invention by either modern ethnic category remains unproved.

### E. Ṣàngó’s institution of Egúngún

**[Ọ̀yọ́/Brazilian priestly narrative]** Ṣàngó, the deified Ọ̀yọ́ ruler, is said to found, command, or domesticate the ancestral masks. The relationship is expressed through royal incorporation, red color, *bàtá* drums, and *ọ̀ṣé Ṣàngó* emblems. It may encode the monarchy’s appropriation of powerful lineage or frontier associations. It does not prove that ancestor veneration began under a historical Ṣàngó.

### F. Named ancestor manifestations

**[Local cult history]** Egúngún are frequently individual rather than mythically generic. A 2018 Ọ̀yọ́ ethnography records Mowuru, a renowned Old Ọ̀yọ́ warrior whose power was invoked after Ifá consultation to help an Aláàfin overcome political and spiritual enemies. Mowuru was not claimed as the biological ancestor of the compound housing him. This demonstrates that Egúngún can embody civic or royal power beyond straightforward patrilineal descent.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Social organization

The basic unit is often an *ìdílé* or compound. Senior men control restricted knowledge and the costume, but women’s labor and authority remain indispensable in textile donation, praise, genealogy, food preparation, mourning, singing, and ritual offices such as *Ìyá Àgan* or locally comparable titles.

The Alápìnnì stands at the summit of the royal Ọ̀yọ́ hierarchy in Johnson’s account. Other recorded offices include Elefi, Olohan, Oloba, Aladafa, and Oloje, though functions and spellings vary. In Bahia, offices include Alágbà/Alagbá, Alápini, and Ojé, with named female dignitaries and supporters; the exact hierarchy is terreiro-specific.

### Festival sequence

There is no universal calendar, although many southwestern Nigerian festivals occur between May and July. A typical sequence may include:

1. divination and selection of dates;
2. repair and addition of cloth;
3. offerings in the grove or ancestral room;
4. an all-night vigil, prayer, kneeling, and invocation;
5. emergence of senior manifestations;
6. compound visits;
7. procession to palace, market, or civic center;
8. praise singing, drumming, satirical speech, and acrobatic whirling;
9. blessing, adjudication, collection of gifts, and public competition;
10. return to the sacred enclosure.

Johnson calls the preceding vigil *ìkúnlẹ̀*, “kneeling,” during which worshippers petition departed parents.

### Offerings

Documented offerings include kola nut, water, palm oil, prepared foods, alcohol in some communities, fowl, goats, and other animals. Cloth itself is a major votive substance: each layer records a descendant’s expenditure and the ancestor’s accumulated social body.

**Human sacrifice:** General precolonial Yoruba royal and funerary contexts did include documented or alleged human sacrifice, and colonial authors often sensationalized the subject. The sources examined here do not establish routine human sacrifice as a defining Egúngún offering. Claims that touching or exposing the masker formerly incurred death concern sanctions and belief, not sacrificial liturgy. A dossier should neither deny broader Yoruba sacrificial history nor transfer every reported royal killing to Egúngún without evidence.

### Policing and adjudication

**[Secure nineteenth- and twentieth-century evidence]** Egúngún could preserve order in private and lineage life by publicly naming offenses, shaming wrongdoers, demanding payment, or dramatizing antisocial conduct. The apparent ancestor could say what an ordinary dependent could not safely say.

This policing function had two faces:

- moral enforcement and dispute resolution;
- coercion by powerful cult families, harassment, whipping, extortion, political intimidation, and settlement of private scores.

Colonial Ibadan records show permits, specified routes, police patrols, bans on whips, arrests of masked performers, and prosecutions. Alápansánpá’s followers became notorious for conflict, assaults on police, and political factionalism. Arresting a supposed ancestor in an ordinary court visibly weakened the institution’s inviolability.

Modern government restrictions continue. Lagos police have confined processions to designated spaces, while Ọ̀yọ́ authorities warn against roadblocks, weapons, thuggery, vendetta, and interference with other residents. These measures document living contestation, not disappearance.

### Gender and danger

Women commonly sing and may hold important cult-related offices, but principal masking is predominantly male. Claims that all female participation is forbidden are false; claims that gender exclusion is insignificant are equally false. Rules vary concerning entry into the grove, menstruation, pregnancy, knowledge of the wearer, and proximity to manifestations.

The concealment secret is enforced socially. Reports that revealing the human bearer was once punishable by death occur in early and later accounts, but precise frequency and legal procedure cannot be established.

### Cult buildings and archaeology

Egúngún worship is centered less on monumental temples than on groves, compound rooms, palace precincts, costume houses, and processional routes. Textiles decay and are ritually renewed; sacred enclosures are rebuilt. This helps explain the limited ancient archaeological signature.

Old Ọ̀yọ́ archaeology establishes an imperial capital with elite compounds, industries, walls, trade, horses, and long-distance connections, but does not yet give an independently datable “first Egúngún shrine.” Ilé-Ifẹ̀ archaeology similarly establishes deep artistic and urban histories without naming Egúngún.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before the nineteenth century

**[Reconstruction]** Ancestor veneration is certainly older than its first written description, but its age cannot be calculated from the age of Yoruba civilization as a whole. Oral traditions place institutional formation:

- in Ṣàngó’s era;
- under Aláàfin Ofinran, conventionally around 1500–1550;
- on the Nupe frontier;
- in primordial time when the dead first returned.

These are historical memories and charter myths, not contemporary records.

### Old Ọ̀yọ́ imperial period

**[Probable historical development]** The strongest reconstruction locates codification in Old Ọ̀yọ́, where lineage ancestor ritual, royal authority, Nupe contact, cavalry warfare, and mobile populations intersected. Egúngún followed Ọ̀yọ́ military and migrant networks and became incorporated into royal ceremony.

### Nineteenth-century dispersal

The collapse of Old Ọ̀yọ́ and the Yoruba wars displaced Ọ̀yọ́ populations southward. Egúngún spread or intensified in Ibadan, Ogbomoso, Igbomina, Egba, Awori, and other communities. Local peoples adopted, resisted, or combined it with their own masquerades.

This is also the period of massive forced migration to the Americas. People categorized as Nagô or Lucumí carried ancestor concepts, songs, offices, and ritual knowledge to Brazil and Cuba.

### Christian and Muslim encounters

CMS missionaries from the 1840s onward condemned ancestral masquerade as “heathen,” deception, or spirit worship. Yoruba converts also translated, debated, and reclassified older practices. Islam had a longer presence; Muslim participation and opposition varied by town and family. Na’allah records that Islam contributed to the decline of comparable Nupe masking traditions.

Suppression was not a single empire-wide ban. Conversion, schooling, ridicule, municipal regulation, prosecutions, and intra-family conflict gradually altered the institution, while many Christians and Muslims continued to sponsor or attend festivals as family or civic obligations.

### Colonial period

Colonial administrators alternated between indirect recognition and public-order regulation. Festivals were useful displays of “custom,” but officials restricted routes, whips, night movement, and political mobilization. Newspapers and party competition magnified festival violence in colonial Ibadan.

### Postcolonial Nigeria and Benin

Egúngún remains active as religion, heritage, entertainment, tourism, and local politics. Costumes increasingly incorporate synthetic fabric, photographs, corporate logos, national colors, and current events. Such additions continue the historic cumulative logic of the ensemble rather than necessarily representing secularization.

The complex also spread or survives among Yoruba-related communities in the Republic of Benin and Togo. Fon *Kulito* and other ancestor masquerades may have interacted with Yoruba forms, but simple one-way derivation should not be asserted without local evidence.

### Bahia and Itaparica

**[Secure diaspora history with disputed early dates]** Juana Elbein dos Santos reported traditions assigning early Itaparica houses to Vera Cruz (1820), Mocambo (1830), Encarnação (1840), and Tuntun (1850). These dates derive substantially from terreiro memory and retrospective genealogy. The first short published reference identified by dos Santos is Nina Rodrigues’s notice concerning 1896.

The Tuntun/Olukotun community and the lineage that later maintained Omo Ilê Agboulá preserve a tradition extending into the nineteenth century. Omo Ilê Agboulá itself was organized in its recognizable form in the first quarter of the twentieth century by figures including Eduardo Daniel de Paula, Tio Opê, Tio Serafim, and Tio Marcos. It became one of Brazil’s most prominent houses dedicated specifically to Egúngún.

Bahian practice differs from most Nigerian forms:

- individually named eminent male ancestors or *bàbás* predominate;
- the sacred garment is managed inside a walled terreiro;
- contact is mediated by *ojés* with *ixan* staffs;
- ritual secrecy is intense;
- possession by an òrìṣà and manifestation of a *bàbá* are institutionally distinguished.

Mestre Didi—Deoscóredes Maximiliano dos Santos—was initiated in 1925 at Tuntun by Marcos Theodoro Pimentel, later became a major priest and artist, and translated Egúngún-related forms into modern sculpture. His art is reception and ritual philosophy, not merely museum imitation.

Omo Ilê Agboulá received Brazilian federal heritage recognition in 2015. In 2026 Bahia’s government consulted at least seventeen Itaparica Egúngún terreiros, evidence of continuing expansion and public recognition.

### Cuba

The transatlantic history is different. Lucumí religion retained *egún* devotion—feeding, praying to, and divining with one’s dead—but most scholarship concludes that the hereditary masked Egúngún society did not survive intact. Dispersal of lineages under slavery helps explain the loss.

Late-twentieth- and twenty-first-century Nigerian contact enabled reconstructed Egúngún organizations. Ruthie Meadows documents localized all-male masquerade practice in Baracoa and Havana. These are authentic contemporary religious formations, but their historical claim is reconnection or re-Africanization rather than unbroken colonial Cuban continuity.

### Argentina

Afro-Brazilian religions entered Argentina substantially from the 1960s. Frigerio and Carozzi document their stigmatization, adaptation, and public search for religious citizenship. More recent practitioners have pursued “African roots” through Nigerian and transnational initiation. An Egúngún society operating in Caseros, Buenos Aires Province, held documented festivals in the early 2010s. This is a modern transnational establishment, not an ancient Argentine survival.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Ethnography, photography, and museums

Pierre Verger’s mid-twentieth-century photographs helped establish the global image of Egúngún as spectacular layered cloth in motion. Robert Farris Thompson’s *Flash of the Spirit* placed Yoruba aesthetic continuities within an Atlantic framework. Pemberton, the Drewals, Lawal, Abiodun, Apter, Matory, and others shifted discussion from static “masks” to performance, agency, cloth, voice, political history, and transatlantic circulation.

Museums often display empty ensembles vertically and label them “costumes.” This preserves material evidence but removes motion, sound, medicines, lineage ownership, and the living body. Recent conservation research explicitly treats these as composite, changing objects.

### Literature and popular visual culture

Egúngún imagery has entered Nigerian theater, festival photography, fashion, music videos, contemporary art, and global design. The most influential modern transformation is aesthetic extraction: the layered silhouette is used while its lineage and ritual restrictions are omitted.

Mestre Didi’s sculptures form a different case. They are modern artworks made by an initiated priest and consciously articulate Nagô ancestral forms. They should be dated as twentieth- and twenty-first-century artistic theology, not mislabeled as anonymous ancient artifacts.

### Neotraditionalism and online religion

Modern practitioners circulate standardized charts, English-language prayers, purchasable “ancestor shrines,” and claims that anyone can independently “work with Egungun.” Some derive from legitimate diaspora adaptation; others detach the institution from lineage, initiation, and locality.

Particularly doubtful modern claims include:

- Egúngún as one universal òrìṣà with a single personality;
- fixed universal colors, crystals, zodiac signs, or planetary rulership;
- “Egungun-Oya” as an ancient pan-Yoruba òrìṣà of divination;
- every ghost, spirit guide, or elevated dead person being an Egúngún;
- a universally applicable solitary initiation reconstructed from books;
- claims of an unchanged “Egyptian,” Atlantean, Ugaritic, or prehistoric origin.

No significant historical role for Egúngún is documented in Renaissance Hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, European ceremonial magic, or classical grimoires. Its presence in modern occult and neopagan practice is recent borrowing.

### Politics, heritage, and conflict

Egúngún can serve:

- Yoruba cultural nationalism;
- royal legitimacy;
- Black Atlantic ancestral identity;
- anti-racist heritage campaigns;
- municipal tourism;
- local party mobilization.

Political use is not modern in origin, but nationalist language about one timeless pan-Yoruba Egúngún system often smooths over the institution’s specifically Ọ̀yọ́ history and local variation.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Origin dispute

The principal positions are:

- **Johnson:** introduction from Nupe/Tápà during Ofinran’s reign.
- **Nadel and Olajubu:** some apparent Nupe forms may instead derive from Yoruba populations or reciprocal frontier movement.
- **Na’allah:** linguistic and performance evidence, especially Dadakuada, favors Nupe origin.
- **Kalilu:** costume history points to Old Ọ̀yọ́ and its Nupe-adjacent zone.
- **Willis and recent scholarship:** the issue is less an isolated invention than political formation through mobility, kinship, gender, and imperial expansion.
- **Willson:** contemporary ritual codification can plausibly be traced to Ọ̀yọ́, but colonial documents cannot settle primordial origins.

No consensus justifies “invented by the Nupe” or “invented by the Yoruba” as established fact.

### Is Egúngún a mask, ancestor, or performance?

Early writers often described a disguised man falsely “personating” the dead. Later functionalist anthropology called it ancestor worship. Performance and art-history scholarship instead emphasizes transformation: cloth, medicine, voice, choreography, spectators, and ritual authority jointly bring ancestral presence into being. These descriptions reflect different analytical frameworks as well as informants’ varying willingness to disclose restricted knowledge.

### Is it exclusively male?

Male masking and male control of restricted spaces are strongly documented. Yet a simple “men’s cult” model hides women’s material, vocal, genealogical, and sacerdotal labor and the myths of prior female ownership. Current scholarship therefore distinguishes who wears, who authorizes, who manufactures, who praises, who inherits, and who may enter each space.

### Ifá as “scripture”

Bascom, Abimbola, Epega, Neimark, and others made oral verses available as books. Publication encouraged readers to treat a collected verse as canonical scripture. Specialists caution that an Odù is a generative archive performed through a diviner, not a fixed chapter with one authoritative wording. A modern publication date is secure; the antiquity of its individual verse usually is not.

### The archaeology problem

Neither the antiquity of Ilé-Ifẹ̀ art nor the age of Old Ọ̀yọ́ proves a matching date for surviving Egúngún forms. Claims that an Ifẹ̀ head “is Egungun” require contextual evidence. The earliest securely catalogued Egúngún objects are much later than Ifẹ̀’s classical corpus.

### Diaspora purity

Older scholarship sometimes ranked Brazil as a purer African survival and Cuba or Umbanda as more syncretic. Contemporary work instead examines how all traditions select, rebuild, and authorize. Itaparica is historically exceptional, but its present system is itself a diaspora formation. Cuban reconstructed masquerade is not “fake” because recent; it is recent and must be described as such.

### Human sacrifice and sensationalism

Missionary and colonial accounts often collapsed sacrifice, criminal punishment, warfare, and ritual killing into an image of African barbarity. Conversely, apologetic writing sometimes denies every uncomfortable practice. For Egúngún specifically, animal offerings and violent sanctions are documented; routine cultic human sacrifice is not established by the evidence surveyed. Broader Yoruba royal/funerary sacrifice must be studied separately.
