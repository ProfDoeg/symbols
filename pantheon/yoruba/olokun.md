# Olokun: Research Dossier

**Class:** Òrìṣà/divinity or powerful water spirit. In Ilé-Ifẹ̀ and several Yoruba traditions Olókun is commonly female; in Edo/Benin religion Olókun is predominantly male; Cuban Lucumí houses preserve conflicting male, female, and indeterminate formulations. These are regional traditions, not a single recoverable “original gender.”

**Earliest secure attestation:** No securely dated premodern inscription names Olókun. The earliest datable material connected with the name is the **Igbo-Olokun/Olokun Grove archaeological landscape at Ilé-Ifẹ̀**, where indigenous glassmaking flourished approximately the eleventh–fifteenth centuries CE. Its modern place-name and continuing sacred associations securely connect the site with Olókun, but archaeology has not recovered an inscription identifying the deity or proving that the site already bore that name in the medieval period. The earliest unambiguous written descriptions are nineteenth-century missionary and ethnographic records, especially A. B. Ellis’s *The Yoruba-Speaking Peoples* (1894), followed by Leo Frobenius’s observations at Ifẹ̀ in 1910–11.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EARLY MATERIAL]** securely dated archaeological or documentary evidence.
- **[RECORDED ORAL TRADITION]** an oral account documented at a stated date; its narrative setting may be much earlier.
- **[LATER CANONICAL]** an influential twentieth-century codification or widely accepted ritual formulation.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** a geographically or lineage-specific form.
- **[SYNCRETISTIC]** a historically produced identification or fusion.
- **[FOLKLORE]** a circulating narrative without secure evidence for antiquity.
- **[SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** a modern inference from linguistic, archaeological, historical, or comparative evidence.
- **[MODERN INVENTION/RETELLING]** a demonstrably recent formulation or one lacking evidence for the antiquity sometimes claimed for it.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

Olókun is the “owner,” “possessor,” or personification of **òkun**, the sea or immense waters. The figure governs the inaccessible depth, the wealth concealed beneath water, prosperity, beads, coral, royal magnificence, fertility, healing, and—because the sea both enriches and destroys—flood, drowning, and uncontrollable power. In Edo royal theology Olókun is the aquatic counterpart of the Ọba: the unseen ruler of a wealthy submarine kingdom from whom coral, authority, and prosperity proceed. In Ifẹ̀ traditions Olókun is often a wealthy female divinity, sometimes a primordial being and sometimes remembered as an ancient woman, queen, trader, or wife of Odùduwà. In Cuba, Olokun generally denotes the abyssal ocean, distinguished from Yemayá’s nearer, visible waters.

### Names and forms

- **Yoruba:** **Olókun**, conventionally analyzed as *oní òkun*—“owner/possessor of the sea.” Yoruba phonological contraction changes *oní* before a noun into *oló-* or *ọlọ́-* depending on vowel harmony. **[LINGUISTICALLY SECURE]**
- **Edo:** **Olókun/Olokun** can denote the supernatural being, “the sea,” or, in older lexical documentation, the Ethiope River/Ugbọ̀tọ̀n waterway. Songs may address the deity simply as **Òkùn**, “Sea.” The transparent Yoruba analysis does not prove that the cult itself originated among Yoruba speakers: recent linguistic discussion allows borrowing, reborrowing, or long Yoruba–Edo interaction and regards ultimate priority as indeterminate. **[SCHOLARLY DEBATE]**
- **Imalẹ̀ Òkun / Ìmalẹ̀ Òkun:** “divinity/spirit of the sea,” an explanatory Yoruba title. **[LATER RECORDED TRADITION]**
- **Olókun Ṣẹ̀níadé/Sèníadé:** a prominent Ifẹ̀ cult name. Modern Ifẹ̀ scholarship treats Ṣẹ̀níadé as Olókun’s particular manifestation or praise-name. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Ajé / Ajé Olókun:** wealth, market prosperity, or a wealth-bearing power associated with Olókun. Ajé is also independently personified, so “Ajé equals Olókun” is an overstatement. **[REGIONAL/LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Aggana Erí/Agana Eri:** in Cuban Ocha discourse, a named “road” or interior manifestation associated with sea foam and the water-bearing Olokun vessel. Orthography and explanation vary by house. **[AFRO-CUBAN REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Somú Gagá/Sumú Gagá and Akaró:** Cuban names for beings or powers accompanying Olokun, commonly interpreted as life and death. Their African antecedents are disputed and their standard pairing is best treated as Cuban ritual elaboration. **[LATER CANONICAL/REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Yemayá-Olokun:** a Cuban and modern transnational coupling, sometimes expressed by calling Olokun a *camino* of Yemayá. It is not a timeless African equation. **[SYNCRETISTIC]**
- **Poseidon:** Frobenius’s 1913 identification of the Ifẹ̀ head and deity with an “Atlantic African Poseidon” belonged to his Atlantis hypothesis. It is not indigenous tradition and has no archaeological support. **[DISCREDITED MODERN INVENTION]**

### Gender and classification

Yoruba lacks grammatical gender in third-person pronouns, which can make English renderings appear less fixed than the underlying ritual tradition. Nevertheless, gender variation is real rather than purely grammatical:

- **Ilé-Ifẹ̀:** Olókun Ṣẹ̀níadé is usually female and may be called Odùduwà’s wife or an immensely wealthy woman. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Edo/Benin:** Olókun is normally male, a son or subordinate of Osanobua, possessing wives, attendants, and a royal underwater court. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Other Yoruba and transnational accounts:** Olókun may be male, female, paired, or described as androgynous. Claims that Olókun was universally androgynous in antiquity cannot presently be established.
- **Cuba:** gender varies by lineage and by whether the vessel is received through Ocha or Ifá. Published popular schemes assigning “female Olokun to santeros” and “male Olokun to babalawos” describe some houses, not a universal Lucumí canon. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Archaeology

Igbo-Olokun lies near the northern gate of ancient Ilé-Ifẹ̀. Excavation and compositional analysis have established primary glass production there in the early second millennium CE, with crucibles, cullet, beads, glass debris, terracotta, and associated materials. The major phase belongs approximately to the eleventh–fifteenth centuries. This is among the earliest known primary glassmaking industries south of the Sahara. Because beads signify wealth, light, royalty, and ritual efficacy in later Yoruba thought, the site fits Olókun’s later domain; nevertheless:

1. no excavated inscription names the deity;
2. the medieval name of the workshop is unknown;
3. glass production proves an industry, not the gender, myths, or precise rites of its divine patron.

Thus “Olókun was worshipped at medieval Igbo-Olokun” is plausible historical continuity, not epigraphically demonstrated fact. **[EARLY MATERIAL + SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**

### The “Ori Olokun” head

In 1910–11 Frobenius excavated or caused local people to excavate in Olokun Grove and obtained a naturalistic copper-alloy head which he called **Ori Olokun**, “Head of Olokun.” Colonial authorities compelled its return after complaints about the circumstances of acquisition. Its subsequent history is confused; an object long considered a modern copy may possibly be the head Frobenius saw.

Frobenius’s find established that a revered head was kept or buried in the grove in 1910. It did **not** establish:

- that the image was manufactured as a portrait of Olókun;
- that it depicts a woman;
- that all naturalistic Ifẹ̀ heads are divinities;
- that its archaeological context was undisturbed.

Comparable Ifẹ̀ metal heads and figures are generally assigned to the classic Ifẹ̀ florescence, approximately the twelfth–fifteenth centuries. The label “Olokun head” is therefore a modern find-name and cult attribution, not a surviving medieval caption. **[CONTESTED MATERIAL]**

### Written and oral corpora

- **A. B. Ellis, *The Yoruba-Speaking Peoples of the Slave Coast* (1894):** identifies Olokun as god of the sea and Olosa as goddess of the lagoon; reports offerings and alleged human sacrifice. Ellis wrote as a colonial officer and often generalized diverse local evidence, but his account is among the earliest extended printed attestations. **[EARLY DOCUMENTARY]**
- **Samuel Johnson, *The History of the Yorubas*:** manuscript completed by 1897, lost, reconstructed by Obadiah Johnson and published in 1921. It is an indispensable Yoruba-Christian compilation of oral history, vocabulary, political memory, and religion, but not a transparent transcription of precolonial tradition. **[EARLY DOCUMENTARY/LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Leo Frobenius, *The Voice of Africa* (1913):** records the Ifẹ̀ grove, head, local custodianship, and early colonial encounter. His observations remain evidence; his Atlantis/Poseidon interpretation is discredited. **[EARLY DOCUMENTARY + MODERN INVENTION]**
- **Northcote Thomas’s Edo work (1910s), Ling Roth, Talbot, Egharevba, Bradbury, Ben-Amos, and later Benin ethnography:** document Olókun shrines, priests, songs, mud sculpture, coral symbolism, and connections with kingship. They also show that no documentary source securely dates the cult’s beginning.
- **Ifá corpus:** Olókun occurs in orally transmitted *ẹsẹ Ifá* attached to various Odù. Bascom’s *Ifa Divination* (1969), Abimbola’s publications, Bascom’s *Sixteen Cowries* (1980), and later Epega/Neimark collections record different performers and ritual environments. These books date the recorded performances, not the origin of an Odù or verse. No finite book is “the original complete Odu Ifá.”
- **Oríkì:** praise poetry calls Olókun ruler/owner of boundless water and possessor of wealth. Oríkì are performance archives: particular recorded versions must be dated to collection or performance rather than assigned wholesale to antiquity.
- **Bolaji Idowu, *Olódùmarè: God in Yoruba Belief* (1962):** influential theological organization of Yoruba religion. It is valuable testimony and interpretation, not an ancient creed. **[LATER CANONICAL/LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Lydia Cabrera, *El Monte* (1954), and Fernando Ortiz’s Afro-Cuban studies:** record twentieth-century Cuban testimony about Olokun, ritual vessels, plants, masks, stories, and relations with Yemayá. Their material derives from living Cuban lineages, not verbatim medieval Africa.
- **Pierre Verger, Roger Bastide, and Reginaldo Prandi:** essential for comparing West African and Brazilian orixá systems. Their very limited treatment of Olokun as an independent Brazilian cult is evidence of relative absence, not proof that no individual terreiro ever honored the name.
- **John Mason and Henry John Drewal:** document Cuban Olokun material, masks, drums, Fermina Gómez’s house, and possible visual connections with African masquerade. Morphological resemblance does not by itself prove direct continuity.
- **Garcés Marrero (2022):** synthesizes African and Cuban oral traditions and carefully distinguishes Olokun’s abyssal sea from Yemayá’s accessible waters. **[MODERN SCHOLARSHIP]**

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

- **Ocean and abyss:** the remote, dark, unknowable water beneath or beyond the visible sea. **[WIDELY ATTESTED]**
- **Wealth:** coral, beads, fish, valuable goods, fertility, commerce, and treasures imagined beneath the water. In Benin this domain was strengthened by maritime exchange and royal control of coral regalia. **[RECORDED ORAL TRADITION + HISTORICAL RECONSTRUCTION]**
- **Royalty:** Olókun’s underwater palace mirrors the Ọba’s court; coral collars, beaded crowns, brass, ivory, white chalk, and rich cloth materialize this parallel. **[EDO REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Danger:** flood, shipwreck, drowning, and the overwhelming return of water over land. **[MYTHIC/REGIONAL]**
- **Fertility and health:** cool water, abundance, childbirth, and long life occur in Yoruba and Cuban ritual interpretation. **[LATER RECORDED TRADITION]**

### Edo shrine imagery

Benin household and public shrines commonly include whitewashed mud architecture, **orhue** white chalk, ritual pots containing water, coral or glass beads, brass staffs, ceremonial swords, stools, ladders, pythons, mud figures, fish, crocodiles, and royal attendants. White conveys coolness, purity, joy, and Olókun’s watery realm. The python acts as companion or messenger. Olókun may appear crowned and richly dressed like an Ọba.

Olókun mud sculpture was often produced or maintained by women, including priestesses and women connected with craft guilds. This does not make the male Edo deity female; maker, priest, and represented being need not share gender.

### Ifẹ̀ imagery

No surviving medieval Ifẹ̀ object can be identified as Olókun solely from its form. The “Ori Olokun” head is associated with the grove through its recorded find history, but its represented identity remains unproved. Glass beads and beadmaking debris are firmer evidence for a wealth-producing ritual landscape than the head is for Olókun’s physical appearance.

### Cuban receptacles and emblems

Olokun is normally **received** as a consecrated assemblage rather than represented by a public anthropomorphic idol. Depending on lineage, it may include a large covered jar or *tinaja*, water or a dry interior, stones, shells, metal implements, chains, masks, a serpent, a small figure, anchors, rudders, propellers, and other marine signs. Secrecy limits outside verification, and published lists frequently combine distinct houses.

Common colors—dark blue, black, white, and combinations thereof—and numbers such as seven, nine, or twenty-one are lineage-dependent. Internet charts presenting one fixed number, day, or color should be classified as **[MODERN SYSTEMATIZATION]**.

### The chain and mask

The Cuban image of a masked or concealed Olokun chained in the depth gives narrative form to the sea’s contained danger. Olokun masks reportedly danced in several Cuban towns in older practice, especially around New Year observances; twentieth-century documentation associates special drums and masquerade with Fermina Gómez’s Matanzas lineage. This is genuine Afro-Cuban tradition, but a direct, unbroken derivation from a specific Nigerian mask society has not been demonstrated.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

There is no single genealogy.

- **Ifẹ̀ tradition:** female Olókun may be the wife of Odùduwà and remembered as an ancient possessor of exceptional wealth. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Primordial Yoruba cosmology:** Olókun already occupies the waters before dry land is formed. This makes genealogy secondary or absent. **[LATER RECORDED MYTH]**
- **Edo tradition:** male Olókun is commonly a child or subordinate of the creator Osanobua/Osalobua. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Cuba:** Olokun may be distinct from Yemayá, an older/deeper manifestation of her, her parent, or a paired complement. No formulation governs all Ocha and Ifá houses. **[REGIONAL VARIANTS]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The primordial waters and creation of land

**[LATER RECORDED YORUBA MYTH]** Olókun occupies the limitless waters before habitable land. A creator or delegated divinity—usually Ọbàtálá or Odùduwà in broader Yoruba creation narratives—establishes dry earth. Some modern retellings make Olókun an explicit opponent of land creation; others merely presuppose Olókun’s watery domain. The strong antagonistic version should not be projected into every Yoruba creation account.

### The flood and chaining

**[AFRO-CUBAN LATE ELABORATION]** In Cuban narratives Olokun, enraged or unable to tolerate human offenses, raises the sea to destroy the world. Ọbàtálá, Òrúnmìlà, or both intervene and bind Olokun with chains in the abyss. Olokun thereafter remains powerful but contained. The story explains the chain in ritual assemblages, the deity’s concealment, and the recurrent danger of the ocean.

The identity of the restrainer varies. Treating one version as “the original Odu” is unjustified without a named reciter, Odù, collection, and date.

### Contest of wealth and magnificence

**[EDO RECORDED ORAL TRADITION]** Olókun possesses a palace of dazzling wealth beneath the water. The Ọba or a culture hero reaches that realm and obtains coral, brass, beads, or regalia. In one cycle Oba Ewuare acquires coral from Olókun; in another his agent Okhuaihe does so. These traditions legitimate royal monopoly over coral and explain the material likeness between Olókun’s court and Benin’s palace.

The stories are not contemporary records of Ewuare’s fifteenth-century reign. They were recorded much later, although they plausibly preserve memories of the growing importance of overseas luxury goods.

### Olókun and the Ọba

**[EDO POLITICAL MYTH]** The terrestrial king and aquatic king are counterparts. The Ọba’s coral clothing, ivory, brass, and ritual whiteness proclaim participation in Olókun’s abundance. Oral history credits Oba Ohen, conventionally placed in the fourteenth century, with promoting Olókun worship as a state cult; some accounts add Ewuare. No contemporary fourteenth-century proclamation survives.

### Olókun Ṣẹ̀níadé as wealthy woman

**[IFẸ̀ REGIONAL FOLKLORE]** Olókun appears as an exceptionally wealthy woman, trader, finance officer, queen, or wife of Odùduwà. Her beads and commercial power become divine attributes. This may preserve historical memory of female economic office or personify the bead economy; identifying a particular historical woman behind the deity remains speculative.

### Fermina Gómez and the Cuban Olokun

**[MODERN CULT HISTORY]** Cuban tradition credits **Fermina Gómez, Ocha Bi**, of Matanzas with preserving or transforming Olokun rites, maintaining a celebrated set of Olokun drums, and inaugurating or renewing an open-sea feeding ceremony in 1944. Claims that she alone introduced Olokun to Cuba compete with traditions naming earlier priestesses, including Monserrate González. Fermina’s historical importance is secure; exclusive-foundress claims are lineage memory.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Ilé-Ifẹ̀

Igbo-Olokun/Olokun Grove was simultaneously a sacred landscape and a major industrial area. Frobenius excavated there in 1910–11; later archaeological campaigns investigated the grove and other Ifẹ̀ sites. Urban encroachment, illicit digging, and land seizure have destroyed much context. A separate shrine at Ilode is associated in local memory with Olókun’s residence.

Offerings recorded in Yoruba practice include cool water, white chalk, beads, cloth, food, fish, kola, and animal sacrifice. Exact liturgies vary. Olókun praise emphasizes immeasurable water, royal status, and wealth.

### Benin

Olókun worship has been practiced at household, neighborhood, specialist, and royal levels. Priests are called **ohen**, with women prominent among devotees and shrine makers. Shrines contain water vessels and modeled scenes of a wealthy court. Annual festivals, possession, songs, drumming, purification, and petitions for children, health, business success, and protection remain documented.

Oral histories connect the cult’s royal advancement with Oba Ohen and Ewuare. Modern historians stress that the cult’s actual point of origin cannot be substantiated. Assertions that it was founded around 900 CE under the Ogiso dynasty are retroactive oral chronology, not contemporary documentation.

### Human sacrifice

Ellis reported in 1894 that in extreme circumstances humans were offered to several Yoruba divinities, including Olokun, and that an Olokun victim’s body was cast into the sea. He also described British pressure in 1886 to obtain agreements abolishing human sacrifice and resistance at Ifẹ̀.

This is a concrete colonial-era allegation, not archaeological proof of a timeless Olokun requirement. Ellis’s hostile vocabulary and generalizations require caution, but the report should neither be suppressed nor converted into evidence that every Olokun community practiced human sacrifice. Later Benin descriptions report animal substitution in rites once said to have used a human victim. **[EARLY DOCUMENTARY, CONTESTED SCOPE]**

No reliable evidence supports “temple prostitution” as an institution of Olokun worship.

### Cuba

Olokun is generally received for stability, health, prosperity, longevity, or a divinatory prescription. Two broad ritual complexes are often distinguished:

- **Ocha/Iworo form:** commonly a water-bearing covered vessel associated with Aggana Erí.
- **Ifá/Babalawo form:** commonly described as dry or constructed differently and linked ritually with Oduduwá or Orula.

The distinction is real in many houses but not universal. Public descriptions omit secret contents and often conflate lineages. Offerings may occur at the sea; older masquerade and drum traditions were localized rather than universal Cuban practice.

### Brazil and the Río de la Plata

Olokun is conspicuously absent as a major independent orixá from classic Brazilian Candomblé ethnographies. Yemanjá absorbed the public oceanic domain, while Oxum governs fresh water and wealth in other registers. Olokun may appear in myths, recent Africanizing liturgies, individual houses, or imports from Cuba/Nigeria, but there is no evidence of a Brazilian cult historically comparable in scale to Yemanjá, Xangô, or Oxum.

Brazilian Umbanda and Batuque expanded into Uruguay and Argentina from the 1950s–60s, with major Argentine growth after democratization in the 1980s. Frigerio and Carozzi document this transnational field, but they do not establish Olokun as a major Argentine cult. Modern Argentine Olokun devotion should therefore be investigated house by house. **Absence from the main ethnographic pantheons is itself the finding.**

## 7. Historical evolution

### c. eleventh–fifteenth centuries: Ifẹ̀ urban florescence

Igbo-Olokun produced glass and beads within the classic Ifẹ̀ economy. A sacred association with Olókun is plausible but not epigraphically demonstrable. Naturalistic heads belong broadly to this cultural florescence, though the “Olokun head” cannot be securely identified as the deity.

### Fourteenth–sixteenth centuries: Benin royal consolidation

Later oral history credits Oba Ohen with elevating Olókun worship and Ewuare with acquiring coral or treasures from the sea ruler. Increasing Atlantic trade made a deity of overseas wealth especially resonant. This is a strong historical interpretation, but not proof that trade created the cult.

### Nineteenth century: missionary encounter and suppression

Christian missions and colonial agents documented Yoruba and Edo religion while campaigning against sacrifice and “idolatry.” Conversion, church discipline, Islamic expansion, and colonial legal systems reduced or privatized many cult practices. Yet Olókun worship survived in Ifẹ̀, Benin, household religion, royal symbolism, performance, and oral literature.

### 1894–1921: ethnographic textualization

Ellis, Johnson, Frobenius, Thomas, and others converted localized and performed knowledge into printed pantheons. Their books preserved material while also fixing fluid identities and imposing comparative categories such as “sea god” or “Poseidon.”

### Cuba, nineteenth–twentieth centuries

Enslaved and free Africans and their descendants reconstructed orisha religion under colonialism. Olokun developed within Lucumí Ocha and Ifá through lineage transmission, material secrecy, cross-ethnic exchange, and Cuban ecology. By the early twentieth century Matanzas and Havana lineages possessed distinctive vessels, masks, drums, and narratives.

### Brazil, nineteenth–twentieth centuries

Candomblé formed through multiple African inheritances. Olokun did not develop the strong independent institutional presence found in Cuba; Yemanjá became the preeminent public ocean orixá. Later transatlantic “re-Africanization” and digital exchange have made the name more visible.

### Late twentieth century–present

Migration and books by practitioners have globalized Olokun worship. Nigerian Ifá, Cuban Lucumí, US Orisha practice, Brazilian traditions, and online neopagan discourse now interact. This has generated both renewed access to elders and new composite systems that join mutually incompatible Edo, Ifẹ̀, Cuban, and popular-internet claims.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

Frobenius’s Atlantis theory was the first internationally influential modern reinvention. Unable to credit African artists fully, he imagined an external Mediterranean civilization and called the head an African Poseidon. Archaeology and technical study discredited the theory.

From the late twentieth century, English-language practitioner literature recast Olokun as a universal “spirit of the ocean,” sometimes combining:

- female Ifẹ̀ Olókun;
- male Edo Olókun;
- Cuban chains, masks, and Aggana Erí;
- Yemayá;
- Mami Wata;
- Western elemental, Jungian, New Age, or prosperity symbolism.

Such syntheses may be valid modern religious creations, but they are not evidence of a single ancient theology.

Claims that Olokun has one universal crystal, zodiac sign, tarot correspondence, chakra, “frequency,” or fixed candle color are **[MODERN INVENTION]** unless explicitly presented as the practice of a named contemporary group.

There is no documented historical role for Olokun in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, or early Theosophy. Appearances in modern occult correspondence lists are recent appropriation, not survival from those movements.

The modern dispute over being “crowned directly” to Olokun in Lucumí illustrates authority formation online. Many Cuban-lineage practitioners deny that direct Olokun initiation belongs to inherited Lucumí practice, while some contemporary teachers claim African or newly restored authority for it. Public assertions alone cannot establish an unbroken Cuban lineage. **[CONTESTED MODERN PRACTICE]**

No significant extremist political appropriation of Olokun is documented in the consulted scholarship. Commercial statues, jewelry, readings, ritual kits, and online initiations are widespread, but antiquity should not be inferred from marketing.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Origin

Neither a uniquely Yoruba nor uniquely Edo origin is demonstrable. The Yoruba name has a transparent etymology, but cult and vocabulary moved within a long-connected Yoruba–Edo region. Edo oral claims of Ogiso-period antiquity are not contemporary evidence. **Conclusion: origin indeterminate.**

### Gender

Older encyclopedic accounts often seek one answer. Ethnography instead supports locally stable differences: female at Ifẹ̀, male in Benin, multiple Cuban formulations. “Androgynous” can accurately describe some modern theological syntheses but should not erase regional gender.

### The head

The object called Ori Olokun has three distinct questions:

1. Was a head genuinely excavated from or displayed at the grove in 1910? Yes.
2. Is the presently known head the same object or a copy? Disputed.
3. Does it depict Olókun? Unproved.

Frobenius’s name became more certain in popular culture than the evidence allows.

### Ifá dating

Published Ifá verses are invaluable primary testimony to twentieth-century performers and lineages. Because the corpus is oral, expandable, and context-dependent, assigning an undifferentiated “ancient” date to every printed verse is methodologically indefensible.

### Benin royal ideology

Ben-Amos and subsequent scholars analyze Olókun imagery as a language of wealth, whiteness, coral, foreignness, and royal power. The question is not whether Olókun was “really” a sea god or a political metaphor: ritual being and political materiality reinforce one another.

### Brazilian absence

The limited record is not well explained by saying Brazilians “forgot” Olokun. Diasporic traditions selected and recomposed cults under demographic, institutional, and local ecological conditions. Yemanjá’s expansion helped occupy the public maritime field, while Cuban institutions developed a distinctive portable Olokun assemblage.

### State of evidence

The archive consists of:

- archaeological materials from Igbo-Olokun and wider Ifẹ̀;
- dispersed Ifẹ̀ and Benin artworks in Nigerian and foreign collections;
- colonial archives and CMS missionary papers;
- ethnographic notebooks, recordings, published oríkì, and Ifá performances;
- living shrines and lineages;
- Cuban material assemblages whose contents may properly remain restricted.

Damage to Igbo-Olokun through looting and development has irretrievably reduced archaeological context. Colonial extraction dispersed objects and distorted provenance.

### Evidentiary conclusion

The fullest defensible reconstruction is not a single biography. It is a layered history:

1. an Olokun-named Ifẹ̀ landscape associated with medieval glass wealth;
2. a predominantly female Ifẹ̀ divinity;
3. a predominantly male Edo sea king closely tied to royal authority and coral;
4. nineteenth-century textualization under mission and colonial rule;
5. a distinct Cuban abyssal deity embodied in vessels, chains, masks, drums, and competing Ocha/Ifá lineages;
6. limited historic institutionalization in Brazil;
7. a modern transnational figure increasingly assembled from all these forms.

The continuities—sea, depth, wealth, beads, royalty, danger—are substantial. The differences in gender, genealogy, ritual form, narrative, and institutional history are equally substantive and should not be harmonized away.
