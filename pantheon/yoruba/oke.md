# Oke: Research Dossier

**Class:** Òrìṣà—a divine power embodied in hills, mountains, elevated ground, and particular sacred rocks. “Oke” can designate a generalized mountain òrìṣà, a named local hill-divinity such as Òkè-Ìbàdàn or Òkè Olúmọ, or, in diaspora taxonomies, a minor orisha associated with Obatalá, Orisha Oko, and Oggué. The traditions do not consistently classify Oke as one individual with a single gender or genealogy.

**Earliest secure attestation:** A. B. Ellis, *The Yoruba-Speaking Peoples of the Slave Coast of West Africa* (London, 1894), pp. 81–82, is the earliest presently verifiable, unambiguous published description of Oke as “the god of Mountains.” Ellis’s account was based on late nineteenth-century observation and testimony in the Yoruba-speaking region. It establishes the cult before 1894 but does not establish its date of origin. No securely identified precolonial inscription, archaeological image, or Ifẹ̀ sculpture bearing Oke’s name is presently known.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** directly documented before or during the early colonial period.
- **[LC] Later canonical or influential formulation:** widely circulated in twentieth- or twenty-first-century textualized tradition.
- **[RV] Regional or lineage variant:** associated with a particular city, cult-house, or diaspora lineage.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** an author’s organization of plural traditions into a theology or pantheon.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** historically contingent equation or ritual association between distinct beings.
- **[F] Folklore/oral tradition:** recorded tradition whose claimed events cannot independently be dated.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an academic interpretation, not itself an indigenous myth.
- **[MI] Modern invention or currently unverified modern claim:** not traceable to the old sources cited for it.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identification

**[EA]** Ellis records *Oke* as both the Yoruba common noun “mountain, hill” and the name of “the god of Mountains,” worshipped especially in rocky or mountainous country. He presents Oke as capable of sending falling rocks and landslides when neglected. This is the clearest early evidence for a generalized mountain power rather than merely one city’s tutelary deity.

**[RV]** Local evidence complicates that apparently singular identity. Òkè-Ìbàdàn is a specifically Ibadan hill-divinity and civic protector, characterized in modern ethnography as female and praised as Àtàgé Olómú Òrù, approximately “the elevated one whose breasts are like great water-pots.” Òkè Olúmọ is the divine power associated with Olumo Rock and the historical refuge of the Ẹ̀gbá at Abeokuta. Other rocks and hills may likewise possess distinct Oke identities. “Oke” therefore functions both as a proper name and as a category or title for localized powers of elevation.

### Yoruba forms

- **Òkè:** “hill, mountain; elevated place.” This is the ordinary Yoruba noun and the most defensible etymology of the divine name.
- **Òrìṣà Òkè:** “òrìṣà of the hill/mountain.”
- **Òkè-Ìbàdàn / Oke-’Badan:** the sacred hill and tutelary divinity of Ibadan.
- **Òkè Olúmọ:** the divine hill or sacred power of Olumo Rock at Abeokuta.
- **Olóòkè / Olóke:** morphologically “owner/lord of the hill” (*oní* + *òkè*, with assimilation), found particularly in modern Nigerian and Brazilian religious usage.
- **Olorokê / Oloroke / Olôke / Olooke:** Brazilian forms, especially in Efọn/Efon Candomblé.
- **Atage / Àtàgé; Àtàgé Olómú Òrù:** Ibadan cult-name and praise-title for the female power of Oke-Ibadan. Exact tone-marking and segmentation vary in publications.
- **Atajue Olomu Ore:** a form circulating on contemporary Cuban religious websites. It appears related to the Ibadan praise-name but is linguistically corrupt or retranscribed; it should not be treated as an independently verified ancient epithet.
- **Oke-té:** Afro-Cuban form documented for Santiago de Cuba in twentieth-century ethnography.
- **Oqué, Obalóke, Obaloke, Obbaloke, Ibanloke, Imbaloke:** modern Afro-Cuban spellings or titles circulating in ritual manuals and online summaries. Their age and exact Lucumí derivations remain unestablished.

The spelling **Ọ̀kẹ́**, found on some contemporary English-language religious sites, reverses or changes the expected Yoruba tones and vowels. Unless a lineage supplies a separate etymology, standard Yoruba *òkè*, “hill/mountain,” is preferable.

### Gender and classification

- **[EA] General Oke:** Ellis uses masculine English pronouns and calls Oke a god.
- **[RV] Ibadan:** Oke-Ibadan/Àtàgé is explicitly a goddess. The male Abòkè priest traditionally dresses and wears his hair in a feminine manner during the festival because the indwelling power of the hill is female.
- **[RV] Olumo:** frequently treated as a localized hill-divinity or sacred power; gender is not consistently specified.
- **[RV/LC] Cuba:** usually grammatically masculine in Spanish accounts, represented as a minor orisha or as an orisha closely dependent on Obatalá. Some houses subsume Oke under a “road” or manifestation of Obatalá; other sources maintain Oke as a separate fundamento accompanying Obatalá.
- **[RV/LC] Brazil:** Okê/Olooke is an orixá or *imolé* of mountains, especially important in Efọn houses. Wider Ketu Candomblé often gives Oke little independent public prominence.

Thus “Oke is male,” “Oke is female,” and “Oke is merely a road of Obatalá” are each local classifications, not universally valid definitions.

### Equations and syncretisms

- **Oke–Obatalá:** **[RV/SI]** Cuban practice often places Oke close to Obatalá; some houses describe Oke as a camino of Obatalá, while others regard Oke as an independent minor orisha who accompanies him. “Obatalá Okeilu” or Oke-ilu is also listed in modern camino catalogues, but such catalogues frequently conflate African place-cults, independent funfun divinities, honorifics, and Cuban ritual manifestations. The equation cannot be projected backward into nineteenth-century Yorubaland.
- **Oke–Orisha Oko:** **[RV/SI]** Mid-twentieth-century Cuban field reports state that some santeros considered Oke identical with Orisha Oko, while other Havana practitioners distinguished the mountain-owner Oke from the agricultural deity. The similarity of the Spanish spellings *Oke/Oko* probably encouraged the conflation, but the Yoruba nouns are different: *òkè*, hill, versus *ọ̀kọ̀/oko* in agricultural compounds.
- **Oke-té–Santa Marta:** **[RV/SI]** Recorded in Santiago de Cuba; Havana informants in the same comparative account reportedly did not know this equivalence.
- **Oke–San Roberto:** **[RV/SI/LC]** Appears in later Cuban saint-orisha tables, with a feast date of 7 June. Documentation is later and less substantial than the Oke-té/Santa Marta identification.
- **Oke–Santiago Apóstol:** **[RV/LC]** Appears in recent Cuban manuals, but the older regional table associates Santiago with Ochosi, not Oke. It should therefore be treated as a late or house-specific assignment.
- **Oke–Orisha Oko–Oggué “earth trilogy”:** **[RV/LC]** Common in contemporary Cuban manuals. It organizes mountain, cultivated earth, and horned/herd-animal powers into a functional triad. No nineteenth-century Yoruba source presently verifies this as a pan-Yoruba triad.

No reliable evidence was found for a traditional equation with a Greco-Roman, Norse, Egyptian, Mesopotamian, Persian, or Indian mountain deity.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The evidentiary problem

Yoruba religious knowledge was transmitted chiefly through oral poetry, divination verses, ritual speech, names, shrines, and performance. The absence of an inscription naming Oke is therefore not evidence that the cult began in the nineteenth century. It means only that its earlier history cannot presently be fixed by a datable written artifact.

The famous Ifẹ̀ terracotta and copper-alloy heads, usually dated broadly between the twelfth and fifteenth centuries, establish a sophisticated sacred and royal artistic culture at Ifẹ̀. They do **not** securely attest Oke. No surviving head has been identified as Oke by an inscription or uncontested iconographic attribute. Frobenius’s discoveries at Ifẹ̀ in 1910 similarly transformed knowledge of Yoruba art but do not supply an authenticated ancient image of this orisha. Attributing an anonymous Ifẹ̀ head to Oke would be modern speculation.

### 1894: Ellis

**[EA]** Ellis gives the first clearly datable description:

- Oke means mountain or hill.
- Oke is god of mountains.
- People in mountainous or rocky regions worship him.
- Neglect may bring falling boulders or landslides.
- A fallen boulder can signal that Oke requires something.
- Ellis alleges that, following a major rock disaster, a human victim was offered to avert Oke’s anger.

Ellis is invaluable as a near-contemporary witness but must be read as a colonial officer working through informants and the evolutionary “nature-worship” categories of late Victorian anthropology. His assertion about human sacrifice is evidence that the allegation circulated or was reported to him; it is not, without a named event or corroborating local record, proof that every Oke cult practiced it.

### Samuel Johnson

**[EA/LS]** Samuel Johnson completed the lost original manuscript of *The History of the Yorubas* in 1897; his brother Obadiah reconstructed it from Samuel’s notes, and the work appeared in 1921. It is foundational for Yoruba political history and oral traditions, including Ibadan and the nineteenth-century wars. It does not provide a comparably discrete Oke theology. Its silences are significant: Oke was not one of the pan-Yoruba figures around which Johnson organized his historical narrative.

### Ifá corpus

The Ifá corpus is not a single fixed scripture. It consists of orally transmitted verses associated with 256 odù, recorded differently by diviners, towns, lineages, and collectors.

- William Bascom’s *Ifa Divination: Communication between Gods and Men in West Africa* derives from fieldwork chiefly in Ifẹ̀ in 1937–38 and later visits; published in 1969.
- Wande Abimbola’s *Ifá: An Exposition of Ifá Literary Corpus* appeared in 1976 and treats Ifá as oral literature with variable performances.
- C. L. Adeoye, E. M. Lijadu, Abimbola, Bascom, and later Epega/Neimark publications preserve different selections and editorial principles.
- Bascom’s *Sixteen Cowries* (1980) records the related but distinct ẹ̀rìndínlógún corpus from Yoruba and Cuban informants.

**Finding:** No single universally recognized “Oke cycle” comparable to the major Ọ̀rúnmìlà, Èṣù, Ògún, Ṣàngó, Ọbàtálá, or Ọ̀ṣun cycles emerges from the standard published corpora. Contemporary practitioners assign Oke narratives to **Òfún Méjì**, **Èjì Ogbè**, and **Ìrẹtẹ̀-Ògúndá/Ìrẹtẹ̀ Ààyá**, but the specific verses circulating online are rarely supplied with an identifiable diviner, collection date, Yoruba transcript, and published critical edition. These assignments are therefore **[RV/LC]**, not a securely reconstructed ancient canon.

This does not mean that no authentic Oke verses exist. It means their precise transmission histories remain inadequately published.

### Oríkì and festival poetry

**[F/RV]** Oke-Ibadan songs and praise-names constitute the richest identifiable local oral corpus. They praise Àtàgé as the large-breasted provider and protector of Ibadan and recall:

- Lagelu and his followers taking refuge on the hill;
- a time of hunger when the refugees survived on snails, hot maize pap, and wild fruits;
- Ibadan’s military past;
- fertility and communal renewal;
- sexual license, ribald speech, and temporary inversion of normal decorum.

The surviving published texts are modern recordings of oral performance. Their subject matter may preserve nineteenth-century or earlier memories, but individual lines cannot automatically be dated to the events they narrate.

### Afro-Cuban corpus

**[RV]** Rómulo Lachatañeré’s work, republished in *Actas del Folklore*, preserves particularly valuable mid-twentieth-century testimony:

- some Cuban santeros equated Oke with Orisha Oko;
- Santiago de Cuba knew Oke-té and identified the being with Santa Marta;
- other Havana cults retained a more recognizably Yoruba understanding of Oke as owner of hills and mountains;
- the relevant Lucumí expression was recorded as *ilé oke*, “house/land of the hill.”

Lydia Cabrera’s *El Monte* (1954) is the foundational wide-ranging ethnography of Afro-Cuban sacred nature, plants, orishas, and ritual knowledge. Its information came from named and unnamed Afro-Cuban religious specialists only one or two generations removed from slavery. Cabrera’s work is not a pristine transcription of “African mythology”: it documents Cuban traditions as they existed in the twentieth century.

### Brazilian corpus

Pierre Verger’s works, Roger Bastide’s *Le Candomblé de Bahia* (1958), and Reginaldo Prandi’s *Mitologia dos Orixás* (2001) document Brazilian traditions at different historical moments. Prandi prints two Oke narratives derived from oral and published sources:

1. Oke emerges as the first mound of land.
2. Oraniyan raises Oke from beneath the primordial sea ruled by Olokun.

These are **[RV/LC] Brazilian textualizations**, not securely datable precolonial Yoruba texts. Prandi’s anthology has become highly influential, so later retellings often present its synthesized prose as an ancient fixed scripture.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**[EA]** Oke’s irreducible domain is elevated and rocky land: mountains, hills, ridges, cliffs, large rocks, and the powers manifested through them.

Associated functions vary:

- **Rockfall and landslide:** Ellis’s Oke can dislodge rocks as punishment.
- **Shelter and defense:** Oke Olumo protects refugees and warriors.
- **Civic identity:** Oke-Ibadan embodies the city’s origin, continuity, and protection.
- **Fertility:** Ibadan traditions associate the hill-goddess’s breasts with nourishment, human reproduction, animals, and soil productivity.
- **Stability and endurance:** common in modern diaspora exposition; a reasonable symbolism of mountains, but poorly documented as an old formal doctrine.
- **Agricultural fertility:** prominent in Cuba, partly through association or conflation with Orisha Oko.
- **Elevation, prestige, and success:** found in modern Nigerian and Brazilian devotional interpretations.
- **Cosmogonic first land:** especially prominent in Brazilian Olooke narratives.

### Physical form

There is no pan-Yoruba anthropomorphic standard.

- **[EA/RV] Natural form:** the hill, rock, boulder, or mountain itself is the primary manifestation and cult-object.
- **[RV] Oke-Ibadan:** imagined as a massively breasted female power. The priest’s feminine costume makes her gender visible in performance.
- **[RV] Cuba:** frequently represented by one smooth, rounded, flat stone—often white, black, brown, or caramel-colored—kept in a shallow dish, frying-pan-like vessel, or covered receptacle.
- **[LC] Modern illustrated Oke:** an elderly light-skinned or white-haired man standing on a mountain is a recent devotional image, apparently formed under the influence of Obatalá imagery. It has no demonstrated precolonial pedigree.

### Symbols and substances

- Stone, boulder, hill, mountain.
- White cloth or white coloring in Cuban houses, reflecting the Obatalá association.
- Covered gourd or vessel placed on the ground.
- Eggs, yam, milk, honey, toasted maize, sweets, pigeons, and other offerings in modern Cuban manuals; prescriptions vary by house.
- Snails, tortoise, fish, dogs, and hot maize pap in published descriptions of Oke-Ibadan festival provision. These are civic-festival materials, not necessarily universal Oke offerings.
- Leaves used in Cuban initiatory herb preparation. Recent manuals name *alacrancillo*, *bejuco guaro*, and *candelilla*, but botanical identifications and house practice vary.
- No ancient sacred color, number, weekday, or planet is securely attested. White and the number eight occur in modern Cuban sources; claims that these are universal or primordial are **[LC/MI]**.

### Iconographic recognition

An Oke object can be identified securely only when provenance, ritual testimony, or documentation connects it to the cult. An isolated Yoruba stone, white vessel, or carved figure is not self-identifying. Museum catalogs commonly assign anonymous objects to better-documented orishas; no famous ancient sculpture is securely Oke.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No early source supplies Oke with a stable parentage, spouse, or children.

- **[EA]** Ellis provides no genealogy.
- **[RV]** Oke-Ibadan is linked by covenant to Lagelu, the culture-hero/founder, but he is not described as her divine parent or husband.
- **[RV/LC]** Cuban sources place Oke beside or beneath Obatalá, occasionally treating Oke as a camino of Obatalá.
- **[RV/LC]** Brazilian Efọn discourse sometimes makes Olooke the father of Oxum. This is a lineage-specific claim and not pan-Yoruba.
- **[MI]** Recent websites call Oke a brother of Agayú because one represents a cool stable mountain and the other volcanic force. No early Yoruba or classic Afro-Cuban source located here supports that sibling genealogy.

### Every documented divine interaction

The short list is itself an important result: Oke has far fewer securely published god-to-god episodes than the major pan-Yoruba orishas.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. Oke’s anger and the falling rocks

**[EA] Source:** Ellis, 1894.

Oke inhabits or is embodied in mountainous and rocky country. Neglecting his wants risks the fall of great rock masses onto human dwellings or their destruction by landslide. A falling boulder serves as a message that an offering is required. Ellis further states that after a major calamity a human victim was offered to turn away the god’s anger.

This is not an extended narrative with characters and dialogue but an etiological and ritual belief: it explains rockfall as divine agency and sacrifice as restoration of the relationship.

**Development:** Modern treatments normally omit the punitive landslide and human-sacrifice allegation, replacing them with stability, patience, protection, and “high vibration.” Those benevolent abstractions are later selective reinterpretations.

### B. Lagelu and Oke-Ibadan

**[F/RV] Sources:** Ibadan oral history, twentieth-century local histories, festival ethnography published in 2021.

In the civic tradition, Lagelu and his people, displaced during the destruction or abandonment of an earlier settlement, take refuge on Oke-Ibadan. Conditions are desperate. The refugees live on snails, wild fruit, and hot pap, reportedly using snail shells as vessels. The hill shelters them and enters a covenant with Lagelu to protect and nourish the emerging community.

Oke-Ibadan becomes Àtàgé, the city’s maternal hill-goddess. Her enormous breasts symbolize the ability to feed children and sustain the community. The annual festival re-enacts the inversion and deprivation of the founding period: work ceases, foods recalling the refuge are supplied, obscene and historical songs are sung, and the male Abòkè priest adopts female presentation.

**Variants:** Accounts differ about which settlement of Ibadan the episode belongs to and how directly present-day Ibadan descends from Lagelu’s community. The basic festival tradition is not a neutral chronological record; it is civic memory that legitimizes belonging and office.

### C. Olumo as shelter of the Ẹ̀gbá

**[F with historical core/RV] Sources:** Ẹ̀gbá traditions, nineteenth-century settlement history, modern historical research.

During the Yoruba wars and displacement of the early nineteenth century, groups who became the consolidated Ẹ̀gbá community used the granite outcrop of Olumo as refuge, lookout, defensive position, and shelter. Abeokuta—conventionally interpreted as “under the rock”—grew around this refuge. The rock’s saving historical function was sacralized as Oke Olumo, “the hill deity who shelters the distressed.”

The historical use of the rock during the formation of Abeokuta is much better supported than supernatural details about its intervention. Annual animal sacrifice at its shrine demonstrates continuing religious treatment of the rock, while its later development as a tourist monument adds civic and commercial layers.

Olumo should not simply be renamed “Oke” as though it were the sole or universal mountain orisha. It is a particular sacred rock and localized divine presence.

### D. Oraniyan raises Oke from Olokun’s sea

**[RV/LC] Source:** Prandi, *Mitologia dos Orixás* (2001), drawing on Brazilian oral and published traditions.

At first everything is water, the domain of Olokun, and Ọlọ́fin is dissatisfied with an undifferentiated sea. Empowered by Ọlọ́fin, Oraniyan brings the first mound or mountain up from the depths. Oke rises above the water. Because dry land now exists, terrestrial life becomes possible. Oke is thus both the first mountain and the condition for habitation.

A related narrative describes matter accumulating into a growing mound until earth is formed. These tales place Oke in cosmogony, close to the widespread Yoruba motif of the first patch of dry land at Ifẹ̀.

**Historical caution:** Prandi’s polished literary presentation dates to 2001. The motifs may be older, but the printed version cannot be cited as a verbatim ancient Ifá text unless its individual oral source is identified.

### E. Oke consults Orunmila in wartime

**[RV/LC] Source:** contemporary Cuban religious publication, 2022.

During war involving Ifẹ̀, Oke retreats to a mountain called Oshunta and consults Orunmila. The odù Èjì Ogbè appears. Orunmila prescribes an offering including pigeons, mangoes, a jar, different woods, and cloths of several colors. After making the sacrifice, Oke withstands or escapes the enemy.

The tale functions as an Ifá exemplum: danger becomes manageable through divination and correctly performed *ẹbọ*. Because the accessible version supplies neither an older publication nor a Yoruba verse, it is evidence for contemporary diaspora teaching, not for a datable ancient episode.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### General hill cult

**[EA]** Ellis describes worship wherever communities inhabited rocky or mountainous country. This suggests a distributed class of local cults rather than a single central temple or priesthood. The hill itself, a dangerous rock face, or a significant boulder could be both divine manifestation and shrine.

Offerings appear to have been occasioned by danger, rockfall, agricultural need, protection, civic memory, or an annual calendar. There is no evidence for a centralized trans-Yoruba Oke priesthood.

### Oke-Ibadan

The priestly title is **Abòkè**, borne by the ritual head of the Oke-Ibadan community. Modern ethnography records:

- rites at the Oke-Ibadan shrine;
- provision of festival materials by the Olubadan or civic authorities;
- suspension of farming for the day;
- consumption or ritual memory of hot pap, snails, and wild foods;
- public procession;
- ribald and historical songs;
- the male Abòkè’s female dress and braided hair;
- prayer for children, prosperity, safety, and communal success.

The festival is simultaneously cult, local historiography, fertility rite, and assertion of Ibadan identity. Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century Christian and Muslim observers often categorized its sexual songs and temporary license as immorality. Such descriptions must be read as evidence of religious competition as well as observation.

The suggestion that the festival preserves a prehistoric Yoruba matriarchy is **[MSR]**, advanced in a modern comparative thesis. The female deity and formerly female priesthood may be locally remembered facts; they do not by themselves prove a prehistoric social system.

### Olumo Rock

Olumo became a sanctuary in the formation of Abeokuta, conventionally dated to around 1830. Its religious and political functions include:

- refuge and defense;
- collective symbol of Ẹ̀gbá survival;
- shrine of Oke Olumo;
- annual animal sacrifice;
- ritual offices associated with Itoko and Ìjẹmọ groups;
- later tourism, state heritage management, and commercialization.

The Jagunna of Itoko is reported as a principal ritual custodian/high priest. Religious access and authority have at times intersected uneasily with government tourism projects. The rock remains both a living sacred site and a public heritage attraction.

### Sacrifice

- **Human sacrifice:** Ellis alleges a human victim after catastrophic rockfall. The report is early but nonspecific. Modern scholarship confirms that human sacrifice existed in parts of nineteenth-century Yorubaland and became a focus of conflict among chiefs, enslaved people, Christian converts, and British officials, but that wider evidence does not independently demonstrate a regular Oke sacrifice everywhere. The responsible conclusion is: **one early ethnographer explicitly reports it; frequency, place, and ritual procedure remain unverified**.
- **Animal sacrifice:** securely documented at Olumo and in local Oke festivals. Species vary.
- **Food offerings:** snails, pap, yam, fruit, honey, maize, and other foods are locally documented.
- **Cuban practice:** Oke may be received as a stone fundamento in a low dish or covered vessel. Its stone can be used in crushing or preparing sacred herbs for initiation. Precise contents are initiatory and house-specific; published “recipes” cannot be assumed universal.

No evidence was found for temple prostitution attached to Oke. Sexual songs and phallic gestures at Oke-Ibadan are ritual license and fertility symbolism; calling them prostitution would be unsupported.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before the nineteenth century

**[F/MSR]** Hill cults are likely older than their first documentation because they were embedded in place, lineage, and oral practice. Archaeology establishes long settlement and sacred kingship in Yorubaland but has not securely identified an ancient Oke shrine by inscription. A claim that Oke was worshipped in the Stone Age, at the creation of Ifẹ̀, or for a specified number of millennia is theological or speculative, not archaeological chronology.

### Early nineteenth century: war, refuge, and city formation

The Yoruba wars produced displacement and new political formations. Olumo’s role in the creation of Abeokuta gave a sacred rock immediate historical significance as fortress and refuge. Ibadan’s civic tradition similarly anchored communal survival in a protective hill and its goddess.

This period probably intensified the political functions of particular hill cults: Oke was not merely “nature” but territorial memory, the ground of citizenship, and a power validating offices.

### Mid-to-late nineteenth century: missionary encounter

Christian missions and Islam expanded in Abeokuta, Ibadan, and elsewhere. Missionary writing often grouped oriṣa worship under “idolatry,” while converts’ journals nevertheless created some of the earliest written descriptions of Yoruba practice. J. D. Y. Peel’s reconstruction from CMS archives shows that Christianity did not simply erase an inert old religion; conversion, competition, translation, schooling, and political struggle helped produce the modern category “Yoruba religion.”

No documented single decree specifically abolishing Oke worship has been located. Its decline or privatization occurred through wider processes:

- Christian and Muslim conversion;
- mission education;
- colonial legal intervention against certain sacrifices;
- reclassification of shrines as superstition or heritage;
- movement away from lineage-based ritual offices;
- absorption of hill symbolism into Christian prayer-mountain practice.

### 1894–1921: textualization

Ellis fixed a concise “god of mountains” profile in print. Johnson organized Yoruba historical traditions into a national history but did not elevate Oke into the rank of a dominant pan-Yoruba god. These publications influenced later encyclopedic pantheons, which tended to turn localized powers into a list of discrete gods with uniform biographies.

### Twentieth-century Cuba

The enslaved Africans and their descendants who created Lucumí/Ocha traditions brought together cults from multiple Yoruba regions under new plantation, urban, cabildo, and kinship conditions. Minor local orishas could be:

- preserved as separate ritual objects;
- attached to a better-known orisha;
- confused through similar names;
- identified with Catholic saints;
- remembered differently in Havana and Santiago.

Oke exemplifies all four processes. Oke-té/Santa Marta survived in Santiago; some Havana informants retained a mountain-owner; others equated Oke with Orisha Oko. Later houses positioned Oke beside Obatalá or within an Oke–Oggué–Orisha Oko triad.

Calling Oke simply “a road of Obatalá” describes some Cuban systems but erases evidence that other houses preserve an independent mountain orisha.

### Twentieth-century Brazil

Oke remained marginal in much public Candomblé literature but survived especially as **Olooke/Olorokê** in Efọn houses. Efọn Candomblé associates Olooke and Oxum with the nation’s sacred identity. Prandi’s 2001 publication of Oke cosmogonies made the figure more visible beyond initiated communities.

Brazilian terms require care: *Okê Arô!* is principally a salutation to Oxóssi and does not by itself attest worship of the mountain orixá Okê. Search results and popular writing often confuse the salutation with the deity.

### Christian appropriation of the mountain

From the early twentieth century, Aladura and later Pentecostal Christians developed **orí-òkè**, “mountain-top,” prayer centers. Yoruba sacred geography—elevation, seclusion, divine encounter—continued, but the addressed being became the Christian God, Jesus, Mary, or the Holy Spirit. This is not continuous Oke worship, although it is a transformation of the religious use of hills.

Modern Christian prayer mountains include sites named for mercy, miracles, deliverance, safe childbirth, and divine power. Scholars debate whether this represents continuity, appropriation, replacement, or a shared West African sacred-landscape logic.

### Present

Oke remains:

- a locally worshipped power in Nigerian communities;
- the tutelary goddess of the Oke-Ibadan festival;
- Oke Olumo at a living shrine and tourist heritage site;
- a minor but ritually meaningful orisha in Cuban Ocha;
- Olooke/Olorokê in some Brazilian Efọn and related houses;
- a subject of global Isese and online devotional revival.

There is no reliable census of Oke devotees. Claims that the cult is either extinct or globally widespread are both overstated.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Neotraditional and reconstructionist religion

Global Isese communities increasingly circulate standardized profiles of lesser-known orishas. For Oke these commonly include:

- “stability, resilience, elevation, ancestral presence”;
- a white-haired male appearance;
- fixed colors, numbers, offerings, and personality traits;
- attribution to one odù;
- claims of an unbroken universal cult.

Some elements may derive from genuine lineages, but internet profiles often combine Nigerian, Cuban, and Brazilian material without identifying provenance. This creates a new composite Oke that did not exist as such in the old record.

### Occult and New Age use

The dedicated search found no substantive role for Oke in Renaissance Hermetism, the Theosophical Society, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Aleister Crowley’s systems, or classic European grimoires. Claims that Oke has an ancient planetary correspondence, tarot card, Kabbalistic sphere, angelic seal, or universal “mountain frequency” are **[MI]** unless supported by a named modern author. They are not recoveries of traditional Yoruba doctrine.

Contemporary “work with Oke” material on occult websites generally treats the deity as an individually accessible archetype. That model differs from initiatory or lineage-based Yoruba and diaspora practice, in which shrines, divination, inherited office, priestly authorization, and community are central.

### Psychology

No major Jungian school appears to have developed Oke as a distinct archetype. Interpreting Oke as “the archetype of stability,” “higher consciousness,” or “the mountain within” is modern psychological allegory.

### Heritage and tourism

Olumo Rock is the most visible modern public representation. Tourism advertising emphasizes geology, panoramic views, caves, warriors, and Ẹ̀gbá refuge. The shrine may be displayed as “culture” even while custodians regard it as a living religious place. This produces a recurrent tension: heritage institutions make the sacred rock publicly consumable while ritual authorities retain restrictions and annual obligations.

Oke-Ibadan likewise appears in civic cultural programming, scholarship, local news, and heritage discourse. Its ribald performance has sometimes been softened in public descriptions.

### Visual culture and media

Oke has not acquired a stable mass-media image comparable to Thor, Zeus, Ṣàngó, or Yemọja. Modern digital art often depicts a muscular or elderly man fused with a mountain. Such images are visual inventions derived from the domain, not copies of documented Yoruba cult art.

### Political and nationalist appropriation

Oke-Ibadan and Olumo function as civic emblems. Their political use is principally local—asserting indigeneity, unity, survival, and historical precedence. No sustained Oke-centered extremist movement was found. Broader assertions that Yoruba religion derives from Atlantis, ancient Egypt, or a primordial global civilization sometimes enlist mountain and creation imagery; these are modern diffusionist or nationalist constructions unsupported by Oke-specific evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### One god or a category of place-powers?

Ellis’s wording encouraged encyclopedias to present Oke as one god. Local evidence instead supports a spectrum:

1. the common noun *òkè*, hill;
2. a generic class of sacred hills or their powers;
3. independent localized beings such as Oke-Ibadan and Oke Olumo;
4. an abstract pan-Yoruba “orisha of mountains” produced partly by comparison and textualization;
5. diaspora fundamentos reconstructed from memories of these forms.

The most cautious model treats “Oke” as a family of related cult concepts, sometimes personified as one orisha and sometimes localized as distinct beings.

### Is Oke-Ibadan the same being as Ellis’s Oke?

The common hill identity supports relationship, but the gender and civic mythology differ. Oke-Ibadan is female, maternal, and tied to Lagelu; Ellis’s generalized Oke is male in English translation and chiefly explains rockfall. They should be cross-referenced, not automatically collapsed.

### Is Olumo merely an Oke shrine?

Olumo is both a geological outcrop and a named sacred protector with a particular Ẹ̀gbá history. “Oke Olumo” can be translated as the Olumo hill-divinity, but Olumo’s identity is not exhausted by the generic category.

### Human sacrifice

Ellis’s report cannot be discarded merely because it is disturbing, but neither should it be inflated. Olatunji Ojo’s study demonstrates that human sacrifice was a real and contested institution in nineteenth-century Yorubaland, particularly in Ondo, and that its suppression involved internal opponents as well as colonial pressure. It does not independently verify an Oke-wide institution. The dossier therefore preserves Ellis’s allegation at its proper evidentiary weight.

### Syncretism

Older scholarship often explained Catholic-orisha correspondences as camouflage under slavery. Later scholars emphasize more complex processes: multiple religious participation, analogical theology, cabildo politics, colonial classification, ritual creativity, and strategic public presentation. Oke’s several Cuban saints—Santa Marta, San Roberto, and Santiago in different sources—show why a single fixed “Catholic equivalent” is historically misleading.

### Ifá authority

Modern accounts frequently introduce a myth with “Ifá says” without identifying the odù performance, diviner, collection, language, or date. Since the corpus varies legitimately, absence from Bascom or Abimbola does not prove fabrication. It does, however, prevent secure dating. An academically usable citation needs the Yoruba verse, performer or lineage, collection circumstances, odù assignment, and edition.

### Primary evidence and archives

Important repositories and bodies of evidence include:

- Ellis’s 1894 published ethnography;
- Samuel and Obadiah Johnson’s papers and the 1921 history;
- CMS missionary journals used by Peel;
- Nigerian university repositories preserving Ibadan festival research;
- field recordings and notes behind Bascom’s Yoruba studies;
- Lydia Cabrera’s papers in the Cuban Heritage Collection at the University of Miami;
- Fernando Ortiz Foundation publications;
- living shrine custodians and festival communities at Ibadan and Abeokuta;
- Brazilian terreiro archives, especially Efọn genealogies;
- oral ritual corpora whose public documentation remains fragmentary.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- That an Ifẹ̀ head or other ancient sculpture has been proved to depict Oke.
- That Oke has one universally accepted gender.
- That Oke is everywhere merely Obatalá under another name.
- That Oke and Orisha Oko are linguistically identical.
- That Oke was universally offered humans.
- That “Okê Arô” proves independent worship of Oke wherever Oxóssi is honored.
- That fixed colors, number eight, a weekday, astrological planet, or tarot correspondence are ancient pan-Yoruba doctrine.
- That Oke was worshipped in Atlantis or borrowed from an Egyptian mountain god.
- That contemporary polished online patakí are verbatim ancient scriptures.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Historical record |
|---|---|
| **Before c. 1800** | Sacred-hill traditions may be old, but no presently identified inscription or securely attributable image names Oke. Claims of prehistoric antiquity remain oral tradition or inference. |
| **Early nineteenth century** | Yoruba wars and displacement give Olumo Rock a historically reconstructable role as refuge and defense for the emerging Ẹ̀gbá settlement at Abeokuta. |
| **c. 1830 onward** | Abeokuta develops around Olumo; the rock becomes a central symbol of survival and a shrine of Oke Olumo. |
| **Nineteenth century** | Ibadan’s civic traditions consolidate around Oke-Ibadan/Àtàgé, Lagelu, refuge, fertility, and communal protection. |
| **Mid–late nineteenth century** | Christian and Islamic expansion challenges oriṣa cults. Mission journals begin producing written evidence about Yoruba religion, though no single known entry establishes the first Oke cult. |
| **1894** | A. B. Ellis publishes the earliest clear general description of Oke as mountain god and reports rockfall beliefs and an allegation of human sacrifice. |
| **1897** | Samuel Johnson completes the original manuscript of *The History of the Yorubas*; it is subsequently lost. |
| **1910** | Leo Frobenius excavates and removes or documents major artworks at Ifẹ̀. These discoveries reshape Yoruba art history but do not securely identify Oke iconography. |
| **1921** | Obadiah Johnson publishes his reconstruction of Samuel Johnson’s history. |
| **Early–mid twentieth century** | Oke survives in Cuban Lucumí traditions in several forms: independent mountain-owner, Oke-té/Santa Marta, identification with Orisha Oko, and close association with Obatalá. |
| **1937–38** | Bascom undertakes major Ifá fieldwork at Ifẹ̀. His publications later establish rigorous comparative documentation of the corpus but no universal Oke cycle. |
| **1954** | Lydia Cabrera publishes *El Monte*, foundational documentation of Afro-Cuban sacred nature and religious practice. |
| **1958** | Bastide publishes *Le Candomblé de Bahia*, an influential systematic account of Brazilian Candomblé. |
| **1961** | *Actas del Folklore* preserves comparative Cuban testimony about Oke, Oke-té, Orisha Oko, and regional Catholic identifications. |
| **1969** | Bascom publishes *Ifa Divination*. |
| **1976** | Wande Abimbola publishes *Ifá: An Exposition of Ifá Literary Corpus*. |
| **1980** | Bascom publishes *Sixteen Cowries*, important for understanding the variability and diaspora transmission of Yoruba divination narratives. |
| **Late twentieth century** | Olumo increasingly becomes a state-promoted tourist and heritage destination while retaining shrine functions. |
| **2001** | Prandi publishes *Mitologia dos Orixás*, giving wide circulation to Brazilian Oke creation narratives. |
| **2001/2003** | Peel publishes *Religious Encounter and the Making of the Yoruba*, reconstructing nineteenth-century religious transformation from CMS archives. |
| **2005** | Olatunji Ojo publishes a major historical study of slavery and human sacrifice in nineteenth-century Ondo, providing context for—but not direct corroboration of—Ellis’s Oke claim. |
| **2020–21** | Detailed modern ethnographic study of the Oke-Ibadan festival and the Aboke community appears in *Yoruba Studies Review*. |
| **2020s** | Oke receives renewed global visibility through Isese networks, Brazilian Efọn revival, Cuban digital religious publishing, and AI/digital iconography. Standardized online profiles often erase regional variation. |
| **Present** | Oke persists as local hill-divinities in Nigeria, a minor orisha/fundamento in Cuban practice, Olooke in some Brazilian houses, and a subject of reconstructionist devotion. No reliable global count exists. |

## 11. Sources

### Primary and early documentary sources

- A. B. Ellis, *The Yoruba-Speaking Peoples of the Slave Coast of West Africa* (1894), especially pp. 41 and 81–82. The digitized text supplies the earliest explicit description of Oke.
- Samuel Johnson, ed. Obadiah Johnson, *The History of the Yorubas from the Earliest Times to the Beginning of the British Protectorate* (1921; manuscript reconstructed from work completed in 1897).
- Leo Frobenius, *The Voice of Africa* (1913). Useful for the history of Ifẹ̀ archaeology and early twentieth-century European interpretation; not a secure primary source for Oke.
- Rómulo Lachatañeré and associated Cuban folklore publications, reprinted in *Actas del Folklore*. Important for regional Cuban distinctions involving Oke-té, Santa Marta, Orisha Oko, and Havana/Santiago practice.
- Lydia Cabrera, *El Monte* (1954). Foundational mid-twentieth-century Afro-Cuban ethnography.
- William Bascom, *Ifa Divination: Communication Between Gods and Men in West Africa* (1969).
- Wande Abimbola, *Ifá: An Exposition of Ifá Literary Corpus* (1976).
- William Bascom, *Sixteen Cowries: Yoruba Divination from Africa to the New World* (1980).
- Roger Bastide, *Le Candomblé de Bahia* (1958).
- Reginaldo Prandi, *Mitologia dos Orixás* (2001).

### Modern studies

- J. D. Y. Peel, *Religious Encounter and the Making of the Yoruba* (2000/2003).
- Olatunji Ojo, “Slavery and Human Sacrifice in Yorubaland: Ondo, c. 1870–94” (2005).

- Contemporary Cuban and Brazilian practitioner publications, used here only as evidence for current or recent tradition when no older provenance is supplied.
