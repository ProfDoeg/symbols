# Babalu-Aye (Shopona, Obaluaiê, Omolu): Research Dossier

**Class:** Òrìṣà (Yorùbá divine power/deity); in Fon and related Gbe traditions, a *vodun*; in Cuban Lucumí, Arará, Brazilian Candomblé, Batuque, and Umbanda, an orisha/orixá or vodum governing epidemic disease, bodily affliction, earth, death, and healing.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest presently identifiable, securely dated written notice under this name is the Reverend C. A. Gollmer’s report from Badagry in June 1853, published in the *Church Missionary Record/Gleaner* in 1854. Gollmer called **Sopona** “a new sort of Orisha, the god of smallpox” and described the deity being paraded through the streets. This is evidence for a public cult at Badagry in 1853—not evidence that the deity originated then. The cult and its orally transmitted theology must be older, but neither the Ilé-Ifẹ̀/Oyo archaeological record nor the Ifẹ̀ sculptural corpus currently supplies a securely identified pre-nineteenth-century image or inscription naming Ṣọ̀pọ̀nná.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** contemporary nineteenth- or early-twentieth-century documentary evidence.
- **[OR] Recorded oral tradition:** an oral text documented by a named collector; its recording date is known, but its age before recording usually is not.
- **[PS] Priestly/learned systematization:** theology organized by diviners, priests, or writers into a coherent scheme.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** locally or lineage-specifically attested tradition.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** historically formed equation with another deity or saint.
- **[LA] Late accretion:** development demonstrably later than the earliest records.
- **[F] Folklore/devotional tradition:** circulating belief whose earlier history cannot be established.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** inference proposed by historians, anthropologists, linguists, or art historians.
- **[MI] Modern invention/reformulation:** a recent doctrine or representation presented as new, or lacking evidence of comparable antiquity.
- **[U] Unestablished:** repeated claim for which adequate documentation has not been located.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Ṣọ̀pọ̀nná is the feared and propitiated Yorùbá controller of smallpox. The governing conception is double rather than contradictory: the power that sends an epidemic can withhold it, and the one who inflicts bodily eruption can also heal it. Nineteenth-century documentation stresses smallpox and dangerous vengeance; twentieth-century African and diaspora traditions broaden the domain to infectious and eruptive diseases, leprosy, wounds, disability, cemeteries, death, poverty, and general healing. **[EA→LA/RV]**

### Principal names

- **Ṣọ̀pọ̀nná / Ṣọ̀npọ̀nná / Ṣànpọ̀nná**: modern Yorùbá orthographies. Colonial spellings include **Sopona, Shopona, Shapona, Shonponno, Sonponno, Saponna**, and **Sanponna**. Tonal and consonantal variation reflects inconsistent nineteenth-century transcription as well as dialect. Its derivation is uncertain. The popular equation with the ordinary word for smallpox may reflect the deity’s tabooed identification with the disease rather than a recoverable historical etymology. **[EA/MSR]**
- **Ọbalúayé / Ọbalúwáiyé; Portuguese Obaluaiê/Obaluaê**: conventionally parsed as *ọba* (“king/ruler”) + *olú/olúwa* (“lord, eminent one”) + *ayé* (“earth/world”): “king who is lord of the earth/world.” Kọ́lá Abímbọ́lá gives the fuller underlying form **Ọba-olúwa-ayé**. **[PS]**
- **Bàbálú-Ayé / Babalú Ayé**: commonly glossed “father, lord of the earth/world,” especially prominent in Cuba and the wider Caribbean. Its treatment as simply the “original African name” is misleading: it is a title related to Ọbalúayé whose extraordinary prominence is diasporic. **[RV/SI]**
- **Ọmọlú / Omolu / Omulu**: often glossed *ọmọ Olú*, “child/son of the Lord.” In Brazil it may denote the deity generally, the old or afflicted manifestation, or a form distinct from a younger Obaluaiê. These distinctions vary by nation and house and must not be projected back as a single ancient Yorùbá taxonomy. **[RV/PS]**
- **Sakpata / Sagbata / Sapata / Xapanã**: Gbe-region and Afro-Brazilian forms. In Dahomey/Benin, Sakpata is a vodun of smallpox and earth with his own priesthood, myths, and political history. He is historically related to Ṣọ̀pọ̀nná but is not merely a differently spelled Yorùbá deity. **[EA/RV]**
- **Asojano / Asojuano / Azowano**: Cuban Arará names, often connected by scholars and practitioners with Gbe epithets such as *Azohani*. **[RV/MSR]**
- **Olóde**, “owner/lord of the outside”: reflects shrines and offerings placed outside settlements or in bush spaces. **[OR/PS]**
- **Baba**, “Father,” and circumlocutions such as “the owner of the earth,” “the hot earth,” or “the one whose name is not spoken”: euphemistic avoidance. **[OR/F]**
- **Alápá-dúpẹ́**, conventionally interpreted “one who kills and is thanked”: a praise-title expressing the obligation to acknowledge the deity even after death. The exact morphology and distribution require better primary linguistic documentation than most English summaries provide. **[OR/U]**
- **Atótó!**: Brazilian salutation generally understood as a command or request for silence—appropriate to a dangerous, secretive deity. Its exact historical derivation is disputed. **[RV/PS]**

### The unspeakable name

Idowu and later ethnographers record avoidance of Ṣọ̀pọ̀nná’s direct name because speaking it might attract the power it denotes. **[OR]** The practice explains the proliferation of titles, but the strongest popular formulation—“no Yorùbá ever spoke the name”—is too absolute. Missionaries, converts, priests, ethnographers, and devotees plainly recorded and uttered it. The evidence supports situational ritual avoidance, not universal linguistic impossibility.

### Classification shifts

Samuel Johnson’s manuscript, completed in the 1890s and published posthumously in 1921, called Sopona one of the “demons” infesting the world. That is Johnson’s Christian missionary classification, not neutral Yorùbá terminology. In the same account Sopona operates as a divine minister of punishment beside Ogun. **[EA]** Idowu’s *Olódùmarè* (1962) reclassified the figure within a systematic theology of subordinate *òrìṣà*. **[PS]** Lucumí calls him an orisha; Arará, a vodun/fodún; Candomblé, an orixá or, in Jeje houses, vodum; Umbanda frequently treats Omulu/Obaluaê as a divine “line,” “force,” or “throne.” **[RV/MI]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The 1853 Badagry notice

Gollmer’s 1853 report describes Sopona as a smallpox orisha publicly paraded at Badagry and calls the cult an importation from Porto-Novo. **[EA]** The report is valuable because it is contemporary, but its “new” language concerns Badagry, not the deity’s absolute origin. A later historical study observes that Gollmer may have confused Yorùbá Sopona with Porto-Novo Sakpata; alternatively, the episode may document movement of a related Gbe cult into a politically unsettled port. **[MSR]**

### CMS archive

The Church Missionary Society’s Yorùbá Mission archive begins in 1844 and contains journals, correspondence, précis, medical reports, and vernacular publications. These sources are indispensable but polemical: missionaries routinely translated *òrìṣà* as “devils,” interpreted sacrifice as “superstition,” and wrote to demonstrate Christianity’s necessity. **[EA/source criticism]** Peel and McKenzie use this material to reconstruct nineteenth-century religious practice while warning against treating missionary description as transparent ethnography.

### Samuel Johnson

Johnson’s *History of the Yorubas* was largely completed by 1897, lost in transit, reconstructed, and published in 1921. He describes:

- Sopona as smallpox personified or governed;
- a representation made from stripped palm branches, daubed with camwood;
- hot parched corn or beniseed cast on the object when vengeance was invoked;
- exclusive burial rights claimed by the deity’s devotees over smallpox victims;
- payments and goods demanded from victims’ families;
- animal offerings including tortoise, snail, fowl, pigeon, goat, armadillo, and “ground pig”;
- the suspicion that priests possessed a direct means of spreading infection;
- Sopona and Ogun as agents executing divine punishment. **[EA]**

Johnson is an invaluable Yorùbá author, but also an Anglican clergyman whose “demon” and “Satan” terminology belongs to Christian apologetic discourse.

### Ifá, cowrie divination, and praise poetry

The Ifá corpus consists of 256 named *odù*, each containing variable bodies of verses, narratives, prescriptions, songs, and sacrificial instructions. There is no single fixed “Book of Ifá.” Bascom’s *Ifa Divination* (1969), Abimbola’s *Ifá: An Exposition of Ifá Literary Corpus* (1976), later publications by Epega and Neimark, and Bascom’s *Sixteen Cowries* (1980) record different performers, towns, divinatory systems, and editorial choices. **[OR]**

Ṣọ̀pọ̀nná appears in divinatory material as a dangerous divine person, receiver of sacrifice, master of sores or epidemic affliction, and sometimes an exile punished for misconduct. **[OR]** A story assigned in modern circulating Ifá literature to **Ìrosùn-Ìrẹtẹ̀/Iroso Batrupon** says that divination was performed for Shopona, whose bad character caused people to hate hearing his name; he refused sacrifice, and Orunmila ceased defending him. Its accessible publication history is late and lineage-specific; it should not be called an “ancient canonical text.” **[RV/PS]**

*Oríkì* praise-poems emphasize heat, earth, eruption, fearful kingship, the deity’s capacity to kill, and the paradox of thanking him. Each performance is potentially recomposed. An undated online “oríkì Ọbalúayé” is not evidence of antiquity unless tied to a collector, performer, place, and recording date. **[OR/source warning]**

### Dahomean documentation

Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century European observers document devastating smallpox in Dahomey but do not always name Sakpata. Snelgrave described King Agaja as pockmarked in the 1730s. Le Hérissé’s *L’ancien royaume du Dahomey* (1911) recorded traditions that the cult entered Dahomey from Dassa or Mahi country. Herskovits’s *Dahomey* (1938) recorded Sakpata as an earth and smallpox vodun with a complex divine family. **[EA/OR]**

The competing origin claims are irreducible:

- Sakpata priests’ traditions made Mahi country the source. **[OR]**
- Le Hérissé reported introduction through Dassa, whose people were related to Egba Yorùbá and worshipped a comparable Sanponna. **[OR]**
- Political historians note intense Oyo–Dahomey interaction and possible Yorùbá transmission. **[MSR]**
- Other Brazilian and popular accounts reverse the direction and describe Omolu as a Dahomean deity assimilated by the Yorùbá. **[PS/U]**

The evidence supports a historically entangled disease-cult complex across Yorùbá and Gbe-speaking regions, not a securely proved single point of origin.

### Diaspora corpora

- Fernando Ortiz’s early-twentieth-century Afro-Cuban studies documented Lucumí and Arará practice but employed evolutionary and criminological assumptions now rejected. **[EA/source criticism]**
- Lydia Cabrera’s *El Monte* (1954) preserves extensive Cuban testimony, ritual language, plants, stories, and identifications; it is an ethnographic-literary compilation, not a transcription of an ancient canon. **[OR]**
- Roger Bastide’s *Le Candomblé de Bahia* (1958) analyzed Bahian ritual organization and African “nations.” **[MSR]**
- Pierre Verger’s *Notes sur le culte des orisa et vodun* (1957) and *Orixás* compared West African and Bahian practices using photographs and fieldwork. **[OR/MSR]**
- Reginaldo Prandi’s *Mitologia dos Orixás* (2001) compiled 301 myths from published African and American sources. Its numbered Omolu/Obaluaiê narratives preserve documented variants, but the compilation itself is modern and deliberately cross-regional. **[PS]**

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

1. **Smallpox:** the earliest consistently documented specialization. **[EA]**
2. **Eruptive and contagious disease generally:** measles, chickenpox, sores, skin disease, fever, leprosy, and tuberculosis appear in twentieth-century accounts. **[LA/RV]**
3. **Healing and protection:** a logical and ritually documented counterpart to affliction; priests diagnosed, isolated, buried, variolated, or treated sufferers. **[EA/OR]**
4. **Earth:** strongly developed in the Ọbalúayé and Sakpata complexes. In Gbe theology Sakpata belongs to an earth pantheon; in Brazil the earth, cemetery, decomposition, and return of the body become central. **[RV]**
5. **Poverty, disability, exile, and social abandonment:** especially prominent through the Cuban wounded Lazarus image. **[SI/LA]**
6. **HIV/AIDS and modern epidemics:** a late extension documented in contemporary devotion and art, not a precolonial attribute. **[LA]**

### Historical iconography

Johnson’s stripped palm-branch broom smeared with camwood is the earliest detailed representation securely tied to Sopona. **[EA]** This differs sharply from the anthropomorphic carved “Shapona statues” now reproduced in museums and books.

The CDC Museum possesses wooden assemblages commissioned in 1969 near Abeokuta, including a figure layered with cowries, fur, and animal remains. Kimberly Morris argues that such anthropomorphic “Shapona figures” were produced partly for foreign public-health workers and collectors. They therefore cannot automatically be treated as old shrine types. **[MSR/MI]**

### Brazilian form

Omolu/Obaluaiê in Candomblé is recognizable by:

- the **azé/azê**, a hood and skirt of raffia or *palha-da-costa* concealing the head and body;
- the **xaxará**, a hand-held ritual whisk or broom of palm or straw, often decorated with cowries and gourds;
- cowries, small gourds, beads, and dark or earth-toned materials;
- ritual popcorn, **doburu/deburú**, interpreted as eruptions, healed sores, or “flowers of the old man”;
- slow, bent, trembling, or earth-directed dance;
- associations with cemetery ground and the outside of the central sacred room. **[RV]**

The story that the hood conceals smallpox scars is widespread, but its presentation as universal African antiquity is unwarranted. Raffia is especially tied to Gbe/Jeje Sakpata and Afro-Brazilian ritual history. **[RV/MSR]**

### Cuban form

Lucumí/Arará vessels are frequently earthenware or gourds with perforations, cowries, sticks, medicines, and raffia. The perforations are compared to skin lesions. **[RV]** Popular Cuban Catholic imagery instead shows the poor Lazarus: an elderly or disabled man in rags, using crutches, covered in sores, accompanied by dogs. That image derives from Luke 16’s beggar Lazarus, not Lazarus of Bethany and not an ancient Yorùbá prototype. **[SI]**

### Colors, days, numbers, animals

Lists assigning purple, brown, black, white, red-black combinations, Monday, Wednesday, Friday, or December 17 vary by house and nation. Likewise numerical correspondences such as 7, 13, or 17 are not pan-Yorùbá constants. **[RV]**

Dogs are securely present in Johnson’s animal-offering list and become iconographically dominant in Cuba through the biblical dogs licking Lazarus’s sores. Guinea fowl, goats, fowl, snails, tortoises, and pigeons occur in different sacrificial systems. **[EA/RV]**

## 4. Genealogy and relations

No single securely early genealogy survives.

- In one recorded divine-government account, the supreme deity sits with Orisha-nla and Ifa while Ogun and Sopona execute punishment. This supplies office, not parentage. **Johnson manuscript, c.1890s; [EA].**
- In Dahomean theology recorded by Herskovits, Sakpata belongs to the earth pantheon and may be made a child of Mawu-Lisa. **1930s fieldwork; [OR/RV].**
- In widely circulated Candomblé myths, Nanã is Omolu’s biological mother; she abandons or hides him because he is diseased or ugly, and Iemanjá/Yemọja raises him. **Twentieth-century collections; [OR/RV].**
- Some variants make Oxalá/Oxalufã his father. **[RV]**
- Ossaim/Ọ̀sányìn, Oxumarê, and Euá may be his siblings as children of Nanã. This family is especially prominent in Brazilian systematization and should not be treated as a securely attested pan-Yorùbá genealogy. **[RV/PS]**
- Cuban Lucumí and Arará houses give differing parents, spouses, siblings, roads, and initiatory affiliations. **[RV]**

### Documented divine interactions

The following entries record interactions found in identifiable documentary or compiled oral sources. Prandi’s 2001 compilation draws from heterogeneous twentieth-century collections; its stories are not all mutually compatible.

- **Olódùmarè/Olorun:** assigns or recognizes Ṣọ̀pọ̀nná’s punitive terrestrial function in later systematic accounts. Idowu, 1962. **[PS]**
- **Orisha-nla/Ọbàtálá:** appears as divine counsellor while Sopona executes judgment; Johnson, manuscript c.1890s/published 1921. **[EA]**
- **Ifá/Ọ̀rúnmìlà:** appears as counsellor in Johnson’s divine court; in an Ifá story he refuses to defend Shopona after the latter rejects sacrifice. Johnson 1921; late-recorded Ifá variant. **[EA/RV]**
- **Ogun:** jointly serves as an agent of divine vengeance; Johnson 1921. **[EA]**
- **Nanã:** gives birth to, abandons, hides, punishes, or later receives Omolu, depending on the Brazilian story. Verger and later Prandi collection, twentieth century. **[RV]**
- **Yemọja/Iemanjá:** rescues or adopts the abandoned child and later gives him pearls, explaining the title “lord of pearls.” Afro-Brazilian variants collected in the twentieth century; Prandi 2001. **[RV]**
- **Oyá/Iansã:** at a divine feast blows away or transforms his covering; his sores become popcorn or his beauty is revealed. Other versions say he grants Oyá authority over the dead. Prandi 2001 and related Candomblé narration. **[RV]**
- **Ogun:** makes or provides the raffia covering in some Brazilian variants when the afflicted deity cannot attend the orishas’ gathering. Twentieth-century Brazilian oral tradition. **[RV]**
- **Oxum:** persuades the divine powers to restore Omolu after his death, or petitions for his resurrection. Prandi 2001 compilation. **[RV/LA]**
- **Oxumarê:** appears as sibling in the Nanã family complex. Joint episode beyond genealogy varies by collection. **[RV]**
- **Ossaim:** appears as sibling and as possessor of herbal knowledge relevant to curing disease; explicit joint narratives are less consistently recorded. **[RV; absence of secure early interaction]**
- **Euá/Yewá:** appears as sibling in Brazilian genealogical arrangements; no securely early narrative interaction located. **[RV]**
- **Xangô:** appears with Omolu in divine gatherings in Brazilian narratives, but no stable early antagonism or alliance was found. **[RV/U]**
- **Exu:** mediates sacrifice and divination in the general Ifá/cowrie framework; a specific early Ṣọ̀pọ̀nná–Exu episode was not securely located. **[U]**
- **Iku (Death):** contemporary theology places Babalú-Ayé close to Iku because he can deliver or withhold sufferers from death. This is an association rather than a securely early narrative encounter. **[PS/LA]**
- **Mawu-Lisa:** parents or sovereigns of Sakpata in Dahomean accounts; Herskovits, 1930s. **[OR/RV]**
- **San Lázaro:** not an interaction within an old myth but a Cuban historical identification joining the orisha to both biblical Lazaruses. **[SI]**
- **São Roque/Saint Roch:** Brazilian identification based on plague-protection and bodily sores. **[SI]**
- **Saint Sebastian:** documented in some Brazilian regional traditions, including Recife, through the saint’s wounded body. **[SI/RV]**

No securely dated precolonial written source supplies an exhaustive divine family or canonical interaction cycle. Claims that every named orisha participated in one fixed primordial court are modern harmonization.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The disobedient or beautiful sufferer

Omolu is born beautiful or becomes proud, violates a prohibition, attends a forbidden celebration, or disobeys his mother. Smallpox or sores cover him; he becomes lame, ashamed, and isolated. **[RV]** The moral emphasis varies: disobedience, sexual excess, failure to sacrifice, arrogance, or the dangers of exclusion. These versions are chiefly twentieth-century African-diaspora recordings, not securely datable ancient texts.

### Abandonment by Nanã and rescue by Iemanjá

Nanã gives birth to a diseased child and abandons him beside water, in mud, or on a beach. Crabs or other creatures injure him. Iemanjá finds, heals, and raises him. **[RV]** The story integrates a Gbe-associated elder mother, a Brazilian maritime Iemanjá, and the disease deity. It is a particularly clear example of diaspora pantheon-building rather than recoverable biography.

### The feast and Iansã’s wind

The orishas assemble, but the covered and scarred Omolu stays apart or is mocked. Ogun supplies a raffia garment in some versions. Iansã raises a wind that lifts the straw; his wounds burst into luminous popcorn or reveal a beautiful body. He thereafter shares the feast and, in some versions, gives Iansã dominion over cemetery spirits. **[RV/LA]** Popcorn’s ritual centrality and the myth mutually explain one another; the direction of borrowing cannot be proved.

### Exile and return

Sonponno breaks a social or ritual prohibition and is expelled. He wanders hungry, lame, or covered in sores, carrying disease wherever he goes. After humility, sacrifice, or healing, he returns with control over affliction. **[OR/RV]** Harold Courlander’s *Tales of Yoruba Gods and Heroes* preserves a literary twentieth-century version; it is a retelling based on oral sources, not an ancient manuscript.

### Division of divine powers

When powers are distributed, Ṣọ̀pọ̀nná receives disease, the earth, or the secret of pestilence. One narrative explains pocks as the grains of the earth emerging through human skin: the eldest son receives earth and grain, while Shango receives the sky. This was recorded during the twentieth-century eradication campaign. **[OR/PS]**

### Conquest of Dahomey and acquisition of cult

Prandi lists stories in which Obaluaiê conquers Dahomey, Sakpata becomes king among the Jeje, or Xapanã gains worship among the Yorùbá. **[OR/RV]** These are mythic charters for historical transmission. They do not independently resolve whether the cult moved from Yorùbá territory to Dahomey, the reverse, or repeatedly in both directions.

### Death and resurrection

In one Brazilian cycle Obaluaiê dies and is restored after Oxum intervenes. **[RV/LA]** The motif resembles broader African ideas of earth, death, and renewal and may have gained additional resonance in Christian Brazil. Direct Christian borrowing is possible but not demonstrated merely by resemblance.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Shrines and cult centers

No monumental “Temple of Ṣọ̀pọ̀nná” comparable to a classical stone temple has been securely excavated. Shrines were often perishable, located in compounds, groves, outside town, or near isolation and burial areas. Archaeological claims that particular ancient Ilé-Ifẹ̀ deposits prove Ṣọ̀pọ̀nná worship are presently unestablished. **[U]**

Johnson records a broom-like shrine object and burial privileges. **[EA]** Twentieth-century evidence documents shrines at Abeokuta and across southwestern Nigeria, Dahomey/Benin, and Togo. CDC observers in the late 1960s reported villages and an Abomey shrine associated with a hereditary priesthood. **[EA]**

### Priesthood and epidemic authority

Priests handled bodies and belongings that others feared to touch, performed burial, levied ritual fees, propitiated the deity, prescribed medicines, and in some areas practiced variolation. **[EA]** Their authority was simultaneously religious, medical, mortuary, and economic.

Colonial observers accused some priests of creating cases to enlarge mortuary payments. Johnson repeated the allegation; Dr. Oguntola Sapara later claimed that infected scabs or fluids were applied to victims. **[EA/allegation]** Variola scabs were also used in controlled variolation, a practice capable of immunizing but also transmitting disease. Colonial reports often failed to distinguish preventive inoculation from assault or accidental spread.

### Offerings

Documented offerings include palm oil, camwood, parched corn, sesame/beniseed, dogs, goats, fowl, pigeons, snails, tortoises, and other animals. **[EA/RV]** Brazilian offerings prominently include popcorn, beans, maize preparations, palm oil, and foods placed on earth or in marginal locations. **[RV]**

No source located securely documents human sacrifice as a distinctive, regular offering to Ṣọ̀pọ̀nná. Human sacrifice occurred in some nineteenth-century Yorùbá and Dahomean political, funerary, and religious contexts, but transferring general evidence to this cult would be unsound. **[absence finding]**

### Disease control

Recent scholarship argues that sacred groves and extra-urban ritual spaces could function as isolation zones, while priests removed infectious bodies and possessions. **[MSR]** These practices should not be romanticized as equivalent to germ theory, but neither should the cult be reduced to obstruction. It could isolate, treat, variolate, terrify, exact wealth, and resist colonial vaccination—sometimes simultaneously.

### Brazilian ritual

In Bahian Candomblé, Omolu/Obaluaiê dances under raffia with the xaxará; popcorn may be passed over bodies in cleansing rites. The annual **Olubajé**, a collective meal associated with Obaluaiê, distributes foods of multiple orishas and dramatizes healing, community, and exchange. Details vary by nation and house. **[RV]**

### Cuban ritual and pilgrimage

Lucumí and Arará rites distinguish roads or forms of Babalú-Ayé/Asojano and maintain consecrated vessels whose contents are initiatory secrets. **[RV]** Public devotion concentrates on **16–17 December** at the Santuario Nacional de San Lázaro in El Rincón near Havana. Pilgrims walk barefoot, crawl, wear sackcloth, drag stones, carry candles or images, and fulfill vows for healing. Tens of thousands have participated, including during the officially atheist decades after the Cuban Revolution. **[EA/SI]**

The church’s official image is Lazarus the bishop, but popular devotion most strongly identifies Babalú-Ayé with Luke’s wounded beggar Lazarus. Lazarus of Bethany, the beggar, the bishop-saint, and the orisha coexist without forming a tidy theological identity.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1853: reconstructable but undated

Smallpox was long present in West Africa, and the interlocking Yorùbá/Gbe cult complex almost certainly predates its first European-language notice. **[MSR]** No securely named inscription, dated image, or archaeological cult object currently carries Ṣọ̀pọ̀nná back to ancient Ifẹ̀ or Old Oyo. Claims of millennial antiquity are plausible as general oral-cultural continuity but unproved for this named deity.

### 1853–1890s: documentary emergence

Gollmer saw the public procession at Badagry in 1853. Recurrent epidemics struck Lagos and inland Yorùbá communities later in the century. Missionaries described appeasement of Sopona while portraying the cult as demonic competition. Johnson documented shrine form, burial economy, and accusations of induced infection. **[EA]**

### Dahomean nineteenth century

Smallpox affected royal bodies and warfare: accounts describe rulers pitted or killed by the disease and armies forced to retreat. Sakpata’s priests acquired substantial authority, creating tension with royal centralization and later French public-health campaigns. **[EA/OR]** The cult’s prominence did not mean every outbreak was accepted passively; botanical treatment and prophylaxis coexisted with divine causation.

### Colonial suppression: 1907, 1909, or 1917?

The often-repeated single date “the British banned Shopona worship in 1907” compresses several developments.

- **1907:** repeated by the CDC Museum and much popular literature as the ban date. **[secondary tradition]**
- **1909:** recent historical scholarship identifies an anti-smallpox law under which Sopona worship and resistance to vaccination became illegal. **[EA/legal-historical claim]**
- **1917:** Nigerian biographical literature connects the Witchcraft and Juju Ordinance or its application against the cult with Sapara’s disclosures. **[secondary/legal claim]**

The safest conclusion is that **suppression was a process spanning the first two decades of the twentieth century**, involving investigation, public-health regulation, anti-“juju” law, and enforcement. Without the exact gazetted ordinance text, 1907 should not be repeated as an uncontested single event.

Dr. **Oguntola Sapara**, a Yorùbá physician appointed at Epe in 1896, reportedly entered the cult covertly and concluded that some priests used material from smallpox victims to spread infection. **[EA but transmitted through later biographies and reports]** The central allegation is medically possible and appears in both Johnson and Sapara traditions, but colonial officials had institutional reasons to portray a powerful indigenous medical corporation as criminal. It should therefore be reported as a documented accusation supported by named observers, not as proof that the cult universally practiced deliberate infection.

Vaccination politics involved more than “superstition versus science.” Compulsory measures, distrust of colonial administration, variable vaccine effectiveness and storage, indigenous variolation, priestly economic interests, and different theories of causation all affected acceptance. **[MSR]**

### Atlantic transformations

Enslaved and free Africans from Yorùbá, Fon, Ewe, Mahi, and related populations carried disease cults to Cuba and Brazil. The resulting forms were not frozen African survivals:

- Cuba joined Lucumí Babalú-Ayé, Arará Asojano, folk Catholic San Lázaro, healing vows, and a major pilgrimage. **[SI]**
- Bahia joined Yorùbá Obaluaiê/Omolu with Jeje Sakpata forms, raffia iconography, popcorn rites, cemetery symbolism, and São Lázaro/São Roque. **[SI/RV]**
- Rio Grande do Sul Batuque commonly retained **Xapanã**. **[RV]**
- Trinidad Orisha retained Shakpana/Ṣakpanna as a disease and healing power. **[RV]**

### Eradication and survival

WHO and CDC campaigns in the 1960s encountered resistance from some Sakpata/Ṣọ̀pọ̀nná communities, especially in Dahomey, Togo, and western Nigeria. Public-health teams also negotiated with local authorities and indigenous vaccinators. **[EA]** WHO declared smallpox eradicated in 1980. The deity did not disappear: his domain expanded to other epidemics, chronic disease, disability, HIV/AIDS, bodily vulnerability, and general healing. **[LA]**

### Argentina and transnational expansion

Brazilian Umbanda and Batuque spread to Uruguay and Argentina from the 1960s, with rapid Argentine growth in the early 1980s. Frigerio and Carozzi show that this was institutional migration through temples, initiatory networks, public festivals, and mass media—not an isolated survival from slavery in Argentina. Omulu/Obaluaê or Xapanã traveled within those Brazilian religious formations. **[EA/MSR]**

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### “Babalú” and mass entertainment

Margarita Lecuona wrote the Afro-Cuban song **“Babalú”**, first popularized by Miguelito Valdés around 1939–41. Desi Arnaz recorded it in 1946 and made it his signature performance on stage, film, and *I Love Lucy*. **[EA]** Its invocatory lyrics and percussion refer to Babalú-Ayé, but Arnaz’s theatricalized performance detached the song from ritual community and helped transform the divine name into a North American entertainment catchphrase. Critics have accordingly read it as both Afro-Cuban cultural transmission and commercialization.

### Visual arts and museums

The CDC’s 1969 commissioned figures became icons of smallpox eradication. Their status is paradoxical: genuine works by Yorùbá religious specialists, but apparently shaped for foreign medical patrons. **[MI/MSR]** They document the encounter between cult, collecting, and global health rather than an unchanged ancient iconographic canon.

Brazilian artists, including Carybé and later photographers and performance artists, made Omolu’s raffia body a major image of Afro-Brazilian religion. Contemporary art connects the covered body with racialized illness, AIDS, secrecy, vulnerability, and transformation. **[LA]**

### Umbanda theology

Older Umbanda and Candomblé discourse often treats Omulu and Obaluaê as names or ages of one being. Rubens Saraceni’s **Umbanda Sagrada**, systematized through teaching and publication from the 1990s, separated them more sharply into cosmic “thrones” or energetic functions—Obaluaê as evolution, Omulu as paralysing generation/death in some formulations. **[MI]** This theology is a documented modern Brazilian innovation, not a securely ancient Yorùbá doctrine.

Likewise, chakra correspondences, precise colored candles, “vibrational rays,” karmic evolution, and geometrical *pontos riscados* belong to modern Umbanda, Spiritist, esoteric, or New Age synthesis. **[MI]**

### Occult and neopagan appropriation

English-language occult websites increasingly detach Babalú-Ayé from initiation and reconstruct him as an archetype of trauma, “shadow healing,” disability, or technological collapse. A contemporary “Temple of Gu,” for example, presents him as a techno-animist “Lord of Sacred Vulnerability and Restorative Mercy.” **[MI]** Such reinterpretations may be sincere new religions, but they are not evidence for precolonial theology.

No substantial independent European ceremonial-magic, Renaissance Hermetic, Golden Dawn, or medieval grimoire tradition of Babalú-Ayé exists. Claims to the contrary are modern borrowing from living African-diaspora religions. **[absence finding]**

### Politics and public religion

Colonial governments portrayed the cult as an impediment to vaccination; postcolonial public-health narratives sometimes made the eradicated deity a symbol of science’s triumph. Conversely, contemporary African and diaspora practitioners interpret his survival as evidence that an orisha is more than the pathogen once most closely associated with him. Both are historically situated political readings.

No significant extremist appropriation specific to Babalú-Ayé was located. **[absence finding]**

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debates

### Origin: Yorùbá or Gbe?

The evidence does not justify a single answer.

- Gollmer’s 1853 description suggests movement from Porto-Novo to Badagry.
- Le Hérissé’s informants attributed Dahomean Sakpata to Dassa/Egba-related sources.
- Mahi priests claimed Mahi origin.
- Oyo–Dahomey domination, warfare, intermarriage, enslavement, and trade enabled movement in both directions.
- Brazilian “Nagô” and “Jeje” institutions later recombined these materials.

“Ṣọ̀pọ̀nná = Sakpata = Omolu” is therefore useful as a map of historical equivalence but false if it implies an unchanging singular identity. **[MSR]**

### Disease god or earth god?

Older summaries call him simply the smallpox god. Scholars of Sakpata emphasize earth sovereignty, burial, fertility, grain, and political territory. Other researchers caution that defining Sakpata primarily as a fertility god underplays the cult’s specific formation around smallpox. The safest formulation is historically layered: epidemic authority is early and central; earth sovereignty is also old in the Gbe complex; generalized fertility and “transformation” receive greater emphasis in later systems.

### Criminal priests or indigenous public-health specialists?

Colonial testimony documents allegations of extortion and deliberate infection. Other evidence shows corpse removal, isolation, pharmacology, and variolation. Neither “murderous cult” nor “misunderstood epidemiologists” accounts for the whole institution. Priests could possess genuine prophylactic expertise while some individuals exploited fear or spread infection. The surviving archive disproportionately represents missionaries, colonial officials, and biomedical doctors, so the priests’ own voices remain fragmentary.

### Archaeology and images

The Ifẹ̀ heads and Oyo archaeology illuminate Yorùbá urbanism, kingship, and ritual history, but no famous Ifẹ̀ head has been securely identified as Ṣọ̀pọ̀nná. **[absence finding]** Modern illustrations that place the deity in an “ancient Yorùbá” raffia costume frequently project Candomblé imagery backward.

### The Ifá problem

Bascom, Abimbola, Epega, Neimark, and contemporary priests do not transmit identical corpora. Verses may be genuine oral performances without being universally known, chronologically ancient, or fixed to only one *odù*. Publication creates apparent canons from locally selected material. A responsible dossier must cite performer, collector, locality, and date rather than saying merely “Ifá says.”

### Lost and surviving evidence

Relevant holdings include:

- Church Mission Society papers for the Yorùbá Mission, 1844–1934, at the Cadbury Research Library, University of Birmingham;
- nineteenth-century CMS periodicals and *Ìwé Ìròhìn*;
- Nigerian colonial medical and legal archives;
- ethnographic fieldnotes and photographs of Verger, Herskovits, Cabrera, Bascom, and later scholars;
- ritual objects in Nigerian, Brazilian, Cuban, European, and North American collections;
- CDC Museum Shapona figures commissioned in 1969.

Secrecy, perishable shrine media, tropical deterioration, colonial removal, conversion, and the oral nature of the corpus all limit reconstruction.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before the nineteenth century:** A smallpox/earth cult complex develops somewhere within the interconnected Yorùbá–Gbe region. Exact date and direction of transmission are unestablished. **[MSR]**
- **1730s:** Snelgrave reports Dahomean King Agaja as pockmarked; this documents smallpox, not explicitly Sakpata worship. **[EA]**
- **Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries:** Traditions place Sakpata’s establishment in Dahomey through Mahi or Dassa/Yorùbá channels. **[OR]**
- **1853:** Reverend Gollmer sees Sopona, “god of smallpox,” paraded at Badagry and calls the cult a new import from Porto-Novo. **[EA]**
- **1854:** Gollmer’s report appears in CMS publication. **[EA]**
- **1869:** Major documented smallpox outbreak at Lagos. **[EA]**
- **1884:** Severe outbreak at Abeokuta. **[EA]**
- **c.1890s:** Samuel Johnson completes his reconstructed *History*, including a substantial description of Sopona’s shrine, offerings, burial rights, and alleged dissemination of disease. **[EA]**
- **1896–97:** Oguntola Sapara takes medical charge at Epe and reportedly begins investigating the cult. **[EA/biographical tradition]**
- **1907:** Later CDC and popular accounts date a British ban to this year. The exact legal act requires qualification. **[secondary claim]**
- **1909:** Anti-smallpox legislation makes Sopona cult practice associated with vaccination resistance illegal, according to recent historical scholarship. **[EA]**
- **1911:** Le Hérissé publishes Dahomean traditions about Sakpata and Dassa/Yorùbá connections. **[OR]**
- **1917:** Some Nigerian accounts date decisive anti-cult ordinance or enforcement to this year. **[disputed chronology]**
- **1921:** Johnson’s *History of the Yorubas* is published posthumously. **[EA]**
- **1930s:** Herskovits records Sakpata theology in Dahomey; Afro-Cuban religious music makes “Babalú” internationally audible. **[OR/EA]**
- **1939–41:** Miguelito Valdés popularizes Margarita Lecuona’s “Babalú.” **[EA]**
- **1946:** Desi Arnaz records and films “Babalú,” later carrying it into American television culture. **[EA]**
- **1954:** Lydia Cabrera publishes *El Monte*. **[OR]**
- **1957:** Pierre Verger publishes comparative notes on orisha and vodun cults. **[MSR/OR]**
- **1958:** Roger Bastide publishes *Le Candomblé de Bahia*. **[MSR]**
- **1962:** E. Bolaji Idowu publishes *Olódùmarè*, systematizing Yorùbá theology. **[PS]**
- **1960s:** Umbanda and Batuque expand from Brazil into Uruguay and Argentina. **[EA]**
- **1967–70:** WHO/CDC eradication teams encounter active Shapona/Sakpata priesthoods and vaccination resistance in West Africa. **[EA]**
- **1969:** Figures are commissioned near Abeokuta for foreign eradication personnel; Bascom publishes *Ifa Divination*. **[EA]**
- **1976:** Wande Abimbola publishes a major exposition of the Ifá literary corpus. **[PS]**
- **1980:** WHO declares smallpox eradicated; Bascom publishes *Sixteen Cowries*. **[EA]**
- **Early 1980s:** Afro-Brazilian religions undergo rapid public expansion in Argentina. **[EA]**
- **1990s:** Rubens Saraceni systematizes Umbanda Sagrada, including new distinctions between Omulu and Obaluaê. **[MI]**
- **2001:** Reginaldo Prandi publishes *Mitologia dos Orixás*, assembling numerous African and diaspora Omolu/Obaluaiê narratives. **[PS]**
- **Twenty-first century:** Devotion continues in Nigeria, Benin, Togo, Cuba, Brazil, Trinidad, the United States, Argentina, Uruguay, and transnational initiatory networks. HIV/AIDS, chronic disease, disability, and pandemic vulnerability become additional fields of interpretation. **[LA]**
- **Every 16–17 December:** The Rincón pilgrimage publicly renews Cuban San Lázaro/Babalú-Ayé devotion. **[EA/SI]**

## 11. Sources and assessment

The evidentiary hierarchy for this dossier is:

1. dated nineteenth-century CMS and Yorùbá documentary sources;
2. colonial legal and medical records, read critically;
3. named ethnographic collections with known fieldwork dates;
4. recorded Ifá, cowrie, *oríkì*, Lucumí, Arará, Candomblé, and Vodun testimony;
5. comparative historical scholarship;
6. practitioner theology for present-day belief;
7. popular and occult sources only as evidence of modern reception.

The most important negative findings are equally significant:

- no securely identified ancient or medieval inscription names Ṣọ̀pọ̀nná;
- no Ifẹ̀ head is securely identifiable as this deity;
- no single canonical genealogy exists;
- no single fixed Ifá text supplies a complete biography;
- no distinctive regular human-sacrifice cult was established;
- “1907” is not a sufficiently secure stand-alone date for suppression;
- the raffia-covered Brazilian image must not automatically be projected into remote Yorùbá antiquity;
- Golden Dawn, Renaissance Hermetic, and medieval grimoire traditions do not contain Babalú-Ayé;
- modern energetic, chakra, or “divine throne” systems are modern Umbanda or esoteric constructions.
