# The Rephaim: Research Dossier

**Class:** A collective, not a single god. In Late Bronze Age Ugaritic sources the *Rāpiʾūma* are an exalted company of dead or death-associated heroes, especially royal ancestors, who can be called “divine ones” and participate in divine banquets. First-millennium Phoenician inscriptions treat the *rpʾm* as the community of the dead. Biblical texts preserve two overlapping but deliberately diminished forms: primordial peoples or giant warriors conquered before Israel, and powerless shades in Sheol.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The Ugaritic consonantal plural **rpʾum** (𐎗𐎔𐎛𐎎) on alphabetic cuneiform tablets from Ras Shamra/Ugarit, principally KTU 1.20–1.22, copied in the thirteenth century BCE and conventionally associated with the scribal milieu of ʾIlimilku. These tablets invite the *Rāpiʾūma* to a feast. No presently secure older reference names this collective unambiguously.

**Central evidentiary conclusion:** The secure record does not warrant presenting “the Rephaim” as one anthropomorphic deity with a biography, temple, fixed iconography, or universal genealogy. It documents a category whose meanings moved among royal ancestors, heroic or semi-divine dead, the dead generally, legendary pre-Israelite populations, and giant warriors. Whether these are developments of one institution or partially independent homonyms remains disputed.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Names and scripts

- **Ugaritic:** **rpʾum**, alphabetic cuneiform 𐎗𐎔𐎛𐎎, conventionally vocalized *Rāpiʾūma*, *Rāpaʾūma*, or *Rapiʾūma*. The final *-m* is the Ugaritic plural ending, not part of the root. Singular **rpʾu**, *Rāpiʾu/Rapaʾu*, occurs as a named or titular figure in KTU 1.108.
- **Phoenician and Punic:** **rpʾm**, 𐤓𐤐𐤀𐤌, the dead or infernal community. Phoenician, like Ugaritic, preserves the consonant *ʾ*.
- **Biblical Hebrew:** **רְפָאִים**, *rĕpāʾîm*. The same consonantal form designates both legendary peoples and the shades of the dead. The singular or collective **הָרָפָה**, *hā-rāfāh*, “the Rapha/the giant,” occurs in the Philistine-warrior traditions of 2 Samuel 21 and 1 Chronicles 20.
- **Greek Septuagint:** usually **γίγαντες**, “giants,” including in several underworld passages where modern translations prefer “shades”; elsewhere transliterated forms such as **Ραφαϊν** occur.
- **Latin Vulgate:** commonly *gigantes* for the ancient peoples and sometimes *mortui* for the dead.
- **English:** Rephaim, Repha’im, Rephaïm, Rephaites; Ugaritic specialists commonly retain *Rāpiʾūma* or *Rapiuma*.

There is no ancient evidence that “Rephaim” was the proper name of one god. “Rapiu,” however, can function as an individual divine or ancestral name/title.

### Etymology: unresolved

Three principal explanations remain in debate:

1. **“Healers” from West Semitic rpʾ, “heal.”**  
   The Ugaritic form can be parsed as an active participle, “healers.” The Göttingen edition of the Ugaritic poetic corpus accepts this derivation as plausible. It gains contextual support from KTU 1.108, where Rapiu possesses protective or beneficent power, and from the wider possibility that powerful ancestors aided descendants. Greek translators rendered Hebrew *rĕpāʾîm* as **ἰατροί**, “physicians,” in Isaiah 26:14 and Psalm 88:11 LXX, which may preserve a wordplay or an older interpretation. It is not proof that healing was the collective’s primary cultic function: no Ugaritic text explicitly depicts the *rpʾum* curing a patient.

2. **“Weak, feeble, limp” from Hebrew rph/rpʾ.**  
   This suits the powerless dead of Isaiah 14 and other biblical poetry. Some scholars understand the biblical vocalization as a polemical wordplay: beings remembered elsewhere as mighty are recast as “weak ones.” The shift between final *ʾ* and *h* and the existence of the Ugaritic and Phoenician spellings make a simple derivation uncertain.

3. **“Great/chiefs,” connected with Akkadian *rabû/rabiʾu*.**  
   This has been proposed to explain royal and warrior associations but is phonologically and lexically less secure than the West Semitic alternatives. It remains a modern hypothesis, not an ancient gloss.

The common popular claim that *Rephaim* straightforwardly means “giants” is therefore misleading. “Giants” is an ancient interpretive translation, firmly established by the Septuagint and encouraged by biblical descriptions of exceptional stature, but it is not a demonstrated etymology.

### Documented descriptions and titles

- **rpʾum / rpʾim:** the collective itself.
- **ʾilnym**, “divine ones”: parallel to the Rephaim in KTU 1.6 vi 45–49 and applied in the Rephaim material. This marks divine status or participation, though not necessarily equality with major gods.
- **mtm**, “dead” or “men”: paired with the company in the closing hymn of the Baal Cycle. The ambiguity is significant: the same consonants can sustain interpretations emphasizing human heroes or the dead.
- **rpi arṣ**, approximately “Rapiu/Rephaim of the earth,” where *arṣ* may mean the terrestrial world or, more probably in these contexts, the underworld.
- **qbṣ dtn/ddn**, “assembly/clan of Ditanu/Didanu”: KTU 1.15 and 1.161.
- **rpʾu mlk ʿlm**, “Rapiu, king of eternity”: KTU 1.108.
- **gṯr**, “mighty one/hero”: applied to Rapiu in KTU 1.108.
- **Ancient Rephaim:** KTU 1.161 invokes the old or primordial members of the group before naming historical Ugaritic kings.
- **Kings (*mlkm*):** KTU 1.161 places dead kings in parallel ritual company with the Rephaim, but whether *mlkm* and *rpʾum* are fully identical classes is debated.
- **“Shades”:** an English contextual rendering of Hebrew *rĕpāʾîm* in Sheol passages, not a literal ancient gloss.
- **“Giants”:** Septuagintal and subsequent interpretive classification.
- **“Amorites,” “Canaanites,” “aboriginal peoples”:** biblical historical-geographical classifications, not securely independent ethnic records.

### Geographic names

The **Valley of Rephaim**, Hebrew **עֵמֶק רְפָאִים**, lay southwest of ancient Jerusalem. It appears in Joshua 15:8; 18:16; 2 Samuel 5:18, 22; 23:13; 1 Chronicles 11:15; 14:9. Its name proves that the term was embedded in Judahite geography; it does not establish that a historical ethnic group of giant stature inhabited the valley.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Ugaritic corpus

The relevant tablets come from Late Bronze Age Ugarit, destroyed around 1190–1180 BCE. Their composition and copying belong broadly to the fourteenth–thirteenth centuries BCE.

#### KTU 1.20–1.22: the Rāpiʾūma texts

**Securely attested early material.** These three damaged alphabetic-cuneiform tablets are the nucleus of the dossier.

The surviving narrative summons or describes the *rpʾum*. They harness horses and chariots and journey for three days to a prepared feast. The banquet continues for seven days. El is the host or divine authority behind the meal; Baal is named at its culmination, and “warriors of Baal” and “warriors of Anat” appear in the damaged text. Agricultural settings—threshing floor, plantation, or estate—occur, but their ritual meaning is uncertain.

The tablets do **not** plainly say:

- that all Rephaim were giants;
- that they were demons;
- that they fathered the Nephilim;
- that they built megalithic monuments;
- or that their banquet involved human sacrifice.

The tablets were first published by Charles Virolleaud: the first in 1936 and the remaining material in 1941. Wayne Pitard’s 1992 re-edition used improved epigraphic analysis and remains foundational. The attribution of all three tablets to ʾIlimilku is plausible but not epigraphically certain: KTU 1.20 may be in a different hand from 1.21–22.

#### KTU 1.6 vi 42–54: closing hymn of the Baal Cycle

**Securely attested early material.** The hymn addresses the solar goddess Shapash:

> “Shapash, the Rephaim are beneath you … the divine ones are beneath you; around you are the gods; behold, the dead are around you.”

Translations differ over whether *tḥtk* means “under your authority,” physically “beneath you,” or “your companions.” All readings associate Shapash directly with the Rephaim and the dead. Her daily passage between the worlds supplies a plausible theological setting.

#### KTU 1.15 iii: Kirta

**Securely attested early material.** The king Kirta is promised elevation “among the Rephaim of the earth” and “in the assembly of Ditanu.” Scholars variously interpret this as royal apotheosis, admission among heroic ancestors, or exaltation within a mythic dynastic community. It is evidence for a close relationship between kingship and the Rephaim, though not proof that every Ugaritic king automatically became one.

#### KTU 1.17–1.19: Aqhat

Daniʾilu, Aqhat’s father, is repeatedly called **mt rpʾu**. Proposed translations include “man of Rapiu,” “man of healing,” “man of the place Rapiu,” or a heroic/ancestral affiliation. Because the construction is uncertain, it cannot securely establish that Daniʾilu was already a Rephaite while alive.

#### KTU 1.108 = RS 24.252

**Securely attested early material; interpretation disputed.** This drinking song invokes **Rapiu, king of eternity**, enthroned or associated with *ʿṯtrt* and *hdrʿy*, commonly compared with biblical Ashtaroth and Edrei in Bashan. He is mighty and is asked, together with the underworld Rephaim, to exercise paternal care and power in Ugarit.

Rapiu may be:

- the eponymous leader of the collective;
- a deified primordial king;
- a title or aspect of Baal;
- or a figure related to the underworld god Milku.

The text does not settle these alternatives.

#### KTU 1.113: the Ugaritic king list

This damaged list of kings has been associated with royal mortuary commemoration. Divine determinatives or ritual context may mark deceased kings as divinized. It supplies institutional background but does not call every listed king a Rephaite in an unambiguous sentence.

#### KTU 1.161 = RS 34.126: royal funerary liturgy

**Securely attested late-Ugaritic ritual evidence**, composed or performed around the transition from Niqmaddu III to Ammurapi, near the end of Ugarit’s history.

The liturgy repeatedly summons:

- the Rephaim of the earth;
- the assembly of Didanu;
- named ancestral figures;
- the ancient Rephaim;
- Ammishtamru;
- and Niqmaddu.

Shapash is instructed to blaze or descend and accompany the dead below. A counted sequence of sacrifices culminates in a bird offered as a *šlm* offering. The text closes by asking peace for Ammurapi, his sons, his house, Ugarit, and its gates.

This is the clearest evidence that invocation of dead royal ancestors served dynastic succession and the welfare of the living kingdom. Calling it “ancestor worship” is possible but broader than the evidence: “royal mortuary cult,” “ancestor invocation,” or “funerary liturgy” is more precise.

### Phoenician and Punic evidence

#### Tabnit sarcophagus, KAI 13

**Securely attested regional continuation, Sidon, approximately sixth–early fifth century BCE.** Tabnit, priest of Astarte and king of Sidon, curses anyone who opens his coffin:

> May you have no offspring among the living under the sun, nor a resting place with the *rpʾm*.

The inscription is carved on a reused Egyptian anthropoid sarcophagus discovered near Sidon in 1887. Here the Rephaim are the community in whose company an honored dead person expects burial or repose.

#### Eshmunazar II sarcophagus, KAI 14

**Securely attested regional continuation, Sidon, fifth century BCE.** Its expanded tomb curse denies the violator a resting place with the *rpʾm*, burial in a grave, and descendants. The usage is collective and mortuary, not gigantic.

#### Neo-Punic KAI 117 from al-Amruni, Libya

**Regional late continuation, dating disputed within the Roman-era Neo-Punic corpus.** A bilingual dedication has been restored/read as **lʾl[nm] rʾpʾm**, “to the gods, the Rephaim,” corresponding to Latin **D(is) M(anibus) Sacrum**, “sacred to the divine Manes.” The restoration is not perfectly secure, but if correct it shows *rpʾm* functioning as the Punic equivalent of the collective divine dead.

No Carthaginian tophet inscription securely invokes the Rephaim. The tophet evidence and the debate over child sacrifice must therefore not be imported into their cult.

### Hebrew Bible

The biblical writings preserve distinct literary uses assembled over many centuries. Individual passages cannot be dated merely by the putative age of their stories; most reached written forms during the first millennium BCE, with substantial exilic and post-exilic editing.

#### Rephaim as legendary peoples or warriors

- **Genesis 14:5:** Chedorlaomer’s coalition defeats Rephaim at Ashteroth-karnaim.
- **Genesis 15:20:** Rephaim appear among the peoples whose land is promised to Abram’s descendants.
- **Deuteronomy 2:10–11:** the Emim, numerous and tall like the Anakim, were “accounted Rephaim.”
- **Deuteronomy 2:20–21:** the land of Ammon was also considered a land of Rephaim; the Ammonites called them Zamzummim.
- **Deuteronomy 3:11:** Og of Bashan alone remained “of the remnant of the Rephaim”; his enormous bed or sarcophagus was displayed at Rabbah.
- **Deuteronomy 3:13:** Bashan is called “the land of Rephaim.”
- **Joshua 12:4; 13:12:** Og, ruling at Ashtaroth and Edrei, is again called a remnant of the Rephaim.
- **Joshua 17:15:** the hill country is associated with Perizzites and Rephaim.
- **2 Samuel 21:15–22:** four formidable Philistine warriors are described as descendants of *hā-rāfāh*. Ishbi-benob, Saph, an unnamed six-fingered warrior, and Goliath or Goliath’s kin belong to this cycle.
- **1 Chronicles 20:4–8:** the parallel revises names and attribution, including Sippai and Lahmi, “brother of Goliath.”

The texts connect the Rephaim with antiquity, extraordinary stature, Bashan, Transjordan, and enemy warriors. They do not supply a birth myth for a giant race. Deuteronomy also states that YHWH destroyed analogous peoples for Moab and Ammon, making the ethnography part of a theological land-history.

Og’s iron **ʿereś** is traditionally translated “bedstead,” producing the famous measurement of nine by four cubits. “Sarcophagus” is also possible. The object’s reported size characterizes Og as immense but is not archaeological proof of a historical giant.

#### Rephaim as shades in Sheol

- **Job 26:5:** the Rephaim tremble beneath the waters and their inhabitants.
- **Psalm 88:10–12:** the lamenter asks whether the dead or Rephaim will rise to praise God.
- **Proverbs 2:18:** the adulterous woman’s house descends to death and her paths to the Rephaim.
- **Proverbs 9:18:** Wisdom’s rival conceals that the Rephaim are in her house and her guests in the depths of Sheol.
- **Proverbs 21:16:** one who strays from wisdom rests in the assembly of the Rephaim.
- **Isaiah 14:9:** Sheol stirs the Rephaim, “all the leaders of the earth,” to greet the fallen king of Babylon.
- **Isaiah 26:14:** the dead tyrants are dead and the Rephaim will not rise.
- **Isaiah 26:19:** in contrast, “your dead shall live”; whether this directly reverses verse 14’s fate of hostile Rephaim is debated.

Biblical poetry depicts these Rephaim as conscious enough to tremble, assemble, speak, or recognize a new arrival, but politically and physically powerless. That is markedly different from Ugaritic chariot-riding banqueters who bless royal succession.

### Later Jewish and Christian elaboration

**Later canonical interpretation:** The Septuagint’s **γίγαντες** helped unify the shades and ancient populations under the category “giants.” This translation substantially shaped Christian reception.

**Second Temple and rabbinic accretion:** Texts such as 1 Enoch, Jubilees, and the Book of Giants greatly expand the story of the Nephilim and giant offspring of the Watchers. They do not establish that the Ugaritic Rephaim were fallen angels. Later Jewish interpretation sometimes assimilated Rephaim, Nephilim, Anakim, Emim, and Gibborim into one pre-Flood or post-Flood giant complex. Rabbinic stories in which Og survives the Flood by clinging to Noah’s ark or riding outside it are late exegetical legends.

**Josephus:** *Jewish Antiquities* retells Og and Israel’s conquest, emphasizing Og’s great size and courage. This is a first-century CE literary elaboration of biblical tradition, not independent Bronze Age evidence.

Neither Philo of Byblos as preserved by Eusebius, Lucian’s *De Dea Syria*, nor Herodotus preserves an identifiable cult or narrative of the Rephaim. They illuminate Phoenician religion and mortuary custom more generally but should not be made direct witnesses where they are silent.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**At Ugarit:**

- royal ancestry and dynastic continuity;
- the organized community of heroic dead;
- participation in divine banquets;
- travel by horse and chariot;
- mediation of blessing, stability, or paternal protection;
- residence in or access to the underworld;
- possible association with agricultural abundance, though a fertility function is inferred rather than explicitly stated.

**In Phoenician and Punic sources:**

- honorable repose among the dead;
- membership in a collective infernal or ancestral community.

**In biblical prose:**

- legendary aboriginal populations;
- exceptional stature and martial strength;
- conquered inhabitants of Transjordan, Bashan, and Canaan;
- defeated Philistine champions.

**In biblical poetry:**

- the weak yet sentient inhabitants of Sheol;
- the antithesis of active, praising life.

### Characteristic attributes

- **Chariots and horses:** explicit in KTU 1.20–22. These express royal or warrior status.
- **Feasting and drinking:** central to KTU 1.20–22 and 1.108.
- **Seven-day banquet:** the Rephaim feast for seven days in KTU 1.20–22; seven is a narrative-ritual duration, not securely their “sacred number” in a broader system.
- **Royal thrones or assembly:** reflected in Isaiah 14 and Ugaritic dynastic texts.
- **Great stature:** securely a biblical characteristic of Og, Emim, Anakim, and related warriors; not stated of the Ugaritic *rpʾum*.
- **Weakness:** a biblical poetic characterization of the dead, possibly reinforced by wordplay.
- **Healing:** etymologically proposed and perhaps implicit in beneficent ancestral power, never narrated as a characteristic act.

### Iconography

No surviving image is securely labeled as a Rephaite, and no distinctive Rephaim iconographic type has been established. Proposed identifications of anonymous enthroned ancestors, banquet scenes, chariot warriors, or funerary figures remain conjectural.

Consequently, the Rephaim cannot reliably be recognized in art by giant size, wings, horns, serpents, or megalithic architecture. Those motifs belong to modern composite imagery unless a specific object has independent identification.

The principal material witnesses are textual objects:

- the Ugaritic clay tablets;
- Tabnit’s Egyptian-style basalt sarcophagus, now in the Istanbul Archaeological Museums;
- Eshmunazar II’s sarcophagus, in the Louvre;
- and the Neo-Punic funerary inscription from Libya.

### Sacred animals, colors, celestial bodies, plants, or calendar days

None is securely attested specifically for the Rephaim. Horses belong to their narrative transport; they are not demonstrated sacred animals. Their relationship with Shapash is theological and underworld-related, not proof that the sun was “their planet.” Modern lists assigning them black, Saturn, particular herbs, sigils, or weekdays are inventions.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Collective origin

No surviving Ugaritic text narrates the creation or birth of the Rephaim. They appear as an already constituted company. Their ancestry is institutional and dynastic rather than biological.

The principal proposed relationships are:

- **Didanu/Ditanu:** ancestor, founder, patron, or eponym of the assembly to which the Rephaim belong.
- **Rapiu:** eponymous head or representative of the company.
- **Dead kings of Ugarit:** historical rulers incorporated into, or ritually associated with, the ancient company.
- **Kirta and Daniʾilu:** legendary royal heroes linked with the Rephaim, but the precise relation varies with translation.

There is no ancient source making the Rephaim children of El, Baal, Anat, fallen angels, or the Nephilim.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The journey to El’s feast

**Early attested myth: KTU 1.20–22.**

The Rephaim are summoned to a banquet. They ready their horses, harness chariots, and travel for three days. They arrive in a cultivated or ceremonial landscape and feast for seven days. El, Baal, and divine-warrior language frame the event. The fragmentary state of the tablets prevents a secure continuous plot.

Major interpretations include:

- **Royal-ancestor banquet:** deceased heroic kings periodically join the gods and receive offerings.
- **Mortuary ritual myth:** the narrative supplies a mythic model for royal funerary meals.
- **Fertility ceremony:** their arrival at threshing floors or plantations renews agricultural abundance.
- **Human cultic personnel:** the Rephaim are living chariot warriors, ritual officials, or a guild impersonating ancestral beings.
- **Mixed category:** legendary living heroes whose posthumous collective became the royal dead.

The first interpretation presently fits KTU 1.108, 1.161, the Baal Cycle’s “dead/divine ones,” and Phoenician mortuary usage most economically. The living-functionary and fertility interpretations remain historically important but lack an explicit statement identifying the participants as ordinary human officials.

### Royal admission among the Rephaim

**Early attested royal ideology: Kirta, KTU 1.15.**

Kirta’s extraordinary royal standing is expressed through a place among the Rephaim of the earth and the assembly of Ditanu. This resembles a promise of heroic immortality or ancestral apotheosis. It is not a full narrative of his death, and whether it refers to present rank or future posthumous admission remains disputed.

### Rapiu’s drinking rite

**Early attested liturgical song: KTU 1.108.**

Rapiu, king of eternity and mighty one, drinks amid music or festal performance. The text requests his fatherly strength and that of the underworld Rephaim for Ugarit. The combination of drinking, royal title, ancient geography, and communal blessing makes this the best evidence for the beneficent side of the tradition.

The equation Rapiu = Baal is possible because Baal receives healing language elsewhere and may share royal titles, but KTU 1.108 does not state the equation unambiguously.

### The descent of the royal dead

**Early ritual narrative: KTU 1.161.**

At a royal death or succession, ancient Rephaim and named kings are summoned. Shapash accompanies their return below. Repeated sacrifices and the closing peace formula bind mortuary transition to Ammurapi’s legitimate rule. This is less a myth told for entertainment than a performed drama of dynastic continuity.

### Og, last of the Rephaim

**Later Israelite canonical elaboration: Numbers 21; Deuteronomy 3; Joshua 12–13.**

Og, king of Bashan, confronts Israel and is defeated. Deuteronomy calls him the sole remaining remnant of the Rephaim and cites his enormous bed or sarcophagus as a visible relic. He rules at Ashtaroth and Edrei—place-names intriguingly comparable to those in KTU 1.108.

Two readings must be distinguished:

- The biblical authors preserve a transformed memory of Transjordanian heroic or royal-ancestor traditions.
- They construct a legendary primordial enemy whose defeat legitimates Israelite possession.

The geographic overlap is real; direct literary borrowing from KTU 1.108 is unproved. The Ugaritic tablets had been buried centuries before Deuteronomy assumed its extant form, so any relationship would be through shared or transmitted West Semitic traditions, not a biblical scribe consulting the Ras Shamra tablet.

### The shades greet the king of Babylon

**Biblical poetic transformation: Isaiah 14.**

Sheol awakens its Rephaim—identified with former rulers—to receive a newly fallen emperor. They mock him: the world-conqueror has become as weak as they are. This passage most clearly preserves the royal identity of the dead while reversing their Ugaritic prestige. Their thrones remain, but their power is null.

### Wisdom’s banquets of death

**Biblical wisdom tradition: Proverbs 2, 9, 21.**

Those seduced by folly unknowingly enter a house whose guests are the Rephaim. The older banquet of exalted dead has become a moral image of fatal error. Whether Proverbs deliberately polemicizes ancestral feasting or merely uses established underworld vocabulary cannot be demonstrated.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Ugaritic royal mortuary practice

KTU 1.161 provides direct textual evidence for:

- invocation of royal ancestors by name;
- incorporation of recent kings into an older ancestral company;
- ritual lamentation;
- the involvement of Shapash;
- repeated sacrifices;
- a bird as a *šlm* offering;
- and petitions for the king, dynasty, capital, and gates.

KTU 1.108 and 1.20–22 add drinking, feasting, music, horses, and chariots. The likely institutional setting was the palace and royal mortuary establishment, not a freestanding “Temple of the Rephaim.” No such temple has been securely excavated or named.

The **marziḥu/marzēaḥ**, a socially organized drinking or banqueting association attested at Ugarit and later in the Levant, has often been connected with the Rephaim feast. KTU 1.21 may contain the term or a related context, but the extent to which every *marzēaḥ* was a funerary or ancestral institution is disputed.

### Feeding the dead

Food and drink offered to ancestors are well attested in neighboring Mesopotamian *kispum* practice and help contextualize the Ugaritic rites. Comparison is not identity: the Ugaritic ceremony has its own vocabulary and royal theology.

Archaeological tomb assemblages at Ugarit include vessels and installations compatible with mortuary meals and offerings. They rarely identify the recipient by name, so material remains cannot by themselves be assigned specifically to the Rephaim.

### Israel and Judah

The Hebrew Bible prohibits necromancy and consultation of the dead, notably in Deuteronomy 18 and the story of Saul and the medium of Endor in 1 Samuel 28. The summoned Samuel is called an **ʾĕlōhîm** rising from the earth, a valuable analogy for a divinized or numinous dead person, but he is not called one of the Rephaim.

Scholarly positions divide:

- Theodore Lewis and others see substantial continuity between West Semitic mortuary care, Ugaritic royal ancestors, and Israelite beliefs about active dead.
- Brian Schmidt argues that the evidence does not establish an Israelite ancestor cult comparable to Ugarit’s; care or commemoration of the dead and episodic necromancy should not automatically be called worship.

The biblical Rephaim passages describe the dead but never command offerings to them. Any Israelite “cult of the Rephaim” is therefore a reconstruction, not a directly attested institution.

### Human sacrifice and the Carthage tophet

No surviving Rephaim text prescribes, narrates, or receives human sacrifice. The Carthage tophet—its cremated infant and animal remains, stelae, and dedications to Baal Hammon and Tinnit—is a separate evidentiary complex. Whether all deposits were sacrifices or included other infant burials remains debated, but neither interpretation links them to the Rephaim.

Claims that the Rephaim demanded children, were worshipped through the tophet, or were identical with Molech are unattested modern conflations.

### Priesthood, festivals, oracles, and pilgrimage

- No priest titled “priest of the Rephaim” is known.
- No annual Rephaim festival is securely named.
- No Rephaim oracle sanctuary or pilgrimage center is attested.
- Their rites were likely administered by palace and cult personnel within royal funerary practice.
- The seven-day feast in KTU 1.20–22 may reflect ritual duration, but its calendar date is lost.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before the thirteenth century BCE

**Modern reconstruction.** Amorite dynastic ideology and Mesopotamian ancestor rites may stand behind the Ugaritic institution. Didanu resembles ancestral or tribal names found in earlier Amorite contexts. No pre-Ugaritic text securely names the same collective, however.

### Fourteenth–thirteenth centuries BCE: Ugaritic florescence

The Rephaim appear as prestigious, mobile, banqueting, partly divine heroic dead. Royal epics link Kirta and perhaps Daniʾilu to them. KTU 1.108 associates Rapiu and the underworld collective with blessing Ugarit. KTU 1.161 demonstrates their use in actual royal succession ritual.

This is the strongest period for speaking of a “cult,” although it was apparently a royal ancestral cult rather than public worship of an independent pantheon.

### Around 1190–1180 BCE: destruction of Ugarit

Ugarit’s destruction ended the palace archive and the institutional setting documented by the tablets. No source records a formal suppression of the Rephaim. The archaeological silence results from political collapse, not demonstrated religious persecution.

### First millennium BCE: regional divergence

In Sidonian Phoenician, *rpʾm* remained a positive or neutral designation for the honored dead: exclusion from their resting place was a curse.

In Israelite and Judahite literature, the term divided into:

- legendary inhabitants defeated by ancestral enemies and Israel;
- giant warriors whose extinction demonstrated divine conquest;
- and weak inhabitants of Sheol.

This is best understood as transformation and polemical demotion, not simple preservation. The biblical writers remember royal or heroic potency while insisting that such beings are dead, defeated, and unable to rival YHWH.

### Hellenistic and Roman periods

Greek **gigantes** encouraged the physical-giant interpretation. Phoenician/Punic mortuary terminology apparently survived sufficiently long for *rpʾm* to be compared with the Roman *Di Manes*. Jewish retellings expanded Og and combined multiple giant traditions.

No evidence demonstrates a continuing autonomous Rephaim priesthood.

### Late antiquity through the medieval period

Christian and Jewish commentators inherited the Septuagintal giant category. The Rephaim were increasingly harmonized with:

- Nephilim;
- Anakim;
- the Watchers’ offspring;
- demons generated from dead giants;
- and legendary enemies of Israel.

These combinations are exegetical systems, not recoveries of an intact Canaanite tradition. Islamic literature developed giant and primordial-race traditions around figures such as ʿŪj ibn ʿUnuq, sometimes equated with Og, but “Rephaim” did not become a stable Islamic supernatural class.

### Modern period

The 1928 discovery of Ugarit and publication of its tablets radically altered interpretation. Scholars could now compare biblical *rĕpāʾîm* with a securely Bronze Age West Semitic *rpʾum* collective. The Rephaim ceased to be explicable solely as a biblical tribe of giants.

There is no documented continuous modern community worshipping the Rephaim from antiquity. Contemporary invocations are revivals or new constructions based on translations of Ugaritic material.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship-driven revival

Modern Canaanite and Levantine reconstructionists sometimes honor the *Rāpiʾūma* as ancestral dead, royal ancestors, or a broader community of beneficial predecessors. Such practice is inspired by KTU 1.108 and 1.161 but necessarily reconstructive: the full ritual instructions, calendar, priestly rules, and theology did not survive.

### Occult and demonological material

The Rephaim are absent as a defined order from the major medieval and early-modern Solomonic grimoires, the Renaissance planetary-magic corpus, the Golden Dawn’s standard hierarchies, and classical Theosophical lists. Claims that they constitute an ancient demonic order with sigils, planetary correspondences, or named princes are modern inventions.

Recent online occult writing sometimes equates them with:

- demons;
- fallen angels;
- the Nephilim;
- “watcher bloodlines”;
- nonhuman interdimensional entities;
- vampires;
- or initiates who obtain superior rank after death.

These systems have no basis in the Ugaritic, Phoenician, or biblical sources unless explicitly presented as modern creative theology.

### Pseudoarchaeology

Common unsupported claims include:

- that dolmens in Bashan are Rephaim tombs because only giants could move the stones;
- that the “Gilgal Rephaim” or Rujm el-Hiri was certainly built by the biblical Rephaim;
- that giant skeletons have been suppressed by museums;
- that Og’s measured bed proves a superhuman biological race;
- and that the Rephaim descend from extraterrestrials.

Megalithic monuments are real archaeological features, and ancient observers may have associated imposing ruins with primordial peoples. No inscription identifies such a monument as Rephaite, and no authenticated giant skeleton supports the biological claims.

### Literature and popular media

Modern fantasy frequently uses “Rephaim” or “Rephaite” for giant, angelic, demonic, undead, or vampiric species. These portrayals normally derive from the Septuagint, Enochic harmonization, or modern biblical dictionaries—not directly from Ugaritic royal ancestor religion.

Notable reinventions include the Rephaim in contemporary fantasy and supernatural fiction, where the name carries an atmosphere of biblical antiquity while the beings’ anatomy and powers are newly authored. The term also appears in games and online world-building as a generic ancient race. None supplies evidence for ancient belief.

### Political appropriation

The biblical extermination of Rephaite populations has sometimes been folded into modern providential conquest rhetoric or racialized theories of primordial peoples. There is no historical basis for identifying any modern ethnic population as biological descendants of the Rephaim. Conversely, claims that the biblical accounts document genocide against an archaeologically recoverable “giant race” confuse theological ethnography with modern population history.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Discovery and publication

- **1928–1929:** Ras Shamra/Ugarit was identified and excavation began.
- **1936:** Virolleaud published the first major Rephaim tablet.
- **1941:** he published the additional fragments/tablets.
- **1949–1952:** John Gray argued for relationships among Ugaritic Rephaim, fertility, cultic personnel, and biblical tradition.
- **1960:** André Caquot’s “Les Rephaïm ougaritiques” surveyed competing interpretations.
- **1974:** Conrad L’Heureux’s study emphasized rank and relationships among El, Baal, and the Rephaim.
- **1970s–1980s:** improved editions and studies by Margulis, Lewis, de Moor, Caquot, Sznycer, Gibson, and others refined the texts.
- **1989:** Theodore J. Lewis’s *Cults of the Dead in Ancient Israel and Ugarit* developed the mortuary interpretation.
- **1992:** Wayne Pitard’s new edition of KTU 1.20–22 corrected readings through epigraphic examination.
- **1994:** Brian Schmidt’s *Israel’s Beneficent Dead* challenged expansive claims about Israelite ancestor worship.
- **1997 onward:** translations in *The Context of Scripture*, Parker’s *Ugaritic Narrative Poetry*, Wyatt’s *Religious Texts from Ugarit*, and Smith and Pitard’s *Baal Cycle* volumes made the corpus more critically accessible.
- **2000s–2020s:** high-resolution imaging and new philological studies strengthened the royal-ancestor model while retaining uncertainty about the collective’s precise membership.

### Principal controversies

#### Were they gods, dead humans, or living cult officials?

The texts call them divine ones, dead/men, chariot travelers, and banquet guests. These categories were not mutually exclusive at Ugarit: a dead king could retain human identity while acquiring divine status. The royal-ancestor interpretation currently explains the widest range of evidence, but “minor gods” and “legendary heroic ancestors” may describe aspects of the same category.

The living-guild theory accounts for realistic travel and agricultural settings but struggles with KTU 1.6 and 1.161, where underworld and funerary associations are explicit.

#### Were they healers?

The root rpʾ strongly invites “healer,” and some ancient Greek translations reflect that interpretation. Nevertheless, no surviving narrative describes medical action. “Healers” should be labeled a philological interpretation, not an established office.

#### Are biblical giants and shades the same beings?

The identical Hebrew spelling and Ugaritic/Phoenician background favor historical relationship. The precise direction of development is disputed:

- heroic kings became ancestral dead and later generic shades;
- shades were retrojected as primordial races;
- or once-separate traditions were joined by wordplay and ethnographic theology.

The sources cannot determine a single linear evolution.

#### Did biblical monotheism deliberately demote them?

Isaiah’s powerless former kings and Deuteronomy’s exterminated giants make a polemical pattern plausible. Yet the biblical corpus contains no explicit statement, “these were once worshipped ancestors whom we now reject.” “Demotion” is therefore a persuasive historical model, not a documented reform decree.

#### Are they connected with Nephilim?

Only through later harmonization. Genesis 6 never calls the Nephilim Rephaim. Numbers 13 associates Nephilim with Anakim; Deuteronomy associates Anakim-like peoples with Rephaim. This chain enabled later synthesis, but it is not an original equivalence in any one early text.

#### Bashan, Ashtaroth, and Edrei

The convergence between Rapiu’s geography in KTU 1.108 and Og’s Ashtaroth/Edrei in Deuteronomy and Joshua is among the strongest arguments for shared regional tradition. Whether the Ugaritic place-words should be identified exactly with the biblical cities remains philologically debated.

### Evidence that does not exist

- No securely identified statue or relief of a Rephaite.
- No temple dedicated solely to the collective.
- No complete ritual calendar.
- No Rephaim creation myth.
- No marriage, consort, or offspring tradition.
- No connection to the Carthage tophet.
- No proof of a biological race of giants.
- No continuous cult from antiquity to modernity.
- No ancient Rephaim sigil, seal, color, planet, or occult hierarchy.
