# Mot: Research Dossier

**Class:** **god and personification**—Ugaritic *Môtu*, “Death,” is grammatically masculine, called a son of El, and represented as a speaking, ruling divine power. He is also the condition, place, and agency of death; the texts deliberately blur deity, underworld, and abstraction.

**Earliest secure attestation:** **Late Bronze Age Ugarit, probably fourteenth–thirteenth century BCE**, in the alphabetic-cuneiform Baal Cycle, especially KTU 1.4–1.6. Its tablets were recovered at Ras Shamra, ancient Ugarit, and KTU 1.6 names the scribe Ilimilku and King Niqmaddu in its colophon. The exact reign intended—and therefore the narrow dating—remains disputed, but the tablets necessarily predate Ugarit’s destruction around 1190 BCE. KTU 1.5 and 1.6 are now in the Louvre. The six-tablet cycle comprises RS 3.361, 3.346/3.367, 2.[014]+, 2.[008]+, 2.[022]+, and 2.[009]+. [John C. L. Gibson’s corpus survey](https://brill.com/previewpdf/display/book/9789004294103/B9789004294103-s007.xml)

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

**[Securely attested Late Bronze Age material]** Mot is Death conceived as an active member of the Ugaritic divine family. He is Baal’s adversary, El’s son, ruler of a subterranean or liminal realm, possessor of a limitless throat and appetite, and ultimately a power that can be defeated but not permanently abolished. In KTU 1.4–1.6 he receives messages, issues invitations and threats, swallows or otherwise causes Baal’s death, is mutilated by Anat, returns after seven years, fights Baal, and submits after Shapash threatens him with El’s displeasure.

Mot is not simply “the Ugaritic Hades.” At different moments:

- he is a named god;
- *mt* is the ordinary word “death”;
- “Mot” can designate his land or dwelling;
- entry into his mouth represents dying;
- his bodily destruction is narrated through agricultural and culinary operations;
- death remains operative even after that destruction.

This fluidity is intrinsic to the poetry, not a defect to be regularized.

### Name and script

- **Ugaritic:** 𐎎𐎚, alphabetic-cuneiform **mt**.
- **Conventional vocalization:** *Môtu*, *Motu*, or *Môt*. Because Ugaritic normally omits vowels, the written form alone is *mt*.
- **Northwest Semitic lexical family:** Hebrew מָוֶת *māwet*, “death”; Phoenician and Aramaic *mwt*; Arabic مَوْت *mawt*; Akkadian *mūtu*. The divine name is the common noun “death” personalized, not demonstrably a separate etymological formation.
- **Hebrew poetic form:** מוֹת *môt* can occur as a bound or poetic form, while מָוֶת *māwet* is the ordinary absolute form. Identifying every personified Hebrew occurrence with the cultically distinct Ugaritic god would exceed the evidence.
- **Greek transmission:** Philo of Byblos, as preserved by Eusebius, has **Μοὺθ**, commonly transliterated *Mouth*, *Muth*, or *Mouthos*, for a child identified with Thanatos and Pluto. His cosmogony also contains a differently functioning **Μώτ**, often interpreted as mud, slime, or a primordial watery mixture. These two Greek forms must not be automatically conflated.

### Attested titles and descriptions

**[Securely attested: KTU 1.4–1.6]**

- **mt bn ilm**—“Mot, son of El/the gods.” In context *ilm* most naturally marks El as father, though Ugaritic spelling does not distinguish the singular divine name from all possible plural constructions without context.
- **ydd il ġzr**—often rendered “Beloved of El, the hero” or “El’s beloved, the warrior.” Both the vocalization and the precise force of *ġzr* are reconstructed.
- **the insatiable one:** Mot says that his appetite is like a lion’s in the steppe and a great sea creature’s in the sea; “seven portions” occupy his plate and a river fills his cup. KTU 1.133 independently repeats part of this speech, proving circulation or scribal reuse outside the surviving master sequence. [Wyatt’s presentation of KTU 1.133](https://pdfcoffee.com/religious-texts-from-ugarit-whole-pdf-free.html)
- **vast-mouthed Death:** imagery gives him a lip extending toward earth and another toward heaven, with a tongue toward the stars; Baal is to descend into his mouth.
- **ruler:** Mot possesses a throne, kingship, and judicial scepter in KTU 1.6 VI, although his kingship does not displace El’s ultimate patriarchal authority or Baal’s royal station.

### Places associated with Mot

The path to Mot leads to the underworld-like land named in damaged and difficult lines of KTU 1.4 VIII. Translations variously render the landmarks as mountains at the edge of the earth and Mot’s city as *Ḥamray/Ḥamri*, “Miry,” or similar reconstructions. His residence is associated with pit, mire, decay, dust, and the boundary where ordinary vitality ends. The proper names and some topographical details remain philologically uncertain.

### Misleading modern names

- **“Maweth” as a separate Canaanite god:** generally a modern Anglicized representation of Hebrew *māwet*, not evidence for an independently attested deity distinct from Mot.
- **“Moloch/Molech”:** unrelated. The similarity of modern spellings has generated confusion, but Mot is not the biblical *Molek/Moloch* or the Punic *mlk/molk* sacrificial term.
- **“God of sterility,” “god of the desert,” or “god of drought”:** interpretive shorthand. The texts associate Mot with lifelessness, heat, failed fertility, and Baal’s disappearance, but do not preserve an official portfolio list naming him patron of a discrete “desert” or “sterility” domain.
- **Satan or a chief demon:** **modern invention or theological assimilation**. No Ugaritic text classifies Mot as a demon, rebel angel, or moral principle of evil.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The Ras Shamra evidence

Ugarit was rediscovered after the 1928 discovery of a tomb near Minet el-Beida; excavations at Ras Shamra began in 1929. The archive revolutionized the study of Northwest Semitic religion because it preserved native ritual, administrative, lexical, diplomatic, and poetic material rather than only hostile biblical descriptions.

The Baal Cycle is Ugaritic poetry impressed on clay in a thirty-sign alphabetic cuneiform script. Six tablets survive, but they are incomplete. Approximately half of the original text is lost, and several apparent contradictions may result from lacunae, parallel traditions, or deliberate poetic ambiguity. The tablets’ sequence is a modern reconstruction supported by plot, tablet form, and colophons—not an ancient numbered “book.”

The cycle’s surviving witnesses are unique copies. It would be mistaken to call the extant spelling of every episode a pan-Canaanite canon. Dennis Pardee emphasizes that only about fifty of Ugarit’s roughly 175 religious texts qualify as literary compositions and that the Baal Cycle is the largest. [Pardee, “Ugaritic Literary Composition”](https://academic.oup.com/british-academy-scholarship-online/book/14187/chapter-abstract/168042623)

### Principal primary texts

#### KTU 1.4 VII–VIII

**Language/support/date:** Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform on clay; fourteenth–thirteenth century BCE.

**Role of Mot:** After Baal obtains his palace, he challenges or boasts against his enemies. Mot’s dangerous realm and devouring jaws are introduced. Baal sends messengers to Mot but warns them not to approach too closely, lest Mot crush them like a lamb or kid in his mouth.

**Evidentiary significance:** This is the earliest surviving narrative setup of the Baal–Mot conflict. It establishes the mouth/throat metaphor before Baal’s death.

#### KTU 1.5

**Language/support/date:** Ugaritic; clay; same scribal cycle.

**Role:** Mot indignantly answers Baal. He proclaims his voracious appetite and orders Baal to descend. Baal sends a submissive message agreeing to become Mot’s servant forever. A damaged narrative then reports Baal’s death or discovery as dead in the land associated with Mot.

Mot’s preserved boast compares his throat with the appetite of a lion and a creature in the sea. The orthography also creates a recurrent philological problem: *mt* can mean the god *Môtu*, the noun “death,” or, under a different vocalization, *mutu*, “man.” Matthew Suriano’s lexical study stresses this ambiguity. [“An Analysis of Words for ‘Death’ in Ugaritic”](https://doi.org/10.1515/9781646020386-006)

#### KTU 1.6 I–III

**Role:** El and Anat mourn Baal. Anat searches for him, confronts Mot, demands her brother, and eventually seizes Mot:

> She split him with a blade,  
> winnowed him with a sieve,  
> burned him with fire,  
> ground him with millstones,  
> scattered him in the field.

Birds consume his remains. The text applies the complete processing of grain to a divine body. Whether this represents harvesting, sacrificial destruction, corpse annihilation, or an intentionally grotesque combination remains debated. Paul L. Watson argued that the passage need not make Mot literally into grain; agricultural verbs may instead totalize Anat’s violent elimination of a hostile power. [Watson, “The Death of ‘Death’ in the Ugaritic Texts”](https://scispace.com/papers/the-death-of-death-in-the-ugaritic-texts-7xwhg02fqc)

El dreams that the heavens rain oil and wadis run with honey, takes this as evidence that Baal lives, and sends Anat to locate him. The order of events does **not** produce an entirely smooth modern “Death kills Rain; Goddess kills Death; Rain resurrects” plot: the tablet is damaged, Baal’s reappearance is partly mediated through El’s dream, and Mot later reports his own destruction.

#### KTU 1.6 V–VI

**Role:** “In the seventh year,” Mot returns and accuses Baal of causing his mutilation. He demands one of Baal’s brothers to eat. After further damaged action, Mot and Baal fight:

- like wild bulls, they gore;
- like serpents, they bite;
- like stallions, they kick;
- Mot falls and Baal falls.

Shapash warns Mot that El may overturn his throne and break his scepter. Mot becomes afraid and yields; Baal’s kingship is affirmed.

**The seven-year issue:** The text says Mot returns or speaks **in the seventh year**. It does not explicitly describe an annually reenacted agricultural calendar, nor does it clearly state that Baal remains dead for seven years. The common “seven-year cycle of vegetation” reconstruction is therefore **modern scholarly interpretation**, not the literal narrative wording.

A recent online presentation supplies both transliteration and translation of the key passages, but its claims about a universal dying-and-rising-god pattern and Christianity go beyond what the tablet itself establishes. [KTU 1.5–1.6 transliteration](https://tianmu.org/good-work-library/canaanite/the-baal-cycle-baal-and-mot)

#### KTU 1.133 = RS 24.293

**[Securely attested, but secondary witness]** A scribal exercise or “memory variant” repeating Mot’s appetite speech from KTU 1.5 I, then diverging. Its existence confirms that the speech circulated independently or was used in scribal training; it does not prove an independent public ritual to Mot. [Ugaritic corpus index](https://dornsife.usc.edu/wsrp-temp/wp-content/uploads/sites/155/2023/05/Ugaritic-Texts.pdf)

#### KTU 1.82

**[Possible/contested]** An incantation includes *mt* among dangerous beings or forces, alongside figures such as Resheph, Hauron’s servants, and Tunnanu. Whether every occurrence represents the fully personified Mot rather than death as a harmful force depends on reconstruction and context.

#### KTU 1.107

Translations sometimes identify death among powers invoked or controlled in an anti-venom setting. The text is damaged, and Mot’s role should not be expanded into an established healing cult.

#### KTU 1.23, “The Birth of the Gracious Gods”

This myth contains the difficult expression *mt w šr*. It has been read as “Death-and-Prince,” as a compound divine designation, or differently. It is not a secure independent narrative of the familiar Baal Cycle Mot. The phrase should not be used to supply Mot with an unambiguous twin, consort, or birth myth.

#### Kirta (KTU 1.14–1.16)

The Kirta epic repeatedly concerns mortality, illness, bereavement, and descent toward death, but the common noun *mt* must not automatically be capitalized into Mot. No secure, extended personal encounter between Kirta and the god Mot survives. [Online text of the Kirta/Keret epic](https://kchanson.com/ANCDOCS/westsem/kret.html)

#### Aqhat (KTU 1.17–1.19)

Aqhat is killed, mourned, and sought, and the narrative reflects Ugaritic ideas about death. It does not preserve a clear Mot episode comparable to KTU 1.5–1.6. Claims that Mot personally kills or receives Aqhat are reconstructions, not explicit surviving narrative.

### Amarna letters

The fourteenth-century BCE Akkadian diplomatic letters use cognates of “death,” “to die,” and “dead.” They do **not** securely attest Mot as a named Canaanite god. Their inclusion in discussions of Mot usually illuminates Late Bronze Age language and politics, not his cult or mythology.

### Hebrew Bible

#### Hosea 13:14

The eighth-century BCE prophetic context addresses Ephraim/Israel:

> From the hand of Sheol shall I ransom them?  
> From Death shall I redeem them?  
> Where are your plagues, O Death?  
> Where is your sting/destruction, O Sheol?

The Hebrew lacks the interrogative particle in the opening clauses, permitting opposite readings:

1. a promise to ransom Israel from Death; or
2. rhetorical questions refusing ransom, followed by a summons to Death and Sheol to punish Israel.

The punitive reading fits the immediate judgment context particularly well; the Septuagint and Paul’s reuse in 1 Corinthians 15:55 helped establish the triumphant-over-death reading in later Jewish and Christian reception.

**Classification:** **Israelite poetic personification with probable West Semitic mythic inheritance**, not evidence that Hosea’s audience worshipped Mot. Recent scholarship describes Hosea 13–14 as polemically appropriating imagery associated with YHWH’s rivals. [Oxford Handbook of Hosea](https://doi.org/10.1093/oxfordhb/9780197639597.013.16); [translation problem discussed by Francis Landy](https://brill.com/view/journals/bi/3/1/article-p35_3.xml)

#### Isaiah 25:6–8

On YHWH’s mountain, YHWH prepares a feast and “swallows up Death forever” (*billaʿ hammāwet lāneṣaḥ*). This reverses Mot’s defining action: at Ugarit, Death swallows; in Isaiah, YHWH swallows Death.

**Classification:** **later Israelite theological transformation**, probably drawing upon a widespread West Semitic “devouring Death” image. Direct literary dependence on KTU 1.5 cannot be proved; the Ugaritic tablets were buried, and the imagery could have circulated through oral or other written traditions. The passage’s date is debated because Isaiah 24–27 is widely treated as a later compositional unit, often placed in the exilic or postexilic period rather than securely in the eighth-century prophet’s lifetime. [Discussion of “Mot as the Swallower”](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3Ac50562f6-8f26-43ea-826c-b24d00e5686b/files/m8a6adfbb4317339362f90b147efe65c0)

#### Other biblical personifications

- Isaiah 5:14: Sheol enlarges its throat and opens its mouth.
- Isaiah 28:15, 18: Jerusalem’s rulers boast of a covenant with Death and an agreement with Sheol.
- Habakkuk 2:5: Death is insatiable and gathers nations.
- Jeremiah 9:20–21: Death climbs through windows and enters palaces.
- Job 18:13: “the firstborn of Death” consumes the sufferer.
- Psalm 49:15: God will ransom the poet from Sheol’s power.
- Song of Songs 8:6: love is “strong as Death.”

These share the cultural vocabulary of devouring and sovereign Death. None names a sanctuary, priesthood, or sacrifice to Mot.

### Philo of Byblos through Eusebius

Philo of Byblos wrote a Greek *Phoenician History* around the late first or second century CE. Its fragments survive principally in Eusebius’ fourth-century *Praeparatio evangelica* 1.9–10. Philo claimed to translate or transmit Sanchuniathon, supposedly an extremely ancient Phoenician writer.

In the theogonic genealogy, Rhea bears Cronus children; the youngest is consecrated as a god and called **Muth/Mouth** by the Phoenicians, whom Philo correlates with Greek **Thanatos** and **Pluto**. This supplies Muth with a divine genealogy and a Greek interpretive equation.

**Classification:** **late learned Phoenician-Greek systematization containing possible older Phoenician material**. The transmission chain is:

1. alleged Phoenician Sanchuniathon;
2. Philo’s Greek adaptation;
3. Eusebius’ Christian quotation and polemical framing.

The date and even literary identity of “Sanchuniathon” remain debated. Modern scholarship generally allows genuine Phoenician traditions in Philo while rejecting the claim that the surviving Greek text is a transparent second-millennium document. [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/550498080); [Edwards, “Philo or Sanchuniathon?”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/classical-quarterly/article/abs/philo-or-sanchuniathon-a-phoenicean-cosmogony/734872EFB21D8D12A52AAD0FC9DEFDB3); [CNR overview of Phoenician religion](https://iris.cnr.it/handle/20.500.14243/446622)

Philo’s primordial **Μώτ** is described in a cosmogonic context as a muddy or putrid watery mixture from which creation develops. It is not securely identical to **Μοὺθ**, the divine child equated with Death and Pluto. Treating “primordial mud Mot” and Ugaritic Death as one seamless god is a modern harmonization.

### Lucian, Herodotus, Josephus, Phoenician inscriptions

- **Lucian, *De Dea Syria*** describes Syrian temple practice and the cult at Hierapolis in the Roman imperial period but does not supply a secure cult of Mot.
- **Herodotus** discusses Phoenician religion, Melqart/Heracles, and Carthaginian practices, but does not securely name Mot.
- **Josephus** preserves material about Phoenician history and cites Menander of Ephesus and other sources; he gives no independent Mot mythology.
- **KAI Phoenician and Punic inscriptions:** no securely identified temple dedication, priesthood, festival, or theophoric personal name establishes a Phoenician-Punic cult of Mot.

The negative evidence matters. “Canaanite, Phoenician and Punic god Mot” is defensible as a broad cultural label only if the uneven record is stated: direct mythological characterization is overwhelmingly Ugaritic; first-millennium Phoenician continuity is tenuous and largely dependent on Philo; Punic cult is unproved.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**[Securely attested]**

- death and dying;
- loss of vitality;
- the underworld or destination of the dead;
- devouring and consumption;
- opposition to Baal’s life-giving storm power;
- interruption of fertility following Baal’s absence;
- kingship within Death’s own sphere.

Mot is not a psychopomp: no surviving text shows him guiding souls. He is not a judge of the dead in the Egyptian or later Greek sense. He does not distribute postmortem rewards and punishments.

### Characteristic powers

- **Universal appetite:** his throat can consume even gods.
- **Swallowing:** the mouth is both bodily organ and entrance to death.
- **Killing or holding Baal:** the surviving poetry treats Baal as dead in Mot’s domain, although the precise mechanics are lost.
- **Return after annihilation:** cutting, burning, grinding, and scattering do not permanently destroy him.
- **Combat:** he can fight Baal as an equal until Shapash invokes El’s superior authority.
- **Constraint:** he fears El’s sanction; Anat can seize and dismember him; Shapash can mediate or coerce; he cannot establish an uncontested permanent kingship.

### Form

No securely identified ancient image of Mot survives. The texts construct him verbally:

- colossal mouth, lips, tongue, throat, and gullet;
- appetite like desert lion and sea creature;
- hands or body capable of grappling;
- throne and scepter;
- a body divisible by blade, fire, millstone, birds, and field.

Modern skeletal, horned, mummiform, black-robed, or demonic images are **modern inventions**, usually derived from later European Death iconography or fantasy art.

### Emblems and correspondences

Secure:

- mouth and throat;
- dust, pit, mire, and underworld terrain;
- blade, sieve, fire, millstones, field, and birds in Anat’s destruction sequence;
- throne and scepter.

Not securely attested:

- a sacred animal belonging to Mot;
- a dedicated color;
- a planet, constellation, weekday, number, metal, incense, or plant;
- a canonical weapon wielded by Mot;
- a cult statue;
- a personal sigil.

The lion and sea creature in Mot’s speech are similes for appetite, not proof that they were his sacred animals. Seven is present in his meal imagery and the “seventh year,” but no text establishes “seven” as his cult number.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

| Relation | Evidence | Classification |
|---|---|---|
| **El: father** | Mot is repeatedly *bn ilm*, “son of El,” KTU 1.5–1.6 | Securely attested |
| **Baal: brother/fellow son of the divine family?** | Baal and Mot operate within El’s household, but the texts do not give a simple biological sibling formula between them | Common reconstruction, not explicit in every occurrence |
| **Anat: no secure kinship to Mot** | She calls Baal her brother and destroys Mot | Secure interaction; no direct Mot–Anat genealogy |
| **Mother** | No mother of Mot is securely named in Ugaritic texts | Absence of evidence |
| **Consort or offspring** | None securely attested | Absence of evidence |
| **Rhea as mother of Muth** | Philo/Eusebius’ Greek-Phoenician genealogy | Late learned systematization |
| **Cronus/El as father of Muth** | Philo’s interpretive genealogy | Late syncretistic systematization |
| **Thanatos and Pluto** | Philo says Greeks understand Muth through these names | Datable interpretatio graeca, not original Ugaritic identity |

### Every securely or plausibly documented named divine interaction

- **Baal — summons, submission, killing/swallowing, return, and combat.** Mot orders Baal to descend into his mouth; Baal professes servitude; the narrative announces Baal dead; Mot later fights him to a draw before yielding. **Source:** KTU 1.4 VIII–1.6 VI, fourteenth–thirteenth century BCE. **Tier:** securely attested Ugaritic narrative.

- **Anat — search, demand, seizure, and annihilation.** Anat demands Baal from Mot, seizes Mot, cuts, winnows, burns, grinds, scatters, and exposes him to birds. **Source:** KTU 1.6 II, fourteenth–thirteenth century BCE. **Tier:** securely attested Ugaritic narrative.

- **El — filial subordination and threatened punishment.** Mot is El’s son; Shapash warns that El will remove the supports of Mot’s dwelling, overturn his throne, and break his scepter if he continues fighting Baal. El is not preserved directly striking Mot. **Source:** KTU 1.5–1.6 VI. **Tier:** securely attested relationship; threatened rather than enacted interaction.

- **Shapash — boundary-crosser, messenger, and coercive mediator.** Shapash moves between the upper world and Mot’s realm, assists the search surrounding Baal’s death, and finally compels Mot to yield by invoking El. **Source:** KTU 1.6. **Tier:** securely attested Ugaritic narrative.

- **Gupan and Ugar — message-bearing encounter.** Baal sends his named divine messengers Gupan-and-Ugar to Mot, warning them not to approach the devourer too closely. They convey speeches rather than fight him. **Source:** KTU 1.4 VIII and 1.5. **Tier:** securely attested, though the damaged passage complicates details.

- **Baal’s unnamed brothers — threatened victims.** Mot demands that Baal surrender one of his brothers for Mot to eat. The subsequent victim’s identity and outcome are damaged and debated. **Source:** KTU 1.6 V. **Tier:** securely attested threat; victim identification uncertain.

- **Athtar — indirect succession relation only.** After Baal’s death, Athtar is tested as a replacement king but proves physically inadequate for Baal’s throne. Mot and Athtar do not converse or fight in the surviving text. **Source:** KTU 1.6 I. **Tier:** securely attested joint narrative context; **no documented direct interaction**.

- **Athirat — indirect political relation only.** Athirat participates in choosing Athtar after Baal’s death. No direct Mot–Athirat scene survives. **Source:** KTU 1.6 I. **Tier:** secure indirect relation; **no direct interaction**.

- **Resheph, Hauron, Tunnanu and other harmful powers — joint incantation setting.** Mot or “death” may occur beside these figures in KTU 1.82. The text does not narrate a meeting or alliance. **Tier:** possible regional ritual association, not a mythic interaction.

- **Cronus, Rhea, Thanatos, Pluto — Philo’s genealogical and interpretive relations.** Muth is the youngest child in a Greek-formulated genealogy and is equated with Thanatos and Pluto. **Source:** Philo of Byblos through Eusebius, second–fourth centuries CE. **Tier:** late learned Phoenician-Greek systematization.

There is no securely attested interaction between Mot and Yam, Dagan, Ashtart, Melqart, Eshmun, Tanit, Baal Hammon, Molech, or a biblical “Satan.”

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

## Baal descends to Mot

**Earliest source:** KTU 1.4 VII–1.5, Late Bronze Age.

After Baal’s palace is completed, the storm god asserts royal power. His declaration antagonizes Mot, whose dominion cannot be abolished by architectural kingship. Baal sends Gupan-and-Ugar toward Death’s realm but instructs them to display humility and keep their distance.

Mot responds with the rhetoric of appetite. His throat is never filled; seven portions lie on his plate and a river is poured into his cup. Baal must enter his mouth as other living beings do. Baal then sends a startlingly submissive answer: he is Mot’s servant forever.

What follows is fragmentary. Baal couples with a heifer, apparently producing a substitute or double according to some readings. Messengers report to El that Baal lies dead “in the earth.” El descends from his throne, sits in dust, cuts himself, and performs mourning gestures. Anat conducts an equally violent lament.

### Interpretive disputes

1. **Mot literally swallows Baal.**  
   Supported by Mot’s invitation and mouth imagery. The actual swallowing scene is not preserved.

2. **Baal sends a substitute.**  
   Some reconstructions interpret the sexual episode and calf as a ruse allowing Baal to evade permanent death. The text is too damaged to establish a complete substitution plot.

3. **Baal truly dies and returns.**  
   El and Anat explicitly respond to him as dead, and later material presents him alive again. Mark S. Smith nevertheless cautions against mechanically classifying Baal as a Frazerian “dying and rising god”; the story’s political, cosmic, and ritual dimensions exceed a vegetation allegory. [Smith, “The Life and Death of Baal”](https://academic.oup.com/book/12858/chapter-abstract/163142589)

4. **Seasonal drought allegory.**  
   Baal’s disappearance naturally suggests interruption of rain and fertility, but the poem never says “this happens every dry season.” The seven-year line actually resists a simple annual calendar.

## Anat destroys Mot

**Source:** KTU 1.6 II.

When Mot admits his role in Baal’s disappearance, Anat seizes him. The sequence escalates through every conceivable form of bodily destruction: cutting, winnowing, burning, grinding, scattering, and consumption by birds.

### Interpretive disputes

- **Agricultural cycle:** Mot becomes grain; his processing clears the way for vegetation and rain.
- **Anti-funerary annihilation:** proper burial preserves identity, whereas grinding, scattering, and exposure deny Mot a recoverable corpse.
- **Ritual dramatization:** the actions may reflect a rite performed against a destructive divine force.
- **Literary hyperbole:** Anat’s standard ferocity is intensified to show that even Death can be “killed.”

No excavated rite duplicates the sequence, so claims of an annual Ugaritic ceremony that literally enacted Mot’s threshing are **modern reconstruction**.

## Baal returns

El dreams of rainlike abundance—oil from heaven and honey through the wadis—and concludes that Baal lives. Shapash and Anat participate in the search. The text does not narrate a medically or metaphysically explicit resurrection. “Baal returns to life” is reasonable narrative shorthand, but the tablet’s mechanism is lost.

El’s dream may express restored fertility; it may also be a recognition oracle. The scene’s abundance is important for Isaiah 25, where a divine feast accompanies the destruction of Death, but direct textual borrowing cannot be demonstrated.

## Mot’s seven-year return and final combat

“In the seventh year,” Mot confronts Baal and recounts Anat’s violence as something Baal caused. That testimony proves Mot survived his own total destruction. He demands a substitute from Baal’s family. Baal and Mot then fight through three animal similes and both fall.

Shapash intervenes, not by overpowering Mot but by reminding him of El’s sovereignty. Mot yields. Baal resumes or retains his throne.

The outcome is **containment, not abolition**. Death remains part of the cosmos. This distinguishes the Ugaritic ending from Isaiah 25:8’s eschatological “Death forever swallowed.”

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### The central negative finding

**No temple, priesthood, sacrificial calendar, votive dedication, or theophoric personal name securely belonging to Mot is known.** He is absent from the standard Ugaritic pantheon and offering lists in which numerous major and minor gods receive animals, food, drink, metal, or garments.

This is particularly significant because Ugarit preserved detailed ritual records, including KTU 1.39–1.47, 1.87, 1.105, and related texts. Mot’s absence cannot prove that no private person ever addressed him, but it makes an institutional “cult of Mot” currently unreconstructable. One modern study summarizes Wyatt’s conclusion: there is no evidence that cult was offered to Mot, and Baal’s becoming food for the unworshipped all-devourer may be part of the poem’s irony. [Study of sacrifice and KTU 1.4](https://etd.ohiolink.edu/acprod/odb_etd/ws/send_file?accession=osu1606754016335887&disposition=inline)

### Possible ritual traces

- KTU 1.82 and related incantations may treat Mot/death as a dangerous force to be repelled.
- KTU 1.133’s scribal copying shows textual transmission, not worship.
- Mourning gestures in the Baal Cycle—dust, laceration, shaved hair, ritual descent—reflect Ugaritic or broader Levantine lamentation practices, but the episode is mourning **against** what Mot has done, not an offering **to** Mot.
- Anat’s destruction of Mot may preserve ritual imagery, but no external evidence identifies an enacted festival.

### Temples and images

No Mot temple or securely identified cult image has been excavated at Ugarit, Byblos, Tyre, Sidon, Carthage, or another Phoenician-Punic site.

Claims that anonymous underworld figures, grotesque masks, funerary stelae, or “sardonic” images depict Mot are speculative unless accompanied by an identifying inscription.

### Human sacrifice and the Carthage tophet

The Carthage tophet, used from approximately the eighth century until Carthage’s destruction in 146 BCE, contained urns with cremated remains of very young humans and animals and stelae dedicated primarily to **Tanit and Baal Hammon**. Ancient Greek and Roman authors accuse Carthaginians of child sacrifice; modern osteological and archaeological debate concerns whether all, some, or few children were killed as offerings rather than dying naturally before cremation.

One osteological study argued that the remains do not support systematic infant sacrifice. [Schwartz et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2822869/) Later responses contested its age estimates and emphasized the votive inscriptions, animal substitutions, deposition patterns, and literary evidence. [Antiquity review of the competing interpretations](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/two-tales-of-one-city-data-inference-and-carthaginian-infant-sacrifice/5006E240CB75A1E324B3230F6DA17389)

For Mot, however, the decisive point is simpler:

- no tophet inscription securely dedicates a victim to Mot;
- *mlk/molk* is not the name *Mot*;
- Baal Hammon is not Mot;
- Greek Kronos in Carthaginian sacrificial testimony is normally associated with Baal Hammon, not Ugaritic Death.

Connecting Mot personally to Punic child sacrifice is therefore **unsupported modern conflation**.

### Temple prostitution

No evidence connects Mot with “sacred prostitution.” More broadly, the old model of widespread institutional temple prostitution in the ancient Near East is heavily disputed. Nothing in Mot’s corpus warrants including it as one of his rites.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Fourteenth–thirteenth centuries BCE: literary formation at Ugarit

Mot is fully personified in the Ilimilku Baal Cycle. He belongs to a royal and scribal theological world in which El heads the divine family, Baal seeks cosmic kingship, Anat executes vengeance, and Shapash crosses the boundary of the dead.

The material may draw on older oral traditions, but no earlier tablet securely names and characterizes Mot. It is therefore methodologically wrong to date the complete Baal–Mot myth to the third or early second millennium merely because the root *mwt* is old.

### Around 1190 BCE: destruction of Ugarit

The city’s destruction ended the archive in which Mot is best documented. No continuous priestly or manuscript chain can be traced from Ilimilku’s tablets into the Iron Age.

### Iron Age Israel and Judah

Personified Death remains a powerful poetic figure. Hosea, Isaiah, Jeremiah, Habakkuk, Job, Psalms, and the Song of Songs use Death or Sheol as speakers, rulers, predators, enemies, or possessors of an appetite.

This is best described as **shared Northwest Semitic imagery transformed within Yahwistic literature**, not proof of continued Israelite worship of Mot. In Hosea, Death can be summoned as YHWH’s agent or challenged; in Isaiah, YHWH reverses and permanently defeats the devourer.

### Iron Age Phoenicia and Punic expansion

The epigraphic record prominently attests Baal, Baal Hammon, Melqart, Eshmun, Astarte, Tanit, Resheph, and other gods. It does not establish Mot as a recipient of public cult. If traditions about divine Death continued, they have left little securely identifiable inscriptional evidence.

### Hellenistic and Roman Phoenician learned tradition

Philo of Byblos’ Muth preserves or reconstructs a Phoenician Death within a Greek genealogy and equates him with Thanatos and Pluto. This is not evidence that all three had always been the same god. It is a datable intellectual translation shaped by euhemerism, Greek theogony, local antiquarianism, and possibly older Phoenician records.

### Late antiquity

Eusebius preserved Philo’s fragments to argue against pagan theology. Mot/Muth therefore survived textually through a Christian apologetic work, not through a continuing identifiable cult.

There is no documented Roman or Christian suppression campaign directed specifically at a temple of Mot. General Christianization displaced Levantine polytheistic institutions, but a named “last cult of Mot” cannot be reconstructed.

### 1928 onward: archaeological rediscovery

The Ras Shamra discoveries restored Mot from a marginal biblical and Phoenician comparison into a major figure of ancient West Semitic studies. Publication of the Baal tablets made it possible to recognize the devouring Death imagery behind or parallel to biblical passages.

### Present

Small modern Canaanite, Natib Qadish, Semitic-neopagan, and Israeli reconstructionist milieus engage the Ugaritic pantheon. Ethnographic work finds that only a small minority of Israeli pagans primarily reconstruct Canaanite religion. [Shai Feraro, “The Return of Baal to the Holy Land”](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/the-return-of-baal-to-the-holy-land-canaanite-reconstructionism-a/)

Modern practitioners vary:

- some do not worship Mot because ancient offering evidence is absent;
- some honor him as death’s inevitability;
- others incorporate him into seasonal rituals based on the Baal Cycle.

All such liturgies are **modern reconstructions or inventions**, even when closely based on ancient text. No continuous community links them ritually to Ugarit.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Comparative religion and the “dying and rising god”

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century comparative religion often treated Baal’s death as an annual vegetation drama and placed Baal beside Osiris, Adonis, Attis, Tammuz, and Jesus. Mot became the personified dry season or harvested grain.

Later scholarship criticized this model for:

- forcing diverse narratives into one seasonal template;
- assuming annual ritual performance without evidence;
- treating the poem as a transparent script for agriculture;
- ignoring kingship and divine-family politics;
- describing Baal’s return with later theological language.

The agricultural dimension is real; its elevation into a universal key is the disputed part.

### Biblical theology

Christian readings frequently make Mot a prototype of “Death, the last enemy” defeated in resurrection. This is a legitimate later reception history, especially through Isaiah 25, Hosea 13, and 1 Corinthians 15, but it is not an attested Ugaritic prophecy of Christianity.

Claims that Anat’s treatment of Mot directly anticipates Christ’s death, Eucharistic bread, or resurrection are **modern theological constructions**.

### Occultism and demonology

Mot is notably absent from the principal medieval and early-modern European grimoire traditions. He is not an Ars Goetia spirit, not a Golden Dawn officer, and not an ancient demon with a transmitted seal.

Internet occult material assigning him:

- a planetary correspondence;
- a sigil;
- a “demonic enn”;
- a rank in Hell;
- colors, incense, crystals, tarot cards, or invocation days

is **modern invention** unless explicitly presented as a contemporary practitioner’s system.

### Neopagan reconstruction

Modern Canaanite reconstruction developed within the broader late twentieth- and twenty-first-century pagan revival. It uses Ugaritic translations, comparative ritual studies, and creative liturgy. There is no reliable global membership count.

A recurrent modern choice is to reenact Baal’s victory or return seasonally. This creatively interprets KTU 1.5–1.6 but cannot be called a recovered Ugaritic festival calendar.

### Nationalist reception

The twentieth-century Hebrew “Canaanite” movement used ancient Levantine myth to imagine a regional Hebrew identity distinct from diasporic Judaism. It was principally literary, artistic, and ideological, not a reconstructed cult of Mot. Later Israeli neopaganism is more religiously eclectic and much smaller. [Study of Canaanites and Israeli neopagans](https://static.hartman.org.il/hed/uploads/2022/12/Reshit_6_full.pdf)

There is no well-documented major extremist movement centered specifically on Mot. Generic claims that Mot is a symbol of modern racial, nationalist, or Satanist ideology require case-specific evidence.

### Games and popular media

The Japanese *Megami Tensei* franchise employs Mot as a summonable or antagonistic supernatural being, usually with an invented mummiform or sarcophagus-like visual design. In *Shin Megami Tensei III: Nocturne*, Mot belongs to the Assembly of Nihilo and appears at the Diet Building. [Megami Tensei entry](https://megatenwiki.com/wiki/Mot)

The design is a modern visual invention; no ancient image supports it. Its significance lies in giving a relatively obscure Ugaritic being a global popular identity as a “demon” or monster, classifications unlike the Ugaritic “son of El.”

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debates

### Editions and translations

The indispensable textual tools include:

- Manfried Dietrich, Oswald Loretz, and Joaquín Sanmartín, *Die keilalphabetischen Texte aus Ugarit*—the KTU numbering and transliteration corpus; its third edition appeared in 2013.
- Mark S. Smith, *The Ugaritic Baal Cycle*, volume I (KTU 1.1–1.2, 1994) and volume II (KTU 1.3–1.4, 2009), with text, translation, and extensive commentary. A corresponding full Smith/Pitard volume for 1.5–1.6 has long been awaited.
- Mark S. Smith and Wayne T. Pitard, work on the Baal Cycle and Ugaritic religion.
- Nicolas Wyatt, *Religious Texts from Ugarit*, a broad translation with philological notes.
- Simon B. Parker, ed., *Ugaritic Narrative Poetry*, including Mark Smith’s translation of the Baal material.
- J. C. L. Gibson, *Canaanite Myths and Legends*.
- Dennis Pardee, ritual and literary studies of the Ugaritic corpus.
- Gregorio del Olmo Lete and Joaquín Sanmartín, *A Dictionary of the Ugaritic Language in the Alphabetic Tradition*.

### Principal disputes

#### Was Mot worshipped?

The current evidence says **not demonstrably**. His absence from offering and god lists is striking. Counterarguments appeal to incantations, literary status, lost evidence, or the possibility that dangerous gods were avoided rather than fed. Those possibilities prevent an absolute claim that nobody worshipped him, but they do not establish a cult.

#### Is Mot a vegetation god?

Mot is associated with Baal’s disappearance and with agricultural processing when Anat destroys him. Yet no text calls him a crop god, and the seven-year interval is incompatible with a simple annual dry-season equation. “Embodiment of death whose myth uses agricultural imagery” is better supported.

#### Does Mot swallow Baal?

The poem prepares precisely that image, and later tradition remembers Mot as the devourer. The decisive lines are damaged or lost. “Mot causes or possesses Baal’s death, represented through swallowing imagery” is more exact than claiming that an intact passage describes ingestion.

#### Does Anat kill Mot permanently?

No. Mot speaks again in the seventh year and fights Baal. Anat’s act is total bodily destruction but not permanent ontological abolition.

#### Is Baal a dying-and-rising god?

Baal is announced dead and later alive, but the Frazerian category carries assumptions about annual fertility ritual, voluntary death, and resurrection pattern that the text does not necessarily share. Smith’s treatment explicitly reassesses that category. [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/12858/chapter-abstract/163142589)

#### Does Isaiah name the god Mot?

Hebrew *māwet* is the ordinary word “death,” and Isaiah personifies it. The swallowing reversal makes comparison with Ugaritic Mot compelling, but Isaiah does not introduce a foreign proper name or acknowledge Mot as an independently existing god. “Mythic personification drawing on the same West Semitic complex” is the cautious formulation.

#### Are Philo’s Muth and Ugaritic Mot identical?

Philo’s identification with Death/Pluto and the Northwest Semitic name support historical relationship. But more than a millennium, language change, Greek interpretation, and an indirect transmission chain separate them. Philo is evidence of a later Phoenician conception, not a verbatim preservation of KTU 1.5.

#### Is Philo’s primordial Mōt the same being?

Not securely. The cosmogonic mixture called Μώτ and the divine child Μοὺθ occur in different functions and spellings. Conflation is possible but cannot be assumed.

#### Does the myth describe human sacrifice?

No surviving Mot passage prescribes human sacrifice. Baal’s descent is described in sacrificial or alimentary language, but that is divine narrative. Carthaginian tophet evidence belongs to Tanit, Baal Hammon, and *mlk* rites, not Mot.

### Evidence lost or endangered

- Roughly half of the Baal Cycle is physically missing.
- Unique witnesses prevent comparison among complete ancient manuscripts.
- Some tablets from Ugarit are dispersed among Syrian and French collections.
- Archaeological disruption and damage in modern Syria have complicated access and conservation.
- Ugaritic vocalization is reconstructed because alphabetic cuneiform normally records consonants.
- The Phoenician work attributed to Sanchuniathon is lost; only Greek fragments mediated through later authors survive.

### Discredited or weak theories still circulating

- Mot was universally worshipped throughout all Canaan and Carthage.
- Mot and Moloch are the same god.
- The Carthage tophet was Mot’s temple.
- The Baal–Mot battle was demonstrably reenacted every year.
- Baal remains dead for exactly seven years.
- Mot is Satan, a fallen angel, or a Goetic demon.
- Every biblical use of “death” names Mot.
- Philo’s text is an untouched Bronze Age Phoenician scripture.
- A modern skeletal or sarcophagus depiction reproduces ancient Mot iconography.
- Mot has an ancient sigil, planet, feast day, or fixed magical correspondence.

## 11. Sources and research assessment

### Primary-source base

The defensible dossier rests chiefly on:

1. KTU 1.4–1.6;
2. the parallel KTU 1.133;
3. possible incantation attestations such as KTU 1.82;
4. Hebrew poetic personifications, especially Hosea 13:14 and Isaiah 25:8;
5. Philo of Byblos as quoted by Eusebius.

The Amarna letters, Kirta, Aqhat, Phoenician-Punic inscriptions, Herodotus, Lucian, Josephus, and Carthaginian tophet reports are valuable chiefly because they delimit the evidence: they do not supply the missing Mot cult that popular reconstructions often assume.

### Overall finding

Mot has an unusually asymmetrical historical record. He is among the most vivid personalities in surviving Ugaritic myth, but almost invisible in administrative religion. He possesses a throne yet no known temple, a divine genealogy yet no known worshippers, and one of ancient West Asia’s most memorable bodies yet no securely identified image.

His longest historical continuity is therefore not institutional but **poetic**:

- Ugaritic Death swallows gods;
- biblical poets transform devouring Death into YHWH’s victim or agent;
- Philo translates Phoenician Muth into Greek Thanatos and Pluto;
- modern scholarship reconstructs the damaged tablets;
- contemporary religion and popular culture invent new rites and images around the recovered name.

That asymmetry—not a harmonized “Canaanite god of death” biography—is Mot’s reconstructable history.
