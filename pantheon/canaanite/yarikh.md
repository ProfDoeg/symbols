# Yarikh: Research Dossier

> **Class:** male lunar god in Amorite and Ugaritic tradition; only indirectly recoverable in later Phoenician, Punic, Ammonite, and Israelite evidence.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Old Babylonian Amorite evidence, approximately nineteenth–eighteenth century BCE. The clearest early witness is the divine name **Yaraḫum**, explicitly equated with Mesopotamian **Sîn** in an Amorite–Akkadian vocabulary; roughly contemporary Amorite personal names also contain the element. The first substantial narrative, ritual, and theological corpus is the thirteenth-century BCE archive of Ugarit.

## Evidentiary labels

- **Securely attested early material:** contemporary Bronze Age texts or artifacts that name Yarikh/Yaraḫum.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** a later text within a continuous literary tradition.
- **Regional variant:** evidence specific to one city, language, or population.
- **Learned systematization:** ancient or modern attempts to arrange gods into equivalences or genealogies.
- **Syncretistic identification:** an explicit or probable historical equation with another deity.
- **Late accretion:** post-Bronze-Age material not demonstrably continuous with Ugaritic tradition.
- **Folklore:** later local tradition without contemporary ancient documentation.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference from language, iconography, or comparative religion.
- **Modern invention:** a recent claim presented as ancient without adequate ancient evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

**Yarikh** is the conventional English spelling of Ugaritic **𐎊𐎗𐎃**, *yrḫ*, conventionally vocalized **Yariḫu**. The same consonants denote the moon, a lunar month, and the god who embodies the moon. He is grammatically and mythologically male. At Ugarit he is a recognized member of the pantheon, recipient of sacrifices, “luminary of the heavens,” aristocratic suitor of Nikkal, and participant in at least one divine banquet.

The ordinary noun and divine name cannot always be distinguished. Context, a divine determinative in syllabic writing, theophoric syntax, divine epithets, or placement in a god list must determine whether a passage means “moon,” “month,” or “Yarikh.”

### Forms of the name

| Form | Language/script | Meaning and status |
|---|---|---|
| **𐎊𐎗𐎃, yrḫ** | Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform | “Moon,” “month,” and the divine name. Securely attested. |
| **Yariḫu / Yariḫ** | Scholarly vocalization | Conventional reconstruction of the Ugaritic name; vowels are not written in ordinary Ugaritic alphabetic script. |
| **Yaraḫum** | Amorite, preserved in syllabic cuneiform | Early Amorite name of the moon god. An Amorite–Akkadian lexical text equates Yaraḫum with Sîn. |
| **Yeraḥ / Yerah** | Conventional Northwest Semitic/Hebrew representation | Used in discussions of Hebrew **יָרֵחַ**, “moon,” and the divine element in names and toponyms. |
| **𐤉𐤓𐤇, yrḥ** | Phoenician script | “Month” or “moon”; in personal names it can be the divine element Yeraḥ/Yarikh. |
| **Arakh / Erakh** | Older scholarly spellings | Attempts to represent forms affected by the loss or weakening of initial *y/w* or by Akkadian orthography. They are not separately attested Ugaritic gods. |
| **Warḫum** | Akkadian cognate | Primarily “month.” Cognate with *yrḫ*, but not itself normally Yarikh’s divine name. |
| **יָרֵחַ, yārēaḥ** | Biblical Hebrew | “Moon.” It is not used unambiguously as Yarikh’s proper divine name in the Hebrew Bible. |
| **יֶרַח, yeraḥ** | Biblical Hebrew | “Month”; related to the lunar cycle. |

The Semitic root is secure: compare Akkadian *warḫum*, Hebrew *yeraḥ/yārēaḥ*, Phoenician *yrḥ*, and Aramaic *yrḥ*. What is uncertain is whether the divine name preceded the common noun, arose through personification of the moon, or represents a distinction that cannot be recovered from the surviving evidence. The recent edition of the Amorite–Akkadian vocabularies treats **Yaraḫum** as the Semitic moon god and explicitly notes the equation with Sîn and the name’s occurrence in Amorite personal names. [George and Krebernik, “Two Remarkable Vocabularies: Amorite-Akkadian Bilinguals!”](https://shs.cairn.info/revue-d-assyriologie-2022-1-page-113?lang=en&tab=texte-integral)

### Epithets and titles

- **nyr šmm**, “lamp/luminary of the heavens.”  
  **Securely attested early material:** KTU 1.24, the wedding poem. It identifies Yarikh through his celestial radiance rather than merely assuming the reader knows that *yrḫ* is the moon.

- **zbl yrḫ**, “Prince Yarikh” or “His Highness Yarikh.”  
  **Securely attested early material:** the title *zbl* appears in Ugaritic literary diction and marks high divine rank. It does not make Yarikh a son of Baal or El.

- **nʿmn ʾilm**, approximately “most gracious/pleasant/handsome of the gods.”  
  **Securely attested early material:** KTU 1.24. In context it emphasizes the suitor’s desirability or beauty.

- **yrḫ kṯy**, often rendered “Kassite Yarikh.”  
  **Modern scholarly reconstruction from an ancient text:** Dennis Pardee interprets this as a localized or foreign hypostasis, possibly reflecting Kassite religious vocabulary. The reading does not establish an independent narrative deity.

- **Yarikh of Larugadu**, effectively a localization rather than a formal epithet.  
  KTU 1.100 sends an invocation to Yarikh at **lrgt**, usually vocalized Larugadu. Identification with Eblaite Arugadu is plausible but not certain.

Descriptions such as “lord of the moon,” “guardian of travelers,” “measurer of time,” or “lord of the night” are common in modern devotional pages. Some are reasonable descriptions of a lunar god; they are not demonstrable Ugaritic cult titles unless accompanied by a tablet reference.

### Equations with other lunar gods

- **Sîn/Nanna:** An Amorite–Akkadian vocabulary explicitly equates Yaraḫum with Sîn. This is a **secure ancient learned equivalence**, not proof that the two gods always possessed identical cults or myths.

- **Kušuḫ:** The Hurrian moon god is functionally comparable and may have influenced KTU 1.24. Some scholars have proposed that the wedding poem adapts a Hurrian myth. Yet KTU 1.111 lists Kušuḫ, Nikkal, and Yarikh separately and gives offerings to both moon gods. The evidence therefore supports coexistence and partial equivalence, not complete identity.

- **Arma:** Anatolian lunar equivalence is possible in multilingual Late Bronze Age religious interpretation, but no surviving Ugaritic text directly says “Yarikh is Arma.”

- **Saḥr/Śahr:** The Aramaic and Arabian lunar god **Śahr** is not etymologically identical with *yrḫ*. Treating the names as interchangeable is a modern conflation.

- **Yarhibol:** Palmyrene theophoric forms with *yrḥ* generally belong to Yarhibol, historically a distinct Palmyrene god with strongly solar characteristics. Yarhibol should not be used as evidence for a continuous cult of Yarikh.

- **Selene/Luna:** No surviving Greek or Roman source securely identifies Yarikh by name with Selene or Luna. Pseudo-Lucian calls Sidonian Astarte a lunar goddess, illustrating a different Greco-Roman lunar interpretation, not the survival of Yarikh under Astarte’s name. [Pseudo-Lucian, *De Dea Syria* §§3–4](https://lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=home%3Atexts_and_library%3Aessays%3Athe-syrian-goddess)

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest secure evidence

#### Amorite Yaraḫum

Two unprovenanced Old Babylonian tablets published by Andrew George and Manfred Krebernik preserve Amorite words and divine names alongside Akkadian equivalents. They are conventionally dated around the early second millennium BCE, approximately 1800 BCE. One entry equates **Yaraḫum** with **Sîn**.

This is the earliest presently available explicit identification of the West Semitic name as a moon god. The tablets do not preserve a myth, temple, ritual, or genealogy. They demonstrate:

1. the divine name existed in an Amorite linguistic setting;
2. learned scribes could equate it with Sîn;
3. the Ugaritic god was not invented at Ugarit.

They do **not** prove that all Amorites shared a single pantheon or that every early personal name containing *yrḫ* refers unambiguously to the god rather than the moon.

Old Babylonian personal names from Mari, Eshnunna, and other Amorite environments furnish approximately contemporary onomastic evidence. Names are valuable evidence for family devotion, but a name by itself supplies little information about myth or institutional cult.

#### The Ugaritic corpus

The largest direct corpus comes from Ras Shamra, ancient Ugarit, principally thirteenth-century BCE tablets written in alphabetic Ugaritic cuneiform. Most were deposited during the city’s final Late Bronze Age centuries and buried when Ugarit was destroyed around the beginning of the twelfth century BCE.

### Principal texts

#### KTU 1.24 = RS 5.194: Marriage of Nikkal and Yarikh

- **Language:** Ugaritic.
- **Support:** clay tablet.
- **Find:** Ras Shamra; discovered during the fifth excavation campaign in 1933.
- **Date:** thirteenth century BCE, with composition or antecedents possibly earlier.
- **Contents:** Yarikh’s desire for Nikkal, negotiation with her father Ḫirḫibi, offer of bridewealth, rejection of an alternative bride, insistence upon Nikkal, and a concluding hymn involving the Kotharat.
- **Status:** securely attested early narrative and the principal source for Yarikh’s mythology.

The excavation context belongs to Claude Schaeffer’s 1933 campaign. [Schaeffer, Montet, and Virolleaud, excavation report](https://www.persee.fr/doc/syria_0039-7946_1934_num_15_2_3736)

#### KTU 1.114: El’s banquet

- **Language:** Ugaritic.
- **Date:** Late Bronze Age.
- **Contents:** At El’s feast Yarikh appears or behaves like a dog beneath the tables. Anat and Athtart recognize or favor him and give him select meat; gods who do not recognize him strike or prod him. A house-porter objects. El’s intoxication and a prescription involving dog hair dominate the rest of the text.
- **Status:** securely attested early, but philologically difficult. Interpretations range from comic myth and ritual burlesque to an etiological story attached to a hangover remedy.

The text does not explain why Yarikh is canine. Claims that the dog is Yarikh’s sacred animal or that Yarikh regularly transforms into a dog go beyond this one enigmatic episode.

#### KTU 1.100: anti-snakebite historiola/incantation

A divine or mythic appeal travels among divine centers seeking aid against snake venom. Yarikh is invoked at **lrgt**, Larugadu. The text makes that locality his mythic or cultic seat. It does not prove that archaeologists have identified a temple of Yarikh there.

#### KTU 1.111 = RS 24.255

A mixed Hurro-Ugaritic sacrificial and betrothal rite. Kušuḫ, Nikkal, and Yarikh receive offerings; the two male lunar gods remain grammatically distinct. This is decisive against a simple equation in which every occurrence of Kušuḫ at Ugarit is merely another spelling of Yarikh.

#### KTU 1.109 = RS 24.253 and related ritual texts

Yarikh receives animal offerings within the royal sacrificial calendar. One sequence gives him a ram after Baal of Ugarit and Baal of Aleppo and before offerings to Anat of Ṣapanu. Another rite provides two bulls at or around the full moon. These texts document cult, not mythology.

Pardee’s edition emphasizes that Ugaritic ritual tablets typically record recipient, offering, date, and sometimes location, while saying little about sacrificial technique or theological purpose. [Dennis Pardee, *Ritual and Cult at Ugarit*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Ritual_and_Cult_at_Ugarit.html?id=8zUvAAAAYAAJ); [Pardee, “Animal Sacrifice at Ugarit”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/topoi_1764-0733_2000_act_2_1_2810?pageId=T1_322)

#### God lists: KTU 1.47, 1.118 and parallels

Yarikh appears in formal enumerations of the pantheon. In one standard sequence he follows the Kotharat and precedes Ṣapanu; in another arrangement he follows Yam/Baal and precedes Kothar. Position in a list indicates recognized divine status but should not automatically be converted into a genealogy or exact rank.

#### Kirta, KTU 1.14–1.16

A damaged passage in the Kirta epic may name Yarikh among gods invited to Kirta’s wedding feast, with the title *zbl*. The reconstruction depends partly upon parallelism and restorations in broken lines. It is therefore **probable early material**, not textually complete proof. The current Göttingen edition displays the proposed restoration and its alternatives. [Edition of the Ugaritic poetic corpus](https://eupt.uni-goettingen.de/edition.html?tido=m1_i0)

#### Aqhat, KTU 1.17–1.19

Yarikh is mentioned in the wider Aqhat corpus or associated textual discussions, but he has no securely preserved independent narrative role comparable to Anat, Danel, Aqhat, or Baal. Assertions that Yarikh actively causes Aqhat’s death, resurrection, or the drought are unsupported.

#### Baal Cycle, KTU 1.1–1.6

Yarikh is not a major actor in the preserved Baal Cycle. Lunar terminology and reconstructed fragments have sometimes drawn him into broader interpretations, but the surviving story is centered on Baal, El, Yam, Anat, Athtart, Shapash, Athtar, and Mot. Yarikh neither fights Baal nor supplies a missing lunar phase of the Baal–Mot combat in any securely preserved passage.

### Amarna letters

The fourteenth-century BCE Amarna correspondence documents Canaanite and Syrian cities, rulers, and personal names. Ugarit itself appears in the broader Akkadian diplomatic record. The letters do not furnish a surviving narrative or explicit cult description of Yarikh. Personal names or place names containing cognate lunar elements may be relevant, but each requires individual linguistic analysis.

It is therefore inaccurate to cite “the Amarna letters” generally as though they contain the wedding of Yarikh or identify Jericho as his sanctuary.

### Hebrew Bible

The Hebrew Bible never securely names Yarikh as a personal god. It is a **hostile or reforming witness to lunar worship**, not a source for Yarikh’s Ugaritic mythology:

- Deuteronomy 4:19 and 17:3 prohibit worship of sun, moon, and heavenly host.
- 2 Kings 23:5 attributes to Josiah the removal of priests who made offerings to Baal, sun, moon, constellations, and heavenly host.
- Jeremiah 8:2 envisages the remains of Jerusalem’s inhabitants exposed before the sun, moon, and host they served.
- Job 31:26–28 treats gestures of reverence toward sun and moon as religious betrayal.
- Ezekiel 8:16 describes solar rather than specifically lunar worship.
- Genesis 1:14–18 famously calls sun and moon “greater” and “lesser lights,” avoiding the ordinary divine names; interpreting this as deliberate demythologization is plausible but remains literary analysis.

These passages establish lunar cult or anxiety about it in Israelite/Judahite religious discourse. They cannot identify the worshiped moon specifically as Yarikh rather than Sîn, a generic personified moon, or another lunar power.

### Later Phoenician and classical texts

No extant passage in Philo of Byblos, as preserved by Eusebius, names Yarikh. Philo’s cosmogony includes sun, moon, stars, and Phoenician gods, but importing the Ugaritic Yarikh into every lunar reference would be circular.

Herodotus does not preserve a Yarikh cult or equation.

Pseudo-Lucian’s *De Dea Syria*, probably second century CE, calls Sidonian Astarte the moon but also reports that a Sidonian priest identified the temple with Europa. This conflicting testimony is valuable for Roman-period interpretation of Phoenician cult, but it supplies no historical bridge from Astarte to Yarikh.

Josephus refers to Jericho extensively but does not document an active cult of Yarikh there.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

#### Moon and nocturnal light

**Securely attested early material.** Yarikh is the moon itself and the “lamp of heaven.” His waxing and waning supplied the observable basis of the month, although no text presents a systematic Ugaritic lunar theology.

#### Calendar and time

**Strong inference from language and ritual.** The common noun *yrḫ* means “month”; Ugaritic sacrificial schedules operate on lunar dates; full-moon rites include Yarikh. This makes calendrical regulation inherent in his celestial identity. No surviving Ugaritic text calls him “lord of time.”

#### Marriage, sexuality, and fertility

**Secure narrative association; broader function partly reconstructed.** KTU 1.24 makes him an ardent suitor and husband. Its bridewealth, marriage negotiation, Nikkal’s association with fruit, and concluding Kotharat hymn link the moon with marriage and reproduction. Whether the tablet was recited at human weddings is plausible, not proven.

#### Birth and gestation

**Modern scholarly reconstruction based on KTU 1.24 and lunar periodicity.** The Kotharat are birth and marriage goddesses, but they are not securely Yarikh’s daughters. Modern lists that call them “the seven daughters of Yarikh” conflate association with genealogy.

#### Underworld or daytime subterranean passage

Some scholars infer that the moon spends daylight in the underworld or that Yarikh has chthonic associations, especially through new-moon disappearance. This is **modern reconstruction**; no preserved myth narrates his daily underworld journey. Claims that he is a psychopomp or gatekeeper are speculative.

### Powers and limitations

The sources attribute light, divine rank, wealth sufficient for extraordinary bridewealth, movement between divine centers, and the ability to participate in ritual and feasting. They do not preserve miracles, battle exploits, cosmic creation, judgment of the dead, or a doctrine of omniscience.

### Iconography

No surviving image is securely identified by inscription as Yarikh. Consequently:

- the lunar crescent is a likely emblem of lunar divinity but not an automatic portrait of Yarikh;
- anthropomorphic male moon gods standing in or beneath a crescent are known elsewhere in Syria and Mesopotamia, but individual examples may represent Sîn, Kušuḫ, Arma, or a local god;
- Ugaritic seals and stelae containing crescents provide evidence for lunar symbolism, not necessarily a named Yarikh cult;
- Punic stelae frequently show crescents and disks, but their inscriptions normally invoke **Baal Hammon and Tanit**, not Yarikh.

It has sometimes been argued that Punic crescents make Baal Hammon a moon god or represent an otherwise unnamed Yarikh. Neither conclusion follows securely. Astral symbols may signal the celestial sphere, divine presence, or several gods.

### Animals, plants, colors, numbers, and days

- **Dog:** associated with Yarikh only in KTU 1.114. It is not securely his cult animal.
- **Bull/cattle:** bulls were offered to him. Lunar horns and bovine horns are visually comparable, but no Ugaritic text calls a bull his sacred animal.
- **Fruit/orchards:** belong more securely to Nikkal-wa-Ib and the nuptial setting.
- **Silver:** prominent in his proposed bridewealth and conventionally lunar in later symbolism, but not securely identified as his sacred metal.
- **Number seven:** lunar phases and the seven Kotharat encourage comparison. No Ugaritic text explicitly assigns seven as Yarikh’s number.
- **Colors:** none securely attested.
- **Named weekday:** none securely attested.
- **Plant:** none securely attested.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

No surviving Ugaritic text securely names Yarikh’s parents, siblings, or children.

The frequently repeated claim that he is a son of **El and Athirat/Asherah** is a **modern systematization** based on the idea that the Ugaritic gods collectively form El’s household. It is not an explicit genealogy. Being included among the gods or “sons of El” as a class is not the same as a text stating that El and Athirat biologically produced Yarikh.

His only securely attested marital relationship is with **Nikkal-wa-Ib** in KTU 1.24. The narrative establishes betrothal or marriage; it does not preserve the birth of a named child.

### Nikkal’s identity

Nikkal derives historically from Mesopotamian **Ningal**, “Great Lady,” wife of Nanna/Sîn. At Ugarit her expanded name **Nikkal-wa-Ib** is commonly interpreted as “Nikkal and Fruit” or “Great Lady and Fruit.” The marriage therefore joins a West Semitic male moon god to a goddess inherited through Mesopotamian and Hurrian cultural transmission.

This is a particularly clear case where syncretism did not simply erase identities: Ugaritic Yarikh, Hurrian Kušuḫ, Mesopotamian Sîn, and Nikkal could be correlated while remaining separately named in ritual.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The Marriage of Nikkal and Yarikh

#### The story

The poem opens by announcing a song of **Nikkal-wa-Ib**, daughter of **Ḫirḫibi**, “king of summer” or “king of the summer fruit” and “king of marriage.” At sunset Yarikh becomes inflamed with desire. The language joins celestial appearance—the moon rising when the sun sets—with sexual and nuptial imagery.

Yarikh sends to Ḫirḫibi and declares his wish to marry Nikkal. He offers lavish bridewealth: great quantities of silver and gold, lapis lazuli, and agricultural produce. The exact numerals and commodities vary among translations because of damage and lexical uncertainty, but the social structure is clear: this is a formal marriage negotiation, not an abduction.

Ḫirḫibi tests or redirects the suitor. He proposes other divine brides, most importantly **Pidray**, associated with Baal. Yarikh refuses the substitution and insists: “With Nikkal is my marriage.” His insistence distinguishes the poem from myths in which a goddess is interchangeable political property.

The marriage is accepted. The latter portion turns toward the **Kotharat**, the goddesses associated with marriage, pregnancy, and childbirth, and includes a difficult passage involving a human female, often read as Prbkṯ. Whether this is one integrated drama, a myth followed by a wedding hymn, or two compositions copied together remains debated.

#### Development and cultural setting

- **Early attested core:** the thirteenth-century BCE tablet.
- **Hurrian/Mesopotamian background:** Nikkal derives from Ningal; her usual divine husband elsewhere is Sîn or his Hurrian counterpart Kušuḫ.
- **West Semitic adaptation:** Yarikh occupies the husband’s role and speaks in Ugaritic poetic diction.
- **Disputed origin:** the poem’s unusual style and divine configuration have led scholars to propose a Hurrian Vorlage or strong Hurrian mediation. This is plausible cultural history, not proof of a lost Hurrian original.
- **Ritual function:** recitation at a wedding or seasonal fertility rite is frequently proposed. No colophon states the performance occasion.

A modern English adaptation and bibliographic guide identifies the tablet as KTU 1.24/RS 5.194 but openly acknowledges taking poetic liberties; it is useful for orientation, not as a critical edition. [Sapiru Project, “The Wedding of Yarikh & Nikkal”](https://sapiru.wordpress.com/2025/07/03/the-wedding-of-yarikh-nikkal/)

### Yarikh as the dog beneath El’s table

KTU 1.114 stages a divine drinking feast. Yarikh is under the tables in canine form or described through canine behavior. Anat and Athtart feed him good meat; other gods mistreat him because they do not know him. A porter protests that the goddesses are feeding a dog. The poem then concentrates on El becoming drunk, collapsing, and suffering the aftermath. Its final medical instructions apparently include dog hair.

Competing explanations include:

1. **Comic myth:** a carnivalesque representation of divine drunkenness.
2. **Ritual etiological tale:** a myth explaining why dog hair entered a remedy.
3. **Lunar symbolism:** the moon at disappearance or humiliation, subsequently restored.
4. **Incognito guest motif:** gods are judged by how they treat an unrecognized divine visitor.
5. **Textual misreading:** “dog” may describe posture or abuse rather than literal transformation.

Only the banquet, canine language, feeding, mistreatment, and remedy are securely textual. A general doctrine of Yarikh as a shapeshifter is not.

### The anti-venom journey

KTU 1.100 belongs to the class of incantations that narrate a crisis and invoke gods at their own seats. Yarikh is addressed at Larugadu. The text’s purpose is practical protection or healing from snakebite; Yarikh is one member of a geographic divine network rather than the sole healer. It supplies the strongest textual basis for Larugadu as his center.

### The Kirta wedding feast

In a reconstructed portion of the Kirta epic, Yarikh may be invited with El, Baal, Kothar, and Resheph to Kirta’s wedding feast. Because the line is damaged, it must be labeled **probable reconstruction**. If correct, it reinforces Yarikh’s connection with weddings but does not make him the officiant.

### No surviving lunar epic

There is no surviving “Yarikh cycle” comparable to the Baal Cycle. Modern retellings sometimes assemble his wedding, lunar phases, an underworld descent, and battle with darkness into a continuous myth. That synthesis is a **modern invention from comparative motifs**.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Ugarit

Yarikh appears in formal deity lists and sacrificial schedules, demonstrating institutional recognition. Relevant rites include:

- two bulls at a full-moon celebration;
- a ram in a sequence involving the two Baals and Anat;
- a ram in another list between the Kotharat and Athtar;
- coordinated offerings with Nikkal;
- offerings alongside, but distinctly from, Kušuḫ.

The Ugaritic ritual corpus links sacrifices to the lunar month. Pardee’s analysis stresses that these texts rarely explain the offering’s meaning. Fertility, calendrical renewal, or royal legitimation may be inferred only where the text supports them.

### Larugadu

KTU 1.100 locates Yarikh at **lrgt**. Scholars compare it with Arugadu/Larugadu in the Ebla region. Earlier Eblaite evidence associates that place with Hadabal, not explicitly Yarikh. Proposed explanations include:

- a later Amorite introduction of Yarikh;
- succession of cults at the same sanctuary;
- mistaken identification of the place names;
- an earlier lunar character for Hadabal.

No excavated building can presently be labeled “Temple of Yarikh at Larugadu.”

### Ugaritic temple problem

The great excavated temples at Ugarit are conventionally assigned to Baal and Dagan, with other sanctuaries and cult rooms known. No temple inscription securely identifies a separate building of Yarikh. His sacrifices may have occurred in a royal, palace, or multi-deity cultic system rather than an exclusive temple.

### Priesthood

No securely identified office “priest of Yarikh” is known from the Ugaritic evidence cited here. The king is the most visible human ritual actor in the prescriptive texts. Inferring a specialized lunar priesthood from Mesopotamian parallels would be unwarranted.

### Festivals and calendar

The full moon was ritually significant, and Ugaritic calendars were lunar. Whether KTU 1.24 belonged to a specific full-moon wedding festival is unknown. Yarikh’s name does not supply a securely reconstructed annual festival title.

### Theophoric personal names

Amorite names containing Yaraḫ/Yariḫ provide strong evidence for household devotion. In later Northwest Semitic sources the same element can be divine, lunar, or calendrical and must be judged name by name.

Phoenician and Punic evidence is especially thin:

- no known monumental Phoenician inscription from Byblos, Sidon, or Tyre dedicates a sanctuary directly to Yarikh;
- Punic inscriptions do preserve the personal name **ʿbdyrḥ**, “servant of Yeraḥ/Yarikh”;
- **yrḥ** by itself can be a hypocoristic personal name;
- calendrical occurrences of *yrḥ*, “month,” are not evidence for the god.

Krahmalkov’s dictionary distinguishes the common noun “moon” from the divine element and records **ʿbd-yrḥ**. [Krahmalkov, *Phoenician-Punic Dictionary*](https://digitalthought.info/Phoenician-Punic-Dictionary.pdf) Frank Benz’s catalogue remains a basic collection of Phoenician and Punic personal names. [Benz, *Personal Names in the Phoenician and Punic Inscriptions*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Personal_Names_in_the_Phoenician_and_Pun.html?hl=en&id=xhdgYn-dVx4C&output=html_text)

### Phoenician and Punic lunar evidence

The Phoenician Karatepe inscription invokes or names sun and moon in a cosmic witness formula, but it does not securely preserve Yarikh as a personal recipient of worship. Lunar crescents on Punic stelae likewise cannot override inscriptions naming Tanit and Baal Hammon.

### Carthage tophet and human sacrifice

The Carthage tophet is relevant because the brief requires examination of Punic religion and contested sacrifice. It is **not evidence for a cult of Yarikh**.

The precinct contained urns with cremated remains of infants, young children, and animals and stelae dedicated primarily to Tanit and Baal Hammon. Interpretations remain disputed:

- Stager, Smith, Xella, and others argue that archaeological, epigraphic, osteological, and classical evidence supports sacrificial killing in at least some cases.
- Schwartz and collaborators argue that age distributions are compatible with perinatal mortality and a special cemetery for infants who died naturally.
- Others accept a mixed institution: cemetery, votive precinct, animal substitution, and occasional human sacrifice.

A peer-reviewed osteological argument against systematic infant sacrifice is available in Schwartz et al. [“Skeletal Remains from Punic Carthage Do Not Support Systematic Sacrifice of Infants”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2822869/). The later methodological exchange emphasizes that the same evidence can support different historical models. [“Two Tales of One City,” *Antiquity*](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/two-tales-of-one-city-data-inference-and-carthaginian-infant-sacrifice/5006E240CB75A1E324B3230F6DA17389)

No published tophet dedication cited in this dossier names Yarikh. Consequently:

> **There is no evidence that children, adults, or animals were sacrificed specifically to Yarikh at Carthage.**

Attaching the tophet to him merely because he is called “Punic” in modern pantheon lists is a modern conflation.

### Temple prostitution

No Ugaritic or Phoenician text establishes temple prostitution in Yarikh’s cult. KTU 1.24 is a divine marriage narrative, not evidence that sexual commerce was performed in a temple. Broad claims about “sacred prostitution” in Canaanite religion have often rested on tendentious translations and classical stereotypes; none can be specifically connected to Yarikh.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Early second millennium BCE: Amorite Yaraḫum

The Amorite god appears in names and in an explicit equation with Sîn. He seems to have been an important family and regional god alongside storm gods such as Hadad. The evidence is dispersed among personal names and lexical scholarship rather than a surviving mythological library.

### Fourteenth–thirteenth centuries BCE: Ugaritic florescence

At Ugarit, Yarikh enters a fully documented pantheon:

- narrative: KTU 1.24 and 1.114;
- magic: KTU 1.100;
- ritual: KTU 1.109, 1.111, and related lists;
- theology: formal pantheon lists;
- onomastics: lunar theophoric names.

This is the evidentiary peak—not necessarily proof of the historical peak of worship, but the period with the richest surviving documentation.

Ugarit’s multilingual society explains the coexistence of Yarikh, Kušuḫ, Nikkal, and Sîn. The evidence shows translation and coordination rather than a single homogenized moon god.

### Around 1200 BCE: destruction of Ugarit

Ugarit was destroyed in the eastern Mediterranean upheavals at the end of the Bronze Age. Its palace and temple archives ceased. No evidence shows that a corporate Ugaritic Yarikh priesthood survived elsewhere. The disappearance of the archive creates a documentation break, not necessarily an immediate end to all popular lunar worship.

### Iron Age Levant

Direct references become rare. Evidence survives chiefly through:

- lunar vocabulary;
- place names;
- personal names;
- astral symbols;
- biblical prohibitions of lunar worship;
- foreign lunar cults, especially Sîn at Harran.

An Ammonite royal name **yrḥʿzr**, “Yarikh/Yeraḥ has helped,” appears on a statue base of approximately 700 BCE. This is good evidence that the divine name remained usable east of the Jordan. It does not establish the full Ugaritic mythology in Ammon.

### Phoenician and Punic worlds

The divine name is conspicuously absent from the chief surviving royal and monumental cult inscriptions. Theophoric **ʿbdyrḥ** and related names imply continuing recognition, but Yarikh was far less visible than Baal Hammon, Tanit, Melqart, Eshmun, Astarte, or Baalat Gebal.

Calling him a major “Phoenician and Punic god” is therefore misleading. A more accurate formulation is:

> Yarikh was a major documented Ugaritic moon god whose name or memory survived marginally in later Northwest Semitic onomastics.

### Israel and Judah

Lunar worship is documented by polemical biblical texts, iconography, and names, but its divine identities are uncertain. The late monarchic and exilic prominence of Mesopotamian Sîn, especially through Assyrian and Harranian influence, means that not every biblical moon cult should be assigned to indigenous Yarikh.

### Hellenistic and Roman periods

Greek and Roman writers recategorized Levantine gods through Selene, Artemis, Aphrodite, Astarte, and other familiar figures. No continuous named Yarikh cult can be demonstrated. Pseudo-Lucian’s lunar Astarte is evidence for ancient interpretatio, not proof that Astarte absorbed Yarikh.

### Christianization and Islamization

No source records a targeted suppression, temple closure, martyrdom, or forced conversion specifically involving Yarikh. General Christianization of Syria and Phoenicia and later Islamization eliminated or transformed traditional polytheistic institutions, but Yarikh had already disappeared as a clearly documented public cult.

Claims that the Islamic crescent derives from Yarikh, that Allah was originally Yarikh, or that the Kaaba housed Yarikh are **modern polemical inventions** without ancient evidence.

### Present

No demonstrable institution maintains an unbroken cult of Yarikh from antiquity. Modern worship is reconstructionist: practitioners use published Ugaritic texts, comparative Semitic religion, lunar observance, and personal innovation. Such worship may be sincere, but it is historically a revival rather than documented continuity.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Rediscovery

The rediscovery of Ugarit in 1928–1929 and the excavation of its archives transformed Yarikh from a faint element in comparative Semitic vocabulary into a narratively recoverable god. The discovery of RS 5.194 in 1933 made the marriage to Nikkal central to his modern image.

### Scholarship and translation

Charles Virolleaud first published important Ugaritic texts. Later editions and translations by Ginsberg, Driver, Gibson, Caquot and Sznycer, del Olmo Lete, de Moor, Parker, Wyatt, Pardee, Smith, and Pitard gradually refined the readings.

Gabriele Theuer’s 2000 monograph, *Der Mondgott in den Religionen Syrien-Palästinas*, is the most extensive focused study. It treats Ugaritic, Phoenician, Aramaic, Transjordanian, Israelite, and Judahite evidence and gives KTU 1.24 special attention. [Publisher description](https://isdistribution.com/work/der-mondgott-in-den-religionen-syrien-palastinas/)

### Occultism

Yarikh is absent from the major Renaissance planetary-magic and Solomonic grimoire traditions, which rely on Luna, Gabriel, Diana, Hecate, Sîn, or generalized lunar intelligences. He is not an established figure in:

- the *Picatrix*;
- Agrippa’s *Three Books of Occult Philosophy*;
- the Golden Dawn’s principal grade rituals;
- Crowley’s central pantheon;
- traditional Jewish angelology.

Recent online “grimoires” assigning Yarikh crystals, tarot cards, incense, colors, chakras, weekdays, or planetary seals are **modern inventions**, normally derived from generalized modern lunar correspondences.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist worship

Yarikh appears in Canaanite, Natib Qadish, Semitic reconstructionist, and eclectic pagan practice, largely since the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries. Common practices include:

- observation of new and full moons;
- reading KTU 1.24;
- offerings of wine, incense, silver-colored objects, or fruit;
- paired devotion to Yarikh and Nikkal.

Only the lunar cycle, marriage narrative, animal offerings, and Nikkal relationship have ancient support. Modern altar layouts, invocations, ethical teachings, and correspondence systems are reconstructed or newly composed. No reliable census establishes the number of worshipers.

### Popular mythology

Modern summaries often add unsupported details:

- **“Son of El and Asherah”** — inference presented as genealogy.
- **“Father of the Kotharat”** — unattested.
- **“Rides a chariot across the night sky”** — borrowed from other moon gods.
- **“Battles Mot each new moon”** — modern comparative synthesis.
- **“Patron of sailors”** — no direct Ugaritic evidence.
- **“Sacred animal: bull”** — plausible symbolism, not explicit cult fact.
- **“Jericho was his capital and contained his great temple”** — hypothesis presented as archaeological fact.
- **“Baal Hammon was Yarikh under another name”** — unsupported.
- **“Allah is Yarikh”** — modern anti-Islamic invention.
- **“Yarikh is a female moon goddess”** — confusion with Selene, Astarte, or modern Western gender symbolism.

### Literature, games, and commercial use

Compared with Baal, Astarte, Anat, and Moloch, Yarikh has a small modern fictional footprint. He appears mainly in mythology databases, role-playing settings, occult lists, devotional art, and self-published fiction. No single film, novel, comic, or game has decisively standardized his popular image.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debates

### Discovery and decipherment

Ugaritic was recognized as a Northwest Semitic language written in a cuneiform alphabet after discoveries at Ras Shamra beginning in 1929. Early editors sometimes treated every Ugaritic text as directly “Canaanite” or as immediate background to the Hebrew Bible. Later scholarship has emphasized Ugarit’s distinctive city, language, royal institutions, Hurrian population, and international setting.

### Dating KTU 1.24

The surviving tablet belongs to Late Bronze Age Ugarit. The poem may incorporate older West Semitic and Hurrian material, but linguistic or thematic archaism cannot supply an exact earlier date. A hypothetical Hurrian original has not been recovered.

### Meaning of Nikkal-wa-Ib

Nikkal is securely related to Ningal. *Ib* is often connected with “fruit,” making fertility central, but exact grammatical analysis varies. The designation should not be flattened into “goddess of orchards” as though that were her only Ugaritic office.

### Is Ḫirḫibi divine?

His epithets “king of summer” and “king of marriage” may designate a god, personified season, mythic ruler, or imported figure. His role as divine bride’s father favors supernatural status, but the text does not provide a conventional god-list biography.

### Hurrian influence

The presence of Nikkal and Kušuḫ and Ugarit’s Hurrian population support influence. Yet Yarikh’s West Semitic name and Amorite antecedents preclude treating him merely as a mistranslated Kušuḫ. KTU 1.111’s separation of the gods is crucial.

### Larugadu and Hadabal

The comparison of Ugaritic **lrgt** with Eblaite Arugadu is plausible. The proposed continuity from Eblaite Hadabal to Amorite Yarikh is not demonstrable. Hadabal’s supposed lunar character is itself partly inferred from the later association of the place with Yarikh, risking circular reasoning.

### Jericho’s name

Biblical Hebrew **יְרִיחוֹ**, *Yərīḥô*, is commonly connected either with:

1. the Semitic moon word/divine name *yrḫ*;
2. a root connected with fragrance or smell, compare Hebrew *rēaḥ*;
3. an Amorite tribal or place name Yariḫū, possibly itself lunar;
4. a Bronze Age name tentatively read “Ruha” on a scarab.

A recent sacred-landscape study calls the name a Hebraization of Yariḫ and reads the conquest ritual as possible lunar parody. That is an explicit **modern reconstruction**, not archaeological proof of a temple. [“Toward the Reconstruction of a Sacred Landscape of the Judean Highlands”](https://brill.com/view/journals/jane/21/1/article-p1_1.xml)

The lunar derivation is linguistically credible, and Jericho’s great antiquity makes preservation of an old toponym possible. What is missing is a Jericho inscription naming Yarikh, a dedicated temple, or a cult image. “Jericho was named for Yarikh” should therefore be stated as a leading hypothesis, not fact.

### Beth Yerah

**Beth Yeraḥ**, “House of the Moon/Yeraḥ,” is a stronger transparent lunar toponym. The name is known from later sources for the large site at Khirbet Kerak near the Sea of Galilee. It need not date to the Early Bronze Age occupation merely because that settlement is ancient; the antiquity of the *name* and the antiquity of the mound are separate questions. The Israel Antiquities Authority’s publication documents the Early Bronze Age settlement, not an excavated Yarikh temple. [IAA, *Bet Yerah: The Early Bronze Age Mound*](https://publications.iaa.org.il/iaareports/22/)

### Phoenician survival

The principal dispute is whether sparse theophoric names justify speaking of continued “worship of Yarikh.” A divine name in **ʿbdyrḥ** does show religious memory or devotion. It does not show a temple, priesthood, mythology, or widespread state cult.

### Biblical lunar religion

Gabriele Theuer argues for important lunar influence in Israel and Judah. Brian Schmidt and others urge caution: Assyrian/Harranian Sîn worship, generic astral worship, and literary polemic may account for much of the evidence. The biblical passages never supply the proper name Yarikh.

### Iconographic identification

A crescent may identify the moon but not which moon god. Depictions should be assigned to Yarikh only where provenance, inscription, and cultural context support that identification. Many popular images labeled “Yarikh” are actually:

- Mesopotamian Sîn;
- Hurrian Kušuḫ;
- Hittite/Anatolian Arma;
- unidentified Syro-Hittite lunar gods;
- modern fantasy art.

### Discredited or unsupported theories

- Yarikh was the universal chief god of all Canaan.
- Every crescent in Phoenician art depicts Yarikh.
- Baal Hammon was simply Yarikh.
- Tanit was Yarikh’s wife.
- Jericho’s walls fell in a historical ritual war between Yahweh and Yarikh.
- Carthaginian children were sacrificed to Yarikh.
- Yarikh became the Jewish or Islamic God.
- A continuous secret Yarikh cult survived through medieval magic.
- Modern lunar correspondences attributed to him are Bronze Age teachings.

| **Early second millennium BCE, probably nineteenth–eighteenth century BCE** | Amorite personal names employ Yaraḫ/Yariḫ; an Amorite–Akkadian vocabulary equates Yaraḫum with Sîn. Earliest secure explicit evidence. |
| **Middle Bronze Age** | Possible lunar toponyms and personal names occur across Amorite-speaking regions. Proposed connection of Jericho’s early name to *yrḫ* remains disputed. |

| **Iron Age I–II** | Direct Yarikh evidence becomes sparse; lunar names, symbols, and worship continue in multiple regional forms. |

| **First millennium BCE–Roman period** | Punic name *ʿbdyrḥ*, “servant of Yarikh/Yeraḥ,” attests the divine element; Punic crescents remain iconographically ambiguous. |

| **Fifth century BCE** | Herodotus describes Phoenician cults but does not document Yarikh. |
| **First–second centuries CE** | Philo of Byblos constructs a Phoenician sacred history; surviving fragments do not name Yarikh. |

| **Late antiquity** | Traditional Levantine polytheistic institutions decline under Christian rule; no specific suppression of Yarikh is recorded. |
| **Seventh century CE onward** | Islamization further transforms Levantine religious life. No evidence shows Yarikh surviving as a named folk god. |
| **1928–1929** | Excavation and decipherment at Ras Shamra begin the modern recovery of Ugaritic religion. |
| **1933–1936** | KTU 1.24 is discovered and receives its early editions and interpretations. |

| **1978** | Gibson’s *Canaanite Myths and Legends* supplies an influential English translation. |

| **2000** | Gabriele Theuer publishes *Der Mondgott in den Religionen Syrien-Palästinas*, the most extensive monographic treatment. |
| **2002** | Dennis Pardee publishes *Ritual and Cult at Ugarit*, providing collated texts and translations of the ritual corpus. |
| **2022** | George and Krebernik publish the Amorite–Akkadian vocabularies, furnishing an explicit early equation Yaraḫum = Sîn. |
| **Twenty-first century** | Yarikh is revived in small reconstructionist and eclectic pagan settings. These practices derive from modern recovery of the tablets, not demonstrable continuous transmission. |
| **2026** | The documentary picture remains dominated by the Old Babylonian lexical/onomastic evidence and the Late Bronze Age Ugaritic archive; no securely identified image, exclusive temple, continuous cult, or Phoenician narrative has emerged. |
