# Horon: Research Dossier

**Class:** male god in Ugaritic, Canaanite, Egyptian, Phoenician, Punic, and Hellenistic West-Semitic traditions; principally an apotropaic and magical deity connected with snakes and poison. A chthonic or underworld classification is a scholarly inference, not an uncontested ancient title.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the West-Semitic theophoric personal name conventionally reconstructed as *Ḥawrānu-abum*, “Horon is father,” in Egyptian Middle Kingdom Execration Texts, probably late Twelfth Dynasty, approximately nineteenth century BCE. Because this is onomastic rather than a narrative or cult text, it securely supports knowledge of a deity named Horon in Syria-Palestine but tells us almost nothing about his character. The earliest substantial description is the Ugaritic snake-incantation tablet RS 24.244 = KTU 1.100, thirteenth century BCE. The earliest secure Egyptian cult attestations belong to the reign of Amenhotep II, approximately 1425–1399 BCE, although the attribution of some foundation plaques to that reign has been questioned.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Horon is a sparsely attested but geographically mobile West-Semitic god. At Late Bronze Age Ugarit he occurs chiefly in magical texts concerned with serpents, venom, exorcism, and destructive curses. He is absent from Ugarit’s standard divine offering lists, so the texts do not support making him a principal member of the Ugaritic state pantheon. In New Kingdom Egypt he became **Hauron**, received cult at Giza, and was joined to the Great Sphinx and the solar-horizon god Harmakhis, *Ḥor-em-akhet*, “Horus in the Horizon.” Egyptian artists consequently rendered him as a falcon or falcon-associated protector. The god’s name also survives in the southern Levantine place-name Beth-Horon, in Punic Sardinia, and finally as Greek Αὑρώνας, *Haurōnas/Auronas*, one of the gods of Hellenistic Jamnia.

### Names and scripts

- **Ugaritic:** 𐎈𐎗𐎐, consonantal *ḥrn*. Modern vocalizations include *Ḥôrānu*, *Ḥōrān*, *Ḥôrōn*, and conventional English **Horon**. Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform normally leaves the vowels unwritten, so the ancient Ugaritic pronunciation cannot be recovered with certainty.  
  **Tier:** securely attested early material.

- **Northwest Semitic/Phoenician-Punic:** 𐤇𐤓𐤍, *ḥrn*. “Horon” reflects the later Canaanite/Phoenician vocalization commonly adopted by modern scholarship.  
  **Tier:** securely attested, though exact vowels remain reconstructed.

- **Hebrew:** חוֹרוֹן or variant spellings within בֵּית־חוֹרוֹן, *Bēt-Ḥôrôn*, “house/temple of Horon.” Biblical manuscripts also exhibit defective spellings such as חרון. The name occurs as a place-name, not as an independently worshiped god in biblical narrative.  
  **Tier:** Iron Age place-name preserved in later literary manuscripts.

- **Egyptian:** normally transliterated *Ḥwrwn*, *Ḥwrwnꜣ*, or similar forms, with substantial orthographic variation. Modern Egyptological forms include **Hauron**, **Haurun**, and **Hawran**. Egyptian spelling does not justify identifying him etymologically with the native Egyptian god Horus, *Ḥrw*.  
  **Tier:** securely attested Egyptian adaptation.

- **Greek:** Αὑρώνας, *Haurōnas* or *Auronas*, in the Delian dedication made by people from Iamnia/Jamnia.  
  **Tier:** securely attested Hellenistic continuation.

### Etymology

No etymology has achieved consensus.

1. **“The deep one” or “one of the hollow/depth.”** The proposal connects *ḥrn* with a Semitic root *ḥwr*, interpreted in terms of a pit, cave, hollow, or depth. This helped generate the older characterization of Horon as an underworld god. The semantic and morphological derivation is not secure.  
   **Tier:** modern scholarly reconstruction, disputed.

2. **Connection with Horon’s dwelling, *mṣd*.** Some scholars translate *mṣd* as “fortress,” “stronghold,” “steppe,” or an underworld locality. Nicolas Wyatt and Udo Rüterswörden regarded the location as evidence of chthonic character; Manfred Krebernik and others consider the inference unproved.  
   **Tier:** contested scholarly reconstruction.

3. **Lunar derivation.** A comparison with Arabic *hilāl*, “crescent moon,” has occasionally led to an astral or lunar interpretation. The phonological and contextual support is weak, and no text calls Horon a moon god.  
   **Tier:** speculative and not established.

4. **Horites, the Hauran, Haran, or Horus.** Similar-looking names have encouraged associations with the biblical Horites, the Syrian region Hauran, the city Haran, and Egyptian Horus. None is a demonstrated etymology. Charles Virolleaud already cautioned against equating Horon with the Horites.  
   **Tier:** rejected or unproved comparisons.

### Titles and descriptive designations

Horon has remarkably few secure ancient epithets.

- **“Great god”** occurs in Egyptian formulations associated with Hauron/Harmakhis.  
  **Tier:** Egyptian regional development.

- **“Lord of heaven”** and **“ruler of eternity,”** or comparable Egyptian honorifics, occur on Giza monuments attached to Hauron-Harmakhis. These belong to Egyptian solar and royal theology rather than the known Ugaritic profile.  
  **Tier:** Egyptian syncretistic identification.

- **“Of Lebanon.”** A Ramesside sphinx fragment from Tell el-Maskhuta bears a designation interpreted as “Hauron of Lebanon.” Its original architectural provenance may have been elsewhere in the eastern Delta.  
  **Tier:** securely attested regional epithet, though the object’s movement complicates its cult geography.

- **“Horon of Yabneh/Jamnia.”** Virolleaud read the Kirta text’s *ḥrn ybn* as “Horon of Yabneh.” The normal modern parsing is *ḥrn, y bn*, “Horon, O son,” because *bn* addresses Kirta’s son Yaṣṣib. The Hellenistic Delos inscription independently proves a Horon of Jamnia, but it does not establish Virolleaud’s reading of the Ugaritic line.  
  **Tier:** Hellenistic association securely attested; Ugaritic epithet rejected by most current translators.

- **“Master of demons,” “god of doom,” and “lord of black magic.”** These are modern interpretive labels, not recovered ancient titles. They depend chiefly on KTU 1.82 and on Horon’s destructive role in Kirta’s curse. KTU 1.100, however, casts him as the uniquely successful healer.  
  **Tier:** modern scholarly characterization, contested.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Middle Bronze Age onomastic evidence

A personal name in the Egyptian Execration Texts has been read as *Ḥwrn(w)-ꜣbwm* or *Ḥawrānu-abum*, “Horon is father.” These ritual documents list foreign rulers and peoples for magical destruction. The name belongs to an enemy individual, not to an invocation of Horon; its importance is that a divine element *Ḥawrānu* appears in an Amorite-type personal name. John Gray regarded this as evidence that belief in Horon existed in Middle Bronze Age Syria-Palestine.

- **Language/support:** Egyptian hieratic on pottery or figurine material.
- **Date:** conventionally late Twelfth Dynasty or early Second Intermediate Period, around the nineteenth–eighteenth centuries BCE.
- **What it proves:** a deity whose name can plausibly be reconstructed as Horon was used in personal naming.
- **What it does not prove:** the god’s functions, iconography, principal sanctuary, or membership in any later Ugaritic pantheon.

**Tier:** earliest secure or highly probable onomastic attestation.

### Egyptian New Kingdom evidence before the Ugaritic tablets

Hauron appears in royal Egyptian documentation beginning around Amenhotep II. Christiane Zivie-Coche places the introduction of Hauron, Resheph, and Astarte into royal Egyptian documentation at the start of that reign, in a Memphite environment connected with Egypt’s military and commercial engagement with the Levant. This is evidence of deliberate Egyptian state incorporation, not merely the private survival of immigrant religion.

The precise Giza chronology is disputed. Foundation plaques reportedly naming Amenhotep II as beloved of “Hauron-Harmakhis” entered the Brooklyn Museum from the antiquities market. Jacobus van Dijk observed that they differ palaeographically and materially from the securely excavated Harmakhis plaques and that Amenhotep II’s temple inscriptions otherwise call the Sphinx Harmakhis, not Hauron. Secure Giza evidence for the compound identity becomes abundant only from the later Eighteenth and especially the Nineteenth Dynasty.

**Tier:** Hauron in early Amenhotep II royal documentation is securely attested; the particular foundation plaques and an equally early Hauron-Sphinx equation are contested.

### Ugaritic corpus

The Ugaritic tablets come from Late Bronze Age Ugarit/Ras Shamra and are generally dated to the thirteenth or early twelfth century BCE. The standard catalogue siglum KTU refers to *Die keilalphabetischen Texte aus Ugarit*.

#### KTU 1.100 = RS 24.244, “Horon and the Serpents”

This almost completely preserved Ugaritic tablet is in the National Museum of Damascus as DO 6587. It measures approximately 24 × 16 × 3.9 cm and is divided by horizontal rulings into fourteen sections. One omitted unit was added in the tablet’s margin. It was excavated in the 1961 season in the “House of the High Priest” or associated priestly/library complex at Ugarit.

The opening identifies a figure as:

> *ʾum pḥl pḥlt bt ʿn bt ʾbn bt šmm w thm*  
> “Mother of Stallion, Mare, daughter of Spring, daughter of Stone, daughter of Heaven and the Deep.”

The precise syntax and ontology of this figure remain debated: she may be a divine mare, a cosmological equine being, or a figure bearing ritual titles. “Mother of the stallion and mare” is another translation. She addresses Shapash as her mother and asks the solar goddess to convey an appeal to a succession of deities. Each appeal concerns a serpent and its venom; the first divine recipients fail or decline. Horon, approached last, acts successfully: he binds or controls the snake, neutralizes or expels its poison, and concludes marriage negotiations with the Mare. The ritual practitioner apparently identifies with Horon so that the divine precedent makes the human treatment effective.

Scholars variously classify the composition as an incantation, a charm, a myth embedded in ritual, a ritual with a mythic historiola, or “paramythological” literature. The tablet’s repetitive structure and practical magical purpose are clear even though several words remain uncertain.

**Tier:** securely attested early material.

#### KTU 1.107

A fragmentary Ugaritic snakebite incantation. Horon and El head a sequence of divine pairs or recipients asked to remove venom; other names include Baal and Dagan, Anat and Ashtart, Yarikh and Resheph, Attar and Attapar, Shahar and Shalim, Milku, Kothar-wa-Khasis, and Shapash. Its preservation does not permit a continuous narrative comparable to KTU 1.100.

**Tier:** securely attested early material.

#### KTU 1.82

A damaged anti-witchcraft or anti-snakebite incantation. Horon and unnamed beings associated with him appear with dangerous figures including Resheph, Mot, and the sea-serpent Tunnanu. Some readings treat the “creatures” or “servants of Horon” as hostile powers; others understand Horon as a controller of such forces. The fragment cannot establish a full demonological hierarchy.

**Tier:** securely attested early text; interpretation disputed.

#### KTU 1.169

A compilation of protective incantations against sorcery or harmful magic in which Horon is named. The damaged state and compressed ritual language allow only limited reconstruction.

**Tier:** securely attested early material.

#### KTU 1.16 VI 54–57, the conclusion of the Kirta Epic

When Kirta’s son Yaṣṣib challenges his infirm father’s right to rule, Kirta curses him:

> “May Horon break, O my son,  
> may Horon break your head,  
> and Athtart-name-of-Baal your crown.”

The tablet breaks shortly afterward, so the curse’s narrative outcome is unknown. Horon functions here as the executing power of a royal malediction, not as a healer. The parallel invocation of Athtart supplies one of Horon’s few explicit divine co-actions.

**Tier:** securely attested early literary material.

#### Absence from the Baal and Aqhat cycles

Horon has no securely read role in KTU 1.1–1.6, the Baal Cycle, or KTU 1.17–1.19, the Aqhat Epic. He is also absent from Ugarit’s principal offering lists, including the parallel pantheon lists KTU 1.47, 1.118, and RS 20.24. This absence argues against presenting him as one of Ugarit’s ruling gods.

**Tier:** securely observed absence of evidence.

### Egyptian magical texts

#### Papyrus Harris 501

The Ramesside magical papyrus includes Hauron in spells directed against dangerous animals. A passage calls on Hauron, Anat, and Resheph in a violent protective context involving the destruction of a wolf or other threat. The papyrus helped nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholars recognize Hauron before the Ugaritic discoveries supplied the Northwest-Semitic spelling.

**Tier:** Egyptian regional adaptation, securely attested but philologically difficult.

#### Other New Kingdom texts

Hauron occurs in personal names, private stelae, votive formulae, and royal monuments. Much of the material clusters at Giza and in the Memphite region; additional evidence comes from Deir el-Medina, the eastern Delta, and objects later discovered at Tanis. Some Tanis objects were relocated from Pi-Ramesses or other Ramesside establishments, so findspot must not automatically be equated with original cult site.

### Beth-Horon sources

The Hebrew Bible names Upper and Lower Beth-Horon in Joshua 10:10–11; 16:3, 5; 18:13–14; 21:22; 1 Samuel 13:18; 1 Kings 9:17; 1 Chronicles 6:53; 7:24; 2 Chronicles 8:5; and 25:13. The narratives concern geography, battles, borders, building, and administration. They neither describe Horon nor explicitly attack his cult. Calling the Bible a “hostile witness” is therefore only partly apt: Israelite and Judahite literature preserves the old divine name while suppressing or ignoring its theological content.

EA 290, an Amarna letter from Jerusalem’s ruler ʿAbdi-Ḫeba in the mid-fourteenth century BCE, mentions *URU É dNIN.URTA*, “city House of Ninurta.” Kallai and Tadmor identified this as Beth-Horon, interpreting the Mesopotamian divine logogram as a scribal equivalent for Horon. Other scholars locate the town south or west of Jerusalem and identify it with Bethlehem, Beth-Anat, Kiriath-Baal, or an otherwise unknown site. EA 290 is therefore **not** a secure Horon attestation.

Shoshenq I’s Karnak campaign list, approximately 925 BCE, includes Egyptian *bt-ḥwrn*, securely identified as Beth-Horon because it follows Gibeon in a geographically coherent sequence. Tell Qasile Ostracon 2, eighth century BCE, records *zhb ʾpr l-byt ḥrn*, “gold of Ophir for/to Beth-Horon.” This probably denotes a consignment to the inland town, though a “temple of Horon” at Tell Qasile has been proposed.

### Punic Antas inscription

Fragments found in 1967 in the Punic sanctuary of Sid at Antas, Sardinia, were initially published separately by Mohamed Fantar. Maurice Sznycer and Maria Luisa Uberti joined them and read part of the inscription as a dedication of a “statue of Horon” to Sid Addir Babi. The reconstructed text is approximately:

> “To the lord, to Sid Addir Babi, a statue of Horon which [the dedicator] vowed … because he heard his voice; may he bless him.”

The object is a small white-marble cylindrical statue base, now in the National Archaeological Museum, Cagliari. It is generally dated to the fourth century BCE, although the first Punic sanctuary at Antas is older.

This is direct evidence that a Horon image could be offered within another god’s Punic sanctuary. It does not establish that Horon was identical with Sid, Shadrafa, Eshmun, or Sardus Pater.

**Tier:** securely attested Punic regional cult; restoration of the fragmentary syntax remains partly conjectural.

### Delos and Jamnia

I.Délos 2308 = CGRN 174, second century BCE, was dedicated on Delos by Zenodoros, Patron, and Diodotos, men of Jamnia:

> “To Heracles and Hauronas, the gods who possess/dwell in Iamnia … as a thank-offering.”

Its ritual rule permits sacrifice of everything except goat products: *θύειν πάντα πλὴν αἰγείου*. This is the latest long-recognized ancient attestation of Horon.

A Hellenistic sling bullet excavated at Yavneh East more recently carries:

> νίκη — Ἡρακλέους καὶ Αὑρώνα  
> “Victory of Heracles and Hauron.”

It independently confirms the pair as protectors of Hellenistic Jamnia and removes any need to derive the Delian pairing solely from an expatriate association.

**Tier:** securely attested Hellenistic regional cult and military invocation.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

1. **Snakebite, venom, and magical healing.** This is the best-attested Ugaritic function. KTU 1.100 makes Horon the only invoked god who successfully deals with the serpent; KTU 1.107 again places him at the head of an anti-venom sequence.  
   **Tier:** securely attested early core.

2. **Exorcism and apotropaic magic.** KTU 1.82 and 1.169 connect him with harmful powers and their control. Whether he commands “demons” or is himself an ambivalent dangerous god cannot be decided from the fragments.  
   **Tier:** securely attested magical context; precise theology disputed.

3. **Destructive curse.** Kirta invokes him to smash Yaṣṣib’s head. Horon’s healing and harming functions are not contradictory in ancient magical logic: the power that controls poison or threatening beings can be directed against an enemy.  
   **Tier:** securely attested early core.

4. **Underworld or chthonic power.** The inference rests on the uncertain meaning of his dwelling *mṣd*, proposed “depth” etymologies, associations with Mot and dangerous beings, and later interpretations of caves or desert. No extant text simply titles Horon “god of the underworld.”  
   **Tier:** contested modern reconstruction.

5. **Desert protector.** In Egypt the Sphinx stood at the desert edge and carried titles such as “lord of the desert.” Van Dijk suggested that Levantine residents recognized in the partly sand-buried Sphinx a manifestation of a native desert god. This is a plausible explanation of the Giza identification, not an explicit ancient account.  
   **Tier:** modern reconstruction of Egyptian syncretism.

6. **Solar and royal protection.** As Hauron-Harmakhis and the falcon protecting Ramesses II, Hauron entered Egyptian solar and kingship theology. No Ugaritic evidence makes him a sun god.  
   **Tier:** securely attested Egyptian development.

7. **Civic and military protection.** The Delos dedication and Yavneh sling bullet make Heracles and Hauron protecting or possessing gods of Jamnia.  
   **Tier:** securely attested Hellenistic development.

### Powers and limitations

KTU 1.100 credits Horon with binding or compelling the snake and removing its venom. He succeeds where eleven earlier addressees do not. In Kirta he is expected to fracture a human opponent’s head. Egyptian images make him a protector of the pharaoh. No text gives him cosmic sovereignty, weather control, creation, resurrection, judgment of the dead, or rulership over the Ugaritic pantheon.

### Ugaritic form

No securely identified Ugaritic image of Horon survives. The serpent is the object of his control, not demonstrably his sacred animal or bodily form. Likewise, the Mare of KTU 1.100 is not certainly his regular consort.

### Egyptian iconography

Egyptian Hauron is represented most clearly as a **falcon**, assimilating him iconographically to Horus/Harmakhis. His most famous monument is the approximately two-meter granite group of the child Ramesses II sheltered against the breast of an enormous falcon. The falcon’s crossed wings protect the king. Ramesses is nude, wears the sidelock of youth, places a finger to his mouth, and holds a *sw* plant. Together with the sun disk, the figures form a rebus for *Ra-mes-su*. The base describes Ramesses as beloved of Hauron.

- **Date:** Nineteenth Dynasty, reign of Ramesses II, approximately 1279–1213 BCE.
- **Find:** Pierre Montet’s 1934 excavations at Tanis, in a later mud-brick chapel or workshop context.
- **Likely original setting:** often assigned to Pi-Ramesses, although Giza has also been proposed.
- **Museum:** Egyptian Museum, Cairo, commonly catalogued JE 64735/GEM 6765; older or popular catalogues sometimes print erroneous numbers.
- **Restoration:** the falcon’s limestone face or beak was found separately.

Hauron could also be represented through the **Great Sphinx itself**. This was not the Sphinx’s original Old Kingdom identity. The equation belongs to New Kingdom reception of a monument already more than a millennium old.

### Other claimed correspondences

- **Tree of death:** a “tree of death” association sometimes used to define Horon as a doom god depends on a difficult magical passage and should not be treated as a standard emblem.
- **Wolf:** the Harris Papyrus connects Hauron with the magical defeat of a dangerous animal; this does not make the wolf his sacred animal.
- **Colors, numbers, weekdays, planets, plants:** no secure ancient system assigns these to Horon. Modern lists doing so are modern inventions unless tied explicitly to an attested text.
- **Eight wives or seven concubines:** damaged magical language has been interpreted as referring to Horon’s seven concubines or eight wives. The reading and referent are uncertain; no names survive.  
  **Tier:** disputed philological reconstruction.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No surviving source names Horon’s parents, siblings, or children. He is not assigned a stable position in El’s family in the Ugaritic myths. The absence of a genealogy is important: attempts to make him a son of El, brother of Baal, form of Resheph, or member of a canonical Canaanite family tree are reconstructions without direct textual support.

### Consort

KTU 1.100 ends in marriage negotiations between Horon and the Mare/*pḥlt*. Depending on the translation, Horon demands that she enter his house and bring a bridal gift composed of serpents or their products; alternatively, the exchange seals the magical neutralization of snakes. She is unique to this tablet, and the text does not call her Horon’s permanent wife.

**Tier:** securely attested episode; permanent consort status unestablished.

### Syncretistic relations

- **Harmakhis/Hor-em-akhet:** Hauron’s most secure fusion. At Giza the names could form Hauron-Harmakhis and eventually function as names of the Sphinx.  
  **Tier:** Egyptian syncretistic identification.

- **Horus:** falcon iconography and the Harmakhis equation brought Hauron into the Horus complex. This does not prove that the names or gods shared an origin.  
  **Tier:** Egyptian iconographic and theological assimilation.

- **Heracles/Melqart:** Hauron and Heracles occur together at Jamnia; Heracles is plausibly the Hellenized Phoenician Melqart. The inscription coordinates two gods rather than explicitly declaring them identical.  
  **Tier:** Hellenistic joint cult; identification of Heracles as Melqart probable but not stated.

- **Eshmun/Asclepius:** older scholarship proposed that the healing Horon survived under the name Eshmun and that the Jamnian pair paralleled Heracles and Asclepius. No bilingual inscription equates Horon with Eshmun or Asclepius.  
  **Tier:** speculative modern reconstruction.

- **Resheph:** both gods control dangerous powers and occur in Egyptian magic, but texts distinguish them.  
  **Tier:** associated, not identical.

- **Sid/Sardus Pater:** Horon’s statue was apparently dedicated in Sid’s Antas sanctuary. That is cultic association, not equation.  
  **Tier:** Punic regional association.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Horon and the serpent: KTU 1.100

**Early attested version.** A cosmological Mare discovers or fears that a serpent’s venom threatens her offspring. She cries to Shapash, her mother, asking that her voice be carried to the gods. Shapash successively transmits substantially the same appeal to a series of divine residences. Each unit names a god and a place and asks for an incantation against the writhing snake and its poison. The repetition dramatizes failure: the ordinary divine powers cannot provide the cure.

The final appeal reaches Horon at *mṣd*. At his face or presence there is a flare of anger or intensity. He prepares himself, binds or compels the serpent, feeds or treats it, and removes the poison. The tablet then changes into a dialogue about marriage. Horon requests the Mare and specifies a bride-price or counter-gift involving serpents; she responds by setting conditions. Because the close combines sexual, marital, and snake imagery, interpretations vary widely.

**Ritual function.** The repeated mythic precedent is a *historiola*: what Horon once accomplished in the divine sphere, the practitioner reenacts for the patient. The French archaeological presentation of the tablet states explicitly that the officiant assimilated himself to Horon to ensure the cure’s efficacy.

**Variants and disputes.**

- The patient may be the Mare herself, her foal, or equids collectively.
- The appeals may ask each god to cure the bite, provide the incantation, or transmit knowledge.
- Horon’s anger may be directed at the snake, the Mare, or the situation.
- The marriage may be a genuine divine union, a metaphor for ritual transfer, or the narrative price for magical knowledge.
- Proposed identification of the Mare with Athirat, Anat, or another major goddess has not won general acceptance.
- Sexual-fertility readings that turn the tablet into evidence for “sacred prostitution” exceed the text. No temple prostitute appears.

**Development.** KTU 1.107 shows that the anti-venom invocation was not unique, but no earlier narrative version survives. There is no basis for reconstructing a lost epic in which Horon permanently defeated a cosmic serpent.

### Kirta’s curse

At the end of the Kirta Epic, the king’s son Yaṣṣib argues that Kirta’s prolonged illness has made him incapable of defending the vulnerable or administering justice. Yaṣṣib demands the throne. Kirta answers not with a battle but with a curse: Horon is to break the son’s head, while Athtart-name-of-Baal breaks his crown. The surviving tablet ends before fulfillment.

Horon’s role is brief but revealing. He is a divine enforcer whose name gives violence to an uttered curse. The text does not call him Yaṣṣib’s murderer, and later retellings that narrate the son’s death invent an ending absent from the tablet.

**Tier:** secure early episode; all claimed outcomes are modern reconstruction or retelling.

### Egyptian Sphinx theology

This is not a continuous literary myth but a historical sacral identification. During the New Kingdom, worshipers treated the Great Sphinx as Harmakhis and then as Hauron-Harmakhis. Hauron thereby became a colossal lion-bodied, royal, solar guardian without ceasing to appear as a falcon in portable sculpture.

There is no surviving Egyptian narrative explaining the fusion. Modern proposals include:

- Levantine soldiers or settlers recognized their desert god in the Sphinx.
- The Egyptian state deliberately incorporated a Levantine protector after imperial expansion.
- Phonetic or visual proximity to Horus eased assimilation.
- Hauron’s apotropaic power made him appropriate as a royal guardian.

These explanations can coexist, but none is an ancient myth preserved in full.

### Jamnian Heracles-Hauron pair

The Delos inscription and sling bullet demonstrate a Hellenistic civic pair, not a surviving narrative cycle. Heracles/Melqart and Hauron “possess” or protect Iamnia and grant victory. No text says they were brothers, father and son, healer and patient, or equivalents.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Ugarit

No temple of Horon has been identified at Ugarit. KTU 1.100 was found in a priestly or learned textual context, showing that trained ritual specialists copied and preserved Horon incantations. His absence from offering lists suggests either:

- a specialized magical god outside ordinary sacrificial schedules;
- a deity worshiped locally or privately rather than by the palace temple;
- or simply gaps in the surviving archive.

The ritual acts implied by the incantations include recitation, identification of practitioner and god, treatment of venom, binding or manipulating a snake symbolically, and perhaps administering plant preparations. Claims that KTU 1.100 records literal snake sacrifice, sexual rites, or temple prostitution are unproved.

### Beth-Horon

Upper and Lower Beth-Horon lay on the strategic route from Gibeon and the central highlands down to the Aijalon Valley. Their likely modern counterparts are Beit ʿUr al-Fauqa and Beit ʿUr al-Tahta.

The name “house of Horon” plausibly began as a sanctuary-name and then became a settlement-name. Nevertheless, no excavated temple inscription from either site identifies an active Horon cult. The biblical texts use the name conventionally and preserve no priesthood, festival, image, or offering to Horon there. The modern archaeological conclusion must therefore be restrained: the toponym is evidence for remembered divine nomenclature, not proof of uninterrupted worship.

### Giza

Giza became Hauron’s best-documented cult center. The chapel northeast of the Great Sphinx is conventionally called the Temple of Amenhotep II or Temple of Hauron-Harmakhis. Its earliest phase honored Harmakhis; Hauron’s secure appearance within the complex becomes clearer under Tutankhamun, Seti I, and the Ramessides.

Evidence includes:

- royal stelae and door-jamb inscriptions;
- private votive stelae;
- royal and personal names invoking Hauron;
- titles identifying Hauron/Harmakhis with the Sphinx;
- continued building or dedication by Seti I and Ramesses II.

The cult served both royal and private devotion. Hauron’s incorporation was part of a broader Egyptian reception of Levantine deities—Resheph, Astarte, Baal, Anat, and Qadesh—within the Memphite and military world.

The much later Inventory Stela, usually assigned to the Twenty-sixth Dynasty, says that Khufu found or established sanctuaries near the Sphinx, including a temple of Hauron. It retrojects contemporary sacred geography into the Old Kingdom. It is not evidence that Khufu worshiped Hauron.

### Deir el-Medina and the eastern Delta

Ramesside workmen and private persons invoked Hauron away from Giza. A monumental falcon group associated with Ramesses II and objects bearing his name show royal patronage in the Delta. “Hauron of Lebanon” attests a form that retained explicit Levantine geography inside Egypt.

### Antas, Sardinia

The Punic sanctuary at Antas was dedicated chiefly to Sid Addir Babi, later identified with Sardus Pater. The first Punic phase is generally placed around the sixth or fifth century BCE and a major rebuilding around 300 BCE. A fourth-century inscription apparently records a statue of Horon offered to Sid. This indicates that Horon remained available within the western Punic divine repertoire, particularly in a sanctuary with healing and protective associations.

No evidence connects Horon with the Carthage tophet. Excavations at the tophet document urn burials, dedicatory stelae, and formulae primarily concerning Baal Hammon and Tanit. Whether the tophet involved regular child sacrifice, exceptional sacrifice, or a special cemetery remains intensely debated, but that debate cannot responsibly be transferred to Horon. No Horon inscription from the Carthage tophet is known.

### Jamnia and Delos

The Delian dedication was made by expatriate Jamnians for themselves, their relatives, and fellow citizens. It implies an organized community capable of establishing a cultic monument abroad. The prohibition against goat sacrifice is Horon’s only explicit surviving food taboo, but it belongs to the **joint cult of Heracles and Hauron at Jamnia**, not necessarily to every form of Horon worship.

The Yavneh sling bullet shows the pair invoked in warfare. It may have served as propaganda, an invocation placed literally on a projectile, or both.

### Priesthood, festivals, pilgrimage, and economics

No priest specifically titled “priest of Horon” has been securely identified in the reviewed evidence. No calendar names a festival of Horon. Giza’s priestly and temple economy belonged to the larger Sphinx/Harmakhis establishment, but the surviving evidence does not allow a separate Hauron priesthood or endowment to be reconstructed. No securely attested oracle or pilgrimage exclusively of Horon is known.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Phase I: Middle Bronze Age, approximately nineteenth–eighteenth centuries BCE

The theophoric name in the Execration Texts indicates a deity Horon known somewhere in Syria-Palestine. His character is unrecoverable.

### Phase II: Egyptian imperial and Ugaritic Late Bronze Age, fifteenth–thirteenth centuries BCE

Under Amenhotep II, Hauron entered Egyptian royal documentation in the Memphite region. This reflects Egypt’s imperial, military, and commercial entanglement with the Levant. By the later Eighteenth Dynasty he was securely associated with Giza, and in the Ramesside period the Hauron-Harmakhis fusion flourished.

At Ugarit, the same deity appears in specialized magical literature. KTU 1.100 presents the clearest early theology: Horon is the decisive controller of serpent venom. The Kirta curse adds destructive enforcement. His absence from offering lists shows specialization, not necessarily insignificance to ordinary sufferers.

### Phase III: Iron Age southern Levant, approximately tenth–eighth centuries BCE

Beth-Horon appears securely in Shoshenq I’s campaign list and the Tell Qasile ostracon. Biblical traditions preserve Upper and Lower Beth-Horon as strategically important towns. Direct cult evidence is absent.

Egyptian worship continued into and beyond the Ramesside period. Later texts and monuments retained Hauron’s Sphinx identity.

### Phase IV: Phoenician and Punic diffusion, sixth–fourth centuries BCE

At Antas, Sardinia, an image of Horon could be vowed in Sid’s temple. This is the clearest western Punic evidence. It shows transmission through Phoenician-Punic networks but does not establish that Horon was broadly prominent in Carthage, Sicily, North Africa, or Iberia.

### Phase V: Hellenistic Jamnia and Delos, second century BCE

Horon became Hauronas/Auronas, joint civic protector with Heracles, probably the Greek presentation of Melqart. The Jamnian cult retained a distinctive goat taboo and was exported by migrants to Delos. The sling bullet demonstrates local military invocation.

### Phase VI: Disappearance and transformations

After the Hellenistic attestations, Horon disappears as a clearly identifiable, independently named ancient god. The Egyptian Sphinx continued under names such as Harmakhis and, in later Egyptian and Arabic reception, names possibly descended through forms of Hauron. Linguistic descent of a Sphinx-name does not prove continued personal worship of the Canaanite deity.

No documentary chain connects ancient Horon worship to medieval magic, Islamic jinn traditions, local Palestinian folklore, or an early-modern esoteric order. No act of Roman, Christian, Jewish, or Islamic suppression specifically directed against Horon is known. His cult appears to have been absorbed, renamed, or abandoned within wider religious transformations rather than ended by a recorded targeted prohibition.

### Phase VII: Modern reconstruction and revival

Modern knowledge began with Egyptian magical texts and monuments and changed decisively after the Ras Shamra discoveries from 1929 onward. Small online Semitic-polytheist and reconstructionist communities now discuss or venerate Horon as a god of healing, venom, magic, exorcism, and liminal places. These communities are real modern religious developments, but their rituals are reconstructions: no continuous initiatory lineage from Ugarit, Giza, Antas, or Jamnia has been demonstrated.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship-driven image

Horon’s modern image is largely a scholarly construction from disconnected corpora. Before Ugaritic was deciphered, Egyptologists knew a Hauron from the Harris Magical Papyrus and Giza. The Ugaritic texts then supplied an indigenous West-Semitic name and serpent-magic context. Virolleaud’s 1936 notice linked Ras Shamra, the Ramesses-Hauron statue, Beth-Horon, and the Delian Auronas.

### Occult and esoteric reception

Horon is absent from the principal Renaissance planetary-magical systems, the standard Solomonic grimoires, Theosophical canon, and the core Golden Dawn grades. Claims that he possesses an ancient planetary seal, fixed color, tarot attribution, qabalistic correspondence, “demonic enn,” or Goetic rank are modern inventions.

Recent occult websites sometimes call him a demon, “chthonic lord,” god of black magic, or ruler of venomous spirits. These descriptions select the darker readings of KTU 1.82 and Kirta while often ignoring his healing action in KTU 1.100. They are contemporary receptions, not ancient titles.

### Neopaganism and reconstructionism

Contemporary Canaanite, Phoenician, and broader Semitic reconstructionists sometimes address him as *Ḥôrānu* or Horon and adapt the Ugaritic snake charm into healing or protection rites. This is a text-based modern reconstruction. The scale appears small and decentralized; there is no reliable membership count or central Horon institution.

### Popular culture

Horon has not acquired a major standardized identity in international novels, films, comics, or games. Modern confusion is more often generated by:

- misspelling or conflating him with Horus;
- assuming “Beth-Horon” proves a fully documented temple;
- calling him an underworld king;
- identifying the Great Sphinx’s original Old Kingdom identity as Hauron;
- treating the Mare as his canonical wife;
- or importing attributes from Resheph, Eshmun, Nergal, Melqart, or Egyptian serpent gods.

### Nationalist appropriation

Adya Gur Horon, a twentieth-century intellectual associated with the Canaanite movement, adopted “Horon” as a personal name. This belongs to modern Hebrew/Canaanist cultural nationalism, not revived evidence about the ancient cult. It illustrates how recovered Canaanite names became tools for articulating a pre-rabbinic or non-diasporic Hebrew identity.

No substantial modern extremist cult centered on Horon was found. That absence should not be replaced by extrapolation from the broader political uses of Canaanite antiquity.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Discovery history

François Chabas published a translation of the Harris Magical Papyrus in 1860, making Hauron known to Egyptology. Excavations at Giza produced the Sphinx-cult material; Montet’s 1934 Tanis excavation uncovered the Ramesses-Hauron falcon group. Ras Shamra excavations beginning in 1929 transformed the subject by revealing Ugaritic *ḥrn*. KTU 1.100, discovered in 1961, established the god’s snake-magic role in exceptional detail. The Antas inscription expanded his range into Punic Sardinia. The Yavneh sling bullet recently confirmed the Hellenistic Jamnian divine pair previously known chiefly from Delos.

### Major positions

- **William F. Albright:** emphasized a chthonic Horon, Middle Bronze Age onomastics, Beth-Horon, and Egyptian evidence. Some of his broad equations are now treated cautiously.
- **John Gray:** synthesized the god’s then-known record and explored possible relations with Resheph, Eshmun, and healing cults. His identifications are historically important but often exceed the direct evidence.
- **Charles Virolleaud:** connected Ugaritic, Egyptian, and Delian evidence but misread, by current consensus, *ḥrn y bn* as “Horon of Yabneh.”
- **Dennis Pardee:** treats KTU 1.100 as ritual and cultic literature employing mythic precedent.
- **Wilfred G. E. Watson and Nicolas Wyatt:** provide influential translations and philological analysis; Wyatt accepts more of the chthonic interpretation than some other scholars.
- **Gregorio del Olmo Lete:** stresses Horon’s positive, efficacious role and criticizes reduction of him to a black-magical demon.
- **Manfred Krebernik:** regards the underworld classification as insufficiently demonstrated.
- **Christiane Zivie-Coche and Jacobus van Dijk:** situate Hauron within Egyptian reception of Levantine gods and critically reassess the dating and authenticity of early Giza evidence.
- **Lipiński, Xella, and Bonnet:** place Horon within the broader transformations of Phoenician and Punic religion, while the sparse corpus limits any continuous biography.

### Central controversies

#### Was Horon an underworld god?

**For:** proposed “depth” etymology; uncertain *mṣd* dwelling; association with Mot and threatening powers; curse function; later desert/cave associations.

**Against:** no explicit ancient title “lord of the underworld”; *mṣd* may mean fortress or steppe; KTU 1.100 presents him as healer; dangerous gods need not be infernal.

**Conclusion:** chthonic character is plausible but unproved; “underworld god” should never be presented without qualification.

#### Was Horon evil or demonic?

KTU 1.82 associates him with dangerous beings, and Kirta invokes him destructively. KTU 1.100, however, makes him the successful divine healer. “Evil god” imports a moral category absent from the texts.

**Conclusion:** he is an ambivalent apotropaic power capable of protection and destruction. “Demon” is a modern reclassification.

#### Is the Mare a goddess and Horon’s wife?

The Mare has cosmic parentage and addresses Shapash as mother, suggesting supra-human status. Yet she occurs nowhere else, and her designation may be ritual or poetic.

**Conclusion:** a divine or mythic equine being is probable; permanent consort status is not established.

#### Did Amenhotep II identify Hauron with the Sphinx?

Royal documentation attests Hauron in Amenhotep II’s broader milieu. The Hauron-Harmakhis foundation plaques attributed to his Giza temple have insecure provenance and anomalous features. Secure compound identifications are later.

**Conclusion:** Hauron entered Egyptian state religion under Amenhotep II; the precise date at which the Sphinx became Hauron is uncertain and may be post-Amenhotep II.

#### Does EA 290 mention Beth-Horon?

The letter’s *Bīt-dNIN.URTA* could conceal a local divine name rendered by a Mesopotamian logogram, but its political geography points some scholars elsewhere.

**Conclusion:** possible, not secure. It should not be used as Horon’s earliest Beth-Horon attestation.

#### Was the Tell Qasile “house of Horon” a temple?

The ostracon could send Ophir gold “to the house/temple of Horon” locally, but the established town Beth-Horon lies about 35 km away and provides the simpler reading.

**Conclusion:** a gold consignment to the town is more likely; a Tell Qasile temple is possible but unproved.

#### Was Horon identical with Eshmun, Resheph, Sid, or Shadrafa?

All have healing or protective features; several occur in overlapping cultic networks. Direct sources distinguish them or merely place them together.

**Conclusion:** relationships and functional analogies are real; identity is not demonstrated.

### Alleged human sacrifice and sexual rites

No text or excavation connects Horon with human sacrifice. The Antas sanctuary evidence concerns votive images; the Carthage tophet evidence concerns chiefly Baal Hammon and Tanit. KTU 1.100’s marriage exchange has sometimes been sexualized into a *hieros gamos* or temple-prostitution theory, but the tablet describes neither prostitution nor a historical temple performance.

**Conclusion:** both associations are unsupported when applied specifically to Horon.

### State of the evidence

The evidence is uneven:

- **Damascus:** RS 24.244/KTU 1.100, DO 6587.
- **Egyptian Museum/Giza and Tanis records:** royal falcon group, stelae, architectural inscriptions.
- **Brooklyn Museum:** disputed Amenhotep II foundation plaques.
- **Cagliari:** fragmentary Antas statue base.
- **Delos:** Hellenistic dedication I.Délos 2308.
- **Yavneh:** sling bullet from the Hellenistic military context.
- **Berlin and other collections:** Execration Text materials.

No continuous archive belongs to Horon alone. Much of the reconstruction rests on a handful of difficult passages, relocated objects, and later syncretistic settings.
