# Attar: Research Dossier

**Class:** masculine deity in the Late Bronze Age Ugaritic tradition and in most Old South Arabian corpora; astral/weather/agricultural god, conventionally associated with Venus. He must not be confused with the Ugaritic and Phoenician goddess ʿAṯtart/Astarte, although their names are cognate and ancient and modern systems sometimes equated or juxtaposed them.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the masculine divine name **ʿṯtr**, conventionally vocalized **ʿAṯtar**, occurs in Ugaritic alphabetic-cuneiform texts from Ras Shamra-Ugarit, principally the tablets of the Baal Cycle copied in the thirteenth century BCE. The decisive narrative is **KTU 1.6 i 43–67**, where Athtar is proposed as Baal’s replacement, cannot fill Baal’s throne, and descends. The text does **not** call him Lucifer, Satan, a demon, or a rebel against El. Later but much more abundant evidence comes from first-millennium BCE South Arabian inscriptions.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** contemporary inscription, tablet, archaeological context, or securely ancient text.
- **[LE] Later literary elaboration:** ancient but later composition or transmission.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a locally distinct manifestation or interpretation.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** ancient or modern construction that organizes fragmentary evidence into a system.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** equation with another deity, whether ancient or reconstructed.
- **[FR] Folklore or reception tradition.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** plausible or influential interpretation not stated by the primary source.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported popular assertion.**

The labels classify the evidence, not the religious truth of any tradition.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

**[EA]** Ugaritic **ʿṯtr** is a male god situated within El’s divine household and associated with kingship, irrigation or terrestrial fertility, and—more controversially in the Ugaritic evidence itself—the morning star. In KTU 1.6 he is called upon after Baal’s reported death. He ascends Baal’s throne on Mount Ṣapānu but is physically unable to fill it: his feet do not reach the footstool and his head does not reach the throne’s top. He descends and assumes or exercises rule **bʾarṣ**, “in/on the earth,” though “underworld” has occasionally been proposed.

**[EA/RV]** Old South Arabian **ʿṯtr** is one of the most widely shared gods of Sabaic, Minaic, Qatabanic and Hadramitic-speaking communities. He heads many divine invocations and appears with numerous local titles. His South Arabian dossier is not simply a continuation of the single Ugaritic story: it is an inscriptionally rich regional history involving rainfall, irrigation, protection, warfare, sacrifice, oracles and sanctuary ownership.

### Written and vocalized forms

- **Ugaritic:** 𐎓𐎘𐎚, **ʿṯtr**, conventionally *ʿAṯtaru* or *ʿAṯtar*. Ugaritic consonantal writing does not preserve the complete vocalization.
- **Ancient South Arabian monumental script:** 𐩲𐩻𐩩𐩧, **ʿṯtr**, normally *ʿAṯtar*.
- **Hadramitic:** **ʿṯtrm**, sometimes **ʿs³trm**, with final *-m*.
- **Moabite:** 𐤏𐤔𐤕𐤓, **ʿštr**, in the compound **ʿŠTR-KMŠ**, “ʿAshtar-Chemosh,” on the Mesha Stele.
- **Aramaic/North Arabian:** forms such as **ʿtr**, **ʿtršmn/ʿtrsmn**, “Attar of Heaven,” and Akkadian renderings of Arabian divine names.
- **Feminine Northwest Semitic cognate:** Ugaritic **ʿṯtrt**, Phoenician **ʿštrt**, Greek **Ἀστάρτη**, Astarte.
- **East Semitic cognate:** Akkadian **Ištar**, normally feminine, although Mesopotamian texts could discuss gendered morning- and evening-star aspects.
- **Ethiopic:** the divine name survives in South Semitic form as **ʿAstar** in pre-Christian South Arabian/Ethiopian religious environments.

### Etymology

**[MSR—broad agreement, exact derivation disputed]** The masculine ʿAṯtar, feminine ʿAṯtart and Akkadian Ištar belong to a very old Semitic divine-name complex. The geographical distribution and recurrent astral associations make an inherited astral deity highly likely. A precise Semitic lexical derivation, however, is not established.

Earlier proposals connected the name with Arabic roots meaning “to be strong,” “to irrigate,” “to appear,” or “to water after rain.” These proposals often began with a presumed divine function and then selected a convenient root; none commands consensus.

**[MSR—minority hypothesis]** A proposed prehistoric borrowing between the Semitic name and Indo-European words for “star” has attracted discussion, but phonological direction, chronology and cultural mechanism remain disputed. It should not be stated as the established etymology.

### Epithets and local forms

- **ʿAṯtar ʿArīṣ/ʿArīẓ**, conventionally “Athtar the Terrible/Awesome/Mighty”: Ugaritic title in the Baal Cycle. The adjective marks formidable status; treating it as ironic because the throne does not fit is a literary interpretation.
- **ʿAṯtar ḏ-Ḏabān/Ḏbn**, “Athtar of Ḏabān”: South Arabian local form; CIH 429 calls him lord of a named locality and thanks him for rescue and protection.
- **ʿAṯtar Šarqān/S²rqn**, often interpreted “Eastern Athtar”: South Arabian form plausibly connected with Venus as morning star, though the exact vocalization and semantics remain debated.
- **ʿAṯtar Ġarbān/Grb**, often “Western Athtar”: traditionally interpreted as the evening-star manifestation.
- **ʿAṯtar ḏ-Qabḍ**, “Athtar of Qabḍ”: chief god of the Minaean Temple B at Barāqish/Yathill. Excavated inscriptions identify the temple’s god and building patrons.
- **ʿAṯtar ḏ-Rṣf**, “Athtar of Rṣf”: invoked in a dedication of an incense burner from as-Sawdāʾ.
- **ʿAṯtar bʿl ʾḏnn**, “Athtar, lord of ʾḏnn”: invoked for the protection of dedicants.
- **ʿAṯtar-Muṣurūn**, approximately “Attar of the borderlands/marches”: an Aramaic divine title from an Iron Age eastern context, commonly connected with deported or mobile Arab communities.
- **ʿAttar-šamayin / Atarsamain**, “Attar of Heaven”: North Arabian/Qedarite form reported in Neo-Assyrian sources.

These names need not represent separate gods. South Arabian practice frequently localized a broadly shared deity through a place, sanctuary, direction or community-specific epithet.

### Equations and non-equations

- **Athtar and Athtart/Astarte:** **[linguistic relationship secure; identity not secure]** They are cognate masculine and feminine names. Ugarit could recognize both as separate divine persons. “Athtar is Astarte’s husband” is not attested.
- **Athtar and Venus:** **[EA in South Arabia; MSR but strong comparatively for Ugarit]** South Arabian directional manifestations, comparative Semitic evidence and the later astral complex make the Venus identification strong. KTU 1.6 itself never names the planet.
- **Athtar and Ashtabi:** **[SI/LS]** A multilingual Ugaritic god list appears to align an ʿAthtar entry with the Hurrian martial god Aštabi. A lexical-list equation is not proof that every myth about one belonged to the other.
- **Ashtar-Chemosh:** **[EA/SI]** On the ninth-century BCE Mesha Stele, the compound receives the population devoted after the capture of Nebo. Whether this is a hypostatic fusion, an epithet of Chemosh, or two names in apposition remains debated.
- **Athtar and Šaḥar/Šalim:** **[MSR, disputed]** Older reconstructions treated Dawn and Dusk as Athtar’s morning- and evening-star hypostases. They occur as independent gods in Ugaritic literature; the identification is unnecessary and is now often rejected.
- **Athtar and Milkom:** **[MSR, poorly supported]** Older scholarship inferred an equation from putative common kingship and astral functions. No explicit ancient equation is known.
- **Athtar and Lucifer/Satan:** **[MI as an identity; MSR as a possible literary background]** Isaiah 14’s *hêlēl ben-šāḥar*, “shining one, son of Dawn,” may reuse a West Semitic morning-star-and-failed-ascent pattern comparable to Athtar. Isaiah does not name Athtar. The equation with the Christian devil is a much later reception of the Latin *lucifer*, not an Ugaritic doctrine.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The Ugaritic tablets

**[EA]** The Baal Cycle survives on six principal clay tablets, KTU 1.1–1.6, written in alphabetic cuneiform. They were excavated at Ras Shamra, ancient Ugarit, beginning in 1929–1930. Their colophons name the scribe **Ilimilku** and associate him with King Niqmaddu. The usual dating is the thirteenth century BCE, probably near its end, although debate continues over which Niqmaddu and how much of the poetry predates the surviving copies. The copies are ancient witnesses to a developed composition, not proof that its stories originated in that century.

### Primary Ugaritic passages

1. **KTU 1.6 i 43–67: the succession episode.**  
   **[EA]** Athirat says that Athtar the Awesome should be made king. Athtar goes up to the heights of Ṣapānu and sits on the throne of Mighty Baal. His feet do not reach the footstool; his head does not reach the top. Athtar recognizes that he cannot be king on Ṣapānu, descends from Baal’s throne, and rules **bʾarṣ**.

2. **KTU 1.1–1.2 and associated palace material.**  
   **[EA, readings disputed]** Older editions identified Athtar in fragmentary exchanges concerning divine houses and Yam. Several readings and reconstructions used in mid-twentieth-century studies are no longer universally accepted. Any claim that El permanently assigned Athtar to live with Yam and Nahar depends on restoration of damaged text.

3. **KTU 1.47 and KTU 1.118 with Akkadian RS 20.24.**  
   **[EA/LS]** These parallel pantheon or sacrificial lists provide evidence for scribal ordering and cross-cultural equations. Athtar’s position and counterpart have been used to argue for an equation with Aštabi. The lists disclose learned cultic classification, not necessarily popular theology.

4. **KTU 1.43 and related ritual texts.**  
   **[EA, identification disputed]** Some older treatments read an astral reference to the setting of Athtar, while others understand the feminine Athtart. Because the unvocalized names are close and tablet readings can be difficult, this cannot bear the full weight of proving Ugaritic Athtar’s Venus identity.

5. **Kirta and Aqhat.**  
   **[Negative finding]** The great Ugaritic epics are indispensable for the pantheon and literary context, but Athtar has no substantial secure narrative role comparable to Baal, El, Athirat, Anat or Athtart. Statements that make him an actor in Kirta or Aqhat ordinarily result from confusion with Athtart or from broad pantheon reconstruction.

### South Arabian inscriptional corpus

**[EA/RV]** From roughly the early first millennium BCE through the fourth century CE, ʿAthtar appears in a large and geographically dispersed corpus. Unlike the Ugaritic narrative, these texts are usually votive, dedicatory, architectural, legal, commemorative or oracular. They document what worshippers asked and thanked him for:

- protection of persons and children;
- military victory and preservation of communities;
- construction and restoration of sanctuaries;
- control or benefaction involving rain and agricultural water;
- curses against anyone damaging an inscription or tomb;
- oracle consultation;
- incense, animal sacrifice and other offerings.

Representative inscriptions include **CIH 419**, invoking Athtar against anyone who destroys a monument; **CIH 429**, thanking Athtar of Ḏabān for rescue; **CIH 441**, invoking Athtar Šarqān against a violator; **CIH 460–466 and 970**, the Jār al-Labbā oracular dossier; and the Barāqish inscriptions of Athtar ḏ-Qabḍ.

### Mesha Stele

**[EA/SI]** The Moabite royal inscription erected by King Mesha, conventionally dated about 840 BCE, says that after Nebo was captured its people were placed under the ban for **ʿAshtar-Chemosh**. This is the clearest first-millennium Levantine attestation of a masculine Ashtar form. It also directly connects the compound deity with warfare and mass killing. Whether the compound preserves an independent Athtar subordinated to Chemosh or represents a fused title is uncertain.

### Dayr ʿAllā

**[EA, reading/interpretation disputed]** The plaster inscriptions from Dayr ʿAllā, normally dated to the eighth century BCE, contain a difficult divine combination conventionally read **Šagar-we-ʿAshtar/Ištar**. The passage’s language, damaged condition and divine syntax prevent certainty. Its juxtaposition of a lunar name and an Ashtar-form has been interpreted as a moon–Venus pair and, more speculatively, as evidence of South Arabian influence.

### Assyrian records and North Arabia

**[EA/RV]** Neo-Assyrian royal records concerning Qedarite deities include an Atarsamain-type name among cult images captured by Sennacherib and restored by Esarhaddon. This supplies independent evidence for “Attar of Heaven” among northern Arabian groups. It is a related regional form, not evidence that Qedarites knew the Ugaritic throne story.

### Hebrew Bible

**[EA as hostile/comparative witness; no explicit Athtar]**

- **Isaiah 14:12–15** addresses *hêlēl ben-šāḥar*, a shining morning-star figure who seeks elevation above the divine stars and the mount of assembly but is brought down to Sheol. Athtar’s failed elevation is a plausible comparative background; direct literary dependence is not demonstrable.
- **2 Kings 23** condemns astral cults and installations associated with the sun, moon and host of heaven. It does not name Athtar.
- **1 Kings 11**, **Judges**, and related polemics name the feminine Ashtoreth/Astartes, Chemosh and Milkom. They should not be silently converted into evidence for masculine Athtar.
- Biblical passages about child sacrifice do not name Athtar.

### Philo of Byblos, Lucian, Herodotus and Josephus

**[Negative finding of importance]** Philo’s Phoenician history, preserved fragmentarily by Eusebius, and Lucian’s *De Dea Syria* are central to later Phoenician and Syrian religion, but they do not securely preserve the Ugaritic masculine Athtar throne episode. Their Astarte/Aphrodite and Syrian-goddess materials concern primarily feminine deities.

Herodotus discusses Arabian celestial worship and identifies an Arabian goddess with Aphrodite under forms such as Alilat; this is evidence for Greek interpretation of Arabian religion, not an explicit account of South Arabian Athtar. Josephus preserves historical and biblical traditions involving Phoenician cults but provides no independent biography of masculine Athtar.

## 3. Domain, attributes and iconography

### Kingship

**[EA]** KTU 1.6 makes Athtar a legitimate candidate for kingship: Athirat proposes him and he is permitted to test Baal’s throne. His failure is one of fitness, scale or allotted cosmic office—not moral wickedness. The episode reinforces Baal’s unique adequacy for kingship on Ṣapānu.

### Earth, irrigation and water

**[EA plus MSR]** After leaving the throne Athtar rules **bʾarṣ**. The normal meaning is “in/on the earth”; translating it “in the underworld” creates an infernal Athtar otherwise absent from secure evidence. South Arabian inscriptions strongly associate Athtar with rain, storm and agricultural well-being. Older scholars therefore reconstructed him as a god of artificial irrigation, contrasted with Baal’s coastal rain. The contrast is suggestive, but no Ugaritic text simply defines his “province” as irrigation.

### Venus and the morning star

**[EA comparatively; MSR for particular Ugaritic passages]** Athtar’s Semitic cognates, South Arabian directional epithets and morning/evening manifestations support an astral identity focused on Venus. The throne episode can be read astronomically: the brilliant morning star rises toward the divine mountain but cannot occupy the sun or storm god’s permanent height and descends. That interpretation is coherent but remains interpretation; the episode does not name Venus.

### Warfare

**[EA/RV]** Ashtar-Chemosh receives the enemy population placed under the Moabite ban. South Arabian Athtar is invoked in political and martial contexts. The learned equation with Aštabi may reflect martial compatibility. None of this proves that the Ugaritic Athtar’s principal domain was war.

### Protection, judgment and divination

**[EA/RV]** South Arabian dedications ask Athtar for rescue and protection and invoke him to punish tomb or inscription violators. The Jār al-Labbā texts identify an oracular domain associated with him. A 2021 reassessment argues that a ritual there involved sacrifice and the controlled dripping or manipulation of liquid, probably blood, to receive divine direction. “Probably blood” is the editor’s reconstruction, not preserved narrative description.

### Physical form and images

**[Negative finding]** No surviving Levantine image can be identified securely as the Ugaritic masculine Athtar. The Baal Cycle describes his body only relationally: it is too small to fill Baal’s throne. This is not necessarily a literal cult-statue description.

**[EA but attribution uncertain]** South Arabian temples produced anthropomorphic, animal and geometric imagery, yet inscriptions rarely label figures unambiguously. Bull, ibex, antelope, crescent, rosette, star and lightning imagery occurs in South Arabian religious art, but assigning any one of these as Athtar’s exclusive emblem is unsafe. There is no secure basis for a canonical color, weekday, number, weapon, plant or mount.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Parentage

**[MSR based on divine-family context]** Athtar is commonly called a son of Athirat and, by extension, El. KTU 1.6 has Athirat nominate him, which strongly indicates a maternal or patronal relationship but does not in the preserved succession speech state a complete genealogical formula. The broader Ugaritic convention that Athirat is mother of the divine family makes the genealogy plausible.

**[MSR, rejected as a universal genealogy]** Older South Arabian reconstruction made the moon god father, sun goddess mother and Venus-Athtar son. This tidy astral triad was once treated as the foundation of South Arabian religion. The inscriptions do not consistently narrate such a family, and recent scholarship is far less confident that every major South Arabian god was primarily an astral body.

### Consort and offspring

**[Negative finding]** No securely attested Ugaritic consort or child of masculine Athtar is known. Athtart/Astarte is not securely his consort. South Arabian manifestations occasionally occur beside maternal figures or goddesses, but no single empire-wide marriage system can be reconstructed.

### Every securely documented or materially proposed divine interaction

- **Athirat — nomination for kingship:** Athirat proposes Athtar the Awesome to replace dead or absent Baal. **Source:** KTU 1.6 i 43–48, thirteenth century BCE. **Tier:** [EA].
- **Baal — attempted succession:** Athtar occupies Baal’s throne, cannot fill it, acknowledges his inadequacy for kingship on Ṣapānu and descends. Baal is absent from the scene but his throne and office structure the interaction. **Source:** KTU 1.6 i 53–67. **Tier:** [EA].
- **El — authorization or acceptance of succession process:** the divine succession occurs within the senior gods’ deliberation; El’s precise speech and agency vary by restoration and translation. **Source:** KTU 1.6 i. **Tier:** [EA, damaged/contextually reconstructed].
- **Mot — indirect succession consequence:** Athtar’s candidacy follows the reported death of Baal at Mot’s hands. Athtar and Mot do not meet or fight in the surviving text. **Source:** KTU 1.5–1.6. **Tier:** [EA; absence of direct interaction].
- **Yam/Nahar — possible shared status or residence:** older readings of fragmentary palace material had El assign Athtar to dwell with Yam and Nahar because he lacked a house or wife. The reading is textually unstable and should not be treated as a secure episode. **Source:** damaged KTU 1.1–1.2 material. **Tier:** [EA fragment; MSR reconstruction].
- **Aštabi — scribal equation:** a multilingual Ugaritic list apparently aligns Athtar with the Hurrian martial deity Aštabi. No narrative meeting occurs. **Source:** KTU 1.47/1.118 and RS 20.24, Late Bronze Age. **Tier:** [SI/LS].
- **Chemosh — compound/fused recipient of the ban:** ʿAshtar-Chemosh receives the population of Nebo devoted by Mesha. **Source:** Mesha Stele line 17, ca. 840 BCE. **Tier:** [EA/SI].
- **Šagar — paired mention:** a damaged Dayr ʿAllā passage names Šagar-and-ʿAshtar/Ištar. No narrative action between them is preserved. **Source:** Dayr ʿAllā plaster inscriptions, eighth century BCE. **Tier:** [EA/RV; reading disputed].
- **Almaqah — joint invocation:** Sabaic inscriptions invoke Athtar and Almaqah in the same protective or dedicatory formulae. This is cultic coordination, not a preserved mythic encounter. **Source:** multiple Sabaic inscriptions, first millennium BCE–early centuries CE. **Tier:** [EA/RV].
- **Ḏāt Ḥimyam and other South Arabian gods:** Athtar appears with them in collective invocations for a dedicant’s safety, family and property. No narrative interaction is known. **Tier:** [EA/RV].
- **Athtart/Astarte — no documented meeting:** both exist in the broader Ugaritic corpus, but no surviving myth securely depicts masculine Athtar meeting, marrying or fighting the goddess. **Tier:** [Negative finding].
- **Šaḥar and Šalim — no secure encounter:** identification as Athtar’s hypostases or siblings is modern reconstruction; no preserved episode depicts an interaction. **Tier:** [Negative finding/MSR].

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Athtar and Baal’s throne

**[EA]** Baal has descended into Mot’s realm and is reported dead. El mourns, and Anat seeks Baal. The divine order requires a ruler. Athirat proposes Athtar the Awesome. Athtar ascends to the heights of Ṣapānu and sits on Baal’s throne. The poem then demonstrates inadequacy spatially: his feet fail to reach the footstool and his head fails to reach the throne’s top.

Athtar himself concludes that he cannot rule on Ṣapānu. He descends from the throne and then “rules in/on the earth.” The text does not depict a battle, forceful expulsion, fall from heaven, condemnation by El, or transformation into evil. His withdrawal may even be prudent acknowledgment of an unsuitable office.

**Function:** The scene vindicates Baal’s unique kingship. It also preserves a division of divine jurisdictions: Athtar is not nothing; he has a rule elsewhere. Political interpretations see Athtar as a model of a lesser king who must recognize the superior sovereign. Agricultural interpretations contrast Baal’s rain with Athtar’s earth or irrigation. Astral readings see the morning star rise and descend without attaining supremacy.

### The reconstructed cosmic-rebellion myth

**[MSR, disputed]** Because Isaiah 14 describes a shining celestial figure aspiring to the mount of assembly and then descending, several scholars have reconstructed a Canaanite myth in which the morning star challenges the chief god. Athtar’s throne scene is the closest surviving West Semitic parallel.

The differences are decisive. Athtar is nominated rather than self-appointed; the throne he tests belongs to Baal rather than explicitly to El; no combat occurs; and he voluntarily acknowledges the mismatch. Accordingly, KTU 1.6 may have contributed a traditional image or narrative structure behind Isaiah without preserving a “Lucifer rebellion.”

### South Arabian mythology

**[Negative finding]** South Arabian religion supplies abundant cult inscriptions but almost no continuous narrative mythology comparable to the Baal Cycle. Modern accounts that give Athtar elaborate adventures, romances, weapons and family dramas usually combine Ugaritic material, comparative Ishtar mythology and modern reconstruction. Such narratives should be labeled modern compositions.

## 6. Cult, temples and ritual practice

### Ugarit

**[EA, limited cult evidence]** Athtar’s literary presence is clearer than his institutional cult. He is not prominent in surviving Ugaritic offering lists in proportion to his dramatic role. There is no excavated Ugaritic temple securely identified as his, no known priesthood titled exclusively for him, and no securely identified cult image.

This imbalance matters. KTU 1.6 may remember a once-important god subordinated within Ugarit’s Baal-centered system, or Athtar may have been primarily a literary and regional figure whose practical cult was small at the city.

### Barāqish/Yathill

**[EA]** Excavation of Temple B at ancient Yathill, modern Barāqish, identified it through Minaic inscriptions as the temple of **ʿAthtar ḏ-Qabḍ**. The building evidence and inscriptions document construction, restoration, political patrons and the god’s local prominence. This is among the clearest archaeologically anchored Athtar sanctuaries.

### Jār al-Labbā

**[EA with interpretive reconstruction]** CIH 460–466, CIH 970 and related texts associate Jār al-Labbā with an “estate/domain of Athtar.” They refer to oracular direction and difficult ritual terminology. Jason Weimar’s reanalysis interprets the rite as sacrifice followed by dripping liquid—probably blood—to obtain an oracle. Earlier copies were poor, and the reading remains subject to revision.

### Offerings and vows

**[EA]** South Arabian worshippers dedicated inscriptions, incense burners and other objects; thanked Athtar for rescue, protection, children, property and success; and invoked him in curse clauses. Animal sacrifice is securely part of the wider South Arabian ritual system. No text examined here proves that human beings were regularly sacrificed to Athtar.

### Human sacrifice: dedicated contested-material finding

**[EA for Moabite killing; not proof of routine cult sacrifice]** The Mesha Stele says that Nebo’s population was killed and devoted to Ashtar-Chemosh. This is warfare under the ban, involving mass killing in a divine context. It is direct evidence of cultically framed violence associated with the compound name.

**[MSR, rejected when transferred indiscriminately]** Older scholars connected Athtar with biblical child-sacrifice passages, the account of St Nilus and other Arabian sacrifice traditions. These comparisons do not supply a contemporary inscription naming Athtar as recipient.

**[Negative finding for Carthage]** The Carthage tophet inscriptions invoke chiefly **Tanit and Baal Hammon**, not masculine Athtar. The tophet contains cremated infant and animal remains; whether all, most or some children were sacrificial victims remains debated. Whatever conclusion one adopts, the evidence must not be reassigned to Athtar merely because Astarte is a cognate name or because Carthage was Phoenician.

### Temple prostitution

**[Negative finding]** No secure Ugaritic or South Arabian source documents “sacred prostitution” in Athtar’s cult. Generalized claims about temple prostitution derive largely from Greek descriptions, biblical polemic and older comparative models. They cannot be attributed to this god without specific evidence.

### Theophoric names

**[EA]** Divine-name elements incorporating Athtar/Ashtar appear in personal names across Semitic regions. Phoenician names such as Bodashtart contain the feminine Astarte, however, and cannot automatically be counted as evidence for masculine Athtar. Gender and local spelling must be checked name by name.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before ca. 1200 BCE: formation and first surviving record

**[MSR/EA]** Linguistic comparison implies a pre-Ugaritic history for the ʿAṯtar/Ištar name complex, but the earliest secure dossier for this particular masculine West Semitic god is Ugaritic. At Ugarit he is already a known divine prince and possible king, suggesting an earlier tradition now lost.

### Thirteenth century BCE: Ugaritic subordination

**[EA]** The Baal Cycle gives Athtar high lineage and eligibility but denies him Baal’s office. His surviving local importance is chiefly literary. The multilingual lists place him in a cosmopolitan Syrian environment that included West Semitic, Hurrian and Mesopotamian equations.

### Ninth–eighth centuries BCE: Levantine and Transjordanian survivals

**[EA/RV]** The Mesha Stele preserves Ashtar-Chemosh in Moab. Dayr ʿAllā may preserve an Ashtar-form beside Šagar. These are regional adaptations, not proof that the complete Ugaritic narrative remained current.

### Early first millennium BCE–third century CE: South Arabian florescence

**[EA/RV]** In Saba, Maʿin, Qataban and Hadramawt, Athtar becomes one of the few widely shared gods above or alongside each kingdom’s patron deity. His epithets localize him without erasing the common name. Temples, dedications, irrigation economies, political campaigns, protection formulae and oracle practice show a living institutional cult.

Calling Athtar “the supreme god of all South Arabia” is too simple. He often heads lists, but Almaqah, ʿAmm, Wadd and Sīn could dominate particular kingdoms and state institutions. List position, universal distribution and political patronage measure different kinds of rank.

### North Arabian transmission

**[EA/RV]** Attar-of-Heaven and other Attar forms appear among Arabian populations known from Aramaic and Assyrian sources. Royal deportation, trade, military service and oasis networks offer plausible transmission mechanisms. These forms need not depend directly on Yemen or Ugarit.

### Fourth century CE: disappearance from official South Arabian religion

**[EA]** South Arabian royal inscriptions undergo a marked religious transformation in the later fourth century. Traditional divine lists, including Athtar and Almaqah, give way to invocations of **Raḥmānān**, “the Merciful,” Lord of Heaven, within a biblically informed monotheist environment associated especially with Ḥimyar. The latest polytheistic inscriptions cluster around ca. 380–400 CE.

This is evidence for disappearance from the official epigraphic sphere, not proof that every household cult ended immediately. The political unification of Ḥimyar, Jewish influence, Christian competition, long-distance trade and new royal ideology all contributed. There is no documented episode in which a specific Athtar temple was converted by force on a known date.

### Sixth–seventh centuries CE

**[LE/EA]** Jewish and Christian regimes competed in South Arabia in the sixth century; Aksumite intervention and later Sasanian authority reshaped the region. By the Islamic conquests of the seventh century, Athtar was no longer present in official South Arabian inscriptions. Later Arabic literature preserves antiquarian memories of pre-Islamic idols but no demonstrably continuous Athtar priesthood.

### Present

**[Negative finding]** No institution has demonstrated an unbroken line of Athtar worship from antiquity. Contemporary devotion occurs within small reconstructionist or eclectic pagan settings and is a revival based on archaeology, translations and comparative religion.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Rediscovery

**[EA/MSR]** European publication of South Arabian inscriptions in the nineteenth century first made Athtar’s southern prominence visible to modern scholarship. Excavation of Ugarit beginning in 1929 transformed him from a comparative divine name into a narrative actor whose failure on Baal’s throne could be read directly.

### Lucifer reception

**[MSR becoming popular MI]** Scholars noticed similarities between KTU 1.6 and Isaiah 14. Popular retellings then collapsed the distinction and declared that “Attar became Lucifer.” The historical sequence is more complex:

1. KTU 1.6 contains Athtar’s unsuccessful enthronement.
2. Isaiah 14 contains a taunt against a human ruler using morning-star and divine-mountain imagery.
3. The Septuagint rendered the figure with **heōsphoros**, and the Latin tradition used **lucifer**, an ordinary term for the morning star.
4. Christian interpretation eventually connected the passage with Satan.
5. Modern comparative works proposed Athtar as part of the older West Semitic background.

Thus Athtar is not called Lucifer in an ancient Canaanite source, and no ancient source says that he became Satan.

### Occult and esoteric literature

**[Negative finding]** Unlike Astarte, Baal, Belial or Astaroth, masculine Athtar has little securely documented role in Renaissance grimoires, ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn or classical demonology. **Astaroth**, a demonological name derived through biblical Ashtoreth/Astarte, is not evidence that masculine Athtar was demonized under that name.

Modern web pages sometimes give Athtar planetary seals, colors, magical correspondences or ranks in a “Canaanite zodiac.” Unless independently sourced, these are modern constructions.

### Neopaganism and reconstructionism

**[FR/MI]** Modern Canaanite reconstructionist religion developed principally in the late twentieth and twenty-first centuries. Individual practitioners sometimes honor Athtar as morning star, irrigation god or failed-but-wise claimant to kingship. There are no reliable membership figures for Athtar-specific worship, no widely recognized central organization and no evidence that reconstructed rites directly reproduce an ancient liturgy.

### Literature, games and commercial imagery

**[FR]** Athtar is much less prominent than Baal, Astarte or Ishtar. Modern mythology sites, role-playing resources and independent art increasingly depict him as a “fallen morning star.” Images often borrow Mesopotamian Ishtar stars, horned crowns or generic Canaanite costumes. Because no securely identified ancient portrait exists, these are modern visualizations.

### Political and extremist appropriation

**[Negative finding]** No substantial, well-documented nationalist or extremist Athtar movement emerged in the reviewed evidence. Broader Phoenicianist, Arab-pagan and regional identity movements may use Canaanite or South Arabian pantheons, but attributing their ideology specifically to Athtar without organizational evidence would exaggerate his role.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Major phases

1. **Comparative Semitic phase:** Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century scholars linked Arabian Athtar, Astarte and Ishtar and tried to reconstruct a universal Semitic astral religion.
2. **Ugaritic phase after 1929:** Newly discovered tablets supplied indigenous Northwest Semitic myth. Virolleaud, Caquot, Gray, Gaster and others debated Athtar’s role, palace, fertility and relation to Yam.
3. **Functional reconstructions:** Gray emphasized irrigation and a desert origin; Dahood stressed Venus; other scholars made Athtar a warrior or royal god displaced by Baal.
4. **Text-critical correction:** Mark S. Smith and later commentators stressed manuscript readings, divine hierarchy and the danger of importing South Arabian evidence across a millennium and great distance.
5. **Cosmic-rebellion debate:** Hugh Rowland Page and Michael Heiser argued, in different ways, for reflexes of an Athtar-type revolt behind biblical texts. Gregorio del Olmo Lete and others objected that KTU 1.6 supplies neither rebellion nor infernal kingship.
6. **South Arabian epigraphic turn:** Corpus-based work replaced the old sun–moon–Venus triad as a universal explanatory key with localized study of sanctuaries, political institutions and ritual vocabulary.

### Principal controversies

#### Was Ugaritic Athtar Venus?

The cognate and comparative case is strong. The direct Ugaritic proof is less explicit than often claimed. A careful formulation is: “probably the masculine Venus deity, with additional royal and terrestrial functions,” not “KTU 1.6 explicitly calls him Venus.”

#### Did he descend to earth or the underworld?

Ugaritic **ʾarṣ** can denote earth, land or the underworld according to context. Athtar’s lack of infernal associations elsewhere and the logic of cosmic jurisdiction favor “earth.” “He became king of hell” is a minority reconstruction.

#### Was he humiliated?

The throne scene dramatizes inadequacy, but Athtar is still nominated, ascends legitimately and receives another rule. “Humiliated dwarf” and “cowardly usurper” are interpretive embellishments.

#### Is South Arabian Athtar the same god?

They share the divine name and likely an inherited astral background. Yet identity across centuries and cultures cannot mean an unchanged personality. Ugaritic narrative, Moabite compound deity, Qedarite heavenly Attar and South Arabian cult forms are historically connected but locally distinct.

#### Is Ashtar-Chemosh male or female?

The unmarked *ʿštr* form and comparison with masculine Athtar favor a masculine component. The compound’s grammar and theology remain debated, and some older translations used “Ishtar-Chemosh,” encouraging a feminine interpretation.

#### Did Athtar receive human sacrifice?

Mass killing devoted to Ashtar-Chemosh is explicit on the Mesha Stele. A regular Athtar cult of child sacrifice is not. The Punic tophet controversy concerns Tanit and Baal Hammon and must not be transferred to Athtar.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before ca. 1300 BCE — [MSR]:** A prehistoric Semitic divine-name complex ancestral to ʿAṯtar, ʿAṯtart and Ištar is inferred linguistically.
- **Thirteenth century BCE — [EA]:** Ilimilku’s Ugaritic tablets preserve Athtar’s candidacy for Baal’s throne, KTU 1.6.
- **Thirteenth century BCE — [EA/LS]:** Ugaritic multilingual pantheon lists place Athtar within scribal systems of divine equivalence, probably including Aštabi.
- **Ca. 840 BCE — [EA]:** Mesha of Moab dedicates the defeated population of Nebo to Ashtar-Chemosh.
- **Eighth century BCE — [EA, disputed reading]:** Dayr ʿAllā text mentions Šagar-and-Ashtar/Ištar.
- **Eighth–seventh centuries BCE onward — [EA/RV]:** monumental South Arabian inscriptions document Athtar across the kingdoms of southern Arabia.
- **Seventh century BCE — [EA/RV]:** Neo-Assyrian records document an Atarsamain-type god among Qedarite cult images.
- **First millennium BCE — [EA]:** temples, dedications, curse formulae and political invocations establish Athtar’s extensive South Arabian cult.
- **Fifth–fourth centuries BCE — [EA]:** Athtar inscriptions occur along southern Arabian commercial and Red Sea networks.
- **Hellenistic period — [Negative finding]:** Phoenician and Punic texts abundantly attest feminine Astarte, but evidence for a continuing masculine Athtar cult in the Phoenician west is sparse or absent.
- **Late first millennium BCE–early centuries CE — [EA]:** Temple B at Barāqish flourishes as a sanctuary of Athtar ḏ-Qabḍ; Jār al-Labbā documents Athtar-associated oracles.
- **Ca. 380–400 CE — [EA]:** traditional gods disappear from official South Arabian royal epigraphy during the transition to Raḥmānān-centered monotheism.
- **Sixth century CE — [EA]:** Jewish and Christian regimes contest South Arabia; no official revival of Athtar is attested.
- **Seventh century CE — [EA/LE]:** Islamic rule consolidates monotheism; Athtar survives primarily as an antiquarian divine name.
- **Nineteenth century — [MSR]:** publication of Old South Arabian inscriptions restores Athtar to scholarly knowledge.
- **1929–1930 — [EA, discovery event]:** excavation of Ras Shamra reveals Ugaritic literature, including the Baal Cycle.
- **Mid-twentieth century — [MSR]:** irrigation, astral-triad and comparative “Canaanite Lucifer” theories proliferate.
- **Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries — [MSR]:** improved Ugaritic editions and South Arabian corpora localize the evidence and weaken overly unified reconstructions.
- **Present — [FR]:** small-scale reconstructionist devotion and popular retellings exist; no continuous cult or standard modern tradition can be demonstrated.

## 11. Sources

### Primary-source and corpus base

The decisive primary evidence is KTU 1.6 and the associated Ugaritic corpus; the Mesha Stele; Dayr ʿAllā; Neo-Assyrian records of Arabian deities; and the Sabaic, Minaic, Qatabanic and Hadramitic inscriptional corpora. South Arabian evidence should be consulted inscription by inscription because local epithets and chronology often matter more than generalized pantheon charts.

Philo of Byblos, Lucian, Herodotus, Josephus and the Hebrew Bible are useful comparative or reception witnesses. Their silence about a masculine Athtar is itself significant: they cannot fill gaps in his dossier merely because they discuss Astarte, Arabian religion, Phoenician cult or astral worship.

### Source-critical conclusions

- Athtar’s failed enthronement is secure.
- His transformation into Lucifer or Satan is not ancient Ugaritic tradition.
- Venus is highly probable but not explicitly named in the throne passage.
- South Arabian cult is abundant and materially documented; South Arabian narrative mythology is not.
- Athtar and Astarte are historically related names but separately attested divine persons.
- Ashtar-Chemosh is secure; its exact theology is not.
- Athtar-specific human sacrifice beyond the Moabite war-ban cannot presently be established.
- No securely labeled image, canonical iconography, consort, offspring, continuous modern cult or ancient occult tradition can be established.
