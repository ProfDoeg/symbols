# Milcom: Research Dossier

**Class:** male territorial, royal, or state **god** of Iron Age Ammon; later biblical “foreign god” and, through confusion with Molech/Moloch, a demonized idol in Christian reception. “Canaanite” is acceptable in the broad Northwest-Semitic cultural sense, but “Ammonite” is the historically precise designation. No evidence establishes Milcom as a specifically Phoenician or Punic god.

**Earliest attestation:** the earliest probable direct attestation is the fragmentary **Amman Citadel Inscription**, found on Jabal al-Qalʿa at Amman and dated paleographically to the late ninth or early eighth century BCE. Its opening is commonly restored **[m]lkm**, “[Mi]lkom,” but the initial letter and context are damaged; it is therefore early and highly probable, not absolutely secure. The earliest securely excavated, unambiguous evidence is indirect: the divine element *Milkom* in the name **Milkom-ʾur**, borne by an official of the Ammonite king Baʿalyasha/Baalis on a seal impression excavated at Tall al-ʿUmayri and dated around 600 BCE. Unprovenanced late-seventh-century seals reading “blessed by Milcom” may be older than that bulla but carry the normal evidentiary limitation of objects without archaeological context.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely or probably attested early material:** Iron Age inscription, excavated object, or early textual witness.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** biblical narrative or editorial construction whose narrated date precedes its extant literary form.
- **[RV] Regional or textual variant.**
- **[LS] Learned systematization or modern scholarly reconstruction.**
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification.**
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or modern reception.**
- **[NE] Not established:** inference for which the surviving evidence is insufficient.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The name

**[EA]** The consonantal divine name is **mlkm**, written in Ammonite/Phoenician script as 𐤌𐤋𐤊𐤌 and in biblical Hebrew as **מִלְכֹּם**, conventionally vocalized *Milkōm* or *Milkom* and anglicized **Milcom**. Greek biblical witnesses offer forms including **Μελχομ**, *Melchom*, and variant spellings.

The name belongs to the Northwest-Semitic root **mlk**, “to rule; king.” The final *-m* has been explained in several ways:

- **[LS]** A nominal ending or “mimation,” producing something like “kingly one” or “the king.”
- **[LS]** A pronominal construction *malkām*, “their king.” This is grammatically possible and creates the recurrent ambiguity between a divine name and an ordinary royal title.
- **[LS]** “King of the people,” from *melek ʿam*, is an older proposal but lacks compelling epigraphic support.
- **[LS]** Milcom may have begun as an epithet, “King,” of the Ammonite form of **El**, rather than as the proper name of an originally independent god. Craig Tyson favors this formulation; Collin Cornell warns that the present evidence cannot demonstrate an actual merger or identity. The disagreement is substantive, not merely terminological.

The onomastic resemblance to Ugaritic **mlk**, Mesopotamian **Malik/Malkum**, biblical **Molech**, and Punic *mlk* sacrificial terminology does not by itself prove that these words designated one god.

### Recorded forms and titles

- **Milkom/Milcom — מִלְכֹּם:** the conventional Hebrew and modern form; secure in the Masoretic Text at 1 Kings 11:5, 11:33, and 2 Kings 23:13.
- **[M]lkm:** probable restoration in the Amman Citadel Inscription.
- **Melchom/Melkom — Μελχομ:** Greek transliteration in biblical and later Christian transmission.
- **Malcam/Malkam — מַלְכָּם:** literally “their king,” but possibly Milcom in Jeremiah 49:1, 3; Zephaniah 1:5; Amos 1:15; 2 Samuel 12:30; and 1 Chronicles 20:2.
- **“God of the Ammonites/sons of Ammon”:** explicit in 1 Kings 11:33.
- **“Abomination/detestation of the Ammonites”:** hostile Judahite classification in 1 Kings 11:5 and 2 Kings 23:13, not an Ammonite cult title.
- **“King”:** plausible semantic title, but no surviving Ammonite text explicitly glosses the name this way.
- **“State god,” “national god,” “dynastic god”:** modern historical descriptions derived from the Citadel inscription, royal context, biblical testimony, and regional comparison—not ancient preserved titles.

No securely attested epithet assigns Milcom a particular element, animal, planet, festival, or calendrical day.

### Milcom, Molech, and Malcam

**[EA/LC; disputed]** These must not be silently collapsed.

1 Kings 11:5 and 11:33 name **Milcom** as Ammon’s god. In the Masoretic Text, 11:7 instead says Solomon built a high place for **Molech**, “the abomination of the Ammonites.” Since the same chapter otherwise expects Milcom, many textual critics regard *Molech* there as a scribal or vocalizing corruption. Some Greek witnesses read Milcom. Conversely, 2 Kings 23 distinguishes the place associated with Molech and fire rites in the Valley of Hinnom (23:10) from Milcom’s high place east of Jerusalem (23:13). This supports keeping the two apart.

In Jeremiah 49:1 and 49:3 the unpointed consonants can be read either **malkām**, “their king,” or **Milkom**. A deity is especially plausible in verse 3 because the figure’s priests and officials go into exile with him. Greek versions explicitly read a form of Milcom in several such passages. Zephaniah 1:5 likewise permits “those who swear by their king,” “by Molech,” or “by Milcom”; the parallel oath by Yahweh makes a second divine name attractive, but the witnesses disagree.

The biblical **Molech** texts associate *mlk* with child sacrifice or “passing through fire.” No Ammonite inscription assigns that rite to Milcom by name. The widespread assertion that Milcom was a bull-headed god to whom Ammonites burned children is therefore **[MA/MI]**, created largely by merging Milcom with later images of Moloch.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Amman Citadel Inscription

**[EA; probable restoration]** Discovered in 1961 on the citadel of ancient Rabbath-Ammon, the gray-black stone fragment measures approximately 24 × 19 cm and preserves eight incomplete lines in an Ammonite language written in an Aramaic-related script. Dates proposed range from the ninth to early eighth century BCE.

The commonly published reconstruction begins:

> “[Mi]lkom, he has built for you the precinct entrances …”

The fragment continues with building vocabulary, threats against hostile entrants, a promise to obliterate opponents, offerings within a portico, and a closing wish of peace. Interpretations include:

- a royal building inscription recording commands received from Milcom;
- a divine oracle in Milcom’s first-person voice;
- a temple or palace dedication;
- a defensive or foundation text with curse and blessing clauses.

The first letter of the divine name and much of the syntax are lost. Accordingly, the inscription probably presents Milcom as patron of royal construction and the protection of Rabbath-Ammon, but it does not securely identify the structure as his temple or supply a myth, genealogy, image, or ritual calendar. The artifact is held by Jordan’s Department of Antiquities; photographs and a translation are published by the West Semitic Research Project.

### Epigraphic and onomastic corpus

**[EA]** Five generally accepted Ammonite personal names contain *mlkm*:

- **ʿbdmlkm**, “servant of Milcom” or “Milcom’s servant” (CAI 1b).
- **mlkmʾwr**, commonly “Milcom is light/flame” (CAI 129), on the excavated Tall al-ʿUmayri bulla belonging to an official of Baʿalyasha.
- **mlkmgd** (CAI 127), perhaps “Milcom is fortune” or “Milcom has granted”; exact segmentation is debated.
- **mlkmʿz** (CAI 136), “Milcom is strength” or “Milcom has strengthened.”
- **mlkmyt** (CAI 147:1:1), on Tall al-Mazar Ostracon VII, conventionally interpreted as “Milcom has given.”

An unprovenanced late-seventh-century seal reads approximately **“seal of Mannu-kī-Inurta, blessed by Milcom”**; another, dated near the end of the sixth century BCE, invokes the deity similarly. Two other purported Milcom seals have been rejected as modern forgeries. The secure corpus must therefore be kept smaller than older catalogs sometimes suggest.

**[LS]** El-compound names overwhelmingly outnumber Milcom-compound names in the Ammonite corpus—estimates approach ninety percent for El names versus roughly seven percent for Milcom. Explanations include:

- El was the popular or familial god while Milcom was the dynastic state god.
- Milcom was a royal epithet of El.
- “El” was a generic word for god and could include Milcom.
- The surviving sample is too small and socially skewed to reproduce the full pantheon.

No one explanation is established.

### Hebrew Bible

The securely pointed occurrences are:

- **1 Kings 11:5:** Solomon “went after” Astarte and Milcom.
- **1 Kings 11:33:** Yahweh condemns the worship of Astarte, Chemosh, and Milcom.
- **2 Kings 23:13:** Josiah desecrates high places attributed to Solomon, including one for Milcom.

Textually disputed occurrences include 1 Kings 11:7; 2 Samuel 12:30; 1 Chronicles 20:2; Amos 1:15; Jeremiah 49:1, 3; and Zephaniah 1:5.

**[LC]** Kings reached its major Deuteronomistic forms in the late monarchic and exilic periods, although its narratives concern Solomon in the tenth century and Josiah in the late seventh. It is a hostile but indispensable witness. Its account may preserve genuine memory of foreign or immigrant cults in Jerusalem; it cannot by itself prove that Solomon personally founded the shrines exactly as narrated.

Jeremiah 49 turns Milcom into the divine representative of Ammon: he possesses Gad and later goes into exile with priests and officials. This is national-deity rhetoric comparable to descriptions of a conquered people and its god.

### Ugaritic, Phoenician, Punic, Greek, and Roman sources

**[NE]** Milcom does **not** appear securely as a character in the Ugaritic **Baal Cycle, Kirta, or Aqhat**, in the Amarna letters, Philo of Byblos’s Phoenician History as preserved by Eusebius, Lucian’s *De Dea Syria*, Herodotus, or the surviving relevant passages of Josephus. These corpora illuminate the wider West-Semitic religious environment but cannot be treated as a Milcom mythology.

Ugaritic god lists contain an **mlk**, and comparable names *Malik/Malkum* occur farther east. Whether any is genetically identical with Milcom is unresolved. The resemblance establishes a shared royal root, not continuity of personality or cult.

**[NE]** No Phoenician or Punic KAI inscription securely documents a cult of Milcom. Punic *mlk* in tophet inscriptions is normally analyzed as a sacrificial term or offering category, though its interpretation remains debated. It is not a secure spelling of Milcom. The Carthage tophet is consequently evidence for Punic ritual and the Molech debate, not direct evidence for Milcom.

A bilingual Canaanite–Ancient North Arabian inscription from **Qasr Bayir** names **MLKM** alongside **QWS** and **KMS**. Ahmad al-Jallad has discussed possible identifications with Milcom, Qos, and Chemosh. The consonantal equation is plausible, but the date, linguistic setting, and absence of explanatory context prevent certainty that MLKM is the Ammonite Milcom rather than a title or related deity.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Attested functions

**[EA]** The Citadel inscription probably places Milcom behind royal building, fortified or monumental entrances, protection of a structure and king, curses on enemies or violators, and blessings on legitimate occupants.

**[LC]** Kings and Jeremiah make him the divine patron and representative of Ammon. Jeremiah’s image of Milcom possessing Gad implies territorial sovereignty; his exile dramatizes Ammon’s defeat.

**[LS]** Likely domains include kingship, dynasty, territorial protection, warfare, public order, and the prosperity of the Ammonite state. A weather-god function has been proposed by analogy with other Levantine state gods but is unattested in an explicit Milcom text.

### Iconography

**[NE]** No surviving image is inscribed “Milcom,” so no depiction is certain.

Several eighth–sixth-century BCE stone statues from the Amman region portray male figures, sometimes bearded and wearing Egyptianizing **atef** crowns. Proposed identifications include Ammonite kings, deified rulers, El, or Milcom. The atef crown indicates Egyptianizing royal-divine language but does not name the figure.

The seal of Milkom-ʾur carries a four-winged scarab or beetle pushing a solar disk, with standards, disks, and crescents. Because the owner’s name invokes Milcom, this scene has been used to infer solar, lunar, celestial, or royal aspects of the god. That conclusion is weak: closely related imagery occurs on other seals whose owners’ names do not invoke Milcom, and the winged scarab may be a royal emblem rather than a divine portrait.

Bull imagery is common on Ammonite seals and is conventionally associated with El or royal strength. Assigning every Ammonite bull to Milcom—or reconstructing Milcom as bull-headed—is unwarranted.

No securely documented sacred color, number, weekday, planet, weapon, plant, or named animal belongs to Milcom.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy and pantheon

**[NE]** No source names Milcom’s parents, birth, siblings, consort, offspring, servants, mount, or mythic enemy.

**[LS]** Astarte or Asherah has occasionally been reconstructed as his consort by analogy with other Levantine pantheons and Ammonite female figurines. No Ammonite inscription joins either goddess to Milcom; the proposal remains speculative.

**[LS]** Milcom’s relation to El has three principal models:

1. distinct gods, with El dominant in domestic names and Milcom prominent in royal ideology;
2. Milcom as the specifically Ammonite royal title of El;
3. Milcom as a once-distinct deity progressively assimilated to El.

The evidence presently cannot chart a chronological merger.

### Every documented god–god interaction or joint episode

There is no surviving Ammonite myth in which Milcom speaks to or acts upon another named god. The complete recoverable list consists of biblical joint appearances:

- **Yahweh — condemnation and dispossession:** Yahweh condemns Solomon’s worship of Milcom and tears most of the kingdom from Solomon’s dynasty; 1 Kings 11:5, 9–13, 31–33, composed or edited principally in the seventh–sixth centuries BCE. **[LC; hostile canonical narrative]**
- **Astarte/Ashtoreth — joint object of Solomon’s apostasy:** both are worshiped in the same episode but do not interact with each other; 1 Kings 11:5, 33. **[LC]**
- **Chemosh — parallel national god and neighboring cult:** Milcom, Chemosh, and Astarte appear in the same condemnation; Solomon’s neighboring high places for Chemosh and Milcom are later desecrated together; 1 Kings 11:7, 33; 2 Kings 23:13. **[LC; textual variant at 11:7]**
- **Molech — scribal or interpretive substitution, not a narrated encounter:** 1 Kings 11:7 reads Molech in the Masoretic Text where the context may require Milcom; 2 Kings 23:10 and 23:13 distinguish their cult places. **[RV/SI]**
- **El — no narrated interaction:** identity, epithet, or merger is a modern reconstruction from names and comparative religion. **[LS/NE]**
- **MLK/Malik/Malkum — no narrated interaction:** possible etymological relatives at Ugarit and in Mesopotamia, but no source connects them personally. **[LS/NE]**
- **Qos and Chemosh/KMS at Qasr Bayir — joint inscriptional appearance if MLKM is Milcom:** three divine names occur together, without narrative action. **[EA/RV; identification uncertain]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

No indigenous Milcom myth survives. Claims about his marriage, battles, death and resurrection, seasonal descent, or creation of humanity are modern composites drawn from other West-Semitic gods.

### Solomon and the high place

**[LC]** In 1 Kings 11, Solomon’s foreign wives turn his heart toward their gods. He follows Astarte and Milcom and constructs high places east of Jerusalem for the gods of neighboring peoples. Yahweh responds by announcing the division of Solomon’s kingdom.

The story functions as a Deuteronomistic explanation for political catastrophe: religious disloyalty causes national division. Milcom is not characterized internally; his role is to embody Ammonite cult as a rival to exclusive Yahweh worship.

Variants matter. Verse 7’s Masoretic “Molech” conflicts with verses 5 and 33’s Milcom. Some Greek witnesses repair the sequence to Milcom. It is therefore possible that the original account consistently named Milcom and that later transmission introduced Molech.

### Josiah’s destruction of the high places

**[LC with plausible historical setting]** In 2 Kings 23:13, Josiah desecrates the high places east or south of Jerusalem attributed to Solomon: those of Astarte, Chemosh, and Milcom. The narrative belongs to the account of Josiah’s late-seventh-century reform.

The passage attests at minimum that a later Judahite author knew a cult site traditionally connected to Milcom. Whether it had operated continuously since Solomon’s reign, had been founded later and retrojected, or was being symbolically grouped with foreign cults cannot be determined.

### Milcom possesses Gad and goes into exile

**[LC/RV]** Jeremiah 49:1 asks why Milcom—or “their king”—has inherited Gad and why his people occupy its towns. Verse 3 orders lament because Milcom will go into exile with priests and officials. This personifies the god as both conqueror and conquered national patron. It is not an Ammonite narrative but a Judahite oracle against Ammon, probably shaped around the late monarchic or early exilic period.

### David takes the crown

**[RV]** In 2 Samuel 12:30 and 1 Chronicles 20:2 David removes a heavy gold crown from **malkām**. The word can mean “their king,” but some Greek witnesses understand Milcom. On the divine reading, David takes the crown from Milcom’s cult statue after capturing Rabbah; the enormous reported weight better suits a ceremonial image than a wearable crown. Later demonological accounts transformed this ambiguous episode into Milcom’s defeat or demotion. The biblical text itself narrates no transformation into a demon.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Rabbath-Ammon

**[EA]** Rabbath-Ammon, modern Amman, is the likeliest principal cult center because it was Ammon’s capital and because the Citadel inscription invokes or probably invokes Milcom in connection with monumental construction. The exact building has not been securely identified.

No excavated Ammonite structure is universally accepted as “the Temple of Milcom.” Proposed candidates include a building on the citadel’s middle terrace beneath or near the later Roman sanctuary and small cultic installations elsewhere in Ammon. Identification exceeds the evidence.

### Jerusalem

**[LC]** Kings locates Milcom’s high place on the hill east of Jerusalem, traditionally associated with the Mount of Olives and called the “Mount of Destruction/Corruption” in the reform narrative. Its construction is credited to Solomon; its desecration to Josiah. No archaeological remains have been securely identified as this shrine.

### Personnel and rites

Jeremiah 49:3 gives Milcom—or Ammon’s king—**priests and officials**, furnishing the only possible textual reference to his organized priesthood. The Citadel inscription appears to mention offerings in a portico, although its broken context prevents a ritual reconstruction.

Theophoric names show individual and courtly attachment: naming a person “Milcom is light,” “Milcom strengthens,” or “servant of Milcom” implies trust in the deity’s beneficence. They do not establish a priestly rank or particular ceremony.

No festival, hymn, prayer, oracle procedure, divination method, temple economy, sacred marriage, or pilgrimage to Milcom survives by name.

### Human sacrifice

**[NE; contested]** There is no direct Ammonite inscription, excavated altar, or text saying that a child was sacrificed to Milcom. The case arises from three conflations:

1. 1 Kings 11:7’s possibly corrupt identification of Molech as Ammonite;
2. biblical prohibitions of the *molek* rite and child-burning;
3. Punic tophet deposits and inscriptions using *mlk* terminology.

The Carthage tophet remains relevant to the broader history of *mlk*: excavations recovered cremated remains of infants and animals, while inscriptions describe vowed offerings. Scholars dispute the proportion of sacrificial killings, substitutions, and burials of children who died naturally. Whatever the resolution, the evidence is Punic and invokes chiefly Baal Hammon and Tinnit, not Milcom. It cannot be transferred to Ammon without independent evidence.

“Temple prostitution” is likewise unattested for Milcom.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the ninth century BCE

**[NE]** No secure Bronze Age Milcom is known. Ugaritic *mlk* and eastern *Malik/Malkum* may represent cognate divine terminology, but a continuous cult into Iron Age Ammon cannot be demonstrated.

### Ninth–eighth centuries BCE

**[EA]** The Amman Citadel inscription probably presents Milcom as patron of royal building and protection at Rabbath-Ammon. If the restoration is correct, this is the earliest evidence for a close god–king–capital relationship.

Assyrian records name Ammonite kings and establish the kingdom’s participation in imperial tribute networks, but they do not describe Milcom’s cult.

### Seventh–early sixth centuries BCE

**[EA/LC]** This is the best-documented phase. Personal names invoke Milcom; the Tall al-ʿUmayri bulla links Milkom-ʾur to King Baʿalyasha/Baalis; unprovenanced seals invoke Milcom’s blessing. Judahite literature identifies him as Ammon’s god and locates his cult in Jerusalem.

The contrast between royal Milcom evidence and abundant El names has produced the “dynastic god versus family god” model, though it remains reconstruction rather than an ancient theological statement.

### Babylonian and Persian periods

Jeremiah’s image of Milcom going into exile belongs to the experience or expectation of imperial conquest. Archaeological and textual evidence indicates that Ammonite identity did not disappear instantaneously, but securely dated postmonarchic worship of Milcom becomes sparse. Tall al-Mazar’s *Milkomyat* may extend onomastic evidence into the fifth century BCE.

### Hellenistic and Roman Amman

Rabbath-Ammon became Philadelphia. Greek and Roman cults, including Zeus, Heracles, and Tyrian or Syrian divine forms, appear in the city’s later history. **[NE]** No inscription establishes that the Roman temple on Amman’s citadel was a continuation of Milcom’s temple or that Heracles/Melqart was an interpretatio graeca of Milcom. Such proposals are possible historical reconstructions, not documented equations.

### Late antiquity through the medieval period

With the Christianization and later Islamization of the region, the named public cult of Milcom disappears from the recoverable local record. No continuous folk cult has been demonstrated. Biblical commentary preserved his name, increasingly placing him beside Molech among condemned idols.

### Present

No organized, historically continuous worship survives. Individual modern polytheists or demonolaters may address Milcom, but no reliable demographic evidence establishes the scale, institutional continuity, or shared ritual content of such practice.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Christian demonology

**[MA/SI]** Biblical polemic already calls Milcom an “abomination,” but it does not classify him as a demon. Christian theological traditions later treated pagan gods generically as demons and collapsed **Milcom, Melchom, Molech, and Moloch**.

Collin de Plancy’s nineteenth-century *Dictionnaire infernal* presents **Melchom** as a demon associated with money or the infernal treasury, commonly paraphrased as the “paymaster” or purse-bearer of Hell. This office has no ancient Ammonite precedent. It is a learned demonological invention derived from the transmitted name.

### Occult and esoteric use

**[MI]** Modern websites and demonological manuals sometimes assign Milcom sigils, planetary correspondences, ranks, incense, colors, directions, or powers over finance, rulership, war, fertility, or flame. None is supported by Ammonite evidence. The money-office descends from later Melchom demonology; fire and child-sacrifice imagery comes from Moloch; solar and lunar claims extrapolate from one royal seal.

Milcom does not have a securely documented role in the classical Solomonic grimoires, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or established Renaissance planetary magic under his own distinguishable identity.

### Literature, art, games, and popular religion

Milcom’s independent cultural profile remains far smaller than Moloch’s. Searches for modern depictions frequently redirect to Moloch, and modern art commonly borrows the latter’s furnace, bull head, enthroned idol, or child-sacrifice imagery. That iconographic package is not ancient Milcom material.

Contemporary “Canaanite reconstructionist” pages occasionally offer prayers or reconstructed attributes for Milcom. Such practices may be sincere modern religion, but where they give him a detailed personality, consort, feast day, sigil, or ritual formula, these are **modern constructions necessitated by the absence of an indigenous liturgy**.

No documented nationalist or extremist movement appears to have adopted Milcom as a central symbol. Modern claims of an ancient, continuous secret cult are unsupported.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### The principal disputes

1. **Was Milcom Ammon’s chief god?**  
   The Bible says yes; probable royal epigraphy supports a prominent state role. The rarity of Milcom names and predominance of El names complicate the title “chief god.”

2. **Was Milcom El?**  
   Tyson and earlier scholars propose an Ammonite royal title of El. Cornell argues that shared royal, bull, or regional characteristics do not demonstrate identity; gods can resemble one another without merging.

3. **Was Milcom Molech?**  
   Older biblical scholarship commonly equated them. Modern text criticism observes that 1 Kings 11:7 may be corrupt and that 2 Kings 23:10, 13 distinguishes cult sites. Similar spelling and later reception explain the merger better than secure Ammonite evidence.

4. **Does *malcam* mean “their king” or “Milcom”?**  
   Each passage must be judged independently. Priests, divine oaths, cult statues, and national personification can favor Milcom; ordinary syntax can favor “their king.” Greek and Hebrew witnesses sometimes diverge.

5. **What does the Citadel inscription say?**  
   “[M]ilkom” is a one-letter restoration. Scholars disagree over divine oracle versus royal narration and palace, fortress, or temple construction.

6. **Can images be identified?**  
   Atef-crowned statues, bulls, scarabs, disks, and crescents belong to Ammonite visual culture, but none bears Milcom’s name. Identifications range from kings to deified kings, El, Milcom, or general royal imagery.

7. **Was Milcom a weather or astral god?**  
   Both are comparative reconstructions. The former rests on the common pattern of Levantine royal gods; the latter on the Milkom-ʾur seal and its celestial symbols. Neither is explicit in a primary source.

### Major modern scholarship

- Walter E. Aufrecht’s *A Corpus of Ammonite Inscriptions* remains fundamental for epigraphy.
- Émile Puech’s work treats the inscriptions and biblical spelling problems, especially Milcom/Molech.
- John Day, *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan*, places Milcom within the wider history of Israelite and West-Semitic religion.
- Mark S. Smith’s *The Early History of God* and *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism* provide comparative models for national gods, El, and pantheon restructuring, although they do not supply otherwise lost Milcom myths.
- Craig W. Tyson argues for Milcom as the royal Ammonite manifestation or title of El.
- Collin Cornell urges a moratorium on insufficiently demonstrated “god mergers.”
- Larry Herr, Randall Younker, Burton MacDonald, Gloria London, and others have developed the archaeological and historical setting of Ammon.
- The *Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible* distinguishes the relevant biblical and comparative evidence.
- Edward Lipiński’s work on West-Semitic religions supplies the larger Phoenician and Aramaean context; Corinne Bonnet’s work on Melqart is valuable for comparison but does not establish Milcom–Melqart identity.

### State of the evidence

The corpus is exceptionally small:

- one fragmentary monumental inscription with a restored divine name;
- several personal names;
- a handful of seals, some unprovenanced and some forged;
- hostile biblical witnesses;
- unidentified statues and seal imagery;
- no surviving indigenous myth, hymn, temple archive, priestly handbook, or securely identified cult image.

This scarcity makes negative conclusions important. The Baal Cycle, Kirta, Aqhat, Philo of Byblos, Lucian, Herodotus, Josephus, and the Carthage tophet do not fill those gaps merely because they concern geographically or linguistically related religions.
