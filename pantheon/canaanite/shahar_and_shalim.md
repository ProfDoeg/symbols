# Shahar and Shalim: Research Dossier

**Class:** Two masculine West Semitic gods, normally paired: Šaḥar, the personified Dawn, and Šalim, the personified Dusk or completion of the day. Their precise astral character—twilight itself, the morning and evening stars, or both—is debated.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Late Bronze Age Ugarit, most clearly on the Ugaritic cuneiform tablet RS 2.002 = KTU/CAT 1.23, conventionally dated to the thirteenth century BCE, before Ugarit’s destruction around 1185 BCE. The tablet was excavated in 1930 in the so-called House of the High Priest at Ras Shamra. The much earlier place-name Jerusalem may contain Šalim’s name, but that is an etymological inference, not a direct attestation of the god.

## Evidentiary labels

- **Securely attested early:** Directly present in a Late Bronze Age Ugaritic text or cult list.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** Biblical or later scriptural reception, not evidence for what Ugaritians themselves believed.
- **Regional variant:** Evidence from another West Semitic locality or language whose identification with the Ugaritic figures is plausible but not automatic.
- **Learned systematization:** Ancient or modern harmonization of fragmentary evidence into a pantheon or continuous myth.
- **Syncretistic identification:** An explicit or proposed equation with another deity or celestial figure.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** A conclusion inferred by modern philology, comparative mythology, or archaeology.
- **Modern invention:** A claim or narrative absent from the ancient evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The pair

**Šaḥar and Šalim**, Ugaritic *šḥr w šlm*, are a fixed pair in several Ugaritic texts. KTU 1.23 recounts their birth after the god El has intercourse with two women. Other texts locate the pair “in the heavens,” include them in divine lists, or invoke them in rituals against poison.

The pair is widely interpreted as complementary deities of the two thresholds of daylight:

- **Šaḥar:** Dawn, daybreak, and possibly the morning star.
- **Šalim:** Dusk, sunset, the completion of the day, and possibly the evening star.

The equation with the morning and evening manifestations of Venus is plausible and widespread in modern scholarship but should not be overstated. Ugaritic religion also possessed the astral deity ʿAṯtar, who has a stronger independent association with the morning star. The surviving texts never say in an unambiguous astronomical formula, “Šaḥar is Venus in the morning and Šalim is Venus in the evening.”

### Names and scripts

| Being | Ugaritic | Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform | Hebrew cognate | Meaning |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Šaḥar | *šḥr*, occasionally written *šhr* in KTU 1.23 | 𐎌𐎈𐎗 | שַׁחַר, *šaḥar* | dawn, daybreak |
| Šalim | *šlm*; vocalization reconstructed as Šalim, Šalīm, Šalem, or Šalēmu | 𐎌𐎍𐎎 | שָׁלֵם, *šālēm*; related to שָׁלוֹם, *šālôm* | dusk or completion; also wholeness, safety, well-being |

The vocalizations are reconstructed because Ugaritic alphabetic writing normally records consonants, not full vowels.

### Šaḥar

**Securely attested early:** The noun *šḥr* means dawn or daybreak in Ugaritic and other Semitic languages. The divine use is secured by its pairing with Šalim in KTU 1.23, 1.100, 1.107, and 1.123.

**Modern scholarly reconstruction:** Dawn may have been understood as the morning star’s parent or temporal setting. Isaiah 14:12’s Hebrew *hêlēl ben-šaḥar*, “shining one, son of Dawn,” shows that Dawn could be personified genealogically in later West Semitic poetic language. It does not establish that Ugaritic Šaḥar had a son named Hêlēl.

### Šalim

The name belongs to the Semitic root Š-L-M, whose semantic field includes completeness, soundness, safety, well-being, fulfillment, and peace.

**Securely attested early:** The pairing *šḥr w šlm* establishes a complementary relationship. Šalim is also independently named in Ugaritic offering lists.

**Modern scholarly reconstruction:** “Dusk” or “sunset” is inferred from the complementary pairing with Dawn and comparison with expressions such as Akkadian *šalām šamši*, “the setting/completion of the sun.” “The Perfect One,” “Well-being,” and “Peace” are possible lexical interpretations but do not prove that Šalim was primarily a god of peace.

### Collective titles and the “Gracious Gods” problem

KTU 1.23 invokes the *ʾilm nʿmm*, commonly translated “Gracious Gods,” “Goodly Gods,” “Pleasant Gods,” or “Beautiful Gods.” They are also called voracious beings whose appetite reaches from earth to heaven.

Older and popular presentations commonly identify these gods directly with Šaḥar and Šalim. That identification is disputed.

- **Identification theory:** Šaḥar and Šalim are the Gracious Gods whose birth and hunger form the text’s central myth.
- **Separation theory:** The tablet narrates two successive births: first Šaḥar and Šalim, then a distinct pair or group called the *ʾilm nʿmm*. Toshio Tsumura emphasized that the Goodly Gods occur in the liturgical frame while Dawn and Dusk appear only in the narrative, and Jean-Marie Husser likewise distinguishes the two births.
- **Royal-ritual theory:** Husser proposes that the *ʾilm nʿmm* represent royal children undergoing an integration rite, while the birth of Šaḥar and Šalim supplies a divine model.
- **Mark S. Smith’s interpretation:** The birth of Dawn and Dusk completes or evokes a conventional divine generation; the following birth of the voracious Goodly Gods is the text’s more immediate concern.

The separation theory best respects the surviving narrative sequence, but the tablet is damaged and no interpretation commands unanimity. Calling Shahar and Shalim “the Gracious Gods” without qualification is therefore unsafe. The structure and disputes are summarized in Husser’s edition and Smith’s specialist monograph. [Husser](https://archimede.unistra.fr/websites/misha/archimede/Revue_Archimede_RAHA/Numero_7/G_HUSSER_2_DOI.pdf) [Smith](https://www.sbl-site.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Smith_Ritual.pdf)

### Claimed equations

- **Morning and evening Venus:** Plausible modern reconstruction; not an explicit Ugaritic equation.
- **ʿAṯtar:** Often connected to Isaiah’s fallen morning star, but no Ugaritic text identifies ʿAṯtar as Šaḥar. Their functions may overlap.
- **Azizos and Monimos:** A proposed continuity with later Syrian paired astral gods. This is comparativist reconstruction, not a documented ancient equation.
- **The Dioscuri:** A functional comparison based on divine twinning and astral liminality, not an ancient identification.
- **Shalman/Šulmānu:** KAI 282 from Sidon dedicates an offering to *Šlmn*, conventionally Shalman. The similar consonants do not establish identity with Šalim. The inscription belongs to the Persian period, approximately 550–300 BCE. [DEAPS edition of KAI 282](https://deaps.osu.edu/text_objects/11722)

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### RS 2.002 = KTU 1.23

**Securely attested early.**

RS 2.002 was found in 1930 in the House of the High Priest at Ugarit. Its upper-right corner is missing and portions of its recto are damaged, but much of its careful alphabetic cuneiform writing remains legible. The recto contains twenty-nine lines separated into small units by horizontal rules; the mythic narrative begins around line 30 and continues on the crowded reverse. The original publication was by Charles Virolleaud in 1933. [Excavation and tablet description](https://archimede.unistra.fr/websites/misha/archimede/Revue_Archimede_RAHA/Numero_7/G_HUSSER_2_DOI.pdf)

Its date is archaeological and palaeographic, not an internal year-date: broadly thirteenth century BCE, within the final centuries of Ugarit.

The tablet combines:

1. Invocations and invitations to feast.
2. Blessings for king and queen.
3. Instructions to recite passages seven times.
4. References to singers or ritual personnel.
5. Agricultural, culinary, sexual, and birth imagery.
6. A myth of El and two women.
7. The births of Šaḥar and Šalim.
8. A subsequent conception and birth of the voracious *ʾilm nʿmm*.
9. Their departure into the steppe and eventual approach to cultivated land.

The physical document therefore supports some relationship between myth and ritual performance. It does not prove that priests or rulers physically reenacted El’s sexual union. Mark S. Smith’s reexamination specifically questions whether KTU 1.23 documents an enacted *hieros gamos* rather than poetic myth associated with a different rite. [Smith’s study](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.5325/j.ctv1bxgzv8.7)

### KTU 1.100 = RS 24.244

**Securely attested early.**

This Ugaritic incantation or historiola concerns snakebite or venom. A female speaker appeals repeatedly to “Mother Šapšu,” the sun goddess, asking her to carry a message to different divine powers. At line 52 she sends the message to *šḥr w šlm šmmh*, “Šaḥar and Šalim in the heavens.” They are asked to remove or overcome the serpent’s venom.

This is important because it:

- Locates the pair in heaven.
- Gives them an operative ritual role beyond their birth story.
- Places them in a sequence of divine powers addressed against poison.
- Connects them through messenger action with Šapšu.

It does not explain why they can counter venom or describe a separate cult myth about them. The translation belongs among the ritual and incantation texts edited by Dennis Pardee. [Pardee, *Ritual and Cult at Ugarit*](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/isbn/9781628376807/html?lang=en)

### KTU 1.107

**Securely attested early.**

Another incantatory text invokes a series of gods against poison. The sequence includes El and Horon, Baal and Dagan, Anat and ʿAthtart, Yarikh and Resheph, ʿAthtar and ʿAthtapar, Ẓiẓẓu and Kāmaṯu, Šaḥar and Šalim, Milku, Kothar-wa-Khasis, and Šapšu.

The pair’s inclusion confirms that KTU 1.100 is not an isolated appearance. The text records co-invocation, not a narrative meeting between Šaḥar and all the other gods.

### KTU 1.123

**Securely attested early.**

This litany or divine list names the pair together at line 11. The surrounding sequence includes El, Dagan and Baal, Yarikh, Kothar-wa-Khasis, ʿAthtar, Šaḥar and Šalim, and personified abstractions or obscure divine names. Its repeated formulas involving *šlm* also create ambiguity between the proper name Šalim and the ordinary noun “peace/well-being.”

The text confirms pantheon membership but supplies no narrative or genealogy. A published transcription gives line 11 as *šḥr w šlm*. [Text and translation of KTU 1.123](https://repozytorium.kul.pl/bitstreams/bcbddb1b-b379-4af2-a8cd-67ad1524f64c/download)

### Ugaritic offering lists

**Securely attested early for Šalim; less clear for Šaḥar.**

Šalim, without Šaḥar, is read as a divine name or epithet in KTU 1.39, 1.41, 1.43, 1.47, 1.90, 1.109, 1.118, and 1.130. The lists include offerings interpreted as cattle and rams. The repeated independent entries suggest that Šalim was more than a literary personification.

Some readings remain vulnerable to the ordinary meaning “well-being” or “completion.” Context—especially placement among named recipients and receipt of an animal—usually favors a deity. A survey of these attestations and their implications appears in the German study of Jerusalem’s early religious landscape. [Survey and references](https://d-nb.info/127950482X/34)

### The Baal Cycle, Kirta, and Aqhat

**Absence of evidence.**

Šaḥar and Šalim do not play narrative roles in the surviving Baal Cycle, Kirta, or Aqhat epics. These compositions are essential for reconstructing Ugaritic divine society, but they do not provide additional myths of the pair.

The pair should consequently not be inserted into Baal’s combat with Yamm or Mot, Anat’s violence, Kirta’s marriage, or Aqhat’s death. Standard translations of those works are collected in Simon B. Parker’s *Ugaritic Narrative Poetry*. [Bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Ugaritic_Narrative_Poetry.html?id=Ed0NAAAAYAAJ)

### Amarna letters

The fourteenth-century BCE Amarna correspondence names Jerusalem as *Urusalim/Urushalim* in letters from its ruler, Abdi-Heba. The letters do not narrate or explicitly mention a god Šalim.

**Modern scholarly reconstruction:** If the city-name means “foundation of Šalim,” it would indirectly attest Šalim’s name and perhaps a local cult earlier than KTU 1.23. Because this depends on disputed etymology, it is not the earliest secure attestation of the deity.

### Hebrew Bible

#### Isaiah 14:12–15

**Later canonical elaboration with older West Semitic imagery.**

The Hebrew text addresses a fallen figure:

> אֵיךְ נָפַלְתָּ מִשָּׁמַיִם הֵילֵל בֶּן־שָׁחַר  
> *ʾêk nāpalta miššāmayim hêlēl ben-šaḥar*  
> “How you have fallen from heaven, shining one, son of Dawn.”

The surrounding poem is explicitly a taunt against the king of Babylon. The celestial figure sought to ascend above the stars of El, sit on the divine assembly’s mountain, and become like the Most High, but was brought down to the underworld.

What the passage establishes:

- *Šaḥar*, Dawn, is capable of mythic personification.
- A celestial “shining one” can be styled Dawn’s son.
- The poet knows or constructs a failed heavenly ascent myth.

What it does not establish:

- That Hêlēl is Šaḥar himself.
- That Hêlēl is Šalim.
- That Ugaritic Šaḥar had a securely attested son named Hêlēl.
- That the poem originally described Satan.
- That the complete story survives in KTU 1.23.

The traditional reconstruction identifies Hêlēl with the morning star, Venus, and often compares the passage to ʿAṯtar’s unsuccessful occupation of Baal’s throne in the Baal Cycle. Objections include the lack of a complete match: the Ugaritic ʿAṯtar episode does not call ʿAṯtar “son of Šaḥar,” and its plot differs from Isaiah’s ascent above the stars. A newer proposal interprets Hêlēl through the Ugaritic chthonic sun rather than Venus. [William Gallagher’s reassessment](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A76163d31-ede1-4f6a-b3ff-4e63b499d448)

The Greek Septuagint rendered Hêlēl as *heōsphoros*, “dawn-bringer”; Jerome’s Vulgate used Latin *lucifer*, “light-bearer/morning star.” *Lucifer* was an ordinary celestial expression, not originally the personal name of Satan. Later Christian exegesis combined Isaiah 14 with traditions about Satan’s fall, Ezekiel 28, and Luke 10:18. That reception produced the familiar fallen angel Lucifer. It is a late interpretive history of Isaiah, not evidence that ancient Ugaritians demonized Šaḥar. [Textual history of Isaiah 14:12](https://www.anselm-project.com/bible/crux/isaiah-14-12-lucifer-or-day-star)

#### Salem in Genesis and Psalms

Genesis 14:18 calls Melchizedek “king of Salem” and priest of El Elyon. Psalm 76:3 parallels Salem with Zion, making Salem a poetic name for Jerusalem.

These passages preserve *šlm* as a place-name, not an explicit god. Hebrews 7:2 later interprets “king of Salem” through Greek as “king of peace,” a theological wordplay rather than historical evidence that Šalim remained an openly worshipped Jerusalem deity.

#### Other uses of *šaḥar*

Biblical Hebrew frequently uses *šaḥar* as the ordinary noun “dawn,” for example in poetic expressions such as “eyelids of dawn.” Such passages may personify the phenomenon poetically but do not automatically attest worship of Šaḥar.

### Philo of Byblos, Eusebius, Lucian, Herodotus, and Josephus

**Absence of evidence.**

No secure appearance of Shahar and Shalim as this Ugaritic pair occurs in:

- Philo of Byblos’ purported Phoenician history as preserved by Eusebius.
- Lucian’s *De Dea Syria*.
- Herodotus’ accounts of Phoenician religion.
- Josephus’ descriptions of Phoenician or Jerusalem traditions.

Their silence matters. It prevents construction of an unbroken Phoenician, Punic, Greek, or Roman literary biography for the pair.

### Phoenician and Punic inscriptions

No presently secure Phoenician or Punic dedication names the pair *Šḥr w Šlm*. Similar forms in personal names can be read either theophorically or as ordinary wishes involving dawn, welfare, or completeness. KAI 282’s Shalman must not be silently converted into Šalim.

The Carthaginian tophet inscriptions overwhelmingly concern Baal Hammon and Tanit, not Shahar or Shalim. The debated question of child sacrifice at Carthage therefore supplies no evidence for sacrifice to this pair. The current comprehensive project for Phoenician and Punic epigraphy is the *Corpus Inscriptionum Phoenicarum necnon Poenicarum*. [CIP project](https://ilc.csic.es/en/webpage/corpus-inscriptorum-phoenicarum-necnon-poenicarum-cip)

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**Securely attested or strongly lexical:**

- Šaḥar: dawn/daybreak.
- Šalim: the complementary end of the day, probably dusk or sunset.
- Both: heavenly location in KTU 1.100.
- Both: recipients or participants in ritual invocation.
- Šalim: recipient of animal offerings in Ugaritic cult lists.

**Probable but reconstructed:**

- Morning and evening manifestations of Venus.
- Regulation or marking of the temporal boundaries of the day.
- Astral or liminal protection.
- Power against venom.

The incantations demonstrate that the pair could be appealed to against poison, but they do not make them specialized medical gods.

### Powers and characteristic actions

The sources give few individualized actions:

- They are born after El’s union with two women.
- Their birth is announced to El.
- A ritual action or offering is ordered in connection with Šapšu and the stars.
- They dwell in heaven.
- They receive an appeal concerning serpent venom.
- Šalim receives sacrificial animals.

Most elaborate descriptions of Shahar “opening the gates of day” or Shalim “closing the western gate” are modern poetic extrapolations.

### Venus

The planet Venus appears as both morning and evening star, making the pairing attractive. Yet the evidence permits several models:

1. The gods are Dawn and Dusk as temporal phenomena.
2. They personify the morning and evening stars.
3. They combine twilight and Venusian meanings.
4. They are divine boundaries whose exact astronomical referents varied.

Since ʿAṯtar independently occupies morning-star territory, an exclusive Venus equation is unlikely.

### Form and gender

The grammatical and contextual evidence favors masculine gods. No ancient text supplies a detailed body description. The two women of KTU 1.23 are female; Shahar and Shalim are not.

### Iconography

**Absence of evidence:** No securely identified statue, relief, seal, stele, coin, or cult image can be assigned to either god. There is no reliable iconographic rule such as “Shahar carries a rising disk” or “Shalim holds an inverted torch.”

Representations of stars, solar disks, paired youths, or Venus elsewhere in the ancient Near East cannot be identified as Shahar and Shalim without inscriptions or a highly specific context.

### Symbols, animals, colors, numbers, plants, days

No source securely assigns them:

- A sacred animal.
- A sacred plant.
- A distinctive color.
- A weekday.
- A canonical number.
- A weapon.
- A crown or garment.
- A temple emblem.

The number seven is prominent in KTU 1.23’s performance instructions and wilderness episode, but it belongs to the ritual’s structure and should not be treated as the pair’s personal sacred number.

## 4. Genealogy and divine relations

### Parentage

#### El

**Securely attested early:** El is the father. In KTU 1.23 he encounters two women, displays or restores his sexual potency, has intercourse with them, and receives word that Šaḥar and Šalim have been born.

#### The mothers

The narrative refers to two women whose precise identity is disputed.

- The ritual portion names **Athirat and Rahmay** in association with the field or countryside.
- A later passage says the Goodly Gods suck the breasts of Athirat.
- Many reconstructions therefore identify the two women as Athirat and Rahmay.
- Other readings regard the narrative women as unnamed human or divine females and deny that the surviving text explicitly names both as the mothers of Šaḥar and Šalim.

Accordingly:

- **El as father:** secure.
- **Athirat as mother:** probable but not textually unambiguous in the birth sentence.
- **Rahmay as second mother:** possible reconstruction.
- **One twin born to each woman:** plausible from the dual structure but not securely recoverable.
- **Shahar and Shalim as literal biological twins:** functional or poetic shorthand; the text speaks of a paired birth but has two women and leaves the exact obstetric arrangement unclear.

### Sibling relationship

The fixed pairing and common birth make them sibling gods. “Twins” is reasonable but slightly more specific than the intact wording permits.

### The Gracious Gods

As noted above, their relationship to the *ʾilm nʿmm* is disputed. If distinct, the Goodly Gods are later offspring of El and the same women and thus siblings or half-siblings of Shahar and Shalim. If identical, “Gracious Gods” is an epithet of the pair. The narrative sequence favors distinction.

### Wider divine family

El’s fatherhood places them among the divine children or household of El. Statements that they are therefore siblings of Baal, Anat, Yamm, Mot, ʿAṯtar, or every Ugaritic deity are learned pantheon systematizations unless a specific text gives the relationship.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The birth of Dawn and Dusk: KTU 1.23

#### Ritual opening

The tablet begins by calling the *ʾilm nʿmm*. It invites divine or ritual participants to eat bread and drink wine and blesses the king, queen, and other participants. Rubrics prescribe sevenfold recitation and responses. Agricultural imagery includes a sterile or widowed staff, a vine that is pruned or felled, dairy preparations, hunting, and cooking.

Because damaged words have been read in radically different ways, reconstructions involving castration, circumcision, ritual drama, seasonal death, or royal marriage must remain hypotheses.

#### El and the two women

The mythic narrative begins with El near the sea or shore. He sees two women. The text dwells on his sexual organ, arousal, and the women’s response. A recurring interpretive problem is whether the women are to be addressed as El’s wives or as his daughters. The women answer or act in a way that leads to union.

El hunts or shoots a bird and roasts it over a fire. The culinary act is intertwined with courtship and sexual potency. The narrative then describes kissing, embracing, conception, and a gestational count.

#### Birth of Shahar and Shalim

The women crouch or travail and give birth to Šaḥar and Šalim. A messenger announces to El that his two wives have given birth and identifies the children as Dawn and Dusk.

An instruction then associates the birth with Šapšu and the stars, commonly translated along the lines of presenting an offering to “Great Lady Šapšu” and the stars. This locates the newborn gods in an astral family or setting without proving that each is a form of Venus.

#### Second conception and birth

El again kisses and embraces the women. A second conception follows. The children born in this second sequence are described as the *ʾilm nʿmm*. Their lips or mouths stretch toward heaven and earth, and they devour birds and fish or everything available.

This is the principal reason to distinguish them from Shahar and Shalim: the pair’s birth has already occurred and been announced before the next conception.

#### Wilderness and cultivated land

The ravenous gods are associated with the steppe or wilderness for seven years, perhaps eight, before they approach a guardian of cultivated land and demand food and wine. The close returns to the feast language of the opening ritual.

#### Function

Proposals include:

- A seasonal fertility rite.
- A New Year or seven-year agricultural-cycle drama.
- A sacred-marriage liturgy.
- A royal wedding or fertility ceremony.
- A rite integrating young royal princes into elite society.
- A myth explaining the transition between wilderness and cultivated land.
- A ritualized opposition and integration of sterility/fertility, hunger/feast, outsider/insider, and chaos/order.

Only the existence of a poetic text with ritual directions is secure. No surviving colophon names the festival or explains the performance.

### The anti-venom cycle

KTU 1.100 and 1.107 preserve a different type of tradition. Rather than telling a story about the pair, they place Shahar and Shalim among divine powers whose names and locations are ritually mobilized against poison.

The role may depend on:

- Their heavenly position.
- Their ability to mark transition from danger to safety.
- Their membership in a comprehensive divine catalogue.
- A lost myth about serpents or healing.

The texts do not allow a choice among these explanations.

### Isaiah’s fallen “son of Dawn”

Isaiah 14 is not a continuation of KTU 1.23. It is a Hebrew royal taunt that uses a celestial fall story.

#### Traditional reconstruction

Hêlēl is Venus as morning star. He attempts to rise above the stars and take the divine throne on the mountain of assembly but falls as daylight overtakes the morning star. Šaḥar is his personified father.

#### ʿAṯtar theory

The closest Ugaritic narrative often proposed is ʿAṯtar’s attempt to sit on Baal’s throne after Baal’s disappearance. He proves too small for it and descends to rule on earth. Similarities include an astral god, a divine throne, ascent, and descent. Differences include the absence of Šaḥar, punishment, underworld humiliation, and rebellion in the Ugaritic episode.

#### Lunar theory

Some scholars emended or associated *Hêlēl* with *Hilāl*, the crescent moon. This has not become the dominant reading and depends on philological assumptions not demanded by the Masoretic consonants.

#### Chthonic-sun theory

Gallagher argues that Hêlēl may evoke the sun’s descent into the underworld rather than Venus. This explains parts of Isaiah’s imagery but remains a modern proposal rather than consensus. [Gallagher](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3A76163d31-ede1-4f6a-b3ff-4e63b499d448)

#### Lucifer

The sequence was:

1. Hebrew *hêlēl ben-šaḥar*.
2. Greek *heōsphoros*, morning star.
3. Latin *lucifer*, light-bearer.
4. Christian identification with Satan’s primordial fall.
5. Medieval and early-modern demonological elaboration.
6. Miltonic and modern literary Lucifer.

None of stages 3–6 transmits an independent cult or myth of the Ugaritic god Shahar. “Shahar is Lucifer” is therefore misleading. Isaiah’s *Šaḥar* is Dawn; Lucifer is a Latin translation of Dawn’s “shining” son.

---

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Ugarit

The best evidence is textual and comes from the city’s royal and priestly archive.

#### Cultic status

- Shahar and Shalim occur as recognized divine names in litanies and incantations.
- Šalim receives animal offerings in sacrificial lists.
- Their myths or names could be integrated into rituals concerning feasting, kingship, agriculture, fertility, and poison.
- The findspot of KTU 1.23 in the House of the High Priest places the tablet in an elite scribal-priestly environment.

#### Offerings

Šalim is assigned cattle and/or rams in Ugaritic lists, depending on the reading of individual tablets. KTU 1.23 includes bread, wine, dairy, herbs, meat or bird preparation, and an offering connected with Šapšu and the stars. It is uncertain which foods were literally offered to Shahar and Shalim.

#### Ritual personnel

KTU 1.23 mentions figures variously translated as singers, princes, entrants, responders, or guild members. The term *bn šrm* has received at least four interpretations: princes, sons of Šarruma, shining ones, or singers. Tsumura favored “singers.” The translation affects whether the ceremony appears primarily royal or liturgical. [Tsumura’s discussion](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/jorient1962/17/2/17_2_49/_article/-char/en)

#### Temple and priesthood

No temple specifically dedicated to Shahar or Shalim has been identified. No priest bears an unambiguous title “priest of Shahar” or “priest of Shalim.” No independent festival of Dawn and Dusk is securely named.

#### Sacred marriage and temple prostitution

KTU 1.23 has often been called a sacred-marriage liturgy because it combines divine intercourse and ritual rubrics. That does not demonstrate intercourse between human performers.

There is no evidence that Shahar and Shalim had a cult of temple prostitution. The Ugaritic *qdšm* were cultic personnel but evidence indicates activities such as sacrifice, divination, and singing; their designation does not by itself mean “sacred prostitutes.” [Study of the Ugaritic *qdšm*](https://www.ub.edu/ipoa/wp-content/uploads/2021/09/19992000AuOr1Dijkstra.pdf)

### Jerusalem

If Jerusalem means “foundation of Šalim,” a local cult is plausible. But no Bronze Age Jerusalem inscription says “Šalim is the city’s god,” and no identified temple or image belongs to him. The place-name is evidence for an old name; the cultic conclusions are reconstructed.

Genesis 14’s Salem and Psalm 76’s equation of Salem with Zion may preserve or reactivate the old name while placing the city wholly under El Elyon or Israel’s God. This is literary reuse, not proof of continuous Šalim worship.

### Phoenician and Punic world

No dedicated sanctuary, priesthood, festival, or sacrifice of Shahar and Shalim is securely attested in Phoenician or Punic territory. The classification “Canaanite, Phoenician, and Punic” is culturally broad; the direct dossier is overwhelmingly Ugaritic.

### Human sacrifice and the Carthage tophet

Human or child sacrifice is a serious controversy in Punic studies, but it cannot be attached to Shahar or Shalim. No tophet dedication invokes either god, and no classical testimony assigns such sacrifice to them. Associating the pair with tophet practice merely because they are West Semitic gods would be evidentially indefensible.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Possible Middle Bronze Age prehistory: approximately 2000–1550 BCE

Egyptian execration texts may preserve an early form of the name Jerusalem. If the toponym contains Šalim, it could carry the god’s name centuries before the Ugaritic tablets.

**Status:** Possible indirect attestation only. A place-name can survive after its original meaning is forgotten, and the divine etymology is not certain.

### Amarna age: fourteenth century BCE

The letters of Abdi-Heba name *Urusalim*. They document the city but say nothing explicit about Šalim’s worship.

**Status:** Toponymic evidence; cult inferred.

### Late Bronze Age Ugarit: thirteenth to early twelfth century BCE

This is the only period with a substantial direct record:

- KTU 1.23 tells the pair’s birth.
- KTU 1.100 and 1.107 invoke them against venom.
- KTU 1.123 includes them in a divine litany.
- Offering lists name Šalim separately.

This is the peak of attestation, not necessarily the peak of actual worship.

### Destruction of Ugarit: approximately 1185 BCE

Ugarit’s destruction ended the scribal and cultic system that produced the surviving tablets. There is no documented institutional continuity of a Shahar-and-Shalim cult from Ugarit into the Iron Age.

### Iron Age Israel and Judah

The common nouns “dawn,” “completeness,” “peace,” and “well-being” remained active. Jerusalem’s name and poetic Salem survived. Isaiah 14 personified Dawn as father of a fallen shining figure.

These are transformations in language and mythic imagery. They do not prove that a temple cult of either god continued in Israel or Judah.

### Phoenician and Punic Iron Age

The pair disappears from securely identified cult inscriptions. A Sidonian dedication to Shalman and ambiguous theophoric names have sometimes been drawn into Šalim’s history, but identity cannot be established.

### Hellenistic and Roman periods

Later Syrian religions included paired astral deities such as Azizos and Monimos. Scholars have compared them to Shahar and Shalim, but no ancient author supplies the genealogical link.

Philo of Byblos, Lucian, Herodotus, and Josephus do not preserve the pair. Their absence prevents a continuous Phoenician classical tradition from being reconstructed.

### Jewish and Christian reception

Jewish scripture preserved Šaḥar as Dawn in Isaiah 14 and Salem as a Jerusalem name. Christian translation and exegesis eventually transformed Isaiah’s morning star into Lucifer, the pre-fall Satan.

Šaḥar himself did not become Satan in the text. The process attached demonological meaning to Hêlēl/Lucifer, the “son of Dawn,” while Dawn remained part of the poetic genealogy.

### Islamic period

Arabic *saḥar* denotes the time immediately before dawn, and *salām* belongs to the cognate Š-L-M root. These lexical continuities are not evidence for survival of the Ugaritic gods in Islamic folklore or worship.

No securely documented Islamic demon, jinn, saint, or astral spirit is a continuation of Shahar or Shalim.

### Modern period

The decipherment of Ugaritic after 1929 restored the pair to historical visibility. Their modern “revival” is primarily scholarly, literary, internet-based, and, on a small scale, reconstructionist religious practice.

There is no evidence of an uninterrupted community worshipping them from antiquity to the present.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and reference literature

Modern reference works commonly describe them as twins, sons of El, gods of dawn and dusk, and manifestations of Venus. Each statement needs qualification:

- “Sons of El”: secure.
- “Twins”: probable summary of their paired birth.
- “Sons of El and Asherah”: plausible but the mothers are not unambiguously named in the birth sentence.
- “The Gracious Gods”: contested.
- “Venus as morning and evening star”: plausible reconstruction, not explicit equation.
- “Shalim was Jerusalem’s patron”: possible inference from the city-name, not direct evidence.
- “Shahar was Lucifer”: erroneous compression of a long reception history.

### Luciferian and occult interpretation

Modern occult writers sometimes identify:

- Shahar with Lucifer.
- Shahar and Shalim as light and darkness.
- The pair as Lucifer and Lucifuge.
- Hêlēl and an invented “Hêlēl ben Šalim” as celestial twins.
- Shalim as an underworld, chaos, or nihilistic god.

These are **modern inventions or syncretisms**. No ancient source names a “Hêlēl ben Šalim,” equates Shalim with Lucifuge, or makes the brothers rulers of moral light and darkness.

The historical connection is narrower: Isaiah calls a fallen shining figure “son of Dawn”; the Vulgate translates that figure as *lucifer*. Shalim is not present in Isaiah 14.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist worship

Canaanite reconstructionism exists as a small modern religious movement, including a documented miniature community in Israel. Its practitioners use Ugaritic texts to compose prayers and rituals for ancient Levantine gods. [Study of Israeli Canaanite reconstructionism](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/the-return-of-baal-to-the-holy-land-canaanite-reconstructionism-a/)

Individual worship of Shahar and Shalim is visible online, but:

- Its scale is not reliably measurable.
- No centralized priesthood or standardized liturgy exists.
- Modern offerings, invocations, gender assignments, sigils, correspondences, and ethical teachings are reconstructions or personal revelations.
- Such practice is a modern religious reception, not continuous ancient tradition.

### National and political appropriation

The proposed derivation of Jerusalem from Šalim is sometimes deployed polemically to argue that the city was “originally pagan,” “originally Canaanite,” or named for a specific national ancestor. The Bronze Age name unquestionably predates Israelite monarchy, but linguistic etymology does not by itself settle modern ethnic, religious, or political claims.

No substantial extremist cult centered on Shahar and Shalim is documented.

### Internet mythology

Recent websites and generated mythology frequently add:

- Temple complexes.
- Individual sacred colors and animals.
- Daily gate-opening rituals.
- Elaborate conversations between the twins.
- A rivalry between dawn and dusk.
- Shalim as a god of “order and stability.”
- Shahar as a blade-bearing conqueror of darkness.
- A direct identity between the twins and the hungry Gracious Gods.

Unless tied to a cited ancient line, these are modern inventions. Some modern presentations acknowledge their evocative additions; others present them as translated mythology.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Discovery and publication

The excavation of Ras Shamra beginning in 1929 transformed the study of Canaanite religion. RS 2.002 was found in 1930 and published by Virolleaud in 1933. Before Ugaritic was deciphered, Šalim’s role in Jerusalem’s name and Šaḥar’s role in Isaiah were necessarily speculative.

### Editions

The principal textual tools include:

- Virolleaud’s editio princeps of RS 2.002.
- CTA and successive editions of KTU/CAT.
- Dietrich, Loretz, and Sanmartín, *Die keilalphabetischen Texte aus Ugarit*.
- Dennis Pardee, *Ritual and Cult at Ugarit*.
- Simon B. Parker, ed., *Ugaritic Narrative Poetry*.
- Nicolas Wyatt, *Religious Texts from Ugarit*.
- Mark S. Smith, *The Rituals and Myths of the Feast of the Goodly Gods*.

Pardee’s collection emphasizes that Ugaritic ritual tablets are direct documentary evidence for Late Bronze Age cult, while distinguishing prescriptive prose rituals from poetic mythic compositions. [Pardee](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/isbn/9781628376807/html?lang=en)

### Main disputes

#### Are Shahar and Shalim the Gracious Gods?

The most important textual dispute. The damaged text’s two conceptions and births favor distinction, while repeated thematic echoes encouraged identification.

#### Who are the women?

Athirat and Rahmay are named in the ritual section, but the narrative women remain less explicit. Calling both “goddesses” or “wives of El” may be correct, but it is partly reconstruction.

#### Is KTU 1.23 a sacred-marriage ritual?

The text certainly narrates divine sex and contains performance instructions. It does not establish enacted ritual intercourse. “Sacred marriage” can describe the myth without proving a human sexual rite.

#### Is the pair Venus?

Their complementarity and heavenly location make the equation attractive. The independent role of ʿAṯtar and the absence of an explicit astronomical equation require caution.

#### Does Jerusalem mean “foundation of Šalim”?

This is widely accepted as a strong possibility, based on *yrw/uru* plus *šlm*. Alternatives include “foundation of peace/well-being” or another analysis of the first element. Even accepting the divine etymology does not prove that Šalim remained Jerusalem’s chief god throughout the Bronze Age.

#### Is Salem in Genesis 14 Jerusalem?

Psalm 76 explicitly associates Salem with Zion, and later tradition identifies Melchizedek’s Salem as Jerusalem. Whether Genesis 14 originally intended the same site remains debated.

#### Is Hêlēl ʿAṯtar, Venus, the moon, or the sun?

No complete West Semitic version of Isaiah’s story survives. The Venus/ʿAṯtar reconstruction remains influential but is not a recovered Ugaritic myth.

#### Do personal names attest worship?

Names containing *šlm* may mean “Šalim is X,” but they may instead use the ordinary noun “well-being.” Jeffrey Tigay and other onomastic scholars warn against turning every ambiguous root into a divine name.

### Overextended theories

The following remain unproved or discredited as statements of fact:

- Shahar and Shalim were the two supreme gods of Canaan.
- They ruled jointly over the whole day.
- Their cult was the source of all divine-twin traditions.
- Jerusalem had a securely excavated Temple of Shalim.
- Melchizedek was historically Šalim’s priest.
- Solomon and Absalom were certainly named after Šalim.
- The pair were worshipped at Carthage’s tophet.
- Shahar was the original Satan.
- Shalim was Lucifuge or the biblical Abyss.
- KTU 1.23 records temple prostitution.
- Every paired Syrian astral god is a direct survival of them.

### State of the evidence

The corpus is exceptionally small:

- One substantial mythic-ritual composition.
- Two anti-venom invocations.
- One divine litany.
- Several disputed or relatively terse offering-list entries.
- Possible indirect survival in a place-name and biblical poetic imagery.

This scarcity explains why a few lines bear an unusually heavy interpretive load.
