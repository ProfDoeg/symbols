# Anat: Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess. In the Ugaritic tradition, Anat is a young martial goddess, hunter, divine enforcer, and exceptionally forceful ally of Baal; Egyptian sources receive her as an armed Asiatic goddess and royal protector. Later Phoenician, Cypriot, Aramaic, and Egyptian evidence preserves her name or compounds formed from it, but not one continuous, uniform theology.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest reasonably secure evidence is the goddess **Ḫanat/Hanat** in Old Babylonian records from the middle Euphrates and Mari, principally cultic and theophoric evidence of the eighteenth century BCE. Identifying Ḫanat with later Ugaritic ʿAnat is the majority reconstruction, not an explicit statement by an ancient text. The earliest extensive description of Anat comes much later, in the thirteenth-century BCE alphabetic cuneiform tablets from Ugarit. Mari’s records establish a cult and name but do not yet establish the violent warrior personality known from Ugaritic poetry. [Jeffery B. Lloyd’s source survey](https://era.ed.ac.uk/items/38bcb2b2-3942-4cc5-bc0b-89912732dfe7)

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Names and scripts

- **Ugaritic:** 𐎓𐎐𐎚, conventionally transliterated **ʿnt**, vocalized by modern convention as **ʿAnatu/ʿAnat**. Ugaritic writing ordinarily omits vowels, so “Anat” is a scholarly vocalization informed by later spellings.
- **Phoenician and Hebrew:** 𐤏𐤍𐤕 / עֲנָת, **ʿnt**, conventionally **Anat/Anath**.
- **Akkadian syllabic:** forms including **A-na-ti** and, for the middle-Euphrates goddess, **Ḫa-na-at**.
- **Egyptian:** usually transliterated **ꜥntjt**, **ʿAntit/Antit**, with variable hieroglyphic spelling.
- **Greek:** Ἀνάθ, **Anath**, where transliterated; in interpretive translations she could instead be rendered **Athena**.
- **Aramaic compounds:** **ʿnt-Btyl**, “Anat-of-Bethel” or “Anat-Bethel,” and the debated **ʿntyhw**, “Anat-of-Yahu/Anat-Yahu.”

The initial consonant is the Semitic pharyngeal **ʿayin**, not a vowel. “Anath” reflects older conventions for representing the final *t*; it is not a different deity.

### Etymology

**Modern scholarly reconstruction; unresolved.** Proposed derivations have connected ʿnt with:

- a “spring” or “fountain”;
- a “furrow”;
- strength or violence;
- sexual activity;
- an ethnonym or a geographic name;
- the middle-Euphrates town and goddess Ḫanat.

None commands consensus. Several sexual or fertility etymologies arose from an older portrayal of Anat as a “goddess of unrestrained love”; that characterization is now widely criticized because Ugaritic texts do not make fertility or erotic love her defining domain. A name’s resemblance to a common noun also does not prove that ancient worshippers understood the deity through that noun. Lloyd concludes that the evidence does not permit a firm original etymology. Wordplay on *ʿnt* (“Anat,” “spring,” “furrow”) may nevertheless have been available to later poets. [Lloyd](https://era.ed.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/1842/30395/LloydJB_1994redux.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1); [Noegel on Ugaritic wordplay](https://faculty.washington.edu/snoegel/PDFs/articles/noegel-antanaclasis-uf-53-2022.pdf)

### Principal Ugaritic epithets

- **btlt ʿnt**, “Maiden Anat” or traditionally “Virgin Anat.” **Securely attested Ugaritic.** *Btlt* identifies youthful status; it need not mean physiological virginity in the later Christian sense. It remains compatible with texts that may imply sexuality, though those passages are disputed.
- **ybmt lʾimm**, one of her most frequent and most disputed titles. Renderings include “sister-in-law of the peoples,” “progenitress of the peoples,” or formulations involving the clan or nation. **Secure words, uncertain translation.**
- **ʿnt ḫbly**, commonly “Anat the Destroyer,” especially in cultic lists. **Securely attested title; exact nuance debated.**
- **ʿnt gṯr(t)**, “powerful Anat” or “Anat the mighty.” **Attested but uncommon.**
- Titles translated “mistress of kingship” and “mistress of dominion” occur in damaged or difficult contexts and should not be converted into universal cult offices without qualification. [Survey of epithets](https://open.bu.edu/bitstream/2144/41974/7/Kim_bu_0017E_15989.pdf)

Egyptian texts add royal-martial descriptions: she gives victory, protects the king, is “mistress of heaven,” may be called a daughter of Re or Ptah, and in Ramesside royal ideology can function as the king’s divine mother or protector. These are **regional Egyptian adaptations**, not proof that Ugarit used the same genealogy.

### Classifications and equations

- **Ugarit:** a goddess within El’s divine household, closely allied to Baal.
- **Egypt:** a foreign-origin goddess fully admitted into Egyptian cult, usually armed and royal.
- **Ptolemaic Cyprus:** explicitly translated as **Athena** in the Phoenician-Greek bilingual KAI 42. This is a datable interpretatio, not evidence that Anat and Athena had always been the same being. [OGIS 17/KAI 42](https://www.attalus.org/docs/ogis/s17.html)
- **Set’s bride or intended wife:** in the Egyptian *Contendings of Horus and Seth*, Neith proposes Anat and Astarte, daughters of Re, as wives for Seth. This is a Ramesside Egyptian narrative arrangement.
- **Tanit:** the once-common identification “Tanit = Anat” is not established. The names, historical distributions, cult titles, and direct inscriptions differ. Carthaginian tophet dedications name **Tinnit/Tanit and Baal Hammon**, not Anat.
- **Atargatis:** a derivation or fusion involving Anat and Astarte has been proposed, but Roman-period Atargatis cannot simply be relabeled Anat. Pseudo-Lucian’s *De Dea Syria* describes the goddess of Hierapolis as a composite interpreted through Hera, Athena, Aphrodite, Selene, Rhea, Artemis, Nemesis, and the Fates; it does not identify her securely as the Bronze Age Anat. [*De Dea Syria* 32](https://lucianofsamosata.info/wiki/doku.php?id=home%3Atexts_and_library%3Aessays%3Athe-syrian-goddess)

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Old Babylonian middle Euphrates

**Secure evidence for Ḫanat; identification with Anat is a scholarly reconstruction.** Eighteenth-century BCE Mari documentation includes Ḫanat in offering lists, legal oaths, administrative material, and personal names. One list gives her sheep; another associates palace women with an oil offering. A letter from an official at the town Ḫanat uses the welfare formula “Dagan and Ḫanat are well.” Her city lay in Suhum on the middle Euphrates.

The evidence establishes a local goddess with a functioning cult. It does **not** identify her as Baal’s sister, a blood-wading warrior, or Aqhat’s killer. Those characteristics first appear in later Ugaritic literature. [Mari dossier](https://era.ed.ac.uk/items/38bcb2b2-3942-4cc5-bc0b-89912732dfe7/full)

### Ugarit

The mythological tablets were excavated beginning in 1929 at Ras Shamra, ancient Ugarit. They are clay tablets written in alphabetic cuneiform Ugaritic, principally from the thirteenth century BCE, before Ugarit’s destruction around 1190 BCE. Several major poems were copied by the scribe **Ilimilku**, who names himself in colophons and worked under a King Niqmaddu; the exact king and dating within the thirteenth century remain debated.

#### Baal Cycle: KTU 1.1–1.6

**Securely attested late Bronze Age corpus.** Anat:

- appears within the conflict over divine kingship;
- restrains Baal, with Astarte, from attacking Yam’s messengers;
- stages or undergoes a spectacular battle in which she kills human warriors and wades through blood;
- washes and adorns herself afterward;
- receives Baal’s message announcing the defeat of his enemies and the establishment of peace;
- supports the construction of Baal’s palace;
- negotiates with Athirat and El;
- searches for and mourns Baal after his death;
- carries his body with Shapash;
- attacks, dismembers, burns, grinds, and scatters Mot;
- later acts as intermediary when El orders a search for the restored Baal.

The standard critical treatment is Mark S. Smith and Wayne T. Pitard, *The Ugaritic Baal Cycle*, supplemented by Nicolas Wyatt, *Religious Texts from Ugarit*, and Simon B. Parker, *Ugaritic Narrative Poetry*. The tablets are damaged; translations regularly differ. [Smith and Pitard, vol. II](https://www.ldsscriptureteachings.org/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/The-Ugaritic-Baal-Cycle-Volume-II.pdf); [online critical Ugaritic edition](https://eupt.uni-goettingen.de/edition.html)

#### Epic of Aqhat: KTU 1.17–1.19

**Securely attested Ugaritic narrative, incompletely preserved.** Kothar-wa-Hasis gives a magnificent bow to the righteous king Danel; Danel passes it to his son Aqhat. Anat desires the bow and offers Aqhat wealth and then life or immortality in exchange. Aqhat refuses, pointing to human mortality and insulting her competence as a woman with a bow. Anat appeals to El, threatens him, and secures freedom to punish Aqhat. She recruits Yatpan, who assumes avian form and kills Aqhat during a feast. The bow is broken or lost; Anat laments that she wanted the bow, not necessarily Aqhat’s death. Aqhat’s death produces drought or failed vegetation, and his sister Pughat undertakes revenge. The conclusion is lost.

The tale does not make the bow Anat’s regular ancient emblem; rather, her frustrated acquisition of it drives this particular narrative.

#### Kirta/Keret Epic: KTU 1.14–1.16

Anat is not a principal actor, but the poem compares royal beauty with Anat and Astarte, and a fragment may describe Anat and Athirat nursing the royal heir Yassib. The restoration of Anat’s name in the nursing passage is probable but partly reconstructed. [Critical edition note](https://eupt.uni-goettingen.de/edition.html?tido=m1_i0)

#### Other Ugaritic texts

- **KTU 1.10:** a fragment involving Baal, a cow/heifer, and Anat; the sequence and sexual implications are disputed.
- **KTU 1.13:** fragmentary divine material involving Anat; reconstruction uncertain.
- **KTU 1.22:** a *Rephaim* text in which Anat appears in damaged context.
- **KTU 1.82:** an incantation containing Anat among protective or hostile divine powers; interpretation uncertain.
- **KTU 1.92:** primarily a hunting episode of Astarte. Claims that it supplies a full Anat-Astarte narrative are insecure because the text is fragmentary.
- **KTU 1.114:** a divine drinking-banquet text; Anat and Astarte appear among deities involved in managing the intoxicated El, but line assignments and translations vary.
- **God and offering lists:** KTU 1.47, 1.118 and parallel syllabic material; Anat receives a stable place in the Ugaritic pantheon.
- **Ritual calendars and offering texts:** including KTU 1.39, 1.41, 1.43, 1.46, 1.109, 1.115, and 1.119, with varying certainty. These are crucial because they attest actual cultic offerings rather than literary characterization.
- **Hurrian ritual texts at Ugarit:** Anat appears without a secure Hurrian equivalent, alongside Hurrian and local deities.

### Amarna evidence

The fourteenth-century BCE Amarna archive is written mainly in diplomatic Akkadian. **A-na-ti** occurs in EA 170, but it is uncertain whether this is an independent divine name, a personal-name element, or another onomastic usage. The Amarna letters do not supply a narrative about Anat comparable to Ugarit. Claims that they independently document her complete warrior mythology overstate the material. [Amarna corpus introduction and ORACC link](https://humanities.tau.ac.il/hebrew/research/el-amarna-akkadian/el-amarna-introduction)

### Egyptian corpus

Evidence becomes substantial in the New Kingdom, particularly the Nineteenth and Twentieth Dynasties.

- Ramesside dedicatory stelae and statues name Anat.
- A securely identified Beth Shean stela, approximately 1300–1200 BCE, portrays a worshipper before Anat and names her in the inscription. [Israel Museum exhibition record](https://museum.imj.org.il/en/exhibitions/2016/pharaoh-in-canaan/page/?id=canaanite-deities)
- A statue from Tanis depicts Ramesses II with Anat’s hand protectively on him.
- Ramesses II calls himself Anat’s beloved and associates her with battle.
- His daughter **Bint-Anat/Bentanath**, “Daughter of Anat,” bore a transparent Semitic theophoric name and later became a Great Royal Wife. [Getty Valley of the Queens report](https://www.getty.edu/conservation/publications_resources/pdf_publications/pdf/qv_vol1_part2.pdf)
- His dog’s name, conventionally translated “Anat is vigorous/Anat-in-strength,” occurs in the Beit el-Wali battle relief; a royal weapon may also have borne Anat’s name.
- *The Contendings of Horus and Seth*, preserved on Papyrus Chester Beatty I from the reign of Ramesses V, names Anat and Astarte as prospective wives for Seth. The surviving manuscript is approximately 1150 BCE, though the composition may be older. [Bibliotheca Alexandrina record](https://inscriptionslibrary.bibalex.org/presentation/monument.aspx?ins_id=13&lang=en&mon_id=2873)
- Papyrus Chester Beatty VII contains a magical-healing narrative in which Anat’s sexual or marital relationship to Seth has been variously interpreted. The damaged language does not justify confidently reconstructing intercourse.

### Hebrew Bible

**Hostile or vestigial witness; not a narrative corpus about Anat.**

Certain or near-certain traces are:

- **Judges 3:31; 5:6:** Shamgar **ben Anat**, “son of Anat.” It may be an ordinary patronymic, a geographic designation, or—plausibly—a military title identifying a warrior or member of a troop under Anat’s patronage. The text itself does not explain it.
- **Joshua 19:38; Judges 1:33:** **Beth-Anat**, “House/Temple of Anat,” a place in Naphtali.
- **Beth-Anoth** and **Anathoth:** possibly derived from the goddess’s name, but not certain in every case.

Proposed hidden allusions in Exodus, Hosea, Jeremiah’s “Queen of Heaven,” and descriptions of Yahweh as a warrior depend on emendation, analogy, or comparative reconstruction. They are not direct attestations of Anat. John Day therefore describes Anat’s Iron Age Israelite presence as vestigial rather than central. [Day, discussion of names](https://fliphtml5.com/qhbxk/wohc/Yahweh_and_the_Gods_and_Goddesses_of_Canaan/134/); [Craigie on Shamgar](https://www.biblicalstudies.org.uk/pdf/jbl/1972_craigie.pdf)

### Neo-Assyrian and Aramaic Anat-Bethel evidence

The divine expression conventionally read **Anat-Bethel** has been connected with Esarhaddon’s treaty with Baal of Tyre and later Aramaic evidence. The Assyrian spelling and segmentation are disputed; some specialists reject the reading of the treaty name as Anat-Bethel altogether. It should not be presented as an uncontested Phoenician divine couple.

### Elephantine

The fifth-century BCE Aramaic papyri from Elephantine and Syene in southern Egypt preserve a Yahwistic military community with a sacrificial temple to YHW.

- The collection account TAD C3.15/Cowley 22 allocates money to **YHW**, **Ešem-Bethel**, and **Anat-Bethel**.
- A judicial oath or damaged divine name has been read **Anat-Yahu**.
- Other documents and names attest Bethel-related gods and Egyptian religious participation.

Three principal interpretations remain:

1. **Distinct-goddess reading:** Anat-Bethel and Anat-Yahu were female deities, possibly consorts of Bethel and Yahu.
2. **Hypostasis reading:** “Anat-of-X” denotes the active presence, sign, or manifestation of the male god rather than a separate spouse.
3. **Syncretic-community reading:** Aramaean and Judean soldiers shared a mixed religious environment; Anat-Bethel need not prove that every Elephantine Judean regarded Anat as Yahweh’s wife.

The common internet statement “Elephantine proves Yahweh had a wife named Anat” is therefore too categorical. The documents prove the divine names and allocations; they do not explain their theology. [Elephantine overview and the account’s translation](https://achaemenica.org/articles/the-elephantine-papyri); [discussion of Anat-Yahu’s Aramaean background](https://repository.up.ac.za/bitstreams/b6e92763-37c0-44e1-92b9-0c043538d058/download)

### Phoenician and Cypriot evidence

Anat is much less conspicuous in first-millennium Phoenician records than Astarte.

The strongest late inscription is **KAI 42**, a Phoenician-Greek bilingual dedication from Larnakas tis Lapithou in Cyprus, conventionally dated to the reign of Ptolemy I. Phoenician Anat is rendered Greek Athena. The equation is explicit, local, and Hellenistic. KAI 43 from the same area concerns a governor’s statue and cultic setting but should not be confused with the Anat-Athena bilingual. [KAI concordance](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kanaan%C3%A4ische_und_Aram%C3%A4ische_Inschriften)

A Maltese Punic inscription has been interpreted as naming **Anat-Astarte**, but reading, provenance, and the relationship expressed by the compound require caution. [University of Malta record](https://www.um.edu.mt/library/oar/handle/123456789/93083)

### Philo, Lucian, Herodotus, and Josephus

- **Philo of Byblos**, surviving through Eusebius’s fourth-century CE *Praeparatio Evangelica*, systematizes Phoenician gods through Greek names. His material is valuable but late, euhemerizing, and mediated; it does not furnish a secure continuous biography of Anat.
- **Pseudo-Lucian**, *De Dea Syria*, second century CE, describes Atargatis’s Hierapolis cult. It is relevant to the later Syrian-goddess environment, but calling its goddess Anat is a modern reconstruction.
- **Herodotus 1.105** describes the temple of “Heavenly Aphrodite” at Ascalon, generally associated with Astarte rather than Anat. It is not direct Anat evidence. [Herodotus 1.105](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Hdt.+1.105&lang=original)
- **Josephus** preserves information about Phoenician and Judean history but gives no independent, securely named Anat cult narrative.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**Secure Ugaritic profile:**

- warfare and violent victory;
- hunting;
- defense and advancement of Baal;
- punishment and vengeance;
- mediation and intimidation within the divine council;
- mourning and mortuary action;
- royal vitality by comparison and, in Egypt, royal protection.

Calling Anat primarily a fertility, sex, love, or motherhood goddess is **older scholarly systematization**, not the clearest result of the sources. Agricultural renewal follows the Baal–Mot conflict, but Anat does not herself personify grain or fertility in an uncomplicated way.

### The blood-wading scene

KTU 1.3 II presents Anat in combat. She places warriors’ heads on her back and hands at her belt, plunges knee-deep—and in parallel lines neck-deep—in warriors’ blood, then fights indoors among furniture before her heart fills with joy. She later washes off the blood, bathes, and adorns herself.

Interpretations include:

- literal mythic battle;
- ritualized mock combat;
- a literary display of ecstatic martial power;
- a royal-war metaphor;
- a scene framed by sacrifice or cult drama.

The text securely describes extreme violence. It does **not** establish that Anat’s historical worshippers performed the same acts or sacrificed humans to her.

### Weapons and appearance

In Ugaritic literature Anat uses or is associated with the sword or blade, bow, hunting equipment, and the implements with which she destroys Mot. Egyptian images can show her with:

- spear;
- shield;
- battle axe;
- Egyptian crown, often described as atef-like;
- long fitted Egyptian dress;
- occasionally wings in disputed identifications.

The bow is central in *Aqhat*, but the myth specifically makes it an object Anat lacks and covets.

### Secure and insecure images

The only safe identifications are images accompanied by inscriptions or very specific context. Egyptian-style named images include the Beth Shean stela and the Ramesses II–Anat statue from Tanis. Numerous Levantine plaques of nude, winged, armed, lion-standing, or “mistress of animals” goddesses have been called Anat, Astarte, Qudshu, Asherah, or all four. Without a label, such identifications are usually **modern iconographic hypotheses**. Egyptian representation also does not preserve a pristine Canaanite costume: foreign gods were translated into Egyptian visual grammar. [Lloyd’s iconographic assessment](https://era.ed.ac.uk/server/api/core/bitstreams/13b36e3f-414e-41f4-8106-02b088481c8c/content)

### Animals, celestial bodies, colors, numbers, and days

- **Birds of prey:** relevant to the *Aqhat* killing because Yatpan assumes avian form and Aqhat’s remains become entangled with birds; not a securely universal sacred-animal cult.
- **Cattle:** appear in fragmentary Baal-Anat material, but a stable “cow goddess” identity is not established.
- **Lion:** martial goddesses can stand on lions in Levantine-Egyptian art, but unlabeled examples cannot automatically be assigned to Anat.
- **Celestial body:** no secure Ugaritic evidence assigns Anat a planet. Equating her with Venus because Astarte/Ishtar is Venus is an unwarranted transfer.
- **Colors, numbers, weekdays:** no ancient source provides a stable set. Modern lists assigning red, Tuesday, Mars, or particular crystals are modern occult correspondences.

## 4. Genealogy and divine relations

### Genealogical variants

Ugaritic texts place Anat in El’s pantheon and call Baal her **brother**. Whether that is literal genealogy, a kinship title among divine allies, or both is debated. Baal is elsewhere “son of Dagan,” while Anat is not securely called Dagan’s daughter. A simple chart making El and Athirat the biological parents of both is therefore a modern harmonization.

Egyptian texts may call Anat:

- daughter of Re;
- daughter of Ptah;
- daughter or protected member of an Egyptian divine family;
- intended wife or companion of Seth.

These are regional Egyptian genealogies.

Anat has no securely attested Ugaritic child. She is not securely Baal’s wife in surviving Ugaritic texts. Egyptian and later comparative evidence has encouraged that reconstruction, but Ugaritic poetry overwhelmingly calls her his sister and ally.

### Every documented divine interaction

The following list separates textual interactions from inferred pantheon membership.

- **Baal — alliance and kinship:** Anat is called his sister, receives his messages, advances his palace claim, mourns and buries him, avenges him upon Mot, and participates in his restoration. **Source:** KTU 1.3–1.6, thirteenth century BCE. **Tier:** securely attested early material.
- **Baal — possible sexual encounter:** fragmentary bovine and genital language has been read erotically, but restoration and identification are disputed. **Source:** KTU 1.10 and difficult passages in the Baal corpus. **Tier:** disputed early material.
- **El — confrontation and negotiation:** Anat approaches or threatens El in pursuit of Baal’s interests and later appeals to him before punishing Aqhat. El uses Anat as an intermediary to Shapash. **Source:** KTU 1.3–1.6; 1.17. **Tier:** securely attested, with disputed wording.
- **Athirat — persuasion and joint mission:** Anat and Baal bring gifts or a request to Athirat; Anat accompanies or follows her as Athirat petitions El for Baal’s palace. **Source:** KTU 1.4. **Tier:** securely attested early material.
- **Athirat — possible joint nursing:** Anat and Athirat may nurse Yassib. **Source:** KTU 1.15 II, damaged restoration. **Tier:** disputed early material.
- **Astarte/ʿAthtart — restraining Baal:** Anat grasps one of Baal’s hands and Astarte the other to stop him attacking Yam’s messengers. **Source:** KTU 1.2 I. **Tier:** securely attested early material.
- **Astarte — paired cultic identity:** a ritual list contains an Anat-and-Astarte binomial or paired entry. **Source:** Ugaritic ritual corpus. **Tier:** securely attested name, theological meaning uncertain.
- **Astarte — joint Egyptian marriage assignment:** Neith gives or promises Anat and Astarte to Seth. **Source:** *Contendings of Horus and Seth*, Papyrus Chester Beatty I, surviving manuscript c. 1150 BCE. **Tier:** regional Egyptian variant.
- **Shapash — recovery and burial of Baal:** Shapash finds or assists Anat with Baal’s body and helps transport it to Mount Saphon. **Source:** KTU 1.6 I. **Tier:** securely attested early material.
- **Shapash — commissioned search:** El tells Anat to order Shapash to search for Baal. **Source:** KTU 1.6 III. **Tier:** securely attested early material.
- **Mot — vengeance:** Anat seizes Mot, cuts him with a blade, winnows, burns, grinds, and scatters him. **Source:** KTU 1.6 II. **Tier:** securely attested early material.
- **Yam/Nahar — claimed prior defeat:** Anat recites enemies she has defeated, including Sea/River in common reconstructions; in the main combat narrative Baal defeats Yam. **Source:** KTU 1.3 III. **Tier:** securely attested boast, narrative relationship uncertain.
- **Tunnan — claimed defeat:** Anat boasts of crushing the twisting sea monster Tunnan. **Source:** KTU 1.3 III. **Tier:** securely attested early mythic claim.
- **Lotan — claimed defeat:** she claims victory over the fleeing or twisting serpent, usually identified as Lotan. **Source:** KTU 1.3 III. **Tier:** securely attested but textually difficult.
- **Arsh/Arish — claimed defeat:** an enemy often rendered Arsh, “Beloved of El,” appears in Anat’s victory list. **Source:** KTU 1.3 III. **Tier:** securely attested name, identity uncertain.
- **Atik — claimed defeat:** another creature or divine opponent appears in the boast. **Source:** KTU 1.3 III. **Tier:** securely attested but poorly understood.
- **Ishat — claimed defeat:** a female destructive being, conventionally “Fire,” appears among defeated enemies. **Source:** KTU 1.3 III. **Tier:** securely attested but identity uncertain.
- **Zabib — claimed defeat:** another female enemy is named in the victory catalogue. **Source:** KTU 1.3 III. **Tier:** securely attested but identity uncertain.
- **Kothar-wa-Hasis — indirect relation through the bow:** Kothar makes the bow Anat later demands from Aqhat; no preserved passage has Anat commissioning it. **Source:** KTU 1.17. **Tier:** securely attested shared episode; direct meeting absent.
- **Yarikh — banquet association:** Anat and Yarikh occur within fragmentary divine banquet material; a strong one-to-one encounter is not securely reconstructable. **Source:** KTU 1.114. **Tier:** fragmentary regional text.
- **Seth — assigned marriage/consort relation:** Neith proposes Anat and Astarte as Seth’s wives; other Egyptian magical material links Anat with Seth’s injury and healing. **Source:** Chester Beatty papyri, Ramesside period. **Tier:** regional Egyptian variant.
- **Neith — marriage assignment:** Neith offers Anat and Astarte to Seth as part of the judgment between Horus and Seth. **Source:** *Contendings*. **Tier:** regional Egyptian variant.
- **Re — daughter/father relationship:** the Egyptian *Contendings* calls Anat and Astarte daughters of Re; Egyptian theology also uses solar filiations. **Source:** Ramesside Egyptian texts. **Tier:** regional Egyptian systematization.
- **Ptah — father relationship:** some Egyptian inscriptions call Anat daughter of Ptah. **Source:** New Kingdom Egyptian epigraphy. **Tier:** regional Egyptian systematization.
- **Mut — joint appearance:** Anat appears with Mut in Egyptian iconographic or dedicatory contexts. **Source:** Ramesside Egyptian monuments. **Tier:** regional cultic juxtaposition; not necessarily a narrative interaction.
- **Athena — explicit equation:** Phoenician Anat is translated as Greek Athena. **Source:** KAI 42/OGIS 17, Cyprus, Ptolemaic period. **Tier:** syncretistic identification.
- **Bethel — compound divine name:** Anat-Bethel appears in Aramaic evidence. Whether this means consort, daughter, hypostasis, or “Anat of Bethel” is unresolved. **Source:** Neo-Assyrian/Aramaic texts and Elephantine account. **Tier:** disputed first-millennium syncretism.
- **Yahu/YHW — compound divine name:** ʿntyhw has been read “Anat-Yahu.” Relationship may be consortship, hypostasis, or a compound title. **Source:** Elephantine Aramaic material, fifth century BCE. **Tier:** disputed regional syncretism.
- **Ešem-Bethel — joint receipt of funds:** Anat-Bethel and Ešem-Bethel receive allocations in the same account. **Source:** TAD C3.15/Cowley 22. **Tier:** securely attested joint cultic accounting, not a narrated interaction.

No secure ancient text records Anat meeting Tanit, Asherah under the biblical spelling, Ishtar, Inanna, Qudshu, Aphrodite, Artemis, Minerva, or Atargatis. Later equations are comparative or syncretic.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Anat’s battle and Baal’s palace

**Early attested: KTU 1.3–1.4.** Anat conducts her terrifying battle, decorates herself with victims’ heads and hands, and wades in blood. After cleansing herself, she receives Baal’s messengers. Baal announces that conflict is ended or his enemies are defeated and asks her assistance.

Baal lacks a palace, a deficiency with political and cosmic significance because established gods possess houses. Anat approaches El and uses intimidating language; the notorious passage may threaten to smash El’s head and make his grey hair run with blood, though the grammatical framing is debated. The decisive diplomatic route runs through Athirat: gifts are prepared, Athirat is won over, and she petitions El. Permission is granted; Kothar builds Baal’s palace.

The episode is not simply “Anat asks and El agrees.” It stages hierarchy, patronage, threat, gift exchange, and Athirat’s intercessory authority.

### Baal’s death and Anat’s destruction of Mot

**Early attested: KTU 1.5–1.6.** Baal descends into Mot’s domain or is swallowed by Death. The land suffers. Anat searches for Baal; Shapash helps her recover his body. Anat mourns with violent self-laceration—cutting skin and cheeks—and buries him on Mount Saphon with offerings.

After a replacement king proves inadequate, Anat encounters Mot and demands Baal back. Mot claims to have consumed him. Later Anat attacks:

> She seizes divine Mot;  
> with a blade she splits him,  
> with a sieve she winnows him,  
> with fire she burns him,  
> with millstones she grinds him,  
> in the field she scatters him.

This agricultural processing of Death is textually secure, but its meaning is debated. It may evoke grain treatment, annihilation, ritual curse, seasonal change, or several simultaneously.

Baal returns, yet Mot returns after seven years and complains of his treatment. Baal and Mot fight directly until Shapash warns Mot that El supports Baal. Thus Anat’s killing of Mot is not permanent and is not, by itself, a complete “resurrection mechanism.” Describing Baal as an annually dying-and-rising vegetation god is an influential modern synthesis, not an explicit Ugaritic doctrinal statement.

### Aqhat and the bow

**Early attested: KTU 1.17–1.19.** Danel receives a son, Aqhat, after divine favor. Kothar gives Danel a remarkable bow, which passes to Aqhat. Anat desires it. She offers silver and gold; when Aqhat refuses, she offers life without ordinary human death. Aqhat rejects the offer and says bows are not women’s equipment.

Anat laughs or reacts with controlled rage, complains before El, and threatens the high god until allowed to act. She contracts Yatpan to kill Aqhat while disguised among vultures. The strike is excessive: Aqhat dies, and the bow falls into the sea or is broken. Anat regrets the lost weapon and perhaps the death. Danel curses the land and searches birds for his son’s remains; Pughat prepares revenge. The ending is missing.

The myth can be read as:

- a warning against insulting divinity;
- a narrative about the limits of human mortality;
- a royal-judicial tale centered on Danel;
- a crisis of succession;
- a myth connected with drought and restoration;
- a gendered contest over elite weaponry.

No single interpretation has displaced the others.

### Egyptian Anat and Seth

**Regional Egyptian variant.** In *The Contendings*, Seth demands recognition against Horus. Neith proposes that Seth be compensated with Anat and Astarte as wives. Their designation as daughters of Re is Egyptian.

A separate magical-healing tradition has Anat seek help when Seth is injured or poisoned. Claims that a papyrus depicts Seth raping Anat, or Anat castrating Seth, often rest on unstable translations or conflation with the sexual contest between Seth and Horus. The surviving evidence must be cited text by text.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Mari and Ḫanat

Administrative records attest offerings, cultic personnel, oaths, and the town named Ḫanat. No excavated sanctuary can yet be described as providing a complete Anat temple plan and theology. Her rank at Mari appears real but not supreme.

### Ugarit

Anat appears in divine and sacrificial lists and receives animals, oil, and other normal offerings. One calendar related to the gods of Mount Saphon gives her rams. The cult lists sometimes call her “Anat the Destroyer.”

No monumental temple at Ugarit has been identified with certainty as “the Temple of Anat” through a dedicatory inscription. The two prominent temple ruins are conventionally associated with Baal and Dagan, but even those identifications require care. Anat’s mythological prominence therefore exceeds the archaeological visibility of a dedicated sanctuary.

Her cult likely involved palace and state ritual because relevant tablets came from elite archives, but ordinary worship is also indicated by names and offerings. The small number of Anat-theophoric names relative to some other gods cautions against treating her as Ugarit’s universally dominant goddess.

### Egypt and Egyptian-controlled Canaan

The best evidence consists of named stelae, royal statues, personal names, temple dedications, and military imagery. Her cult was particularly compatible with Ramesside kingship and the Delta’s connections to western Asia. Tanis and Pi-Ramesses are central, while Beth Shean demonstrates worship in an Egyptian imperial center in Canaan.

Ramesses II’s patronage is unusually personal: he styles himself Anat’s beloved or protected king, gives her name to a royal daughter and martial animal, and represents her in protective proximity. This is institutional royal religion, not merely foreign mercenary folklore.

### Elephantine

The YHW temple had stood before Cambyses conquered Egypt in 525 BCE, according to the community’s later petition. It was destroyed in 410 BCE amid conflict involving priests of Khnum and was rebuilt or authorized for restoration after petitions to Persian officials and Judean authorities. Anat-Bethel’s appearance in a financial account proves cultic expenditure or collection but does not prove that her image stood inside YHW’s temple.

### Cyprus

KAI 42 supplies direct evidence of a Hellenistic Anat cult translated into the language of Athena. The setting reflects Phoenician-speaking communities under Ptolemaic rule and is one of the clearest cases of explicit ancient equivalence.

### Phoenicia and Punic territories

Direct Anat evidence is scattered. She never approaches the epigraphic prominence of Astarte in Phoenicia or Tanit in the Punic west.

The Carthage tophet is relevant mainly as a negative control:

- thousands of stelae principally invoke **Tinnit/Tanit “Face of Baal” and Baal Hammon**;
- cremated infants and animals were deposited there;
- classical authors report child sacrifice;
- scholars dispute whether all, most, some, or none of the children were killed as offerings rather than buried after natural perinatal death.

One osteological study argued that age profiles fit perinatal mortality, while later archaeological syntheses argue that the combined epigraphic, animal, depositional, and literary evidence supports at least some sacrificial killing. [Schwartz et al.](https://journals.plos.org/plosone/article?id=10.1371%2Fjournal.pone.0009177); [Oxford assessment](https://www.ox.ac.uk/news/2014-01-23-ancient-carthaginians-really-did-sacrifice-their-children); [Oxford Handbook overview](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/34280/chapter-abstract/290621967)

**Finding:** none of this identifies Anat as the tophet’s recipient. Substituting “Anat” for Tanit is unsupported. There is likewise no documentary evidence for temple prostitution in Anat’s cult.

## 7. Historical evolution

### c. 1800–1700 BCE: middle-Euphrates Ḫanat

Ḫanat appears in cultic accounts and names in and around Mari and Suhum. If she is Anat’s earlier form, this is an Amorite or west-Semitic cult already embedded in a mixed Mesopotamian environment. Her literary personality is unknown.

### c. 1600–1300 BCE: transmission toward Syria and Egypt

Anat’s name circulates through personal names and west-Semitic communities. Her introduction into Egypt has sometimes been attributed to the Hyksos, but direct cultic material before the later New Kingdom is sparse. A Hyksos introduction is plausible historical context, not securely documented fact.

### c. 1300–1190 BCE: Ugaritic and Ramesside peak

This is the richest documented period:

- the Baal Cycle, Aqhat, Kirta, rituals, and god lists at Ugarit;
- Egyptian royal devotion, images, and names under Ramesses II;
- evidence at Beth Shean and the Egyptian imperial periphery.

Calling it a “peak” refers to the concentration and richness of surviving records, not necessarily the number of worshippers.

### c. 1190–700 BCE: post-Ugaritic fragmentation

Ugarit’s destruction terminates its scribal archive. Anat survives in place-names, personal names, Egyptian religion, middle-Euphrates tradition, and scattered Phoenician material, but there is no surviving first-millennium Anat epic. The Hebrew Bible preserves names but no living narrative cult of her.

### Seventh–fifth centuries BCE: Aramaic compounds and Elephantine

Anat-Bethel and possibly Anat-Yahu attest reconfigured divine naming in Aramaic-speaking military and diaspora settings. This is not demonstrable direct continuity from Ugaritic ritual; it is a later religious formation using an old divine name.

### Fourth–third centuries BCE: Cyprus and Athena

The Anat-Athena bilingual records explicit Hellenistic translation. Anat’s martial character made Athena a plausible interpretive counterpart, but local political and linguistic circumstances—not timeless identity—created the equation.

### Hellenistic and Roman periods

Anat becomes difficult to isolate. Astarte, Atargatis, Athena, Aphrodite, Artemis, and local “Syrian Goddess” forms absorb or share traits. Pseudo-Lucian’s composite goddess illustrates this environment. Absorption is more defensible than claiming uninterrupted, individually named Anat worship.

### Late antiquity and medieval period

Christianization and later Islamization displaced public polytheistic institutions across Syria, Palestine, Phoenicia, Egypt, and North Africa. No documented medieval community maintained a continuously named Anat cult. Anat survives indirectly in manuscripts, biblical place-names, classical excerpts, and material remains—not as a securely continuous folk goddess.

### 1929 onward: archaeological rediscovery

The Ras Shamra discoveries transformed Anat from a faint biblical and Egyptian name into a major reconstructable goddess. Subsequent editions—Virolleaud, Gordon, Herdner, Dietrich-Loretz-Sanmartín, Pardee, Smith and Pitard—continually revised readings.

### Present

Small modern Pagan and Canaanite-reconstructionist communities worship Anat. Their rites are necessarily reconstructed from ancient texts and modern religious creativity because no complete liturgy or unbroken initiatory succession survives.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship’s “fertility and sex goddess”

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century comparativism tended to merge Anat with Astarte, Ishtar, “the Great Mother,” sacred sexuality, and vegetation religion. This produced the still-popular description “goddess of love, sex, fertility, and war.” Modern philological work has dismantled much of that synthesis. Anat’s violence, hunting, divine politics, and loyalty to Baal are secure; a primary fertility office is not.

### Frazerian seasonal mythology

The Baal–Mot conflict was often framed as an annual death-and-resurrection drama of vegetation. Seasonal and agricultural imagery is undeniable, but the tablets do not provide a ritual rubric stating that the drama was performed annually, nor do they show Anat permanently killing Mot. The categorical “dying-and-rising god” scheme is therefore historiographically important but evidentially limited.

### Feminist and psychological readings

Late twentieth-century authors have read Anat as:

- female rage unconstrained by patriarchal domesticity;
- a warrior-maiden archetype;
- a figure negotiating gendered access to weapons;
- a protector whose violence destabilizes conventional femininity;
- a manifestation of a universal “dark goddess.”

The first four may function as literary interpretations. The universal archetype is a modern psychological system, not ancient Ugaritic theology.

### Neopaganism

Canaanite reconstructionism, sometimes called Natib Qadish in particular modern formulations, emerged within late twentieth- and twenty-first-century reconstructionist Paganism. Practitioners use Ugaritic divine names, translated hymns, food and drink offerings, incense, and newly composed prayers. No reliable census isolates Anat devotees, and online visibility should not be mistaken for a large organized religion. [General scholarship on reconstructionist Pagan practice](https://rodnovery.ru/images/knigi/modern-paganism-in-world-cultures.pdf)

Modern associations with crystals, chakra systems, menstrual magic, fixed colors, planets, tarot cards, or a standardized “invocation of Anat” are **modern inventions** unless a practitioner clearly presents them as contemporary devotional creation.

### Ceremonial magic and occultism

Anat was not a central deity of the Renaissance grimoires, the original Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, or Crowley’s foundational Thelemic pantheon. Claims of an ancient Golden Dawn “Anat current,” traditional sigil, planetary seal, or medieval Anat grimoire lack documentary support.

She sometimes enters later eclectic magic through twentieth-century mythology dictionaries, goddess spirituality, and reconstructed Canaanite devotion. Such practices are legitimate modern religious creations but should not be backdated.

### Popular culture

The most visible game adaptation is Atlus’s **Megami Tensei/Persona** series. Anat appears as a recurring demon/persona and, in *Persona 5*, as an evolved form associated with Makoto Niijima. Its robotic motorcycle-like design and game attributes are modern visual invention, not reconstructed Bronze Age iconography. [Megami Tensei record](https://megamitensei.fandom.com/wiki/Anat)

Anat also appears in modern mythological fiction, role-playing supplements, goddess decks, internet art, and occult manuals. These works commonly combine her with Astarte, Ishtar, Athena, Sekhmet, or Tanit and amplify the blood-battle episode into a total personality.

### Political appropriation

Anat has been invoked as a symbol of Levantine antiquity, feminine militancy, regional identity, and occasionally modern nationalism. No single political movement has an exclusive or demonstrably continuous claim to her. Online claims that Anat proves one modern ethnic or national population uniquely “owned” Canaanite religion are modern identity arguments, not findings of the ancient evidence.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Discovery and editions

Before Ras Shamra, Anat was known chiefly through Egyptian material, biblical names, Phoenician inscriptions, and Greek or Roman notices. Ugarit’s discovery supplied a native Late Bronze Age corpus.

Important reference systems and editions include:

- **KTU/CAT:** Dietrich, Loretz, and Sanmartín, *Die keilalphabetischen Texte aus Ugarit, Ras Ibn Hani und anderen Orten*.
- Cyrus H. Gordon’s early grammars and translations.
- Andrée Herdner, *Corpus des tablettes en cunéiformes alphabétiques*.
- Dennis Pardee’s ritual-text editions.
- Mark S. Smith and Wayne T. Pitard, *The Ugaritic Baal Cycle*.
- Nicolas Wyatt, *Religious Texts from Ugarit*.
- Simon B. Parker, ed., *Ugaritic Narrative Poetry*.
- Peggy L. Day, “Anat,” in the *Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible*.
- Theodore J. Lewis, Mark S. Smith, John Day, and Karel van der Toorn on west-Semitic and Israelite religion.
- Izak Cornelius on Levantine goddess iconography.
- Edward Lipiński, Paolo Xella, Corinne Bonnet, and others on Phoenician religion and its western transformations.

### Principal disputes

1. **Ḫanat and Anat:** majority identification, but separated by geography, writing system, and evidentiary character.
2. **Etymology:** unresolved.
3. **“Virgin” Anat:** *btlt* securely marks youthful status; its sexual implications remain debated.
4. **Baal’s sister, wife, or lover:** sister and ally are explicit; wife or sexual partner is inferred.
5. **Fertility goddess:** now generally regarded as reductive.
6. **Blood battle as ritual:** possible, not demonstrated.
7. **Did Anat kill Yam?** Her boast says she defeated sea-monsters or Sea, while the principal cycle credits Baal with Yam’s defeat. The texts should not be harmonized.
8. **Did Anat resurrect Baal?** She recovers, mourns, buries, and avenges him; the mechanics of his return are not narrated clearly.
9. **Anat-Yahu:** separate consort, hypostasis, compound title, or Aramaean syncretism remain live alternatives.
10. **Anat-Bethel in the Assyrian treaty:** reading disputed.
11. **Anat = Tanit:** unsupported as a simple equation.
12. **Anat = Atargatis:** possible contribution to later Syrian-goddess formations, but no direct identity chain.
13. **Anonymous armed goddess images:** usually cannot be individually named.
14. **Human sacrifice:** Anat’s mythology is violent, but there is no direct evidence that her cult practiced human sacrifice.
15. **Temple prostitution:** no evidence specific to Anat; the broader category “sacred prostitution” has itself undergone sustained scholarly criticism.

### Lost evidence and evidentiary limits

Ugarit’s corpus is accidental: tablets survived because the city was destroyed and burned. Entire tablets, endings, ritual commentaries, oral performances, and local archives are missing. Egyptian inscriptions are shaped by royal ideology. Biblical texts are late literary witnesses often hostile to neighboring cults. Phoenician literature largely failed to survive, and Philo’s purported Phoenician history is mediated through Greek and Christian authors. The impression of Anat’s “disappearance” therefore combines real cultic change with drastic preservation bias.

### Evidentiary conclusions

The historically defensible Anat is not one timeless composite. She begins in the record as a poorly characterized Ḫanat of the middle Euphrates; becomes, at Ugarit, the “Maiden Anat,” martial ally of Baal, killer of Aqhat and Mot, mourner, hunter, and divine political actor; becomes in Ramesside Egypt an armed royal protector and daughter of Egyptian gods; persists in first-millennium names and compound deities; is explicitly translated as Athena in Ptolemaic Cyprus; then becomes difficult to distinguish amid wider Syrian and Mediterranean syncretism.

The blood-wading warrior, Mot’s destroyer, and seeker of Aqhat’s bow are firmly ancient. Anat as Baal’s wife, a universal fertility goddess, Yahweh’s unquestionably identified spouse, Tanit under another spelling, Atargatis’s complete former identity, a Carthaginian recipient of child sacrifice, or the holder of an ancient occult correspondence system is not firmly established.
