# Resheph: Research Dossier

**Class:** Male god in Eblaite, Ugaritic, Egyptian, Phoenician, Cypriot, and some Aramaic traditions; a dangerous but protective divine warrior associated especially with epidemic disease, sudden death, battle, archery, royal horses, and healing. In biblical Hebrew he is partly subordinated into Yahweh’s retinue and partly lexicalized as “flame,” “bolt,” “arrow,” “fever,” or “plague.”

**Earliest secure attestation:** The palace archives of Ebla, modern Tell Mardikh in Syria, principally the archive destroyed about **2300 BCE**. Administrative tablets written in cuneiform Eblaite/Sumerian record the god as **Rašap/Rašapum**, often localized as the Rašap of particular towns. These texts prove an established, geographically differentiated cult but preserve no narrative biography. The often-repeated description “Canaanite god” is consequently too narrow for his earliest phase: the first documentation is northern Syrian and predates the securely attested Canaanite and Phoenician evidence by many centuries.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early secure]** Direct third- or second-millennium BCE textual, epigraphic, or securely identified material evidence.
- **[Later attested]** Direct first-millennium BCE or later ancient evidence.
- **[Regional variant]** A locally specific form, title, function, or iconography.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A datable ancient equation or translation between gods.
- **[Learned systematization]** A scribal, priestly, exegetical, or antiquarian ordering that need not describe popular practice.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** A scholarly inference from incomplete evidence.
- **[Modern invention/revival]** A modern religious, occult, artistic, or popular-cultural construction not demonstrably inherited from ancient cult.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

Resheph was not simply “the plague god,” although disease is central to several traditions. His oldest records show a major Syrian god with multiple local manifestations. At Ugarit he receives offerings, appears in god lists and royal epic, destroys human offspring, and is associated with arrows, warfare, the army, gates, commerce, and perhaps the underworld. Egypt adopted him during the New Kingdom principally as a foreign-looking armed god who aided the king, horses, and chariot forces; private Egyptian worshippers also approached him as a healer and “hearer of prayer.” In first-millennium Cyprus his Phoenician cult was translated into local Greek religious language as Apollo. In the Hebrew Bible, *rešep* is simultaneously the residue of a named divine power and an ordinary word for lethal heat, plague, lightning, flame, spark, or arrow.

The polarity is fundamental: a power that sends epidemic disease can also avert or heal it; a violent god can defend a household or king. Münnich’s comprehensive study accordingly rejects the reduction of Resheph to a purely harmful or purely underworld deity and stresses his belligerent, protective, and curative sides.

### Names and scripts

- **Eblaite/Old Akkadian:** *Rašap*, *Rašapum*, conventionally reconstructed from syllabic cuneiform spellings such as *ra-sa-ap*. **[Early secure]**
- **Ugaritic:** 𐎗𐎌𐎔, **ršp**, conventionally vocalized *Rašpu*, *Rašap*, or *Rašp*. Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform normally omits vowels. **[Early secure]**
- **Phoenician:** 𐤓𐤔𐤐, **ršp**, conventionally *Resheph/Reshef*. **[Later attested]**
- **Biblical Hebrew:** רֶשֶׁף, **rešep̄**; plural רְשָׁפִים, *rešāp̄îm*. **[Later attested]**
- **Egyptian:** principally **ršp** and **ršpw**, usually transliterated *Reshep*, *Resheph*, or *Reshpu*. The final *-w* may reflect an Egyptian accommodation of a foreign pronunciation rather than a distinct god. **[Early secure]**
- **Greek translations:** principally **Ἀπόλλων**, Apollo, with local epicleses such as Amyklos, Alasiotas, and Heleitas. Greek does not merely transliterate *Resheph*: Cypriot bilinguals translate one cultic identity into another. **[Syncretistic identification]**

“Rashap,” “Rashpu,” “Reshep,” “Reshef,” and “Resheph” are modern transliteration conventions, not evidence for separate gods.

### Etymology

**[Disputed modern reconstruction]** The divine name and the Northwest Semitic common noun are unmistakably related, but priority and exact original sense remain unsettled.

Biblical Hebrew *rešep̄* denotes or evokes flame, fire-bolt, lightning, burning fever, pestilence, sparks, and arrow-like projectiles. It appears in parallel with pestilence in Deuteronomy 32:24 and Habakkuk 3:5; with arrows in Psalm 76:3; with hail or lightning in Psalm 78:48; with sparks in Job 5:7; and with the flames of love in Song 8:6. Older scholarship frequently derived the god directly from “flame” or “burning.” That fits fiery fever and projectile imagery but does not prove that “flame” was the name’s historically original meaning. The reverse development—a divine name becoming a common noun for his characteristic manifestations—is also possible. The etymology should therefore be recorded as uncertain, not as “Resheph means plague.”

### Secure and proposed titles

- **Rašap of Adani, Gunu, Tunip, and other places:** Localized Eblaite manifestations receiving palace offerings. **[Early secure; regional variants]**
- **Resheph of Mulukku, Muhbānu, Bibit, Gunu, and ʾIlištagmu:** Forms documented in Ugaritic administrative or ritual records. The precise geography of several names remains debated. **[Early secure; regional variants]**
- **Resheph of the Army** (*ršp ṣbʾ* or comparable construction): A military manifestation at Ugarit. **[Early secure]**
- **Resheph the Gatekeeper:** A title or function reconstructed from Ugaritic ritual/astronomical material. The inference that he opens the underworld for the sun is debated. **[Early text; disputed interpretation]**
- **Resheph of commerce/trade:** An Ugaritic or Hurrian-context manifestation associated with commercial protection. **[Early secure title; function partly reconstructed]**
- **Resheph, prince** (*ršp zbl*): The reading in the Kirta tradition has been understood as “Prince Resheph,” though damaged contexts and the semantic range of *zbl* counsel caution. **[Early secure/philologically qualified]**
- **Lord of the Arrow / Resheph of the Arrow:** Phoenician **ršp ḥṣ**, notably KAI 32 from Kition. *Ḥṣ* is normally connected with “arrow,” but proposed cultic nuances remain debated. **[Later attested]**
- **Resheph Mikal:** Phoenician **ršp mkl** at Idalion, translated in Greek as Apollo Amyklos. Whether Mikal began as a separate deity, local title, or place-linked cult name is disputed. **[Later attested; syncretistic identification]**
- **Resheph Alasiotas:** At Tamassos, paired in a bilingual with Apollo Alasiotas, “of Alashiya/Cyprus.” **[Later attested; regional and syncretistic]**
- **Resheph Heleitas/Elenites:** Tamassian epithet paired with Apollo Heleitas, plausibly connected with a marsh or local landscape. Its exact form and etymology are still being refined. **[Later attested; regional and syncretistic]**
- **“Great god,” “lord of heaven,” “lord of eternity”:** Egyptian honorifics. Most are generic divine praise rather than proof of universal sovereignty. **[Early secure; Egyptian regional usage]**
- **“Hearer of prayer,” “healer,” “giver of health”:** Egyptian votive titles reflecting personal petition and healing. **[Early secure; regional development]**
- **“Lord of the estate of the stable of horses”:** The apparently unique epithet on Betu’s Tell el-Borg stela. **[Early secure; local Egyptian military-stable cult]**
- **Resheph-Shulman:** A healing or well-being form cited in older literature. The underlying combinations and translations are less secure than popular summaries imply and should not be made the key to his identity. **[Disputed]**
- **Resheph of the Garden** (*ršp gn*): Attested or reconstructed in Phoenician material, but the reading and referent require inscription-specific treatment. **[Later attested; philologically disputed]**

### Ancient identifications

- **Nergal:** Multilingual god lists and scribal equations align Resheph with the Mesopotamian god Nergal because of shared associations with war, epidemic death, and destructive power. This is an ancient learned equivalence, not proof that they had identical origins, myths, or cults. **[Early secure; learned systematization]**
- **Apollo:** Fourth-century BCE Cypriot bilingual dedications explicitly render Resheph cult names as Apollo cult names. The equation was facilitated by archery, plague-sending, healing, and local political-cultural translation. **[Later secure; syncretistic identification]**
- **Ruda:** Some Aramaic evidence has been interpreted as associating or equating Resheph with the Arabian god Ruda, perhaps emphasizing protective favor rather than plague. The exact mechanism is less explicit than the Cypriot Apollo bilinguals. **[Later attested; disputed syncretism]**
- **Heracles:** Resheph is sometimes connected indirectly to Heracles through the chains Resheph–Nergal and Nergal–Heracles. Direct, universal Resheph–Heracles equivalence is not established. **[Ancient comparative chain; often overstated in modern accounts]**
- **Egyptian Montu, Min, Seth, or Herishef:** Similar armed images and Egyptianizing contexts encouraged identifications in older scholarship. Without an inscription, a “smiting god” cannot securely be called Resheph; resemblance is not equivalence. **[Modern reconstruction, usually disputed]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Ebla: the earliest record

**[Early secure, principally twenty-fourth–twenty-third centuries BCE]** The Ebla archives were found in Palace G at Tell Mardikh. They include thousands of administrative, lexical, diplomatic, and ritual tablets, many deposited shortly before the palace’s destruction around 2300 BCE. Nearly all Ebla references to Rašap are economic: sheep, textiles, precious objects, journeys, cult personnel, and palace disbursements connected with his localized forms.

What this establishes:

- Rašap was already important enough to receive recurrent palace-supported offerings.
- His cult was distributed among several towns rather than confined to Ebla.
- The name entered theophoric personal names.
- His various local forms could be individually named.
- Association with Adamma and equation with Nergal belong to ancient evidence, although their meaning must be distinguished from later mythology.

What it does **not** establish:

- No surviving Ebla myth narrates Resheph’s birth, marriage, battles, or rule of the dead.
- The economic texts do not prove that every later plague, arrow, gazelle, or Apollo attribute already existed.
- His original ethnic or geographic “homeland” cannot be extracted securely from the archive.

The principal modern syntheses are Edward Lipiński, *Resheph: A Syro-Canaanite Deity* (2009), and Maciej M. Münnich, *The God Resheph in the Ancient Near East* (2013).

### Old Babylonian, Mari, Emar, and related Syrian evidence

**[Early secure, second millennium BCE]** Syllabic spellings and theophoric names carry Rašap/Resheph through northern Syria and Upper Mesopotamia. Emar provides personal names and divine references; Hurrian environments appear to have known him as a guardian relevant to traders. The evidence is mostly administrative and onomastic, not narrative. A later Mesopotamian god-list expression conventionally understood as “Lord Resheph” under Nergal illustrates scribal equation rather than Mesopotamian mass worship of an imported Resheph.

### Ugaritic corpus

The texts come from Late Bronze Age Ugarit, principally the thirteenth century BCE, destroyed around 1190 BCE. Their tablets are contemporary cultic documents rather than manuscripts copied millennia later.

- **KTU 1.14–1.16, the Kirta/Keret Epic:** Resheph is the destroying power who “gathers” or takes part of Kirta’s family. In the conventional restoration, a fraction of the king’s children falls to Resheph. The epic also includes a divine assembly blessing Kirta’s renewed family, with a damaged reference commonly read as “Prince Resheph.” **[Early secure, with local textual damage]**
- **KTU 1.41 and duplicate 1.87, festival/vintage rites:** Resheph receives animal offerings among the gods of Ugarit. These texts are direct evidence for ritual placement, not narrative mythology. **[Early secure]**
- **Pantheon/god lists:** Ugaritic and Akkadian columns equate Resheph with Nergal. **[Early secure; learned systematization]**
- **Administrative offering texts:** These attest localized forms—of towns, the army, commerce, or cult offices—and are indispensable evidence for actual institutional worship. **[Early secure]**
- **RS 24.247+, omen compendium:** A malformed animal birth is interpreted as foretelling that “Resheph will consume the progeny.” This is strong evidence for lethal, especially offspring-destroying, power. **[Early secure]**
- **KTU 1.78, astronomical/omen tablet:** Often associated with an eclipse and Resheph’s gatekeeping role. Both the astronomical date and the exact theological interpretation remain contested. **[Early text; disputed reconstruction]**
- **RS 19.013:** A “contemplation” ritual involving Resheph; the genre and action are incompletely understood. **[Early secure text; uncertain interpretation]**
- **Baal Cycle, KTU 1.1–1.6:** Resheph does not play a securely preserved major narrative role. General claims that he fights beside Baal in the cycle should not be made without a line citation. **[Absence finding]**
- **Aqhat, KTU 1.17–1.19:** No securely preserved major Resheph episode. **[Absence finding]**

The standard textual tools include KTU/CAT; Dennis Pardee’s ritual-text editions; Nicolas Wyatt’s *Religious Texts from Ugarit*; Simon Parker’s *Ugaritic Narrative Poetry*; and Mark S. Smith and Wayne Pitard’s critical *Ugaritic Baal Cycle*. The Göttingen EUPT project supplies current photographs, readings, vocalizations, and German translations of the poetic corpus.

### Amarna correspondence

**[Early secure, fourteenth century BCE]** EA 35, a cuneiform Akkadian letter from the king of Alashiya—normally identified with Cyprus—to the Egyptian king, explains reduced copper production by saying that the “hand of Nergal” killed many people. It does not name Resheph. It matters because later Cypriot evidence equates Resheph with Apollo and ancient scribes equated Resheph with Nergal, but converting EA 35’s Nergal automatically into “Resheph” would erase what the tablet actually says.

### Egyptian textual and epigraphic corpus

- **Amenhotep II royal texts, fifteenth century BCE:** The king’s military vigor, horses, and chariotry are described through West Semitic divine imagery including Resheph and Astarte. These are among Egypt’s earliest secure receptions of him. **[Early secure]**
- **Betu stela, Tell el-Borg, eighteenth dynasty:** Resheph and Astarte appear in a stable/chariot context; Betu is “overseer of horses,” and Resheph bears the stable-estate title. **[Early secure]**
- **Ramesside votive stelae:** Approximately forty Egyptian stelae attributable to Resheph are catalogued iconographically, including about twelve inscribed examples from Deir el-Medina. The British Museum’s nineteenth-dynasty stela EA264 preserves a prayer by the necropolis worker Pashed to an enthroned Resheph. **[Early secure]**
- **Royal battle imagery:** Seals and royal images associate him with pharaonic victory, sickle-sword, shield, mace, captives, horses, and chariots. **[Early secure where inscribed; otherwise attribution varies]**
- **Chester Beatty and related medical-magical papyri:** Resheph participates in protective healing systems and the divine mapping of body parts. The particular anatomical word in one passage is uncertain and has been translated as marrow, uterus, male genital anatomy, or an unidentified organ. **[Early secure text; disputed translation]**
- **Later Egyptian litanies:** Ptolemaic or Roman-period cult lists can preserve his name without proving a vigorous contemporary popular cult. **[Later learned survival]**

### Hebrew Bible

The texts reached their surviving literary forms during the first millennium BCE; individual poems may preserve older material. Dating Habakkuk 3’s core and Deuteronomy 32 remains disputed.

- **Deuteronomy 32:24:** Yahweh threatens “wasting hunger” and “consumption by *rešep̄* and bitter destruction.” Resheph may be a personified plague power or already a common noun. **[Later canonical; ambiguous divine/lexical status]**
- **Habakkuk 3:5:** “Before him goes Pestilence (*deber*), and Resheph goes forth at his feet.” The parallel personifications and marching divine retinue make this the strongest biblical survival of Resheph as a subordinated divine being. Many translations replace the name with “plague,” “burning coals,” or “fever.” **[Later canonical; major scholarly dispute]**
- **Psalm 76:3:** Yahweh breaks the “flames/bolts of the bow,” conventionally arrows. **[Lexicalized usage]**
- **Psalm 78:48:** *Rešāp̄îm* accompany hail in the Egyptian-plague recital, variously lightning bolts or destructive agents. **[Canonical; ambiguous personification]**
- **Job 5:7:** “Sons of Resheph” fly upward, conventionally “sparks.” Whether faded mythic beings remain behind the idiom is disputed. **[Canonical; lexicalized or mythic fossil]**
- **Song of Songs 8:6:** Love’s flashes are *rišpê ʾēš*, “flashes of fire,” followed by *šalhebetyah*. This is ordinary poetic fire language, not a clear cultic reference. **[Canonical; lexicalized]**
- **1 Chronicles 7:25:** Resheph occurs as a human name in Ephraim’s genealogy; textual variants make its exact placement uncertain. It is not a narrative appearance by the god. **[Canonical onomastic occurrence]**

Calling the Bible merely a “hostile witness” is too simple. Its editors do subordinate or lexicalize older divine vocabulary, but several occurrences are poetic rather than explicit polemic.

### Greek, Roman, and late literary witnesses

Philo of Byblos, as quoted by Eusebius; Lucian’s *De Dea Syria*; Herodotus; and Josephus are essential for broader Phoenician/Syrian religion, but none supplies a secure, extended myth of Resheph comparable to the Baal Cycle. Their silence is consequential. Apollo equations must be established from the Cypriot bilingual inscriptions, not retrojected from every Greek description of Apollo. Likewise, Carthaginian sacrifice narratives in Greek and Roman authors cannot be assigned to Resheph when their named recipients are normally Kronos/Saturn, Baal Hammon, or Tanit.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

1. **Epidemic and sudden death.** Kirta, the malformed-birth omen, the biblical plague contexts, and comparison with Nergal make this secure. **[Early secure]**
2. **War and royal violence.** Ugaritic military titles and Egyptian royal imagery make this secure. **[Early secure]**
3. **Archery and projectiles.** *Ḥṣ*, Hebrew “bolts of the bow,” and the Apollo equation support the archer profile. Not every image gives him a bow; spear, mace, shield, and sickle-sword are also characteristic. **[Early/later secure]**
4. **Horses and chariotry.** Especially prominent in Egypt’s royal and military reception. **[Regional Egyptian development]**
5. **Healing and apotropaic protection.** Particularly clear in private Egyptian votives and medical-magical texts. **[Regional Egyptian development]**
6. **Trade and safe movement.** Attested in northern Syrian/Hurrian settings, though less central than war and disease. **[Regional variant]**
7. **Gatekeeping and possibly the underworld.** Some Ugaritic titles, Nergal equations, and death imagery support the proposal. Münnich argues that the evidence does not justify defining Resheph as “lord of the underworld.” **[Disputed modern reconstruction]**
8. **Metallurgy.** Proposed from Cypriot images and cultic associations, but not established by an explicit ancient title. **[Modern reconstruction]**

### Iconographic type

**[Secure when inscribed; otherwise attribution must remain qualified]** The best-recognized Egyptian form is a standing or striding male warrior:

- raised arm carrying a mace, spear, or sickle-sword;
- other hand holding a shield;
- short Egyptian kilt or Levantine dress;
- long hair or beard signaling foreign/Asiatic identity;
- high conical or Egyptian White Crown;
- gazelle head or protome fixed to the front of the crown;
- occasionally an uraeus instead of, or alongside, the gazelle feature.

The popular phrase “gazelle-headed god” is misleading. Resheph normally has a human head; a small gazelle head ornaments his crown. Nor is the gazelle ornament unique to him. Other deities could bear comparable appendages, so the motif alone cannot name an uninscribed figure.

The “smiting god” pose was widespread among Late Bronze Age Levantine and Egyptian warrior gods. Uninscribed figurines from Megiddo, Ugarit, or Syria-Palestine may represent Resheph, Baal, Seth-like gods, or another martial deity. Cornelius’s typological work remains foundational precisely because identification is difficult.

### Major objects

- **Inscribed Egyptian stelae, c. 1400–1200 BCE:** The strongest iconographic corpus; many explicitly name Resheph. **[Early secure]**
- **Betu stela, Tell el-Borg:** Resheph and Astarte in an equestrian/military institutional setting. **[Early secure]**
- **Pashed stela, British Museum EA264, nineteenth dynasty:** Enthroned Resheph receiving offerings and prayer from a workman. **[Early secure]**
- **Metropolitan Museum limestone statue 89.2.215, c. 1200–664 BCE:** The only presently known stone statue conventionally identified as Resheph; armed pose and gazelle-crowned head support the identification. Its broad date and lack of an identifying inscription require a slight qualification. **[Strong iconographic identification]**
- **Saite bronze and Hibis temple relief:** Evidence of continued Egyptian knowledge into the first millennium BCE. **[Later attested]**
- **Cypriot sculptures:** Bearded, enthroned, Egyptianizing, and later Hellenized Apollo forms coexist. At Golgoi, a bearded “Resheph/Apollo” type and a beardless, laurel-crowned kithara-playing Apollo illustrate visual translation rather than a single timeless image. **[Later regional/syncretistic]**

### Animals, plants, colors, numbers, days, and planets

- **Gazelle:** Secure in Egyptian iconography but of uncertain theological meaning. It is not securely documented as a sacrificial animal exclusive to Resheph or as proof of a “gazelle god” origin.
- **Horse:** Strong Egyptian royal and military association, not necessarily a universal sacred animal.
- **Lion or horned animal:** Proposed for some uninscribed warrior images; identification remains insecure.
- **Plants, colors, calendrical days, and sacred numbers:** No exclusive system is securely attested.
- **Mars:** Modern tables sometimes assign Resheph to Mars through war and Nergal. No direct ancient Resheph–Mars planetary system is securely documented.
- **Sun:** Claims that he is a solar god usually derive from fire etymology or Apollo and should not be treated as an early core attribute.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No surviving source securely names Resheph’s parents. No early narrative describes his birth. No stable sibling group or offspring is documented.

**Adamma** is repeatedly paired with Rašap in Eblaite cultic evidence and is often reconstructed as his consort. Pairing in offering lists is real; “wife” is an inference. Attempts to connect her name directly with Hebrew *ʾādām* (“human”) or *ʾădāmâ* (“ground”) are disputed.

At Ugarit Resheph is part of the pantheon and receives offerings alongside El, Baal, Anat, Shapash, Athtart, Athirat, Yarikh, Kothar, and others. A list does not make these gods his siblings. No securely attested Ugaritic family tree places him among El and Athirat’s seventy sons, even if such membership is possible in a broad pantheon model.

### Every securely documented or seriously proposed god–god interaction

Because Resheph survives mostly in lists, rituals, and equations rather than myths, the interaction corpus is short.

- **Adamma — cultic pairing, possibly consort:** Ebla administrative offering texts, c. twenty-fourth–twenty-third centuries BCE. **[Early secure pairing; consort status modern reconstruction]**
- **Nergal — direct scribal equation:** Multilingual god lists and learned cuneiform traditions, second millennium BCE. **[Early secure; learned systematization]**
- **El — joint presence in the Ugaritic divine assembly blessing Kirta’s household:** KTU 1.15, thirteenth century BCE. **[Early secure, damaged narrative context]**
- **Baal — joint receipt of offerings in Ugaritic ritual lists:** KTU 1.41/1.87 and related ritual texts, thirteenth century BCE. No securely preserved personal conversation or combat between them. **[Early secure cultic co-occurrence]**
- **Anat — joint receipt of offerings and membership in the same ritual pantheon:** Ugaritic ritual texts, thirteenth century BCE. No secure narrative interaction. **[Early secure cultic co-occurrence]**
- **Shapash — Resheph’s possible gatekeeping relation to the sun’s passage:** KTU 1.78 and associated interpretations, thirteenth century BCE. **[Early text; disputed reconstruction]**
- **Astarte — joint appearance in Egyptian royal equestrian ideology and on the Tell el-Borg stela:** eighteenth dynasty, fifteenth–fourteenth centuries BCE. They rejoice in or sponsor royal horsemanship; no genealogy is supplied. **[Early secure; Egyptian regional interaction]**
- **Qudshu — joint appearance on Egyptian triad stelae, commonly with Min:** principally Ramesside, thirteenth–twelfth centuries BCE. The images establish coordinated cultic presentation, not marriage. **[Early secure regional co-occurrence]**
- **Min — joint appearance with Qudshu and Resheph on Egyptian triad stelae:** Ramesside period. **[Early secure regional co-occurrence]**
- **Kubaba — joint occurrence on a damaged Aramaic stela from Tell Sifr:** first millennium BCE. The damage prevents reconstruction of a narrative relationship. **[Later attested; relation unknown]**
- **Ḥirta and Nanaya — joint appearance in Palmyrene Aramaic dedications:** Roman-period Palmyra. A Greek version aligns the triad with Greek divine names, but the precise cultic hierarchy is uncertain. **[Later attested regional grouping]**
- **Ruda — protective association or possible identification:** first-millennium Aramaic milieu. **[Disputed syncretistic identification]**
- **Apollo — explicit bilingual cultic equivalence:** Resheph Mikal/Apollo Amyklos at Idalion in 388 BCE; Resheph Alasiotas/Apollo Alasiotas and Resheph Heleitas/Apollo Heleitas at Tamassos in the fourth century BCE. **[Later secure syncretistic identification]**
- **Habayu — proposed identity:** Smith has suggested that Habayu, who assaults drunken El in KTU 1.114, might be a name or form of Resheph. No explicit ancient equation survives. **[Modern reconstruction; unproven]**
- **Akha — hostile disease-being overcome by Resheph:** Egyptian medical-magical tradition, New Kingdom. **[Early regional magical interaction; details text-dependent]**
- **Yahweh — subordinate attendant in the divine march:** Habakkuk 3:5 places Resheph at or behind Yahweh’s feet, paired with Deber before him. **[Later canonical; strongest biblical divine interaction]**
- **Deber — paired pestilential attendant:** Habakkuk 3:5. Whether Deber and Resheph remain gods, personified forces, or poetic common nouns is disputed. **[Later canonical; ambiguous classification]**
- **Heracles — no direct secure interaction:** Connections arise through chains of interpretatio involving Nergal, not through a surviving scene between Resheph and Heracles. **[Absence finding]**
- **Eshmun — no secure mythic interaction:** Resheph’s name may occur in Sidonian geography, while Bodashtart’s construction concerns Eshmun’s sanctuary. This does not create a documented Resheph–Eshmun episode. **[Absence finding]**
- **Melqart — no secure narrative interaction:** Their coexistence within Phoenician religion is not an interaction. **[Absence finding]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The destruction of Kirta’s household

**Source:** Kirta/Keret Epic, KTU 1.14–1.16, Ugaritic clay tablets, thirteenth century BCE. **[Early secure]**

Kirta begins bereft of family and dynasty. El directs him toward a campaign and marriage; after complex events his household is restored and blessed. In the epic’s accounting of his losses, portions of his kin are taken by different causes or divine powers, including Resheph. The commonly translated sense is that Resheph “gathered” a fraction of the family, meaning death under his agency. This is the nearest surviving text to a narrative act by Resheph: he is not the epic’s protagonist but a recognized executor of mass or familial mortality.

In a later damaged portion the divine assembly arrives to bless Kirta and his new family; a reading “Prince Resheph” places him within the high divine gathering. The contrast is characteristic rather than contradictory: a god who destroys is also a legitimate member of the assembly that confirms renewed life.

There is no surviving story explaining why Resheph attacks the family, no combat with Kirta, and no appeasement ritual directed specifically to him. Expanded modern retellings that give him dialogue or personal motives are inventions.

### Resheph consumes the progeny

**Source:** RS 24.247+, Ugaritic omen series, thirteenth century BCE. **[Early secure]**

This is not a myth in narrative form but a conditional divinatory statement. A malformed animal fetus—reported as having abnormally short legs—portends that “Resheph will consume the progeny.” It links congenital anomaly, omen logic, and catastrophic mortality. The formulation reinforces his lethal power over descendants but does not prove that he was believed to eat children literally.

### Resheph and the sun’s gate

**Source:** KTU 1.78 and related ritual/astronomical interpretation, Late Bronze Age. **[Early text; disputed reconstruction]**

The tablet has been interpreted in relation to a solar eclipse. Resheph appears in a gatekeeping capacity. One scholarly reconstruction makes him a chthonic gatekeeper who admits Shapash into the underworld during the sun’s nightly or eclipsed passage. Other scholars resist building an underworld office from this damaged and difficult text. It is methodologically safer to state that Resheph, a gate, and an abnormal solar event are textually connected; the full cosmological story is lost.

### No independent Resheph epic

No surviving Ugaritic, Phoenician, Egyptian, Greek, or Latin composition tells a continuous “Myth of Resheph.” He is absent from the principal surviving action of the Baal Cycle and Aqhat. Modern biographies in which he is born, marries, fights named enemies, and rules a defined infernal kingdom usually combine separate offering lists, Nergal parallels, Egyptian art, and modern imagination.

### Biblical subordination narrative

Habakkuk 3 is a Yahwistic theophany, not a Resheph myth, but it preserves a mythic configuration: Yahweh advances from the south in cosmic warfare; Deber marches before him and Resheph at his feet. Older independent epidemic powers have become members or personifications of the national god’s entourage. The text documents theological subordination without narrating the historical process by which it occurred.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Ebla

Palace disbursements of livestock, textiles, and precious goods establish a royally supported cult. Multiple geographic forms imply shrines or cult installations at named towns, but archaeological remains cannot generally be matched securely to each textual Resheph. Priests and attendants occur in administrative networks, yet no complete constitution of a “Resheph priesthood” survives.

### Ugarit

Ritual calendars and offering texts prescribe sheep, rams, or other animals for Resheph among a large divine sequence. KTU 1.41/1.87 belongs to a festival complex frequently called the vintage or first-wine rites. These tablets establish repeated public or palace-linked sacrifice. They do not justify reconstructing a separate plague festival unless a source says so.

Theophoric names and multiple epithets show broad social and institutional familiarity. Resheph’s army and commerce forms imply functional patronage extending beyond a single sanctuary. No excavated Ugaritic temple can presently be identified beyond dispute as “the Temple of Resheph.”

### Egypt

The Egyptian record divides sharply between royal and private use.

- **Royal sphere:** Pharaohs and royal officers invoke the god’s violent force in battle, archery, horse training, and chariotry. The royal appropriation follows Egypt’s imperial encounter with Syria-Palestine during the eighteenth dynasty.
- **Military institutions:** The Tell el-Borg stela connects him with a stable estate and overseer of horses.
- **Domestic/personal sphere:** Small stelae from Deir el-Medina likely stood in household shrines, probably with small offering tables. Egyptian-named workmen—not only resident “Asiatics”—petitioned him. Pashed’s stela shows a kneeling human worshipper and a prayer before the god.
- **Healing:** Medical-magical papyri enlist him against illness or poison. Private epithets praise him as healer and hearer of prayer.
- **Offerings:** Stelae show food, incense, and prayer. No Egyptian source establishes human sacrifice to Resheph.

There is no evidence for “temple prostitution” in his cult. The category itself has frequently been over-applied to ancient Near Eastern religions.

### Phoenicia and Cyprus

Cyprus supplies the most explicit first-millennium institutional evidence.

- **Kition:** KAI 32/CIS I 10 records a dedication to Resheph of the Arrow, apparently involving a priest and altar. The inscriptions situate him within the Phoenician royal city’s public cult.
- **Idalion, Moutti tou Arvili:** A mixed Greek-Phoenician sanctuary produced the 388 BCE bilingual statue-base dedication by Baalrom son of Abdimilk. The Phoenician calls the recipient “his lord Resheph Mikal” and says the god heard his voice; the Greek calls him Apollo Amyklos and says the wish was accomplished. The statue itself is lost.
- **Tamassos/Frangissa:** Fourth-century bilingual dedications align Resheph Alasiotas and Resheph Heleitas with Apollo bearing the corresponding local titles.
- **Political setting:** Kition’s kings conquered Idalion and later Tamassos. Elite bilingual dedication to both local Greek and Phoenician formulations helped legitimate rule across linguistic communities. The syncretism was therefore cultic and political, not merely a philosopher’s comparison.
- **Hellenistic continuation:** Apollo increasingly became the primary Greek name and image; “Mikal” could survive as an Apolline epithet.

### Sidon

Bodashtart’s sixth- or fifth-century BCE building inscriptions for Eshmun’s sanctuary include a geographic expression read as the “district/land of the Reshephs” (*ʾrṣ ršpm*) in some editions. This may preserve a Resheph-linked district or plural divine name. It does not document a Resheph temple inside Eshmun’s sanctuary, and the geography remains debated.

### Aramaic and Palmyrene cult

Damaged Syrian inscriptions pair Resheph with Kubaba; Palmyrene dedications group him with Ḥirta and Nanaya. These late regional traditions stress protection more than epidemic attack. The evidence is real but small, and the cult’s institutional scale cannot be calculated.

### Carthage and the tophet

**[Contested-material finding]** No secure Carthaginian cult of Resheph is presently established. Older scholarship often manufactured “Punic Resheph” by misreading:

- the distinct theonym **Eresh** (*ʿrš*) in personal names;
- **rš**, “head/cape,” in a Baal title;
- unnamed armed figures;
- or Phoenician evidence from Cyprus as if “Phoenician” automatically meant “Carthaginian.”

The Carthage tophet therefore does not belong to Resheph’s documented cult. Its stelae principally name Baal Hammon and Tanit. Human infant sacrifice at Carthage remains a separate dispute: literary witnesses, vow inscriptions, cremated human and animal remains, and demographic analysis have produced competing sacrificial and special-cemetery interpretations. Even if sacrifice occurred, no evidence assigns it to Resheph. Connecting a “plague god” with tophet sacrifice is a modern conflation.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### I. Eblaite establishment, c. 2400–2300 BCE

Resheph first appears as a major northern Syrian god supported by the Ebla palace. Local manifestations and theophoric names show a mature cult already in progress. His narrative character is unrecoverable because the archive is administrative.

### II. Wider Syrian transmission, c. 2100–1600 BCE

Names and cult references spread or persist through northern Syrian, Old Babylonian, Mari-related, and later Emar/Hurrian environments. Ancient comparison with Nergal becomes possible because both gods govern destructive force, warfare, and epidemic death.

### III. Ugaritic and Egyptian florescence, c. 1500–1190 BCE

This is the best-documented phase.

At Ugarit, Resheph belongs to official ritual, local cult geography, divination, royal epic, army, and commerce. In Egypt he is adopted through imperial and military contact with the Levant. Egyptian kings, chariot officers, and stable personnel emphasize martial power; ordinary worshippers increasingly emphasize health and protection. The gazelle-crown image becomes especially visible in Egypt.

“Peak” is defensible only as a peak in surviving evidence, not necessarily the absolute height of ancient devotion.

### IV. Post-Ugaritic contraction and regional persistence, c. 1150–700 BCE

Ugarit’s destruction ends its palace cult and archives. Egypt retains Resheph through the Ramesside period and into the first millennium, but the number and social prominence of attestations decline. Levantine and Aramaic evidence becomes fragmented. Hebrew poetry preserves his name both as a dangerous power subordinated to Yahweh and as ordinary vocabulary.

### V. Phoenician-Cypriot transformation, c. 700–300 BCE

Cyprus becomes the clearest center. Phoenician Resheph acquires local titles and is translated as Apollo in bilingual dedications. The process combines shared functions with Kition’s political expansion into Greek-speaking territory. Images become progressively Hellenized while older bearded and Egyptianizing types persist.

This is not the replacement of an identical “Apollo” name for a timeless god. It is a historically observable negotiation among Phoenician, Cypriot, and Greek cult identities.

### VI. Hellenistic and Roman marginal survival, c. 300 BCE–300 CE

Apollo absorbs or succeeds many Cypriot functions. Resheph’s name becomes rarer. Egyptian Ptolemaic dedications and priestly lists preserve learned memory, but they do not demonstrate a large continuing popular cult. Palmyrene evidence represents a distinct late regional survival or revival.

### VII. Late antique disappearance

No decree is known that individually suppresses Resheph’s worship, and no securely identified Resheph temple has a documented Christian destruction narrative. His disappearance is better explained as gradual absorption, linguistic change, institutional collapse, and the expansion of Christianity and, later, Islam—not as one recorded forced conversion event.

Apollo’s cult was suppressed within the general Christianization of the Roman Empire, but it is impossible to identify which late Apolline practices still carried a specifically Resheph-derived identity.

### VIII. Scholarly rediscovery, nineteenth–twenty-first centuries

Phoenician-Cypriot bilinguals discovered in the nineteenth century made the Apollo equation legible and helped decipher the Cypriot syllabary. Excavation of Ugarit beginning in 1929 supplied the Ugaritic corpus. Ebla’s palace archive, discovered in the 1970s, pushed the documentary history back into the third millennium BCE. Egyptian cataloguing and new finds such as Tell el-Borg refined the distinction between royal and household worship.

### IX. Present

There is no demonstrated continuous community transmitting Resheph worship from antiquity. Individual modern Canaanite/Semitic reconstructionists may honor him, but this is revival based on archaeology and texts. Its scale is not reliably quantified, and it should not be described as an uninterrupted survival.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and public mythology

The modern composite “Resheph, gazelle-headed god of plague, war, thunder, the underworld, Mars, and fire” merges evidence from different eras. Its pieces have differing status:

- plague, war, lethal arrows, and healing: ancient evidence;
- gazelle ornament: Egyptian iconographic evidence;
- full animal head: usually a modern misunderstanding;
- sovereign lord of the underworld: disputed scholarly reconstruction;
- Mars correspondence: modern or indirect astrological systematization;
- fixed family tree: modern invention;
- Carthaginian child sacrifice: unsupported conflation.

### Occult and esoteric traditions

No significant Resheph current has been located in Renaissance hermetism, the canonical Golden Dawn system, Theosophy, or the major early modern grimoires. Modern websites sometimes assign him planetary colors, incense, sigils, feast days, or elemental correspondences. Unless those prescriptions cite an actual ancient source, they are modern inventions.

Aleister Crowley’s **Liber Resh vel Helios** is unrelated: *Resh* is the Hebrew letter ר and the text concerns solar adorations. It is not a ritual to Resheph.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist practice

Canaanite reconstructionism emerged in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries within the wider modern Pagan milieu. Shai Feraro documents a very small Israeli movement whose relationship to ancient Canaan, modern territory, and the twentieth-century Canaanist cultural movement is actively negotiated. Resheph can appear in modern pantheon lists and private devotion, but published ethnography does not demonstrate that he is a principal deity of a large organized movement.

Any modern liturgy addressed to him is necessarily reconstructive: no complete ancient Resheph prayer book or initiation rite survives.

### National and political appropriation

The Israeli Canaanist movement reached its cultural peak in the 1940s and used pre-biblical Canaan as an identity resource. Modern Canaanite reconstructionists sometimes engage the same landscape while differing sharply from Canaanist nationalism. No evidence supports identifying Resheph himself as a major emblem of the historic Canaanist movement or of a current extremist organization. Claims of extremist appropriation require case-specific proof.

### Popular culture

Resheph appears sporadically in fantasy games, role-playing settings, online fiction, deity databases, and modern art, normally as a plague demon or apocalyptic warrior. These depictions materially reinforce the “evil plague god” image but are modern retellings. There is no single novel, film, or game demonstrably comparable in influence to the biblical and museum image traditions.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Major studies

- **William J. Fulco, *The Canaanite God Rēšep* (1976):** First major dedicated English-language synthesis; now supplemented by Ebla discoveries and later epigraphy.
- **Izak Cornelius, *The Iconography of the Canaanite Gods Reshef and Baal* (1994):** Fundamental catalogue and methodological warning about distinguishing uninscribed warrior gods.
- **Paolo Xella, “Resheph,” in *Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible*, 2nd edition (1999):** Concise philological and comparative synthesis, especially influential in biblical studies.
- **Edward Lipiński, *Resheph: A Syro-Canaanite Deity* (2009):** Large historical corpus arranged from Ebla through Egypt, Ugarit, Phoenicia, Aramaic, and the Bible.
- **Maciej M. Münnich, *The God Resheph in the Ancient Near East* (2013):** Current comprehensive reassessment, particularly skeptical of an essential underworld identity.
- **Mark S. Smith, *The Early History of God* and *The Origins of Biblical Monotheism*:** Places biblical Resheph within the transformation of West Semitic divine councils.
- **John Day, *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan*:** Treats biblical survivals and Yahwistic subordination.
- **Mark S. Smith and Wayne Pitard, *The Ugaritic Baal Cycle*; Wyatt and Parker translations:** Essential controls on claims about Ugaritic mythology, including the important fact that Resheph has no major preserved role in the Baal Cycle.
- **Dennis Pardee’s ritual-text publications:** Essential for offering lists and KTU 1.41/1.87.
- **Corinne Bonnet’s work on Phoenician religion and Melqart:** Methodologically important for treating interpretatio and divine names as historically situated translation, although Melqart is not Resheph.

### Principal controversies

#### Where did Resheph originate?

Ebla supplies the earliest secure record, but earliest attestation is not necessarily origin. Proposed western Semitic, northern Syrian, or broader Syrian backgrounds depend on linguistic reconstruction and the accidents of excavation. “Originated at Ebla” exceeds the evidence.

#### Was he an underworld god?

Arguments for:

- equation with Nergal;
- lethal role in Kirta;
- possible gatekeeper function;
- nighttime-sun interpretation;
- associations with death and epidemic.

Arguments against:

- no text calls him king or lord of the underworld;
- Nergal equivalence may be functional and scribal;
- gates are not automatically infernal;
- Egyptian worship stresses war, horses, healing, and protection;
- his victims’ deaths do not establish jurisdiction over the dead.

The safest conclusion is that Resheph had strong death-dealing and perhaps chthonic associations in some contexts, but “lord of the underworld” is not a securely attested universal office.

#### Did “Resheph” originally mean flame?

The Hebrew noun supports a fire semantic field, but the chronology of god-name and common noun is unknown. Fire, fever, lightning, arrows, and divine agency may have influenced one another over centuries.

#### Can uninscribed “smiting gods” be identified as Resheph?

Only probabilistically. Raised weapon, shield, conical crown, beard, gazelle appendage, context, and date must be weighed together. Inscribed Egyptian stelae provide the control group. Older museum attributions often treated every Levantine warrior as either Baal or Resheph.

#### Was the gazelle intrinsic from the beginning?

It is secure in Egyptian iconography, not in the Eblaite archive. No surviving text explains it. An Egyptian iconographic innovation or emphasis is at least as plausible as an immemorial “gazelle god.”

#### Is Habakkuk 3:5 naming a god?

The syntactic parallel between Deber and Resheph, their positions around Yahweh, and external evidence for both as divine powers favor mythic personification. The word’s normal Hebrew meanings allow “plague/fever,” however, and ancient Greek translators already treated such terms as common nouns. The passage deliberately permits or results from the collapse of deity into attribute.

#### Was Resheph Punic or Carthaginian?

Secure Cypriot Phoenician evidence is abundant; secure Punic evidence is not. Older alleged Punic examples frequently dissolve under improved readings. The Carthage tophet cannot be attributed to him.

#### Was Apollo merely a Greek name for Resheph?

The bilinguals make them equivalent in particular sanctuaries and political settings. They do not prove that all Apollo cults derive from Resheph or that all Resheph cults possessed Apollo’s myths. “Translation” and “identification” are better historical terms than universal identity.

### Archives, museums, and losses

- **Ebla tablets:** Excavated at Tell Mardikh; editions in the ARET series and related corpora. The destruction of Syrian cultural heritage and conflict-related access problems complicate present study.
- **Ugaritic tablets:** Excavated at Ras Shamra and Ras Ibn Hani; housed principally in Syrian collections, with photographs, copies, and editions dispersed internationally.
- **Egyptian objects:** British Museum, Metropolitan Museum of Art, Egyptian Museum Cairo, Turin, Berlin, Chicago’s Institute for the Study of Ancient Cultures, Hildesheim, Avignon, and other collections.
- **Cypriot bilinguals:** British Museum and Cypriot collections; early excavation and antiquities-market practices damaged archaeological context.
- **Lost media:** Bronze statues mentioned or implied by surviving bases were commonly melted down. Organic cult equipment, wooden shrines, and most oral mythology have disappeared.

## 10. Chronology

- **c. 2400–2300 BCE:** Ebla palace accounts attest Rašap, localized forms, offerings, and theophoric names. **[Early secure]**
- **c. 2300 BCE:** Destruction of Palace G seals the principal Ebla archive. **[Archaeological horizon]**
- **Early second millennium BCE:** Syllabic attestations and personal names continue across Syria and Upper Mesopotamia. **[Early secure]**
- **c. 1600–1200 BCE:** Emar and related Syrian/Hurrian evidence documents names, cult, and commercial-protective aspects. **[Early secure]**
- **c. 1450–1400 BCE:** Egyptian royal reception under Amenhotep II; Resheph and Astarte enter military and equestrian royal language. **[Early secure]**
- **Fourteenth century BCE:** EA 35 reports epidemic death in Alashiya as the “hand of Nergal.” This is contextual, not a direct Resheph attestation. **[Early contextual evidence]**
- **Fifteenth–fourteenth century BCE:** Betu’s Tell el-Borg stela pairs Resheph and Astarte in a stable/chariot institution. **[Early secure]**
- **Thirteenth century BCE:** Ugaritic ritual, administrative, omen, astronomical, and Kirta texts document Resheph’s cult and lethal power. **[Early secure]**
- **c. 1300–1100 BCE:** Main corpus of inscribed Egyptian Resheph stelae; household devotion flourishes at Deir el-Medina. **[Early secure]**
- **c. 1200 BCE:** Chester Beatty medical-magical traditions incorporate Resheph into healing/protection. **[Early secure]**
- **c. 1190 BCE:** Destruction of Ugarit ends the palace archive and its documented institutional cult. **[Archaeological event]**
- **c. 1200–664 BCE:** Metropolitan Museum limestone warrior statue conventionally identified as Resheph. **[Strong iconographic attribution]**
- **First millennium BCE:** Hebrew *rešep̄* increasingly functions as plague, flame, spark, lightning, or arrow while retaining mythic residues. **[Later canonical development]**
- **Eighth–sixth centuries BCE:** Aramaic and Phoenician evidence preserves protective and geographic forms; exact dates differ by inscription. **[Later attested]**
- **Sixth–fifth centuries BCE:** Bodashtart inscriptions refer, under one reading, to a Resheph-linked district in Sidonian territory. **[Later attested; disputed geography]**
- **Fourth century BCE:** Kition KAI 32 names Resheph of the Arrow. **[Later secure]**
- **388 BCE:** Idalion bilingual equates Resheph Mikal with Apollo Amyklos. **[Later secure syncretism]**
- **Mid-to-late fourth century BCE:** Tamassos bilinguals equate local Resheph forms with Apollo Alasiotas and Apollo Heleitas. **[Later secure syncretism]**
- **Hellenistic period:** Apollo increasingly absorbs the Cypriot cult’s public name and Greek iconography; Mikal can survive as an Apolline title. **[Regional transformation]**
- **Third–first centuries BCE:** Egyptian priestly lists and occasional dedications preserve Resheph’s name, but evidence for popular cult declines sharply. **[Learned survival]**
- **Roman period:** Palmyrene dedications provide limited late evidence for Resheph in a local triadic context. **[Later regional survival]**
- **Late antiquity:** No individually documented suppression; the name disappears through institutional decline, absorption, and wider Christianization. **[Historical reconstruction]**
- **1860s–1880s CE:** Kition, Idalion, and Tamassos inscriptions are discovered and published; the Idalion bilingual contributes to decipherment of the Cypriot syllabary.
- **1929 onward:** Ras Shamra excavations reveal Ugaritic texts.
- **1970s onward:** Ebla discoveries extend Resheph’s record into the third millennium BCE.
- **1976:** Fulco publishes the first major modern monograph.
- **1994:** Cornelius publishes the standard iconographic study.
- **1999:** Xella’s revised DDD entry consolidates textual and biblical scholarship.
- **2006–2007:** Publication of the Tell el-Borg stela sharpens understanding of Resheph, Astarte, horses, and the Egyptian frontier.
- **2009:** Lipiński publishes *Resheph: A Syro-Canaanite Deity*.
- **2013:** Münnich publishes *The God Resheph in the Ancient Near East*, challenging the standard underworld reduction.
- **2016:** Feraro documents contemporary Israeli Canaanite reconstructionism as a small modern religious movement.
- **Present:** Resheph survives in scholarship, museums, Bible translation debates, small reconstructionist milieus, and popular fantasy. No continuous ancient cult or independently verified large revival exists.
