# Shapash: Research Dossier

**Class:** goddess—specifically the female personification and deity of the sun in Late Bronze Age Ugaritic tradition. In later Phoenician evidence the cognate solar deity Šmš is gender-variable; continuity with Ugaritic Šapšu is probable at the lexical level but cannot be treated as an unchanged personal identity.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the thirteenth-century BCE Ugaritic clay-tablet corpus from Ras Shamra-Ugarit, especially the Baal Cycle and contemporary ritual texts, written in alphabetic cuneiform. A feminine solar deity may also stand behind the feminine grammar of the Sumerogram ^dUTU in Amarna letter EA 323 from Ashkelon, ca. mid-fourteenth century BCE, but the logogram does not write the name Šapšu; that identification remains probable rather than secure.

**Evidence labels used:** **[early]** securely attested Late Bronze Age material; **[variant]** a regional or textual variant; **[later]** Iron Age or classical evidence; **[syncretism]** a historically situated equation; **[reconstruction]** modern scholarly inference; **[modern]** modern reception or invention; **[uncertain]** evidence insufficient.

---

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Šapšu is the Ugaritic sun goddess, imagined not merely as a luminous object but as a mobile divine intelligence whose daily course gives her access to heaven, earth, the western horizon, and—on a widespread scholarly reading—the underworld at night. In narrative she communicates divine decisions, transports or assists the dead, searches inaccessible regions, and compels Mot to acknowledge Baal’s kingship. In incantations she relays appeals to geographically dispersed gods. In royal mortuary ritual she regulates the return of dead kings and the *rpʾum*, the Rephaim. Her famous title, conventionally translated “Torch/Lamp/Luminary of the Gods,” is therefore functional: she illuminates and traverses the gods’ cosmos.

### Names

- **Ugaritic:** 𐎌𐎔𐎌, **špš**, the ordinary noun “sun” and the divine name. Since Ugaritic generally omits vowels, vocalizations are reconstructed.
- **Šapšu / Shapshu:** the preferred scholarly vocalization, based on Ugaritic morphology and syllabic evidence.
- **Šapš / Shapsh:** a cautious consonantal or minimally vocalized rendering.
- **Shapash:** common English spelling, influenced partly by Akkadian **Šamaš**. Ola Wikander argues that *Shapash* is linguistically impossible as a genuinely Ugaritic form; it remains entrenched as an English conventional name rather than an ancient written vocalization. [Wikander, *Drought, Death and the Sun*](https://lucris.lub.lu.se/ws/portalfiles/portal/96387483/Ola_Wikander_2014_Drought_Death_and_the_Sun_in_Ugarit_and_Ancient_Israel_a_Philological_and_Comparative_Study.pdf).
- **Phoenician/Punic:** 𐤔𐤌𐤔, **šmš**, cognate with Hebrew **šemeš** and Akkadian **šamaš**. The Ugaritic /p/ corresponds here to /m/ elsewhere in Semitic. The Iron Age form is not *špš*.
- **Hebrew:** שֶׁמֶשׁ, **šemeš**, “sun.” It is often grammatically feminine but can be masculine. Biblical uses do not automatically denote the goddess.
- **Akkadian:** **Šamaš**, normally a male sun god. Equivalence in multilingual god lists is classificatory and translational, not proof that Šapšu was understood as male at Ugarit.
- **Sumerographic:** ^dUTU, the standard cuneiform logogram for a solar deity. In EA 323 its accompanying grammar is feminine, possibly reflecting a southern Canaanite solar goddess. **[uncertain/early]**
- **Greek:** Ἥλιος, “Sun,” or Apollo in philosophical interpretation. These are later translations or equations, not ancient Ugaritic names.

### Etymology

The name is the inherited Semitic word for “sun,” conventionally reconstructed as something like Proto-Semitic *šamš-/šam(a)š-*. Ugaritic *špš* exhibits the Northwest Semitic sound development reflected in its second consonant. Because the same written word denotes both the celestial sun and the goddess, some passages allow debate over whether the text means a personalized deity or the luminary. Divine determinatives, feminine agreement, direct address, and narrative agency usually settle the mythological cases.

Claims that the name means “judge,” “all-seeing one,” or “justice” are **modern semantic extrapolations from Mesopotamian Šamaš**, not etymologies of Ugaritic *špš*.

### Attested titles and descriptions

- **nrt ʾilm špš**, commonly “Šapšu, lamp/torch/luminary of the gods.” The noun *nrt* is feminine “lamp/light.” This is her characteristic title in the Baal Cycle. **[early]**
- **špš ʿlm**, “eternal Šapšu” or “Sun of eternity.” **[early; translation and segmentation can vary]**
- **rbt špš**, “Lady/Great Lady Šapšu.” **[early]**
- **špš pgr**, a difficult title associated with funerary or offering language; *pgr* may mean corpse, funerary offering, or a cultic designation. It should not be translated confidently as a fixed personal epithet. **[early/uncertain]**
- **ʾišḫn špš / ʾišḫn nrt rb**, from KTU 1.161, variously translated “Burn, O Šapšu; burn, O great light,” “shine,” or even through a different verbal analysis, “bow down.” The uncertainty materially affects reconstructions of the rite. **[early/disputed]**
- “Queen,” “mother of the gods,” “judge,” and “psychopomp” are partly descriptive scholarly labels. “Psychopomp” is a persuasive functional interpretation of her underworld and mortuary activity, but it is not an Ugaritic title.

### Classification shifts

- At Ugarit: unambiguously a **goddess**.
- In Iron Age Phoenician-speaking regions: Šmš appears in both feminine and masculine conceptions. A female image with solar characteristics may preserve the older pattern, while Baalbek and later Greco-Roman solar theology favor a male god. **[later/variant]**
- In Hellenistic and Roman learned interpretation: the Phoenician sun could be rendered Helios or Apollo. **[syncretism]**
- In modern neopaganism: revived as a goddess, generally under “Shapash,” “Shapshu,” or “Shemesh.” **[modern]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Archaeological setting

Ras Shamra, ancient Ugarit, was identified after the accidental discovery of a tomb at Minet el-Beida in 1928. Claude Schaeffer’s mission uncovered the city and its previously unknown alphabetic cuneiform writing beginning in 1929. The tablets belong mainly to Ugarit’s final Late Bronze Age phase. The kingdom was destroyed around 1190 BCE; radiocarbon work on nearby Gibala places the regional terminal destruction in 1192–1190 BCE. [Collège de France](https://www.college-de-france.fr/en/agenda/lecture/elements-for-history-of-assyriology/1929-the-discovery-of-ugarit); [Ras Shamra Tablet Inventory](https://ochre.lib.uchicago.edu/); [Kaniewski et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3110627/).

The dating of individual literary compositions is less exact than the dating of the excavated tablets. Most surviving copies belong to the thirteenth century BCE, though oral or written antecedents may be older. Assertions that the myths themselves originated in the third or early second millennium BCE are reconstructions, not manuscript dates.

### Core Ugaritic corpus

#### The Baal Cycle: KTU 1.1–1.6

**Language/support/date:** alphabetic Ugaritic on six clay tablets, written by the named scribe Ilimilku, conventionally thirteenth century BCE. Tablets are divided between the Louvre and Syrian collections.

Šapšu appears in several distinct roles:

1. **KTU 1.2 iii:** she reports to ʿAṯtar that Yamm’s kingship rests on El’s decision and apparently counsels restraint. The broken context makes the exact speech-act debatable.
2. **KTU 1.4 viii:** Baal tells his messengers Gupan and Ugar to accompany Šapšu on her route when taking his message to Mot; her course supplies access to Mot’s underworld realm.
3. **KTU 1.6 i:** she joins Anat’s mourning, helps load Baal’s body onto Anat, and assists its transport to Mount Ṣapānu for burial.
4. **KTU 1.6 iii–iv:** after El dreams that Baal lives, Anat is instructed to have Šapšu search the springs or reaches of the steppe.
5. **KTU 1.6 vi:** she intervenes in the renewed Baal–Mot struggle, warning Mot that El favors Baal; Mot capitulates.
6. **KTU 1.6 vi 45–54:** the Cycle ends with a hymn to Šapšu. Gods and *rpʾum* are “under” her; Kothar-wa-Hasis is associated with her in a philologically difficult conclusion.

The closing lines have produced incompatible translations: Kothar is her “companion,” “magician” and Hasis her “diviner,” or the pilot of a solar vessel. A literal Ugaritic solar barque is therefore **not secure**. [Summary and translation history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shapshu); [Azize, *Phoenician Solar Theology*](https://www.sbl-site.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/12/Gorgias_Azize.pdf).

#### Kirta/Keret: KTU 1.14–1.16

**Date:** thirteenth-century BCE copy, probably by Ilimilku.

Šapšu is invoked in formulaic temporal and cosmic settings, while the Rephaim are central to Kirta’s legitimacy and healing. She is not the protagonist of an independent “Kirta and Shapash” episode. Some attempts to derive a detailed solar theology from every occurrence of *špš* risk confusing the sun as time-marker with the goddess.

#### Aqhat: KTU 1.17–1.19

**Date:** thirteenth-century BCE copy, associated with Ilimilku’s scribal milieu.

The poem’s movements and laments include solar language; the *rpʾum* also belong to its religious background. Šapšu does not have a surviving, sustained intervention comparable to her role in KTU 1.6. Broken tablets make arguments from silence hazardous.

#### The Rephaim texts: KTU 1.20–1.22

Fragmentary literary or ritual compositions concerning *rpʾum*, generally understood as deified royal ancestors, heroic dead, or numinous ancestral figures. Their precise ontology and the character of their feast remain disputed. Šapšu’s relation to them is clearest when these tablets are read beside KTU 1.6 vi and 1.161, but the texts do not authorize a single smooth doctrine of the dead. [Pitard-related discussion](https://www.sbl-site.org/assets/pdfs/pubs/9780884145165_OA.pdf); [Yogev, *The Rephaim* front matter](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004460867/front-4.pdf).

#### KTU 1.23, “The Gracious Gods”

A ritual-myth involving El’s sexual unions and the birth of Šaḥar and Šalim. Šapšu appears in a blessing or cosmic setting, but identifying her with Athirat from poetic parallelism is disputed. Daniel Sarlo has revived a broader proposed equation between Athirat and Šapšu; most presentations retain them as distinct goddesses because Ugaritic texts name and assign them different roles. **[early text; modern disputed reconstruction]**

#### KTU 1.100 = RS 24.244

Often called “Šapšu and the serpents,” a snakebite incantation, myth, or “paramythological” spell. A female figure conventionally called “Mother of the Stallion/Mare” (*ʾum pḥl pḥlt*; both parsing and identity disputed) addresses her mother Šapšu. Šapšu successively carries the appeal to divine residences. The repeated gods fail or provide no effective answer until Ḥoranu acts, uprooting potent plants and neutralizing the venom; a marriage or bridal settlement follows in some readings.

Its genre matters: the narrated precedent was probably operative ritual speech, not entertainment. The fullest early publication was in *Ugaritica V* (1968). [Virolleaud notice](https://www.persee.fr/doc/syria_0039-7946_1969_num_46_3_6097); [recent philological discussion](https://austriaca.at/0xc1aa5572%200x003c1f85.pdf).

#### KTU 1.107

A damaged anti-venom incantation mentioning Šapšu and Ḥoranu. Reconstruction is insecure; it confirms the association but should not be expanded into a second complete myth.

#### KTU 1.82

A damaged protective/incantation text invoking Šapšu with Baal and Anat against or in relation to Tunnan, Resheph, Mot, serpents, and Ḥoranu’s creatures. Syntax and alignments are uncertain.

#### KTU 1.108

A royal/ancestral composition concerning an *rpʾu*, “king of eternity,” and solar-lunar language. Šapšu and Yariḫ occur in cultic relation. Whether the central figure is a deceased king, a divinized royal ancestor, or a god has been debated.

#### KTU 1.161 = RS 34.126

A royal mortuary and accession ritual from Ugarit’s last phase. It invokes the *rpʾum*, the assembly of Didanu, named ancestral figures, and dead kings including Niqmaddu and Ammittamru, then turns to Šapšu. She shines or burns, weeps from above, and directs the summoned dead to descend again to the underworld. Seven numbered offerings and a bird *šlm*-offering follow; peace is pronounced for Ammurapi, his sons, his household, Ugarit, and its gates.

This is the strongest evidence for Šapšu’s institutional relation to royal ancestors. It does not prove a general doctrine that she “ferried every human soul.” [Text and provisional translation](https://renenyffenegger.ch/notes/Geschichte/Quellen/KTU_1_161); [scholarly contextualization](https://doi.org/10.3390/rel16060726?urlappend=%3Futm_source%3Dresearchgate.net%26utm_medium%3Darticle).

### Extra-Ugaritic evidence

- **EA 323:** an Akkadian Amarna letter from Yidya of Ashkelon to the Egyptian king, ca. 1350 BCE. At line 19, ^dUTU takes feminine grammatical reference. This may be a Canaanite female solar deity corresponding to Šapšu, but the personal name is not written. **[probable early parallel]**
- **Phoenician inscriptions:** Iron Age *šmš* occurs as the sun, in divine names, calendrical expressions, and possibly cult titles. Gender varies.
- **KAI 277, Pyrgi gold tablets:** ca. 500 BCE, Phoenician and Etruscan bilingual dedication by Thefarie Velianas to Astarte. It dates the dedication to **zbḥ šmš**, “the month of the sacrifice of the Sun.” This proves a solar festival/month, not that Šmš received the temple, which belongs to Astarte. [DEAPS edition](https://deaps.osu.edu/text_objects/11717).
- Similar month names are reported at Kition around 300 BCE and Larnakas tis Lapithou in the third century BCE.
- **Shipitbaal inscription, Byblos:** *šmš ʿlm*, “eternal sun,” resembles Ugaritic *špš ʿlm*, but “sun” may function as a cosmic witness or image of perpetuity rather than a personal goddess.
- **Eshmunazar II sarcophagus, Sidon, sixth/fifth century BCE:** “life under the sun” is ordinary mortal-world language and does not by itself attest worship.
- **Hebrew Bible:** place-names such as Beth-shemesh, “House/Temple of the Sun,” preserve solar cult geography. 2 Kings 23:11 reports that Josiah removed horses dedicated to the sun and burned its chariots at the Jerusalem temple. Ezekiel 8:16 depicts men worshipping the rising sun. These are hostile witnesses to Israelite/Judahite solar practice, but neither names Šapšu as a goddess. [Beth-Shemesh study](https://brill.com/view/journals/jane/21/1/article-p1_1.xml); [2 Kings research](https://eprints.nottingham.ac.uk/55730/1/Quine%20PhD%20Thesis%202018%20-%20Ritual%2C%20Rhetoric%2C%20and%20the%20Polemic%20Against%20the%20Host%20of%20Heaven.pdf).
- **Pausanias 7.23.7–8, second century CE:** reports a Sidonian who identifies Apollo with the sun and makes him the father of Asclepius conceived as health-giving air. This is late Phoenician philosophical theology, not testimony about Bronze Age Šapšu. [Text](https://www.hellenicaworld.com/Greece/Literature/Pausanias/en/Pausanias7.html).
- **Philo of Byblos via Eusebius:** preserves a Roman-period “Phoenician History” attributed to Sanchuniathon. Its cosmogonic solar material is valuable for late learned Phoenician interpretation but does not securely preserve a narrative of Šapšu.
- **Lucian, *De Dea Syria*:** supplies Roman-period evidence for Syrian cults, especially Hierapolis, but does not document an identifiable cult of the Ugaritic Šapšu.
- **Herodotus and Josephus:** important for Phoenician history and cultic context, but neither preserves a secure Shapash episode. Their inclusion in general Canaanite dossiers must not be mistaken for direct evidence.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and powers

**Securely attested:**

- The physical and divine sun.
- Illumination: “lamp/torch of the gods.”
- Cosmic travel and exceptional geographic access.
- Transmission of messages and requests.
- Access to Mot’s realm and assistance with Baal’s corpse.
- Authority over or supervision of gods and Rephaim expressed in KTU 1.6 vi.
- Direction of ancestral dead in KTU 1.161.
- Mediation in anti-venom ritual.
- Political mediation: she resolves Baal and Mot’s final stalemate by invoking El’s authority.

**Reasonable scholarly reconstructions:**

- **Psychopomp:** well supported functionally, but no Ugaritic word explicitly calls her one.
- **All-seeing witness:** plausible from solar mobility and comparative evidence; less explicitly developed at Ugarit than the judicial office of Mesopotamian Šamaš.
- **Justice:** frequently imported from Šamaš. Šapšu settles a divine political conflict, but Ugaritic evidence does not make a broad legal/judicial portfolio equally explicit.
- **Nightly underworld journey:** strongly inferred from her ability to guide messengers to Mot and control the dead. The texts do not preserve a complete astronomical itinerary.
- **Healing:** she mediates the snakebite appeal, while Ḥoranu performs the cure.

### Powers and limitations

Šapšu can reach places inaccessible to ordinary messengers, carry appeals, help transport divine corpses, search for Baal, and compel Mot through the threat of El’s displeasure. She does not herself defeat Yamm, kill Mot, resurrect Baal in an explicit scene, or cure the bitten figure in KTU 1.100. Her power is connective and authoritative rather than primarily martial.

### Celestial associations

- **Sun:** certain.
- **Day:** certain as physical association.
- **Night and underworld:** probable functional extension.
- **Moon/Yariḫ:** paired in ritual lists as the two great luminaries, not attested as spouses.
- **Horses/chariot:** later Northwest Semitic solar cults used horses and chariots, as 2 Kings 23:11 shows. Applying those objects directly to Ugaritic Šapšu is plausible comparatively but not securely established.
- **Solar barque:** dependent on one disputed translation of KTU 1.6 vi; not a secure attribute.
- No ancient evidence establishes a sacred weekday, number, color, gemstone, incense, or modern astrological correspondence specifically for Šapšu.

### Iconography

No surviving Ugaritic image is securely identified by an inscription as Šapšu. This is the decisive caution.

Proposed representations include:

- Female figures with solar disks.
- An ivory plaque sometimes called the “Ivory of Shapash.”
- Solar emblems on seals and stelae.
- A seventh-century BCE Phoenician bowl from the Bernardini tomb at Palestrina with a female solar figure.

These identifications rest on attributes and comparison, not accompanying labels. Egyptianizing divine hairstyles or disks may denote Hathor, a solarized local goddess, royal imagery, or a generic divine figure. The celebrated Baal stele and the gilded “Baal” figurine from Minet el-Beida are not images of Šapšu.

The absence of a securely inscribed portrait means no canonical body type, garment, weapon, animal, or hand gesture can be assigned to her.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No surviving Ugaritic passage securely states Šapšu’s parents, spouse, or children in a simple genealogical formula.

- Calling her “daughter of El and Athirat/Asherah” is a widespread **modern harmonization**, usually inferred from the generic class “sons/daughters of El” and the divine council. It is not a securely preserved Šapšu genealogy.
- She has no securely attested consort.
- Yariḫ is not securely her husband.
- In KTU 1.100, *ʾum pḥl pḥlt* calls Šapšu “mother.” Whether this marks literal divine maternity, ritual address, or a relational title is disputed.
- Sarlo’s proposed equation of Athirat and Šapšu would radically change her genealogy, but it is a modern minority hypothesis, not consensus.
- Šapšu’s authority is high—Mot fears the consequences she announces—but she operates within El’s kingship and supports Baal’s sanctioned rule.

### Every documented divine interaction

The following list separates actual scenes from mere coexistence in pantheon lists.

- **El/ʾIlu —** Šapšu communicates or enforces El’s decision concerning Yamm, ʿAṯtar, and later Baal; El’s command initiates her search for Baal. **Source:** KTU 1.2 iii; 1.6 iii–vi, thirteenth century BCE. **[early]**
- **Baal/Haddu —** provides the route by which his messengers reach Mot; helps recover and transport his corpse; searches for him after El’s dream; secures recognition of his kingship from Mot. **Source:** KTU 1.4 viii; 1.6 i, iii–vi. **[early]**
- **Anat —** joins her mourning and helps her lift/carry Baal’s body; receives or executes the command that Šapšu search for Baal. **Source:** KTU 1.6 i, iii–iv. **[early]**
- **Mot —** her route reaches his realm; she ultimately warns him that El supports Baal, causing him to concede. **Source:** KTU 1.4 viii; 1.6 vi. **[early]**
- **Yamm —** reports or mediates the fact of his appointment by El; no direct combat is preserved. **Source:** KTU 1.2 iii. **[early; broken context]**
- **ʿAṯtar —** informs or warns him regarding Yamm’s elevation and discourages opposition. **Source:** KTU 1.2 iii. **[early; interpretation disputed]**
- **Gupan and Ugar —** Baal directs his two messengers to accompany Šapšu on the road to Mot. **Source:** KTU 1.4 viii. **[early]**
- **Kothar-wa-Hasis —** appears in the closing hymn as Šapšu’s companion, magician/diviner, or pilot, depending on translation. **Source:** KTU 1.6 vi 49–54. **[early/disputed]**
- **The Rephaim (*rpʾum*) —** are “under” her in the hymn; in royal mortuary ritual she orders ancestral figures downward. **Source:** KTU 1.6 vi; 1.161. **[early]**
- **Yariḫ —** paired with Šapšu in ritual/luminary contexts, including KTU 1.43 and 1.108; no secure marriage or narrative encounter. **[early; joint cultic appearance only]**
- **Mother of the Stallion/Mare (*ʾum pḥl pḥlt*) —** addresses Šapšu as mother and asks her to solicit divine aid against venom. **Source:** KTU 1.100. **[early; figure’s identity disputed]**
- **Ḥoranu/Horon —** receives the relayed appeal and succeeds in overcoming venom; Šapšu mediates the contact. **Source:** KTU 1.100; compare damaged 1.107. **[early]**
- **El, Baal, Dagan, Anat, Athtart, Yariḫ, Resheph, Athirat, Kothar-wa-Hasis, and other locally seated gods —** Šapšu carries the snakebite petitioner’s repeated plea to them in sequence. **Source:** KTU 1.100. **[early; some names/order depend on restoration]**
- **Tunnan —** named within the hostile complex of KTU 1.82, where Šapšu is among protective powers; the exact directness of their interaction is unclear. **[early/uncertain]**
- **Resheph —** co-occurs with Šapšu in KTU 1.82’s protective/hostile structure and receives an appeal in KTU 1.100; no independent narrative encounter survives. **[early]**
- **Athirat —** receives an appeal through Šapšu in KTU 1.100; poetic parallelism elsewhere has prompted a disputed modern proposal that they are two manifestations of one goddess. **[early interaction; modern reconstruction of identity]**
- **Šaḥar and Šalim —** Šapšu appears in the cosmic blessing or environment of the newly born dawn-and-dusk gods in KTU 1.23; claims that she adopts them or is their mother exceed the secure text. **[early/fragmentary]**
- **Astarte/Athtart —** receives the relayed appeal in KTU 1.100. KAI 277 juxtaposes Astarte’s temple dedication with the month of the Sun’s sacrifice, but that is calendrical association, not proof of a mythic relationship. **[early; later cultic juxtaposition]**
- **Apollo —** a Sidonian identifies Apollo as the sun in Pausanias. This is Greco-Phoenician interpretation, not a Ugaritic meeting. **[late syncretism]**
- **Asclepius/Eshmun-related solar theology —** Pausanias’ Sidonian calls solar Apollo the sole father of Asclepius-as-air. Any connection to Bronze Age Šapšu is remote and historical, not genealogical continuity. **[late learned systematization]**

No documented interaction with Tanit, Baal Hammon, Melqart, Dido, Yahweh, or biblical Samson can securely be assigned to Šapšu.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The death and restoration of Baal

**Earliest source:** KTU 1.4–1.6, thirteenth century BCE. **[early]**

Baal summons Mot to his feast and subsequently descends toward Mot’s domain. The damaged narrative makes the exact mechanics of Baal’s death difficult: Mot claims to have consumed him. Anat searches for Baal; Šapšu accompanies her to the body, mourns, and helps load it onto Anat. Anat bears him to Ṣapānu, buries him, and performs massive animal funerary sacrifices.

El dreams of rain and fertility and concludes Baal lives. Šapšu is sent to search. Anat violently destroys Mot, though he later returns; Baal and Mot struggle inconclusively. Šapšu then changes the outcome by warning Mot that El supports Baal and may overturn Mot’s throne. Mot yields. The cycle concludes in praise of Šapšu.

Important disputed points:

- Šapšu does **not** clearly “find Baal’s body on El’s orders” in the first corpse scene; Anat finds or reaches it, while Šapšu helps recover it. Later El orders a search after dreaming Baal lives.
- A heifer/calf substitution theory holds that Baal’s apparent corpse may be a surrogate. This is an ingenious reconstruction, not a transparent statement of the text.
- The myth may encode seasonal drought and rainfall, royal ideology, or both. Reducing every event to an annual vegetation allegory is characteristic of older myth-and-ritual scholarship and is no longer sufficient.
- Šapšu’s underworld function is clear in structure, but the text does not narrate a complete daily death and rebirth of the sun.

### Šapšu and the serpent cure

**Source:** KTU 1.100, thirteenth century BCE. **[early]**

A female equine-associated petitioner appeals to “mother” Šapšu after venomous attack. Šapšu takes the appeal from one divine residence to another. The geography creates a verbal map of the pantheon: El at the rivers’ source, Baal on Ṣapānu, Dagan at Tuttul, and other gods at their proper seats. Repetition heightens ritual force. Ḥoranu finally goes eastward, gathers or uproots powerful trees, and binds or expels the venom. A marriage/bridal sequence links Ḥoranu and the petitioner in some translations.

Variants are philological rather than manuscript-rich: scholars disagree over the petitioner’s name or title, whether her offspring or she herself was bitten, whether the composition is myth or incantation, and what Ḥoranu’s final marriage means. The safe conclusion is that Šapšu is the divine communications network; Ḥoranu is the effective venom-master.

### Šapšu and the royal Rephaim

**Sources:** KTU 1.20–1.22, 1.108, 1.161, and the closing hymn of 1.6. **[early]**

The *rpʾum* arrive for a feast or ritual encounter, travel in chariots, and are associated with kingship, fertility, healing, and the ancestral dead. KTU 1.161 invokes named royal dead and the assembly of Didanu. Šapšu shines or burns, weeps, and commands the invoked beings to descend after their ritual presence.

The evidence supports a recurring link between Šapšu and royal dead. It does not resolve whether every *rpʾu* is a deceased king, whether the Rephaim were originally living warrior elites, or whether “Rephaim” always meant the same class. Biblical *rephaim* can mean ancient giants or powerless shades; that later semantic range should not be projected mechanically into Ugarit. [Recent survey](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/16/6/726).

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Ugarit

Šapšu appears in offering and ritual texts, demonstrating cult beyond literature. Relevant evidence includes:

- Royal mortuary observance, especially KTU 1.161.
- Royal accession and dynastic blessing.
- Snakebite and protective incantations.
- Offering lists in which Šapšu is paired with Yariḫ or receives food/animal offerings.
- Theophoric personal names, though her onomastic footprint is smaller and less straightforward than those of Baal, El, Resheph, or Dagan.

No independently identified “Temple of Šapšu” has been excavated at Ugarit. The city’s monumental temples conventionally assigned to Baal and Dagan cannot be reassigned to her without evidence. Cult may have operated through palace, household, funerary, and shared sanctuary settings.

KTU 1.161 shows an officiated rite with invocations, lament, descent commands, sevenfold offering language, and a concluding peace formula for king, dynasty, city, and gates. It ties ancestor ritual directly to state welfare.

KTU 1.100 and 1.82 show applied ritual expertise—spoken incantation rather than necessarily a public temple festival.

### Phoenician solar cults

The month-name **zbḥ šmš**, “Sacrifice of the Sun,” is the most concrete evidence for a recurring Phoenician solar observance. Its attestation at Pyrgi, Kition, and Cyprus indicates wide calendrical circulation. It does not tell us the victim, officiants, theology, or the deity’s gender at each location.

Beth-Shemesh and related place-names—ʿIr-Shemesh, En-Shemesh—suggest old solar sanctuaries in inland Canaan. A Beth-Shemesh may already occur in Egyptian execration material of the nineteenth/eighteenth century BCE, earlier than the secure personal attestations of Ugaritic Šapšu. A solar cult is therefore ancient, but the deity’s exact name and gender remain unstated.

### Human sacrifice and the Carthage tophet

There is **no evidence that human or child sacrifice was offered to Šapšu**.

The Carthage tophet contained cremated remains of infants, young children, and animals, with votive inscriptions overwhelmingly directed to **Baal Hammon and Tanit**. Debate continues over whether it was chiefly a sacrificial sanctuary, a special cemetery for children who died naturally, or an institution combining both. Osteological studies have reached competing conclusions; inscriptions are votive rather than ordinary funerary texts, and classical accusations are potentially hostile but cannot simply be dismissed. [Schwartz et al.](https://unvis.it/pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2822869); [Kelsey Museum discussion](https://lsa.umich.edu/content/dam/kelsey-assets/kelsey-publications/pdfs/in-the-field.pdf).

Including the tophet in Šapšu’s cult would be guilt by pantheon association. No tophet dedication located in this research names her as recipient.

### Priesthood and economy

No securely identifiable office equivalent to “high priestess of Šapšu” is known from the cited corpus. Ritual specialists, palace scribes, lamenters, and royal cult personnel transmitted her rites, but assigning each text to a distinct solar priesthood would be conjectural.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before ca. 1400 BCE: antecedents

West Semitic solar worship is older than the Ugaritic corpus, as solar place-names and possibly Amorite personal names indicate. Old Akkadian names such as *Umma-Šamaš*, “Šamaš is mother,” have been cited as vestiges of a female Semitic sun, but their relevance to Šapšu is debated. **[reconstruction]**

### Ca. 1350 BCE: southern Canaanite parallel

EA 323’s feminine ^dUTU suggests that a female solar deity was not confined to Ugarit. Because the name is logographic, it cannot prove that Ashkelon pronounced it Šapšu.

### Thirteenth century BCE: Ugaritic florescence

This is the only period in which Šapšu’s personality is extensively documented. Narrative, ritual, magical, and mortuary texts present a coherent cluster of functions: solar luminary, traveler, intermediary, helper of Anat and Baal, controller of ancestral movement, and mediator before dangerous gods.

### Ca. 1190 BCE: destruction of Ugarit

Ugarit’s destruction ended the palace-and-scribal system that copied these myths. There is no evidence of continuous local transmission of the Baal Cycle after this point.

### Iron Age Phoenicia, ca. 1000–300 BCE

The cognate deity is called Šmš, not Špš. Evidence becomes scattered:

- Solar personal and place-names.
- “Eternal sun” language.
- A month/festival of the Sun.
- Both feminine and masculine solar representation.
- Possible female solar imagery at Palestrina and in Phoenician ivory.
- Male solar theology at Baalbek and elsewhere.

This is transformation, not straightforward survival. Mesopotamian Šamaš, Egyptian solar forms, Greek Helios/Apollo, and local Baal traditions offered several models for re-gendering and syncretism.

### Judah and Israel, eighth–seventh centuries BCE

Biblical polemic records solar worship at Jerusalem and other sites. Josiah’s reform narrative suppresses horses and chariots of the sun. Whether these rites descended from Canaanite goddess worship or reflected Assyrian astral influence is disputed; both local continuity and imperial stimulus may have mattered.

### Hellenistic and Roman periods

Phoenician intellectuals could identify the sun with Apollo or Helios. Pausanias’ Sidonian offers a natural-philosophical theology in which sun/Apollo generates health-giving air/Asclepius. Julian’s fourth-century CE *Hymn to King Helios* claims access to Phoenician solar doctrine, but its Neoplatonic monism is separated from Ugarit by more than a millennium.

### Christianization and Islamization

There is no documented decree suppressing a named cult of Šapšu. Solar and local temples in Syria-Phoenicia were variously abandoned, transformed, or closed during late antiquity, but attaching a specific destruction episode to her would be invention. Likewise, there is no demonstrated medieval folk cult preserving her personal name.

### 1928–present: rediscovery

The modern Shapash is fundamentally a product of deciphered Ugaritic tablets. Excavation began in 1929; rapid decipherment in 1930–31 restored a goddess absent from continuous literary memory. Modern editions—CTA, KTU, and critical translations—progressively clarified and revised her dossier.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

Šapšu had little or no distinct Renaissance or early-modern occult reception because her Ugaritic name and texts had not yet been recovered. She is absent from the traditional Solomonic grimoires, Hermetic planetary hierarchies, Golden Dawn grade system, and standard Renaissance demonologies. Claims that medieval magicians invoked “Shapash” are **modern inventions unless supported by a specific manuscript**.

Modern reception falls into three forms:

1. **Scholarship-derived mythography:** popular books and websites retell her as an all-seeing sun goddess, divine judge, psychopomp, and guardian of the dead. “Judge” often derives more from Šamaš than from Ugaritic evidence.
2. **Contemporary Semitic/Canaanite reconstructionism:** individual practitioners and small online communities worship her using translated Ugaritic hymns, reconstructed offerings, and solar observances. No reliable census establishes the number of worshippers or a continuous institution.
3. **Eclectic goddess spirituality:** modern groups sometimes celebrate “Shemesh/Shapash” as a solar goddess. A documented example is a 2022 ritual advertised by Daughters of the Goddess. Its ritual form is contemporary, not a preserved Canaanite liturgy. [Modern ritual notice](https://daughtersofthegoddess.com/2022-shemesh/).

Modern correspondences involving Sunday, gold, amber, citrine, sunflowers, tarot cards, chakras, zodiacal rulerships, or fixed ceremonial colors are **modern inventions** unless explicitly presented as new devotional symbolism. They do not occur in Ugaritic sources.

No major nationalist or extremist appropriation specific to Šapšu was established. Broader invocations of “Canaanite ancestry” can enter modern identity politics, but attributing those movements to this goddess without direct documentation would be misleading.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Editions and translations

Essential modern tools include:

- Manfried Dietrich, Oswald Loretz, and Joaquín Sanmartín, *Die keilalphabetischen Texte aus Ugarit* (KTU), the standard text-number system.
- Mark S. Smith and Wayne T. Pitard, *The Ugaritic Baal Cycle*, critical edition and commentary.
- Simon B. Parker, ed., *Ugaritic Narrative Poetry*.
- Nicolas Wyatt, *Religious Texts from Ugarit*.
- Michael Coogan and Mark S. Smith, *Stories from Ancient Canaan*.
- Dennis Pardee’s editions of ritual texts.
- Gregorio del Olmo Lete and Joaquín Sanmartín, *A Dictionary of the Ugaritic Language*.
- Steve A. Wiggins, “Shapsh, Lamp of the Gods” (1996), the principal deity-focused study.
- Ola Wikander, *Drought, Death and the Sun in Ugarit and Ancient Israel*.
- Joseph Azize, *The Phoenician Solar Theology*.

### Major disputes

**1. Correct vocalization.**  
*Šapšu* is philologically preferable; *Shapash* is a conventional English form influenced by Akkadian.

**2. How powerful was she?**  
Older summaries sometimes call her “minor” because she lacks a known temple and is not the storm god or pantheon head. Her decisive narrative and mortuary roles argue against equating lack of monumental cult with insignificance.

**3. Psychopomp or merely solar traveler?**  
KTU 1.4, 1.6, and 1.161 support access to the dead and regulation of their movement. “Psychopomp” is useful but remains an analytic label.

**4. Did she resurrect Baal?**  
No surviving line explicitly says she raises him. She assists burial, later searches for him, and resolves his conflict with Mot. Direct resurrection is an overstatement.

**5. Did she travel in a solar boat?**  
Only one disputed rendering of KTU 1.6’s final hymn yields Kothar as helmsman. Other translations make him her magician or companion.

**6. Šapšu and Athirat.**  
Similar titles, travel motifs, horses, and cosmic-order functions have prompted an equation. Against it stand texts that name them separately and assign distinct cultic roles. The equation is a minority modern proposal.

**7. The Rephaim.**  
They have been read as deceased kings, heroic warrior ancestors, healing spirits, living nobles, or a category that changed between texts. KTU 1.161 makes some explicitly ancestral and dead; that need not govern every occurrence.

**8. Gender continuity into Phoenician religion.**  
Female solar representations exist, but Iron Age Šmš can be male. There was no single unbroken, uniformly female “Phoenician Shapash.”

**9. Solar cult in Israel.**  
Beth-Shemesh and biblical polemics show solar traditions. Whether Jerusalem’s solar chariots derive from local Canaanite practice, Assyrian influence, or solarized Yahwism remains debated.

**10. Iconographic identification.**  
A solar disk does not automatically identify Šapšu. No securely inscribed portrait survives.

**11. Human sacrifice.**  
The Punic tophet debate concerns Baal Hammon and Tanit, not Šapšu. Associating her with child sacrifice is unsupported.

### State of the evidence

The Ugaritic corpus survives on excavated clay tablets, many broken or abraded. Prewar finds were divided chiefly between Syrian museums and the Louvre. Later discoveries remain in Syrian collections. Modern study depends on photographs, hand copies, joins, and successive editions; proposed joins can materially change a reading. The Ras Shamra Tablet Inventory and CDLI increasingly supply digital metadata. [Louvre collection history](https://www.louvre.fr/recherche-et-conservation/departement-des-antiquites-orientales/l-histoire-des-collections-2); [CDLI Ugarit record](https://cdli.earth/P520406).

Discredited or weak popular claims include:

- a complete ancient biography with fixed parents, husband, and children;
- a securely identified Ugaritic statue;
- universal status as goddess of “truth and justice” identical to Šamaš;
- continuous secret worship from antiquity to modern paganism;
- a direct equation with Egyptian Hathor, Greek Helios, Apollo, or Japanese Amaterasu;
- responsibility for Carthaginian child sacrifice;
- specific tarot, chakra, crystal, or zodiacal correspondences presented as ancient.

## 11. Sources and assessment

The dossier’s firm center is the Ugaritic corpus. The Baal Cycle establishes Šapšu’s narrative agency; KTU 1.100 establishes her ritual mediation; KTU 1.161 and the Rephaim material establish her mortuary role. EA 323 supplies a probable regional parallel. Phoenician inscriptions document a later solar cult but not a stable, unchanged goddess. Biblical material is valuable hostile testimony to solar worship yet never names Ugaritic Šapšu. Classical authors document later interpretations, not Bronze Age biography.

The most important negative findings are equally substantive:

- no secure genealogy;
- no secure consort;
- no named temple;
- no inscribed portrait;
- no evidence of human sacrifice to her;
- no continuous medieval cult;
- no authentic ancient occult correspondences;
- no secure evidence that Phoenician and Punic Šmš everywhere remained female;
- no basis for identifying every solar deity of Syria-Palestine as Shapash.
