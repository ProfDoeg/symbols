# Melqart: Research Dossier

**Class:** God; specifically the tutelary and royal god of Tyre, subsequently a major Phoenician and Punic civic, dynastic, maritime, colonial, and protective deity. Greek and Roman writers normally classified him as a very ancient **Heracles/Hercules**, distinct in origin from the Greek hero son of Amphitryon.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The damaged Old Aramaic **Bir-Hadad/Bar-Hadad statue inscription, KAI 201**, found at Bureij about 7 km north of Aleppo and usually dated between the later ninth and early eighth centuries BCE. Its legible text says that Bir-Hadad erected the statue “for his lord, for Melqart” because the god heard his voice. The dedicant’s identity and patronymic remain disputed; a recent re-reading identifies him as a ruler of Arpad rather than a king of Damascus. The name does **not** occur in the second-millennium BCE Ugaritic mythological corpus, and Melqart cannot simply be inserted into the Baal Cycle, *Kirta*, or *Aqhat* as an otherwise unattested character. [Hackett and Wilson-Wright, “A Revised Interpretation of the Melqart Stele”](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/Publications/SAOC/saoc73.pdf)

## Evidence labels used

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** contemporary inscriptions, artifacts, or relatively early testimony.
- **[LE] Later literary elaboration:** Greek, Roman, Jewish, or Christian literary testimony substantially later than the events or cult described.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** evidence limited to a particular city or region.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian, philosophical, priestly, or scholarly ordering of disparate traditions.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** a historically attested equation or fusion with another god.
- **[ML] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** plausible or disputed conclusions drawn from incomplete evidence.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unverified modern claim.**

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

**[EA]** Melqart was the divine “king” or sovereign of Tyre, closely bound to the city, its monarchy, its overseas networks, and the reproduction of Tyrian political identity. His prominence spread with Tyrian settlement and commerce to Cyprus, North Africa, Sicily, Sardinia, Malta, Ibiza, and southern Iberia. In Tyre he was not merely “a god of trade” or “a sea god”: those descriptions isolate consequences of his civic and colonial position. He protected the city, legitimated kingship, received vows and sacrifices, and became a divine model of foundation and expansion.

**[MSR]** Corinne Bonnet’s *Melqart: Cultes et mythes de l’Héraclès tyrien en Méditerranée* (1988) remains the foundational synthetic study. Its central methodological achievement was to treat “Melqart” not as a fixed nature-god but as a deity constituted through Tyre’s political and Mediterranean networks. Bonnet nevertheless reconstructed some features—especially death, burning, resurrection, sacred marriage, and the Tyrian character of western evidence—more confidently than some later scholars accept. The surviving dossier is too fragmentary to produce a continuous Phoenician “biography.” [Bonnet bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Melqart_Cults_et_mythes_de_l_H%C3%A9racl%C3%A8s.html?id=24aU0AEACAAJ)

### Name and writing

**[EA]** Phoenician: **𐤌𐤋𐤒𐤓𐤕**, consonantal **MLQRT**, conventionally vocalized *Melqart*, *Milqart*, or *Milkqart*. Greek spellings and translations include **Μελκάρθ**, **Μελκάρτης**, and, much more commonly, **Ἡρακλῆς** when the equivalence was assumed. Latin sources use **Hercules**, particularly *Hercules Tyrius* and *Hercules Gaditanus*. Akkadian renderings are conventionally cited as *Milqartu*, but direct cuneiform attestations are sparse.

**[EA/MSR]** The standard etymology divides the name as West Semitic *milk/malk* or *melek*, “king,” plus *qart*, “city”: **“King of the City.”** “The City” is normally Tyre. The form may be a title that became a proper divine name, although no surviving document records that transition.

The following distinction is essential:

- *mlk* in Melqart’s name is the Semitic root “king.”
- Punic **mlk/molk** in tophet inscriptions is a term for a class of offering or sacrificial rite.
- Biblical **Molech/Moloch**, Punic *molk*, Ugaritic *Mlk*, Ammonite Milkom, and Melqart are not interchangeable merely because they share the consonants MLK.

Modern occult sites that list “Moloch” and Melqart as names of one being are performing a modern conflation, not reproducing a demonstrated ancient identity.

### Secure titles and equivalents

- **𐤁𐤏𐤋 𐤑𐤓, *Baʿal Ṣūr*, “Lord of Tyre”** — **[EA/MSR]** a title or conceptual equivalent inferred from Phoenician usage and later descriptions. Whether every occurrence of “Baal of Tyre” means Melqart must be decided contextually.
- **“Our lord Melqart, Lord/Master of Tyre”** — **[EA]**, Phoenician on the bilingual Malta cippi, KAI 47, second century BCE.
- **Ἡρακλῆς ἀρχηγέτης, “Heracles the Founder/Leader”** — **[EA/SI]**, the Greek half of KAI 47. It translates or interprets Phoenician Melqart, “Lord of Tyre,” rather than mechanically reproducing the words. The title emphasizes foundation and colonial leadership. The Louvre dates its cippus to approximately 120 BCE. [Louvre, Malta Cippus AO 4818](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark:/53355/cl010165603)
- **Tyrian Heracles** — **[SI]**, securely present in Herodotus in the fifth century BCE.
- **Hercules Gaditanus** — **[RV/SI]**, the Roman form of the god of Gadir/Gades, although Roman literature increasingly assimilated the sanctuary’s traditions to the canonical Greek Heracles.
- **Eshmun-Melqart** — **[RV/SI]**, a compound divine name in Cyprus, especially the Kition region; it indicates a locally meaningful fusion or coordinated cult, not proof that Eshmun and Melqart were everywhere identical.
- **Resheph-Melqart** — **[RV/SI]**, attested in the fifth-century BCE Ebusus/Ibiza inscription KAI 72.
- **Heracles-Melqart** — modern scholarly hyphenation, useful for an ancient equivalence but not itself an ancient universal name.

### Identifications that require qualification

**Heracles/Hercules. [SI]** Herodotus 2.44, writing in the fifth century BCE, visited Tyre and called the city-god “Heracles.” He explicitly distinguished this ancient divine Heracles from the younger Greek hero, son of Amphitryon. The bilingual Malta cippi later make the equivalence epigraphically explicit: Phoenician “Melqart, Lord of Tyre” corresponds to Greek “Heracles the Founder.” This is therefore not a modern comparison.

**The Greek heroic biography is not automatically Melqart’s mythology.** Once the equation operated, Greek and Roman authors could place the travels, labors, lion skin, and club of Heracles in Phoenician and Punic landscapes. Unless a Phoenician inscription, local image, or clearly Tyrian/Gaditanian source confirms a particular episode, it cannot safely be assigned to a pre-Hellenic Melqart.

**Baal. [MSR/disputed]** Melqart was a *baʿal*, “lord,” but “Baal” is a title borne by many gods. The storm-god Baal/Hadad of Ugarit is not textually identified as Melqart. Older scholarship sometimes treated Melqart as a development of Baal-Hadad; other theories made him a solar, chthonic, marine, vegetation, or dying-and-rising god. These are attempts to recover a “nature” behind civic titles from scattered late evidence. None is a complete ancient definition.

**Biblical Baal under Ahab and Jezebel. [MSR/disputed]** Because Jezebel belonged to the Tyrian royal house, many scholars have identified the Baal promoted in 1 Kings 16–18 with Melqart. John Day and others have argued instead for Baal Shamem/Hadad, especially because the Carmel contest concerns storm, rain, and lightning-like fire. The Hebrew text says only *hab-Baʿal*, “the Baal”; it never writes “Melqart.” The identification is consequently plausible but unproved.

**Sun, underworld, and vegetation god. [MSR]** Solar and chthonic readings draw on late identifications, coin imagery, fire rites, seasonal awakening, and the idea of a tomb or pyre. No early Phoenician creed calls him “god of the sun,” “lord of the underworld,” or “vegetation god.” Those labels should not replace the securely attested civic and royal identity.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The Bir-Hadad/Bar-Hadad statue, KAI 201

**[EA]** The monument is a roughly one-meter basalt statue or stela with a relief of a standing male figure and five lines of Old Aramaic. It was found reused among Roman remains at Bureij north of Aleppo. The text’s stable portion reads approximately:

> “The statue which Bir-Hadad … king of Aram, set up for his lord, for Melqart, which he vowed to him because Melqart heard his voice.”

The inscription proves that by the ninth or early eighth century BCE:

1. Melqart was known beyond Tyre in northern Syria.
2. A ruler could call him “his lord.”
3. He received vowed monuments after answering prayer.
4. A human-shaped divine image could represent him.

It does **not** establish Melqart’s parents, consort, death, resurrection, solar nature, or a connection with Heracles.

The difficult patronymic generated several identifications:

- **Older reconstruction:** Bir-Hadad son of Ṭab-Rimmon son of Hezion, matching 1 Kings 15:18.
- **Alternative:** a son or successor of Hadadezer of Damascus.
- **Another proposal:** Bar-Hadad son of Hazael.
- **Recent re-reading:** Bir-Hadad son of Attar-šumki, ruler of Arpad from the house of Gūš, placing the dedication in the eighth-century politics of northern Syria.

The monument is thus secure; the royal biography attached to it is not. [Hackett and Wilson-Wright](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/Publications/SAOC/saoc73.pdf)

### Ugarit, Amarna, and the problem of prehistory

**[EA—negative finding]** Melqart is absent by name from the Late Bronze Age texts of Ugarit, including the Baal Cycle, *Kirta*, and *Aqhat*. Those works illuminate the older Northwest Semitic religious environment—El, Athirat, Baal/Hadad, Anat, Mot, Yam, Shapash, Kothar-wa-Hasis—but do not document Melqart.

**[EA—negative finding]** The fourteenth-century BCE Amarna letters extensively document the cities, rulers, and politics of the Levant, including Tyre, but do not securely name Melqart. The letters cannot therefore be used as direct evidence for his cult.

**[MSR]** Similarities between Melqart’s later awakening and the Baal Cycle’s conflict with Mot have encouraged claims that Melqart inherited a Baal death-and-return pattern. The chronological and textual gap prevents demonstration. Mark Smith’s work on “dying and rising gods” has been especially important in warning against turning scattered rites into a single ancient myth.

### Phoenician and Punic inscriptions

The primary epigraphic corpus is dispersed through *Corpus Inscriptionum Semiticarum*, *Répertoire d’épigraphie sémitique*, and especially Donner and Röllig’s *Kanaanäische und aramäische Inschriften* (KAI).

Important items include:

- **KAI 201, Bureij:** **[EA]**, later ninth–early eighth century BCE; royal Aramaic vow to Melqart.
- **Kition inscriptions:** **[EA/RV]**, first millennium BCE; Melqart belonged to the royal and sanctuary landscape of Phoenician Kition. Some Heraclean-looking sculpture is associated archaeologically with this setting, but inscriptions do not identify every lion-skinned figure.
- **KAI 72, Ebusus/Ibiza:** **[EA/RV/SI]**, fifth century BCE; a temple dedication involving **Resheph-Melqart**, then Tanit and Gad.
- **Tharros inscriptions:** **[EA/RV]**, Punic Sardinia, principally fourth–second centuries BCE. An official title commonly interpreted as “awakener” or “resuscitator of the god” has been connected with Melqart’s *egersis*, but the divine referent and precise ritual duties are reconstructed.
- **KAI 47, Malta cippi:** **[EA/SI]**, second century BCE. Two Tyrian brothers, ʿAbd-Osir and Osir-šamar, dedicate to “our lord Melqart, Lord of Tyre, because he heard their voice; may he bless them.” The Greek text identifies the recipient as “Heracles the Founder.” These monuments later became crucial to the modern decipherment of Phoenician.
- **Theophoric names:** **[EA]** Names such as ʿAbd-Melqart (“servant of Melqart”), Bod-Melqart (“in the hand of Melqart”), Ger-Melqart, and Hamilcar—usually analyzed as a Melqart-bearing name—show devotion but do not by themselves disclose ritual or myth.

### Hebrew Bible and Josephus

**1 Kings 16–18; 2 Kings 10. [LE/hostile witness]** These passages condemn the royal cult of “Baal” associated with Jezebel, daughter of Ethbaal. Jehu later destroys the Baal temple and kills its personnel. The narrative is invaluable evidence for Israelite polemic against a royal Baal cult but never calls that Baal Melqart. Identification with Melqart remains disputed.

**Ezekiel 26–28. [LE/hostile witness]** The oracles against Tyre describe its ruler’s wealth, maritime reach, arrogance, and imagined divine status. They illuminate hostile Judean representations of Tyrian sacral kingship; they do not name Melqart or establish that the king impersonated him.

**Josephus, *Against Apion* 1.117–119. [LE]** Josephus, writing in the late first century CE, quotes or paraphrases the lost Hellenistic historian Menander of Ephesus, who claimed to translate Tyrian annals. Hiram I was said to have demolished old shrines, built new temples to Heracles and Astarte, and first celebrated—or restored—the “awakening” of Heracles in the month Peritios. This is the principal literary evidence for the royal organization of Melqart’s *egersis*. The underlying Tyrian archival source is lost, so neither its wording nor the precise date of the institution can be independently checked.

### Greek and Roman corpus

**Herodotus 2.44, ca. 430 BCE. [EA/SI]** Herodotus says he visited the Tyrian temple of Heracles. He reports rich votives and two pillars, one of refined gold and another of “emerald” or green stone that shone at night. Tyrian priests told him temple and city had been founded together 2,300 years earlier. Herodotus also visited another Tyrian sanctuary of “Thasian Heracles” and a Phoenician Heracles sanctuary on Thasos. The visit is early evidence; the 2,300-year date is a fifth-century priestly claim, not an archaeologically verified foundation date. [Herodotus 2.44](https://lexundria.com/hdt/2.44/mcly)

**Eudoxus of Cnidus, via Athenaeus 9.392d, fourth century BCE source preserved in a third-century CE work. [LE/RV]** Eudoxus said Phoenicians sacrificed quails to Heracles because Typhon killed Heracles, son of Zeus and Asteria, on his way to Libya; Iolaus revived him by placing a quail under his nose. This is the clearest surviving narrative attached to the awakening, but it survives only through a much later excerpt and is already expressed in Greek genealogical language.

**Diodorus Siculus 17.40–46, first century BCE. [LE/SI]** Alexander the Great asked to sacrifice to the Tyrian Heracles in 332 BCE. Tyre’s refusal and Alexander’s insistence became part of the diplomatic prelude to the siege. Diodorus also says the Tyrians bound Apollo’s statue with golden cords to prevent his desertion, producing a scene in which the city’s gods were enlisted in wartime political imagination. [Diodorus, Book 17](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Diodorus_Siculus/17C%2A.html)

**Strabo 3.5.3, late first century BCE–early first century CE. [LE/RV]** Strabo describes Gadeira and its famous Heracles sanctuary, transmitting foundation traditions according to which Tyrians established the shrine after oracular directions. The sanctuary was separated from the urban settlement and oriented toward the strait.

**Polybius 7.9, preserving the Hannibal–Philip treaty of 215 BCE. [EA through Greek transmission/SI]** The oath invokes “the Genius of Carthage, Heracles, and Iolaus” after Zeus, Hera, and Apollo. The Greek is commonly understood as translating a Punic divine list; Heracles is highly probably Melqart. This makes him an oath witness of the Carthaginian state and army, but not the first or sole god invoked. [Polybius 7.9](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Polybius/7%2A.html)

**Silius Italicus, *Punica* 3.14–44, late first century CE. [LE/RV]** Silius offers the fullest literary description of the Gaditanian sanctuary: an ancient cult, perpetual fire, priests in linen, prohibitions on women and pigs, no anthropomorphic cult image, and bronze reliefs of Heracles’ labors on the doors. Some details may reflect Roman-period Gades; others belong to literary antiquarianism. They cannot all be projected into the Phoenician foundation era.

**Pseudo-Lucian, *De Dea Syria* 3, second century CE. [LE]** The author claims personal knowledge of the Tyrian sanctuary and emphasizes that its Heracles was not the Greek epic hero but a much older god and Tyre’s patron. [*De Dea Syria* 3](https://www.oocities.org/soho/Lofts/2938/deasyria1.html)

**Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 40, fifth century CE. [Late learned elaboration]** Nonnus supplies a luxuriant Tyrian foundation mythology: primeval floating rocks, an eagle, fire, an olive tree, and divine instructions leading to the stabilization and foundation of Tyre. He also multiplies equations between the Tyrian Heracles and numerous gods. The poem preserves late antique Tyrian lore but is not a transparent record of Bronze or Iron Age belief.

**Pseudo-Clementine *Recognitions* 10.24, fourth century CE. [Christian polemical elaboration]** The work lists the Tyrian tomb of Heracles, “where he was burned with fire,” among mortal gods’ tombs. It is evidence for a late tradition about death, burning, and burial, not proof that Melqart began as a deified human king.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Functions

**City and kingship. [EA]** Melqart’s name and titles make his primary sphere civic sovereignty. He embodied Tyre’s city and monarchy without simply being identical to a reigning king.

**Foundation and colonization. [EA/SI]** KAI 47’s Greek *archēgetēs* presents him as founder or leader. His temples in Tyrian overseas settlements acted as religious and political links to the metropolis. “Colonial god” is therefore defensible if it describes a historical network rather than an eternal metaphysical domain.

**Protection and answered prayer. [EA]** KAI 201 and KAI 47 both use the standard votive claim that he “heard the voice” of the dedicator. His protection extended to rulers, private dedicants, cities, soldiers, and treaty obligations.

**Maritime power. [EA/MSR]** Tyrian coins show a male god riding a winged hippocamp above waves with dolphins and holding a bow. The image supports a maritime dimension, although it is not always explicitly labeled. Melqart’s association with seafaring also follows from the geography of his sanctuaries and Tyrian expansion.

**Dynastic and military patronage. [EA/LE]** The Barcids’ Melqart-bearing names, Hannibal’s recorded visit or sacrifice at Gades, and his treaty oath show the god’s importance in Carthaginian elite and military ideology. Claims that the Barcids literally regarded themselves as Melqart’s descendants are possible interpretations of Heraclean political imagery, not a statement preserved in Punic text.

**Renewal and awakening. [LE/MSR]** The *egersis* made awakening central to later understanding of Melqart. Whether this was annual resurrection from death, waking a sleeping cult image, renewal of the king and city, installation of the god, or several meanings combined remains disputed.

### Iconography

No single image is securely identifiable as Melqart merely by appearance before labeled or contextually compelling evidence.

**The Bureij figure. [EA]** KAI 201 provides the earliest labeled representation: a bearded male in Syrian royal/divine dress. It does not yet use lion skin or club.

**“Smiting god” type. [MSR]** Bronze figurines from the Levant and Iberia showing a bearded god in a high cap, advancing with a raised weapon, have often been called Melqart. Without inscriptions or controlled cultic context, some may instead represent Baal, Resheph, or a generic warrior god.

**Cypriot Heraclean type. [RV/SI]** From approximately the late sixth century BCE, Cypriot male figures with lion skin and club can reflect Heracles-Melqart. Yet the type was also available to other local male gods, including Resheph/Apollo-like figures. Iconography alone is insufficient.

**Tyrian coinage. [EA/RV]**

- Persian-period silver: a male deity, usually identified as Melqart, rides a winged hippocamp, holds a bow, and travels over waves and dolphins.
- Hellenistic and later Tyrian silver: laureate or lion-skinned Heraclean head, club, eagle, and civic legends.
- Roman Tyrian coins sometimes place Melqart-Heracles beside a sacred stone or other symbols of Tyrian antiquity.

**Gaditanian coinage. [EA/RV/SI]** Coins of Gadir/Gades show Melqart with Heracles’ lion skin and club; reverses commonly carry tuna, dolphins, or tridents reflecting the city’s marine economy. A British Museum copper issue dated 150–100 BCE depicts Melqart’s head with a club, and a dolphin pierced by a trident. [British Museum coin 1844,0115.72](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_1844-0115-72)

### Attributed symbols

Secure or strongly contextual:

- City and royal authority.
- Club and lion skin, after or during Heraclean assimilation.
- Bow, on Tyrian maritime coinage.
- Hippocamp, dolphins, waves, tuna, and trident in regional maritime iconography.
- Sacred fire in Roman descriptions of Gades.
- Paired pillars or cult stones at Tyre and Gades.

Uncertain or late:

- **Quail:** connected with revival in Eudoxus’ story.
- **Dog and murex shell:** later story makes a dog discover purple dye by biting a murex; the narrative is not early Phoenician evidence.
- **Purple as sacred color:** plausible through Tyre’s industry but not established as an ancient liturgical color of Melqart.
- **Sun, a specific planet, day, number, or zodiacal sign:** no secure Phoenician dossier establishes these. Modern tables assigning him the Sun, Sunday, purple, fire or water, and magical correspondences are modern constructions.
- **Ankh/flower and fenestrated axe as fixed Melqart emblems:** common in modern summaries but dependent on disputed identifications of unlabeled figures.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

**[EA—negative finding]** No surviving Phoenician inscription gives Melqart’s parents, siblings, consort, or children.

**[LE/RV]** Eudoxus’ quail narrative, preserved by Athenaeus, calls the Tyrian Heracles the son of **Zeus and Asteria**. This is a Greek-form genealogy and may encode a local Tyrian relationship, but it cannot be treated as the universal Phoenician family tree.

**[MSR]** Astarte is often presented as Melqart’s consort because Hiram’s temples to Heracles and Astarte are paired in Menander/Josephus and because their cults stood near one another in places such as Kition. Direct Phoenician evidence calling Astarte “wife of Melqart” is lacking.

**[Late learned variants]** Tyros, Astronoë, and other female figures become associated with the Tyrian Heracles in Greek and late antique foundation myths. They are not securely attested as an early canonical spouse.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The awakening or *egersis*

**Earliest report:** Menander of Ephesus, probably Hellenistic, preserved by Josephus around 93–94 CE.

**[LE]** Hiram of Tyre was credited with building or renewing temples to Heracles and Astarte and with first celebrating or restoring the **ἔγερσις**, “awakening,” of Heracles in the Macedonian month Peritios. “Awakening” is the secure ancient term in the transmitted account.

**[LE/RV]** Eudoxus’ quail story supplies a narrative mechanism: Typhon kills Heracles; Iolaus revives him with the smell or warmth of a quail. Phoenicians consequently sacrifice quails to Heracles.

**[MSR]** Scholars have reconstructed several possible ritual sequences:

1. death or disappearance of the god;
2. burning of an effigy on a pyre;
3. lament or funerary observance;
4. awakening or resurrection;
5. renewal of civic and royal power;
6. possibly a sacred marriage with Astarte;
7. participation by an official titled “awakener/resuscitator of the god.”

Not all elements occur in one source. The burning derives partly from late testimony about Heracles’ pyre and tomb; the sacred marriage is inferred; the annual schedule and exact month are not secure for every period or city. Calling the rite “the resurrection festival of Melqart” is therefore an interpretation, albeit a plausible one, rather than a surviving Phoenician liturgical title.

**[Historiographic dispute]** Older “dying-and-rising god” models grouped Melqart with Baal, Adonis, Tammuz, Osiris, and Attis. More recent scholarship asks whether the evidence shows literal death and resurrection, periodic awakening, installation of an image, or civic renewal. Bonnet gave the death-and-revival reconstruction a strong historical formulation; Mark Smith and others caution against converting ritual analogy into a lost universal myth.

### Foundation of Tyre

**[Late learned elaboration]** Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 40, tells that the Tyrian Heracles directed the first inhabitants toward two floating “Ambrosian Rocks.” An eagle perched in an olive tree between them; fire and a serpent were associated with the tree. On divine instructions the settlers sacrificed the eagle, after which the rocks ceased wandering and became fixed, creating the island-city.

The story explains:

- Tyre’s island setting;
- sacred rocks or pillars;
- an ancient olive tree and perpetual fire;
- Melqart’s position as founder;
- the city’s claim to extreme antiquity.

The account is nearly a millennium later than Melqart’s earliest inscription. It may preserve local cultic topography, but the complete narrative is a late antique poetic form.

### Discovery of Tyrian purple

**[LE/F]** A later Greco-Roman tale credits Heracles/Melqart’s dog with biting a murex shell. Its mouth turned purple; the nymph Tyros demanded a robe in the same color, prompting the discovery of Tyrian dye. Versions vary over whether Melqart, the dog, or Tyros deserves credit.

This is an etiological story about Tyre’s famous industry, not a securely archaic myth. Modern retellings often promote it into a central Melqart legend without stating its late evidence.

### The western journey and Gadir

**[RV/LS]** The Melqart of Gadir became inseparable from Heracles’ western travels: the cattle of Geryon, the boundary at the ocean, and the Pillars of Heracles. Strabo records a Tyrian foundation oracle and a sequence of unsuccessful and successful colonial expeditions. The final sanctuary lay on the eastern end of the island complex, while the settlement lay westward.

**[MSR]** The historical core is that Gadir possessed an extremely important Tyrian sanctuary whose god was understood as Heracles/Melqart. It does not follow that the complete Greek Geryon cycle was originally a Phoenician Melqart epic. Greek geography and Phoenician cult reshaped one another.

### The Pillars

There are three related but distinct “pillars”:

1. **Herodotus’ two pillars inside the Tyrian temple:** gold and green/“emerald.”
2. **The paired cultic pillars or bronze columns described at Gades in later literature.**
3. **The geographical Pillars of Heracles at the Strait of Gibraltar.**

**[MSR]** Because Melqart was the Heracles of Gadir, the geographical Pillars of Heracles became associated with him. Claims that Phoenicians literally erected the mountains, or that “Pillars of Melqart” was the original indigenous name of Gibraltar, go beyond the surviving Phoenician evidence.

### Alexander and the god of Tyre

**[LE with historical core]** In 332 BCE Alexander requested entry into island Tyre to sacrifice to Heracles. The Tyrians offered sacrifice at the old mainland sanctuary but refused entry to the island city. The religious dispute was also constitutional and political: admitting a Macedonian king into the central civic shrine would acknowledge his authority. Alexander besieged and captured Tyre, held sacrifices and games for Heracles, and incorporated the sanctuary into his victory.

This episode did not “prove” that Alexander worshipped Melqart as a Phoenician. It shows how the Heracles equation furnished diplomatic language that both sides could understand and contest.

### Hannibal at Gades and Hannibal’s oath

**[LE/historical]** Before the Italian campaign, Hannibal visited the Gaditanian Heracles sanctuary, offered vows, and sought divine support. The visit tied the Barcid campaign to the western Tyrian sacred network.

**[EA through Greek transmission]** In the treaty with Philip V in 215 BCE, Hannibal and the Carthaginian leadership swore before a hierarchy of gods including Heracles and Iolaus. Heracles is normally identified as Melqart. The oath demonstrates state religion rather than a private “oath of eternal hatred” to Rome. The childhood oath story—Hannibal swearing to Hamilcar never to be Rome’s friend—is a separate tradition preserved by Polybius 3.11 and later authors; no surviving text says the oath was sworn specifically to Melqart. [Polybius on Hannibal’s childhood oath](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0234%3Abook%3D3%3Achapter%3D11&ie_sort=freq)

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Tyre

**Temple. [EA literary testimony]** Herodotus saw the sanctuary in the fifth century BCE. It contained numerous votives and two celebrated pillars. Tyrian priests claimed temple and city were 2,300 years old. The reported age belongs to priestly chronology and would place the foundation far earlier than present archaeology warrants.

**Hiram. [LE]** Menander/Josephus attributes a major reorganization to Hiram I, traditionally dated to the tenth century BCE: construction or renewal of sanctuaries for Heracles and Astarte and institution or restoration of the awakening.

**Archaeology. [EA—negative/uncertain]** The exact Iron Age temple has not been securely excavated or identified. Tyre’s continuous occupation, Alexander’s causeway, later monumental construction, shoreline change, and limited access to the island’s oldest levels make confident plans speculative. Modern excavations have sought Phoenician levels, but no excavated building can yet simply be labeled “the temple Herodotus saw.” The frequently repeated claim that the Crusader cathedral was certainly built directly over Melqart’s temple is unproved. [French Ministry of Culture, Tyre archaeology](https://archeologie.culture.gouv.fr/en/tyre)

**Ritual.** Securely or relatively well attested:

- votive offerings;
- prayer and vows;
- sacrifice;
- the *egersis* or awakening;
- royal sponsorship;
- priestly guardianship and transmission of foundation traditions;
- games and processions under Hellenistic and Roman rule.

Reconstructed but uncertain:

- burning an effigy each year;
- full funerary ritual;
- sacred marriage of king/priest and Astarte’s priestess;
- literal royal impersonation of the god;
- a fixed liturgy shared unchanged across Tyre, Carthage, Gadir, and Tharros.

### Gadir/Gades

**[RV]** The sanctuary of Melqart/Hercules Gaditanus was one of the ancient Mediterranean’s most famous western shrines. Strabo places it at the eastern end of the island complex, apart from the city. Silius describes strict priestly costume and taboos, perpetual fire, and an aniconic cult.

Prominent visitors in literary tradition included:

- Hannibal before invading Italy;
- Roman commanders;
- Julius Caesar, who was said to have wept before an image of Alexander there.

The sanctuary’s precise location remains debated. Sancti Petri was the traditional candidate, but landscape reconstruction and LiDAR studies have proposed alternatives in the surrounding paleo-island and marsh system. No surviving ruin can yet be securely presented as the complete temple. [Monterroso Checa, “La ubicación del santuario de Melqart en Gadir”](https://idus.us.es/items/29352b62-8ed2-465b-86d6-e6192cbb9f12)

### Kition and Cyprus

**[EA/RV]** Phoenician Kition integrated Melqart into royal and sanctuary ideology. At Kition-Bamboula, a female deity conventionally compared with Astarte was paired with a Heracles-Melqart-like male figure. In the salt-lake region, inscriptions attest Eshmun-Melqart. Cypriot workshops were especially important in transmitting lion-skin-and-club imagery into Melqart’s visual repertoire.

The cult was not a mere copy of Tyre: Cypriot fusion with Eshmun, Resheph, local “master of animals” imagery, and Greek Heracles produced regional forms. [Bryn Mawr Classical Review, *Kition-Bamboula VI*](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2016/2016.06.35/)

### Carthage

**[EA/LE]** Melqart remained a marker of Carthage’s Tyrian origin. Literary sources report the dispatch of offerings or a tithe to Tyre, though the frequency and continuity varied. By the time of Hannibal, Heracles/Melqart belonged to the gods guaranteeing Carthaginian diplomacy.

Carthage did not simply reproduce Tyre’s hierarchy. Baal Hammon and Tanit became overwhelmingly prominent in the surviving votive inscriptions, particularly those from the tophet. Melqart’s importance was dynastic, interstate, military, and ancestral, but surviving Carthaginian dedications do not justify calling him the sole chief god of Carthage.

### The Carthage tophet and human sacrifice

**[EA/disputed interpretation]** Excavations recovered cremated remains of infants, young children, and animals deposited in urns beneath votive stelae. Punic inscriptions describe *mlk/molk* offerings principally to **Baal Hammon**, later commonly with **Tanit**. Epigraphic formulas identify Baal Hammon and Tanit—not Melqart—as the standard recipients.

A major body of scholarship interprets the combined archaeological, osteological, literary, and epigraphic evidence as showing that at least some children were sacrificial victims; another interpretation views the precinct primarily as a special cemetery for infants who died naturally, sometimes with animal sacrifices. Questions remain about proportions, age estimates, causes of death, frequency, and historical change.

What may be stated securely:

- cremated infants and animals were ritually deposited;
- the precinct was a sanctuary, not an ordinary cemetery;
- the inscriptions use sacrificial and votive language;
- most named recipients are Baal Hammon and Tanit;
- *mlk* is the name of an offering, not evidence that the recipient was Melqart;
- no surviving Carthage tophet formula makes Melqart the regular recipient.

Amadasi Guzzo and Zamora López conclude that the inscriptions define a sanctuary where sacrifices were made, while acknowledging disputes over the victim terms. Their corpus explicitly identifies Baal Hammon and Tanit as the principal recipients. [“The Epigraphy of the Tophet”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/270501350_The_Epigraphy_of_the_Tophet)

**[LE/disputed]** Greco-Roman authors accuse Carthaginians and occasionally Tyrians of child or human sacrifice. Quintus Curtius reports that, during Alexander’s siege, some Tyrians proposed reviving an old custom of sacrificing a freeborn boy, but the elders rejected it. This single late report does not document a regular sacrifice to Melqart.

### Priesthood and personnel

Evidence is fragmentary:

- Tyre had priests capable of guarding antiquarian traditions and temple wealth.
- Hellenistic and Roman inscriptions attest officials whose titles have been understood as “awakener” of the god.
- Silius’ Gaditanian priests wore linen and observed purity restrictions.
- Phoenician sanctuaries had treasuries, artisans, sacrificial personnel, and economically important votive systems.
- No complete Melqart priestly constitution survives.

### Theophoric names and popular devotion

Names incorporating Melqart occur across the Phoenician and Punic world and in bilingual settings. They demonstrate that the god operated at household and personal levels as well as in royal ideology. The repeated votive phrase “because he heard his voice” presents him as accessible to individual prayer.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before ca. 900 BCE: reconstructable background, no named Melqart

The Late Bronze Age provides the Northwest Semitic religious and urban background, but no secure Melqart. Any derivation from Baal, Milku, an ancestral king, an underworld god, or a Tyrian title remains **[MSR]**.

### Later ninth–eighth centuries BCE: first attestation and Tyrian expansion

KAI 201 establishes Melqart in royal northern Syrian devotion. Tyre’s commercial and colonial expansion created sanctuaries and communities in Cyprus and the western Mediterranean. Archaeology supports Tyrian expansion from the ninth–eighth centuries, but literary foundation dates such as Gadir’s traditional 1104 BCE remain unverified.

### Seventh–sixth centuries BCE: Mediterranean consolidation

Melqart’s cult appears through the Tyrian colonial sphere. Heraclean visual types begin interacting with Cypriot and Phoenician imagery. In the Greek world, Phoenician sanctuaries on Thasos and elsewhere supplied a context for equating the Tyrian god with Heracles.

### Fifth–fourth centuries BCE: explicit Greek recognition

Herodotus provides the first extensive eyewitness report and already regards the Tyrian Heracles as older than the Greek hero. Kition, Ebusus, Sardinia, and Gadir show regionally distinct cults. Tyrian coinage develops maritime imagery.

Alexander’s siege in 332 BCE made the sanctuary a center of imperial confrontation. After capture, sacrifice and games for Heracles transformed the god into a bridge between Tyrian civic memory and Macedonian rule rather than abolishing him.

### Hellenistic period

The Melqart–Heracles equation became increasingly explicit and institutionally useful. KAI 47’s bilingual equivalence shows Tyrians themselves presenting Melqart as Heracles the Founder. Local cults nevertheless retained Phoenician names and formulas.

### Punic high period and the Barcids

Carthaginian elites continued to use Melqart as a sign of Tyrian ancestry and Mediterranean legitimacy. Hannibal’s worship at Gades and the treaty of 215 BCE give him a place in military and diplomatic religion. At the same time, Tanit and Baal Hammon dominate the tophet and much surviving Carthaginian votive religion.

### Roman period

Melqart persisted as Hercules Tyrius and Hercules Gaditanus. Roman emperors, soldiers, merchants, and local communities could worship Hercules without erasing the sanctuaries’ Tyrian pedigree. Coins continued to advertise Tyre’s holy and asylum status, its club, sacred stone, and Heraclean head. Roman Gades maintained an internationally famous Hercules cult. Pseudo-Lucian still distinguished the Tyrian god from the ordinary Greek hero.

### Late antiquity and Christianization

Christian authors used the supposed tomb and burning of the Tyrian Heracles to argue that pagan gods were dead mortals. Temples and public sacrifice declined through the combined effects of imperial patronage shifts, Christianization, civic transformation, and the legal suppression of sacrifice in the fourth and fifth centuries CE.

No securely documented continuous Melqart priesthood survives into the medieval period. Claims that medieval Tyrians secretly maintained an unbroken Melqart cult are unsupported.

### Islamic and medieval periods

Melqart ceased to function as a publicly identifiable god. The name and sanctuary traditions survived in classical manuscripts, local antiquarian memory, and geographical lore surrounding Tyre, Cádiz, and the Pillars of Hercules. These are forms of memory, not evidence of continuous worship.

### Early-modern rediscovery

The Malta cippi were published in 1694. In the eighteenth century, Jean-Jacques Barthélemy used their Greek-Phoenician bilingual text in the decipherment of the Phoenician alphabet. Melqart thereby acquired a second historical importance: his dedication helped recover the language in which he had been worshipped.

### Nineteenth–twentieth centuries

Phoenician epigraphy, colonial archaeology, and comparative religion created the modern Melqart. Excavations and KAI organized the inscriptions; Frazerian models cast him as a dying-and-rising vegetation god; later scholarship questioned that taxonomy.

Bonnet’s 1988 monograph shifted attention toward cult networks, local forms, and the political function of the Heracles equation. Recent landscape archaeology at Cádiz and renewed excavation in Tyre continue to test ancient literary geography.

### Present

There is no reliable demographic evidence for a substantial organized Melqart religion or an institution claiming demonstrable continuity from antiquity. Individual Semitic neopagans, reconstructionists, occultists, and online devotional communities invoke him today. Their practices are modern reconstructions based on scholarship, personal revelation, comparative ritual, or eclectic magic.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and heritage

Melqart is prominent in Lebanese, Maltese, Tunisian, Spanish, Sardinian, and Cypriot presentations of Phoenician heritage. Public statues and cultural references in Tyre or Lebanon turn him into a symbol of maritime ancestry and civic antiquity.

**[Modern political reception]** Lebanese Phoenicianism sometimes uses Melqart as evidence of a pre-Arab national ancestry. Uses range from inclusive cultural heritage to polemical claims that Lebanese identity is essentially non-Arab. The ancient cult does not resolve modern ethnic or linguistic identity. Literary scholarship documents modern Lebanese stories in which Melqart addresses contemporary Lebanese people as descendants of his worshippers. [Study of Lebanese identity and the story “Melqart”](https://opendata.uni-halle.de/bitstream/1981185920/108291/635/668386916.pdf)

### Occult and neopagan material

Melqart was not a major named figure in the canonical grimoires, Renaissance planetary magic, the Golden Dawn curriculum, or Crowley’s principal rituals. Assertions that he possesses an ancient fixed set of planetary, elemental, color, or angelic correspondences are therefore not supported by the ancient record.

Judika Illes’ *Encyclopedia of Spirits* (2009) and derivative websites describe him as lord of sea and sky, assign the Sun, water, purple, dog, and hippocamp, and list Astarte, Astronoë, and Tyros as consorts. This is an eclectic modern synthesis mixing ancient evidence, scholarly hypotheses, and modern correspondence tables. It should not be cited as an ancient ritual source. [Modern occult profile](https://occult-world.com/melkart/)

Sites that identify Melqart directly with Moloch, prescribe demonolatry, or present invented invocations as Phoenician rites are **[MI]** unless they disclose modern authorship. No complete ancient prayer to Melqart survives from which a modern liturgy can simply be restored.

### Fiction and games

Melqart appears less frequently as an independent character than as:

- a name for weapons, ships, factions, or artifacts;
- a background deity in fiction about Phoenicia or Carthage;
- the “Sword of Melqart” in *Tears to Tiara II*;
- a historical-mythological reference in strategy-game modifications and reconstructions;
- the eponym of **Melkart**, a crater on Ganymede.

These uses generally select the warrior, royal, or maritime aspects and often merge him completely with Hercules.

### Modern misinformation

Frequently repeated claims that require correction include:

- **“Melqart appears in the Baal Cycle.”** False; he is not named there.
- **“Melqart was certainly the son of Baal and Astarte.”** No early source says this.
- **“Astarte was certainly his wife everywhere.”** Plausible in some reconstructions, unproved as a universal genealogy.
- **“Melqart was Moloch and received the Carthage tophet sacrifices.”** The tophet inscriptions chiefly name Baal Hammon and Tanit; *mlk* is a sacrificial term.
- **“His temple has been excavated intact at Tyre or Cádiz.”** Neither identification is secure.
- **“The Phoenicians called Gibraltar the Pillars of Melqart.”** Possible as cultural interpretation, not preserved in a Phoenician inscription.
- **“Purple, Sunday, the Sun, water, dogs, and a specific magical sigil form an ancient correspondence system.”** Modern synthesis.
- **“Modern worship is an unbroken survival.”** No documentary chain establishes continuity.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Nature of the god

Earlier scholarship tried to isolate a single natural essence:

- solar god;
- marine god;
- vegetation god;
- dying-and-rising god;
- underworld god;
- deified king;
- Baal-Hadad transformed into a city god.

The evidence better supports a historically changing civic deity whose functions expanded with Tyre’s monarchy and networks. Natural and seasonal features may be real without exhausting his identity.

### Etymology and origins

“King of the City” is the dominant interpretation. Disputes concern whether:

- *qart* refers simply to Tyre;
- “City” means the divine city or underworld;
- the name began as a royal title;
- Melqart developed from an earlier unnamed Tyrian Baal;
- he absorbed features of Baal/Hadad, Resheph, Eshmun, or ancestral kingship.

No proposed second-millennium ancestor is textually demonstrable.

### The Bar-Hadad stela

The worn patronymic created a century-long dispute over whether the dedicator was:

- Ben-Hadad I of Damascus;
- Ben-Hadad II;
- a son of Hazael;
- another Aramean dynast;
- Bir-Hadad of Arpad, son of Attar-šumki.

The revised Arpad reading has strong palaeographic and historical arguments but has not made older museum and handbook identifications disappear.

### Was Melqart Jezebel’s Baal?

- **For Melqart:** Jezebel was Tyrian; Melqart was Tyre’s dynastic lord; Josephus later calls her god the Tyrian Belus.
- **For Baal Shamem/Hadad:** 1 Kings emphasizes rain, drought, and heavenly fire, domains of the storm-god; “Baal” normally designates Hadad in much Northwest Semitic evidence.
- **Conclusion:** the biblical text does not decide the question.

### What was the *egersis*?

- **Resurrection interpretation:** quail revival, burning/tomb testimony, “awakener” titles, and analogies with seasonal gods.
- **Cult-image awakening:** the rite may have awakened, installed, or reanimated the god’s image without implying a full death myth.
- **Civic renewal:** it may have renewed kingship, city, and colonial bonds.
- **Composite interpretation:** different cities and periods may have performed different rites under the same translated terminology.

### Melqart and Heracles

The ancient equivalence is certain; its origin and direction are not.

Possibilities include:

- Greeks recognized a foreign god resembling Heracles.
- Phoenician communities adopted Greek Heraclean images to translate Melqart.
- Cyprus and colonial contact zones produced a gradual two-way convergence.
- Greek western Heracles traditions absorbed Tyrian foundation geography.
- Tyrian communities strategically used Heracles to make Melqart intelligible under Greek and Roman regimes.

The Malta bilingual proves equivalence by the second century BCE, not original identity.

### Melqart and Melicertes

Some scholars have compared Melqart with the Greek sea-divinity Melicertes/Palaemon because of name resemblance, maritime associations, death, and revival. Bonnet rejected a simple identity; later researchers have reopened the comparison. Phonetic resemblance and thematic overlap do not establish derivation, especially because Melicertes has a distinct Greek genealogy and cult.

### Child sacrifice

The Carthage tophet debate is genuine, but attaching it specifically to Melqart is usually erroneous. The evidence concerns Punic religion generally and especially Baal Hammon and Tanit. The late report of a proposed Tyrian boy sacrifice during Alexander’s siege is relevant but isolated.

### State of the evidence

The chief losses are severe:

- no Tyrian mythological tablet corpus;
- no Phoenician Melqart hymn;
- no preserved liturgy of the *egersis*;
- no securely excavated central Tyrian temple plan;
- no native narrative of Gadir’s foundation;
- lost Tyrian annals known only through Menander and Josephus;
- ancient images often unlabeled;
- western evidence heavily filtered through “Heracles” and “Hercules.”

Important surviving materials are held or documented in:

- the National Museum of Damascus or Syrian archaeological records for KAI 201;
- the Louvre and National Museum of Archaeology, Valletta, for the Malta cippi;
- Cyprus Museum and excavation archives for Kition;
- museums in Cádiz, Madrid, London, Beirut, Tunis, Cagliari, and elsewhere for coins, inscriptions, and sculpture;
- KAI, CIS, RES, coin catalogues, and site reports.
