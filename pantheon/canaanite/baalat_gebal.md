# Baalat Gebal: Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess; specifically the poliad or tutelary goddess and divine sovereign of ancient Gebal/Byblos.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Egyptian Old Kingdom evidence from Byblos, probably beginning in the later third millennium BCE. Inscribed royal offerings occur in the sanctuary conventionally called the Temple of Baalat Gebal, and an Old Kingdom relief calls an unnamed Byblian ruler “beloved of Hathor, Lady of Byblos.” The temple itself was founded around the twenty-eighth–twenty-seventh century BCE, but attributing its earliest architectural phase specifically to a goddess already called *Baalat Gebal* is an archaeological inference. The earliest unambiguous written identification under a corresponding name is Egyptian *Hathor, Mistress of Byblos* (*Ḥwt-Ḥr nbt Kbn*) in Middle Kingdom religious texts; the first abundant indigenous-context documentation is Akkadian *Bēltu ša Gubla*, “Lady of Gubla,” in the fourteenth-century BCE Amarna correspondence.

---

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

**[Securely attested]** Baalat Gebal was the sovereign goddess of Gebal, the city called Gubla in Akkadian and Byblos in Greek, on the coast of present-day Lebanon. Unlike Baal of Ugarit, Anat, or Astarte, she has no surviving indigenous narrative cycle. Her documented identity is overwhelmingly civic and relational: she owns or embodies the city, legitimates its kings, prolongs their reigns, blesses diplomatic correspondents, receives royal construction and imported prestige objects, and presides over Byblos’s exceptional relationship with Egypt.

Calling her a “Canaanite, Phoenician, and Punic goddess” requires qualification:

- **Canaanite:** appropriate in the broad linguistic-cultural sense for Bronze Age Byblos.
- **Phoenician:** securely appropriate for the first-millennium inscriptions of Byblos.
- **Punic:** only by broad membership in the wider Phoenician religious world. No securely identified Carthaginian cult of *Baalat Gebal* is presently documented. She must not be substituted for Tanit, Astarte, or another Punic goddess without inscriptional evidence.

### Names and forms

- **Phoenician:** 𐤁𐤏𐤋𐤕 𐤂𐤁𐤋, *bʿlt gbl*, conventionally vocalized **Baʿalat Gebal**, **Baalat Gubal**, or **Baalat Gebal**.
- **Literal meaning:** “Lady,” “Mistress,” or “Sovereign of Gebal.” *Bʿlt* is the feminine of Northwest Semitic *baʿl*, “lord, master, owner.”
- **Akkadian:** ^dNIN ša ^uruGubla, read *Bēltu ša Gubla*, “Lady of Gubla,” in the Amarna letters. Shorter forms represented by the logogram ^dNIN and “our lady” also occur.
- **Egyptian:** *Ḥwt-Ḥr nbt Kbn*, “Hathor, Mistress of Byblos”; also formulations equivalent to “Lady of Dendera who resides in Byblos.” *Kbn/Kpn* is the Egyptian name for Byblos.
- **Greek:** Βαλτίς or Βάαλτις, **Baltis/Baaltis**, representing *Baalat*; **Ἀφροδίτη Βυβλίη**, “Byblian Aphrodite”; and in Philo of Byblos’s theological scheme **Dione**.
- **Latin-period reception:** *Venus* may replace Greek Aphrodite in interpretive descriptions, but no evidence establishes *Venus* as her original name.

### Title or proper name?

**[Scholarly dispute]** Older scholarship regularly treated *Baalat Gebal* as merely a title concealing a “real” goddess—usually Astarte, Asherah, Anat, Qudšu, Hathor, or Isis. Anna Elise Zernecke and subsequent researchers argue that it could itself function as a proper divine name. One epigraphic argument is that the components are not separated as an ordinary title such as “king of Byblos” sometimes is. More fundamentally, the indigenous inscriptions never disclose another hidden name.

The most disciplined conclusion is:

1. *Baalat Gebal* is grammatically intelligible as “Lady of Byblos.”
2. In actual Byblian usage it functioned as the goddess’s sufficient and possibly proper name.
3. Foreign equations with Hathor, Isis, Dione, Astarte, and Aphrodite are historical interpretations, not proof that one of those names was her timeless “real identity.” A recent reassessment explicitly cautions that Baalat may have remained an independent divine entity even as her foreign identifications changed over time. [Bouhafs, “Entités divines et lieux de cultes à Byblos”](https://www.enim-egyptologie.fr/revue/2023/11/Bouhafs_ENiM16_p205-228.pdf)

### Datable identifications

- **Hathor — Middle Kingdom securely, perhaps Old Kingdom:** Egyptian interpretatio identifying the city’s lady with Hathor. The equation suited Hathor’s associations with foreign lands, mining, seafaring, luxury goods, and royal patronage.
- **Isis — first millennium BCE development:** suggested by an Osorkon-period object from Byblos, by increasing Isis–Hathor overlap, and later by Plutarch’s Isis-at-Byblos narrative. This is less directly documented than the Hathor equation.
- **Astarte — late and contested:** three late, reportedly unprovenanced inscriptions support an equation, including a bilingual text with Astarte in Greek and Baalat Gebal in Phoenician. Conversely, Philo distinguishes Astarte from Baaltis as separate sisters.
- **Aphrodite — Hellenistic and Roman periods:** Lucian or Pseudo-Lucian calls the goddess of the city’s great sanctuary “Byblian Aphrodite.”
- **Dione — Philo of Byblos, preserved by Eusebius:** learned Greek systematization, not an indigenous Bronze Age identity.
- **Baaltis/Balthi — Roman and late-antique forms:** Greek and Syriac renderings of *Baalat*. The pseudo-Melitonic tradition turns Balthi into a narrative queen or goddess associated with Tammuz.

There is no secure basis for identifying her with the demon Beleth of modern grimoire culture. That equation is a modern occult speculation based mainly on superficial name resemblance.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The third-millennium sanctuary

**[Secure archaeology; divine attribution partly reconstructed]** Excavations by Pierre Montet and especially Maurice Dunand exposed a large sanctuary on the acropolis of Byblos. Its first monumental phases belong around the twenty-eighth–twenty-seventh century BCE. It was repeatedly rebuilt and remained a principal sacred complex into the Classical period.

Egyptian royal stone vessels, tables, reliefs, and other prestige objects from the Fourth through Sixth Dynasties were deposited in or around the sanctuary. A table records an endowment under Pepi I. Another Old Kingdom inscription calls a ruler “beloved of Hathor, Lady of Byblos.” These finds securely demonstrate Egyptian royal engagement with a Byblian sanctuary and an Egyptian conception of its goddess. They do not establish that every object was made for Baalat or that the earliest temple-builders already used the later Phoenician spelling.

The concentration of Egyptian royal objects at Byblos exceeds that at any other Levantine site. Their religious and diplomatic functions were intertwined: offerings to the city’s goddess also maintained access to cedar, oils, wine, resins, and eastern luxury goods. [Arico, *The Contexts of Egyptian Statues in the Levant*](https://jscholarship.library.jhu.edu/bitstream/1774.2/60776/1/ARICO-DISSERTATION-2017.pdf)

### Coffin Texts and Egyptian evidence

**[Securely attested]** Middle Kingdom Coffin Text material calls Hathor “Lady of Byblos” in a spell concerned with protection aboard the solar bark. This is the earliest clearly textual Egyptian equation. It does not describe Baalat’s mythology; it assimilates a foreign place-goddess to an Egyptian goddess whose manifestations could be localized abroad.

Under Thutmose III:

- The Gebel Barkal stela says that ships were constructed from timber from the region or presence of the Lady of Byblos.
- The official Minmose reports work at the temple of “Hathor, Lady of Byblos.”
- A damaged text in Senneferi’s tomb may refer to another expedition involving Byblos.

These texts show that Egyptian state ideology treated the Byblian sanctuary as a Hathoric cult-place.

### The Amarna letters, c. 1350–1330 BCE

**[Securely attested primary corpus]** The principal Late Bronze Age corpus consists of Akkadian cuneiform clay tablets sent from Rib-Addi of Gubla and his successor Ili-Rapih to the Egyptian court. Rib-Addi invokes *Bēltu ša Gubla* approximately twenty-seven times, commonly asking her to grant strength, dignity, or protection to the pharaoh or an Egyptian official. ORACC provides the divine-name entry and searchable tablet corpus. [ORACC: Bēltu-ša-Gubla](https://oracc.museum.upenn.edu/aemw/amarna/cbd/qpn-x-divine/x000000040.html)

Examples include:

- **EA 68:** “May the Lady of Gubla give strength to the king, my lord.”
- **EA 71:** a corresponding blessing in a letter to an Egyptian official.
- **EA 87:** Amun and the Lady of Gubla jointly grant dignity before the king.
- **EA 130:** the Lady is invoked at the beginning of Rib-Addi’s appeal.
- **EA 86:** mentions Ummaḫnu and her husband Milku(r), with Ummaḫnu restored as the “maidservant of the Lady of Gubla.” The damaged tablet makes the details uncertain, but she appears to have sought contact or representation at Pharaoh’s court. If “maidservant” is an official title, she is rare evidence for a prominent female cult functionary in a Bronze Age West Semitic city.

The formulas establish the goddess as a diplomatic patron and guarantor of Byblos’s political order. They give no genealogy and narrate no myth.

### Egyptian “Letter of Hori”

**[Later canonical Egyptian literary evidence]** Papyrus Anastasi I, composed in the Ramesside period, tests or mocks a scribe’s geographical learning: “I will tell you of another mysterious/hidden city. Byblos is its name.… And its goddess—what is she like?” The passage assumes the goddess’s fame but supplies no answer. “Mysterious” may be religiously suggestive or merely satirical.

### Ramesside magical texts

**[Securely attested; syncretistic Egyptian use]** BM EA 9997 + 10309, a collection of incantations copied around the reign of Ramesses XI, invokes the Lady of Byblos with Isis, Nephthys, and Serket in a healing spell for the poisoned child Horus. Here the Byblian goddess operates in an Egyptian magical ensemble; the text does not make her Horus’s mother.

### *Wenamun*, Papyrus Moscow 120

**[Secure text; disputed historical genre]** The *Report* or *Story of Wenamun* survives in one incomplete hieratic papyrus, written in Late Egyptian and now in the Pushkin Museum. Its action is set around year 5 of the Egyptian “Renaissance,” conventionally about 1075 BCE; opinions on the manuscript’s composition range from near-contemporary to roughly a century later. Whether memoir, archival fiction, or literary tale remains disputed.

Wenamun travels to Byblos to acquire cedar for Amun’s ceremonial bark. The prince Zakarbaal initially refuses him. During sacrifice, “the god” seizes a young courtier, who spends the night in prophetic frenzy and commands that the Egyptian envoy be brought forward.

**Crucial negative finding:** the surviving text does **not** call this deity Baalat Gebal. The narrative’s explicit theological dispute concerns Amun and the Egyptian claim to Lebanon’s timber. It contains no unambiguous appearance of “the Baalat of Wenamun.” Identifying the possessing god as Baalat is possible only as a contextual conjecture about Byblian cult, and may be less likely than Amun in the immediate narrative. Lichtheim’s translation and manuscript description are available in *Ancient Egyptian Literature II*. [Lichtheim, *Ancient Egyptian Literature II*](https://indi.ca/content/files/2024/12/Miriam-Lichtheim---Ancient-Egyptian-Literature_-Volume-II_-The-New-Kingdom-Near-Eastern-Center-UCLA-II-University-of-California-Press-1978.pdf)

### The Old Byblian royal inscriptions

These are the most direct indigenous Phoenician witnesses.

#### KAI 4: Yehimilk, c. 950–920 BCE

**[Securely attested, with one scribal restoration]** Yehimilk says he rebuilt a temple and restored ruined sanctuaries. He asks Baalshamem, Baalat Gebal, and “the assembly of the holy gods of Byblos” to prolong his reign. The inscription actually omits the *t* in *Baalat*; editors restore it from the formula and parallel inscriptions. Yehimilk’s insistence that he is a legitimate and upright king may indicate a contested accession, but the “usurper” conclusion is scholarly inference. [DEAPS, KAI 4](https://deaps.osu.edu/text_objects/11444)

#### KAI 5: Abibaal, c. 940–930 BCE

**[Secure object; heavily restored text]** A Phoenician inscription was added to the base of an Egyptian statue of Sheshonq. Abibaal brought it from Egypt for Baalat Gebal and asks her to prolong his days and years over Byblos. Much of the wording is restored from parallels. [DEAPS, KAI 5](https://deaps.osu.edu/text_objects/11445)

#### KAI 6: Elibaal, c. 920–900 BCE

**[Securely attested]** Inscribed across a bust of Osorkon, the dedication calls Baalat Gebal Elibaal’s lady and asks her to lengthen his reign. The Egyptian royal image was repurposed as a prestigious Byblian votive rather than necessarily worshipped as Pharaoh. [DEAPS, KAI 6](https://deaps.osu.edu/text_objects/11446)

#### KAI 7: Shipitbaal, c. 900–880 BCE

**[Securely attested]** Shipitbaal records rebuilding a wall for “the Mistress of Byblos, his lady” and asks her to prolong his days and years. The stone is now in Beirut’s National Museum. [DEAPS, KAI 7](https://deaps.osu.edu/text_objects/11447)

#### KAI 10/CIS I 1: Yehawmilk, c. 450 BCE

**[Securely attested]** This fourteen-line limestone stela is the fullest indigenous statement about her. Yehawmilk says:

- Baalat Gebal made him king;
- he constructed for her a bronze altar, a gold engraving, and a portico or related architectural work;
- she heard his voice and did him good;
- he asks her to bless and preserve him and to grant him favor with the gods and people;
- anyone who damages the work is to have his memorial and posterity effaced before the goddess.

Above the inscription, Yehawmilk offers a cup to the enthroned goddess. The Louvre dates it around 450 BCE and records its dimensions as 112 × 56 × 24 cm. [Louvre, Stèle de Yehawmilk](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark%3A/53355/cl010120347)

### Ugaritic, biblical, and Punic corpora: negative findings

- **Ugarit:** Baalat Gebal does not appear as a character in the Baal Cycle, *Kirta*, or *Aqhat*. Ugaritic *bʿlt*, “lady,” can title other goddesses, including Anat in KTU 1.108, but that does not make Anat the Lady of Byblos.
- **Hebrew Bible:** Gebal and its inhabitants appear geographically and commercially—Joshua 13:5; 1 Kings 5:18 in some translations; Psalm 83:7; Ezekiel 27:9—but Baalat Gebal is not named. Biblical polemic against “the Baals,” Ashtoreth, or Asherah cannot automatically be assigned to her.
- **Punic world:** the Carthage tophet inscriptions invoke Tanit and Baal Hammon, not Baalat Gebal. Consequently, the tophet debate and possible child sacrifice cannot be transferred to her cult.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**[Securely attested]**

- Divine sovereignty and ownership of Byblos.
- Appointment and legitimation of kings.
- Preservation of royal life and length of reign.
- Protection of the city and its political relationships.
- Blessing of the Egyptian king and officials in diplomatic correspondence.
- Reception of construction, altars, precious metalwork, statues, and imported royal gifts.
- By association with Hathor: maritime passage, foreign trade, luxury goods, royal favor, and possibly healing.
- In a Ramesside magical text: removal or healing of poison.

“Mother goddess,” “fertility goddess,” “moon goddess,” “earth goddess,” or “goddess of sacred sexuality” are not securely established as her indigenous Byblian domains. Such labels usually derive from generalized models of “Oriental goddesses” or from equations with Hathor, Astarte, Isis, and Aphrodite.

### Yehawmilk iconography

**[Securely identified]** The Yehawmilk stela supplies her clearest named image. She is:

- seated on a throne;
- larger or hierarchically dominant relative to the offering king;
- crowned with an Egyptianizing horn-and-solar-disc headdress;
- equipped with a scepter terminating in a lotus-like element;
- raising one hand in blessing;
- approached by Yehawmilk, who wears Persian-period court dress and carries a cup;
- placed under a winged solar disk.

The scene is neither purely Egyptian nor simply Persian: it is a Phoenician royal dedication expressed through the international visual languages available to Achaemenid-period Byblos.

### Roman bronze

A first-century CE bronze figure, British Museum ANE 134875, combines a classical body and pose with a dove, horned solar disk, and tall feathers. The University of Washington identifies this composite as Baalat Gebal/Astarte under strong Aphrodite and Isis influence. Since the object lacks an accompanying inscription in the cited record, that exact naming remains iconographic attribution rather than the certainty afforded by KAI 10. [University of Washington, Statue of Ba’alat Gebal](https://depts.washington.edu/silkroad/exhibit/rome/k68545.html)

### Temple figurines

**[Uncertain identification]** Thousands of sheet-metal and bronze votives, particularly male figures in conical caps, came from Byblos’s sacred precincts. They attest intense offering practice but are not secure portraits of Baalat. Many derive from the separate Obelisk Temple. Assigning every “Byblos figurine” to Baal, Resheph, or Baalat is methodologically unsound.

### Animals, plants, colors, planets, numbers, and days

- **Dove:** attested in later composite Aphrodite/Astarte-related iconography, not demonstrably an original Bronze Age emblem.
- **Cow horns and solar disk:** inherited from Hathor/Isis iconography.
- **Lotus:** appears in Egyptianizing royal imagery.
- **Planet Venus:** applicable through later Astarte/Aphrodite assimilation, not independently attested for Baalat Gebal.
- **Sacred day, number, color, or plant:** no secure indigenous evidence.

Modern lists giving her fixed colors, crystals, incense, lunar days, or magical correspondences are modern inventions unless independently sourced.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Indigenous genealogy

**[Absence of evidence]** No Bronze or Iron Age Byblian inscription names Baalat Gebal’s parents, spouse, siblings, or children. No indigenous text makes her the wife of Baal, El, Baalshamem, Resheph, or Adonis. Her prominence did not require a documented male consort.

### Philo’s learned genealogy

**[Late learned systematization]** Philo of Byblos, writing in Greek around the late first or early second century CE and claiming to translate the ancient Phoenician author Sanchuniathon, is preserved mainly in Eusebius’s fourth-century *Praeparatio Evangelica*. In this account:

- Astarte, Rhea, and Dione/Baaltis are sisters;
- all become wives of Kronos, whom Philo elsewhere equates with El;
- Kronos grants Byblos to Dione/Baaltis.

This is evidence for Roman-period Phoenician antiquarian theology, not a recoverable third- or second-millennium family tree. Philo explicitly distinguishes Astarte from Baaltis rather than equating them.

### Every documented divine interaction

Because no indigenous myth cycle survives, the list is short.

- **Baalshamem — joint invocation:** Baalshamem and Baalat Gebal, with the holy assembly, are asked to prolong Yehimilk’s reign. **Source:** KAI 4, c. 950–920 BCE. **Tier:** securely attested early Phoenician cultic association; not proof of marriage.
- **The holy gods of Byblos — joint civic guardianship:** she and the divine assembly legitimate and preserve Yehimilk. **Source:** KAI 4. **Tier:** securely attested.
- **Amun — joint blessing:** Amun and the Lady of Gubla grant dignity to an Egyptian official. **Source:** EA 87, fourteenth century BCE. **Tier:** securely attested diplomatic formula.
- **Hathor — identification:** Egyptians interpret the Lady of Byblos as Hathor and call Hathor “Mistress of Byblos.” **Sources:** Coffin Texts; Old and New Kingdom inscriptions. **Tier:** securely attested Egyptian syncretistic identification.
- **Isis — joint healing appearance:** the Lady of Byblos is invoked with Isis and other goddesses against poison. **Source:** BM EA 9997 + 10309, c. eleventh century BCE. **Tier:** securely attested Egyptian magical syncretism.
- **Nephthys — joint healing appearance:** same spell. **Tier:** securely attested; no genealogy implied.
- **Serket — joint healing appearance:** same spell. **Tier:** securely attested; no genealogy implied.
- **Horus — healing beneficiary:** the goddess participates in an incantatory ensemble healing the child Horus. **Source:** same papyrus. **Tier:** later Egyptian canonical/magical setting.
- **El/Kronos — assigned husband:** Dione/Baaltis becomes one of Kronos’s wives. **Source:** Philo via Eusebius, c. first–fourth centuries CE transmission. **Tier:** late learned systematization.
- **Astarte — sister and co-wife, not identical goddess:** Astarte and Dione/Baaltis are sisters and wives of Kronos. **Source:** Philo via Eusebius. **Tier:** late learned systematization.
- **Rhea — sister and co-wife:** same source and date. **Tier:** late learned systematization.
- **Aphrodite — interpretive identity:** Lucian’s “Byblian Aphrodite” occupies the great Byblian sanctuary. **Source:** *De Dea Syria* 6, second century CE. **Tier:** Greco-Roman syncretistic identification.
- **Adonis — cultic association:** rites for Adonis occur in the sanctuary of Byblian Aphrodite; the goddess mourns him in the wider Aphrodite–Adonis tradition. **Source:** *De Dea Syria* 6–9. **Tier:** Greco-Roman regional cult and narrative; the source does not use the name Baalat Gebal.
- **Osiris — alternative interpretation of Adonis rites:** some Byblians told the author that their mourning commemorated Osiris rather than Adonis. **Source:** *De Dea Syria* 7. **Tier:** Roman-period regional variant.
- **Isis — residence at Byblos:** Isis travels to Byblos seeking Osiris’s coffin. **Source:** Plutarch, *De Iside et Osiride* 15–16, c. 100 CE. **Tier:** Egyptian-Greek literary elaboration. Baalat herself is not named.
- **Tammuz — beloved of Balthi:** Balthi leaves Cyprus and comes to Gebal because of her love for Tammuz. **Source:** pseudo-Melito’s *Apology*, probably late antique in its surviving form. **Tier:** late antique accretion.
- **Hephaestus and Ares — relationships assigned to Balthi:** later interpretive tradition treats Balthi as consort of Hephaestus and lover of Ares, borrowing Aphrodite’s Greek relationships. **Tier:** late syncretistic systematization.

No textual interaction with Ugaritic Baal/Hadad, Anat, Asherah/Athirat, Resheph, Melqart, Eshmun, Tanit, or Baal Hammon is securely documented.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### No surviving indigenous Baalat cycle

**[Secure negative finding]** There is no Baalat Gebal equivalent of the Ugaritic Baal Cycle. The epigraphic record describes patronage, royal selection, blessing, construction, and cult; it does not narrate her birth, combats, death, descent, marriage, or heroic deeds.

### Philo’s allocation of Byblos

**[Late learned systematization]** In Philo’s cosmogonic history, Kronos/El distributes cities or territories among divine rulers and gives Byblos to Dione/Baaltis. The story supplies a charter for her civic sovereignty. It cannot safely be projected back unchanged into Bronze Age religion because:

- Philo wrote in Greek under Roman rule;
- his work survives through the Christian apologist Eusebius;
- Greek genealogical names and rationalizing euhemerism structure the account;
- “Sanchuniathon,” Philo’s claimed ancient source, cannot be independently reconstructed.

### Aphrodite and Adonis at Byblos

**[Roman-period regional tradition]** *De Dea Syria* describes annual rites at Byblos:

1. Adonis is wounded by a boar, according to local claims.
2. The river associated with him turns red, explained as his blood; the author also reports a naturalistic explanation involving red earth.
3. People mourn, beat their breasts, and conduct funerary observances.
4. On the following day they proclaim that Adonis lives and send him into the air.
5. Women who refuse to shave their hair must, according to the author, offer sexual service for one day, with the proceeds dedicated to Aphrodite.

The last claim is ancient testimony, but it does not prove an organized institution of hereditary “temple prostitution.” The category itself is heavily disputed in modern scholarship. Nor does Lucian call the goddess Baalat; identifying the Byblian Aphrodite with her is a plausible continuity argument based on the sanctuary and city, not an explicit statement. [Lucian, *On the Syrian Goddess*](https://lucianofsamosata.info/downloads/lucians_on_the_syrian_goddess_-_hayes_and_nimis_dec_2012.pdf)

Some Byblians reportedly said the rites concerned Osiris rather than Adonis. The author also reports the annual arrival of a floating head from Egypt. These are Roman-period ritual explanations, not proof that the Bronze Age Baalat already had an Osiris-like dying consort.

### Isis at Byblos

**[Greco-Egyptian literary elaboration]** In Plutarch:

1. Seth encloses Osiris in a chest and casts it into the river.
2. The chest reaches Byblos and becomes enclosed in a tree.
3. The king incorporates the tree into a palace pillar.
4. Isis comes to Byblos, sits grieving near a spring, befriends the queen’s servants, and becomes nurse to the royal child.
5. She attempts to burn away the child’s mortality while transforming into a swallow.
6. The queen interrupts; Isis reveals herself and recovers the pillar and coffin.

Plutarch gives the queen’s name variously as Astarte, Saosis, or Nemanous. This is not an indigenous biography of Baalat Gebal, though the localization in Byblos and the queen’s name Astarte encouraged later identification. [Plutarch, *Isis and Osiris*](https://robscholtemuseum.nl/wp-content/uploads/2019/05/Plutarch-Isis-and-Osiris.pdf)

### Balthi and Tammuz

**[Late antique accretion]** Pseudo-Melito says that Balthi, queen of Cyprus, loved Tammuz, son of a Phoenician king, and came to live in Gebal. The narrative merges Baalat/Balthi, Aphrodite, Astarte/Ishtar, and the Tammuz tradition. It cannot be used as evidence for her original genealogy.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Temple of Baalat Gebal

**[Secure archaeology]** The sanctuary stood prominently in the Byblos acropolis and underwent successive phases from the Early Bronze Age through the Roman period. Its earliest monumental establishment is commonly dated around 2800–2700 BCE. Fire, rebuilding, and changes of plan occurred over its long history. The later remains included courtyards, cellae, altars, installations, and a processional approach.

The evidence for cult includes:

- Egyptian royal stone vessels and tables;
- relief fragments;
- metal figurines and other votives;
- imported Egyptian statues reused for Phoenician royal dedications;
- royal building inscriptions;
- bronze and precious-metal furnishings mentioned by Yehawmilk;
- continuity of sacred space under Egyptianizing, Phoenician, Achaemenid, Hellenistic, and Roman visual regimes.

### The Obelisk Temple

**[Related but distinct sanctuary]** The Obelisk Temple, built over the earlier L-shaped Temple around the early second millennium BCE, lay about 100 metres east of Baalat’s temple. It contained standing stones, a large central sacred stone, offering deposits, weapons, faience, and numerous metal figures.

Older publications often called it a temple of Resheph. That attribution depended partly on a hieroglyphic name now read as the Egyptian ram god Herishef-Re, not Resheph. Current scholarship therefore treats the temple’s principal divine owner as uncertain and possibly male. It must not simply be renamed a second Temple of Baalat Gebal. The sanctuary nonetheless belongs to the same urban sacred and economic landscape. [Oxford Handbook of the Archaeology of the Levant](https://deadseaquake.info/pdfs/Archeology%20incl%20Archeoseismicity/Encyclopedias/OHAL.pdf)

### Trade and temple economy

**[Secure context; mechanisms reconstructed]** Byblos was Egypt’s major Levantine partner for cedar and other goods. Egyptian offerings to its goddess served several compatible functions:

- diplomacy with local rulers;
- ritual acknowledgment of a foreign territorial deity;
- theological conversion of commercial payments into gifts to Hathor;
- display of Egyptian royal power and generosity;
- accumulation of imported prestige goods in the local temple.

The temple probably acted as an economic and political institution, but surviving evidence does not permit reconstruction of its accounts, landholdings, or complete personnel.

### Priesthood

- **Ummaḫnu:** probable “maidservant” or priestess of the Lady of Gubla in EA 86; her exact office is obscured by damage.
- **Kings:** Yehimilk, Abibaal, Elibaal, Shipitbaal, and Yehawmilk personally perform or commemorate dedications, building, and petitions. Kingship was closely tied to the goddess without necessarily making the king her high priest.
- **Other clergy:** no complete personnel list, recruitment rules, or priestly hierarchy survives.

### Offerings and ritual

Securely or plausibly attested practices include:

- presentation of Egyptian royal objects;
- dedication of statues and stelae;
- construction and repair of temples and walls;
- altar offerings, including Yehawmilk’s bronze altar;
- libation or presentation of a cup, as depicted on KAI 10;
- deposit of metal figures, weapons, jewelry, and faience;
- sacrifice and possession-prophecy at Byblos in *Wenamun*, although the god involved is not securely Baalat;
- lamentation, hair-cutting, proclamation of renewed life, and sexual offering in the Roman-period Adonis festival described by *De Dea Syria*.

### Human sacrifice and the Carthage tophet

**[No evidence for Baalat Gebal]** Neither the Baalat inscriptions, the Amarna letters, Egyptian texts, nor excavated remains of her temple document human sacrifice. The Carthage tophet controversy concerns urns and inscriptions dedicated principally to Tanit and Baal Hammon. Whether the deposits represent sacrificed children, children who died naturally, or a changing combination remains debated, but none of these positions makes the tophet evidence evidence for Baalat Gebal.

### “Temple prostitution”

**[Contested ancient testimony]** Lucian reports a one-day sexual rite for women who declined to cut their hair during the Adonis mourning at Byblos. It is evidence that a Roman-period author believed or reported such a custom. It is not evidence for a permanent Baalat priestess-prostitute institution, nor for Bronze Age practice.

---

## 7. Historical Evolution

### c. 2800–2300 BCE: emergence

A major sanctuary arose in Early Bronze Age Byblos. Egyptian royal objects and “Hathor, Lady of Byblos” language indicate that the city’s female sovereign was already central to Egypt–Byblos relations. Her indigenous name at this date is not preserved.

### c. 2100–1600 BCE: Hathoric formulation and rebuilding

Middle Kingdom texts securely identify Hathor as Lady of Byblos. The sanctuary was rebuilt; Egyptian objects and styles circulated in large numbers. The nearby L-shaped/Obelisk Temple also flourished, but its god cannot securely be identified with Baalat.

### c. 1500–1300 BCE: imperial-Egyptian integration

Thutmose III and his officials connected shipbuilding, cedar, and temple work to Hathor, Lady of Byblos. In the Amarna age, Rib-Addi’s correspondence presents the Lady as Byblos’s active diplomatic patron. Her name’s repeated appearance in official letters is the fullest Bronze Age evidence of her political importance.

### c. 1100 BCE: Egyptian contraction

*Wenamun* depicts an Egyptian envoy who can no longer command timber by imperial right. The narrative dramatizes changing power relations, but does not explicitly name Baalat. Her absence from the story should not be interpreted as disappearance of her cult.

### c. 950–880 BCE: royal Phoenician epigraphy

Yehimilk and his successors restored sanctuaries, reused Egyptian royal statues as votives, and repeatedly asked Baalat to preserve their reigns. These inscriptions demonstrate strong cultic continuity alongside political autonomy.

### c. 450 BCE: Achaemenid-period renewal

Yehawmilk’s stela is the peak of explicit documentation. Baalat makes the king, hears prayer, grants benefit, receives elaborate architecture and metalwork, and protects the monument. Her image combines Egyptian divine regalia with Persian-period royal presentation.

### Hellenistic period

Baalat was increasingly translated through Astarte and Aphrodite. Late bilingual or parallel inscriptions support this interpretatio, but Philo’s later account still distinguishes Baaltis and Astarte.

### Roman period

The great sanctuary was understood as that of “Byblian Aphrodite,” associated with Adonis’s annual rites. Isis–Osiris traditions also localized divine events at Byblos. A composite classical bronze shows how Aphrodite, Astarte, Isis, and Baalat imagery could converge without erasing their different histories.

### Christianization and abandonment

Christianization progressively displaced civic polytheistic cult. The exact date and agent of the destruction or final closure of Baalat’s Byblos temple are not securely recorded in the sources examined. Constantine’s documented attack concerned the Aphrodite sanctuary at Aphaca, not necessarily the temple at Byblos. Claims that a specific church directly replaced Baalat’s cella require site-specific archaeological proof.

### Medieval to modern period

No documented continuous worship of Baalat Gebal survives through the medieval period. Her sanctuary became an archaeological ruin embedded in successive Byzantine, Islamic, Crusader, Ottoman, and modern Jbeil. The ancient goddess remained accessible through Classical texts and local antiquities rather than an identifiable continuous cult.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Archaeological rediscovery

Nineteenth-century exploration, Ernest Renan’s *Mission de Phénicie*, the 1869 recovery of the Yehawmilk stela, and twentieth-century excavations by Montet and Dunand made Baalat visible as a separately documented deity. The stela entered the De Clercq collection and later the Louvre; a missing fragment is in Beirut.

### Scholarship

Modern presentations have variously made her:

- an unnamed “Great Goddess”;
- a local Astarte;
- a form of Asherah or Qudšu;
- a foreign manifestation of Hathor;
- a precursor of Isis/Aphrodite;
- an autonomous poliad goddess whose identity was repeatedly translated by outsiders.

The last position best accounts for the indigenous practice of simply naming her Baalat Gebal and for the changing, source-specific equations.

### Neopaganism

**[Modern revival]** Small online Phoenician, Canaanite, and eclectic pagan communities invoke Baalat Gebal today. No reliable census establishes the scale of this worship, and there is no demonstrable institutional continuity from antiquity. Modern prayers often call her “Mother Goddess,” “Earth Soul,” “creatrix,” or assign incense, colors, lunar correspondences, and fertility functions. These are modern devotional constructions unless tied to a specific ancient source.

### Occult and demonological claims

**[Modern invention]** Internet demonolatry sometimes identifies Baalat Gebal with Beleth, a king in early-modern Solomonic demon catalogues. No ancient or medieval textual chain derives Beleth from Baalat Gebal, and the phonetic resemblance is insufficient. Claims that she is a “female form,” “emanation,” or true identity of Beleth are contemporary esoteric speculation.

### UFO and New Age reinterpretations

**[Modern invention]** Recent online material connects “the Lady,” Baalat, Hathor, Astarte, Isis, Sophia, Marian apparitions, and alleged extraterrestrial encounters into a universal feminine intelligence. These syntheses document contemporary belief formation, not ancient Phoenician tradition.

### National and cultural reception

Baalat appears in Lebanese and Phoenician-heritage discourse as an emblem of Byblos’s antiquity, maritime commerce, and cultural linkage with Egypt. Such use is modern identity work; it need not imply revival of ancient ritual.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is she Hathor?

- **Strong evidence for identification:** Egyptian inscriptions actually say “Hathor, Lady of Byblos”; the Yehawmilk image is unmistakably Hathoric/Isiac in visual language.
- **Reason not to collapse them:** Egyptian religion routinely translated foreign goddesses as local manifestations of Hathor. The process may reflect Egyptian theological and commercial interests rather than indigenous self-identification.
- **Best formulation:** Baalat Gebal was identified with Hathor, especially by Egyptians; she need not have originated as Hathor.

### Is she Astarte?

- **For:** late bilingual and Greek evidence; Byblian Aphrodite; shared later iconography and Adonis cult.
- **Against:** no Bronze or early Iron Age Byblian text calls her Astarte; Philo distinguishes Baaltis and Astarte.
- **Conclusion:** a later equation is demonstrable; primordial identity is not.

### Is she Asherah/Athirat or Qudšu?

Frank Moore Cross and other comparativists associated her with Asherah/Qudšu. Later iconographic and textual study has weakened the certainty of that equation. Qudšu may itself be an Egyptian construction rather than a securely attested Canaanite proper name. No Byblian inscription calls Baalat *Athirat*.

### Is she Anat?

The equation rests mainly on functional comparison and the generic word *bʿlt*. Ugaritic texts can call Anat “lady,” but no source identifies “Lady of Byblos” with Anat. Lipiński’s proposal remains a scholarly reconstruction.

### Temple naming and archaeological circularity

Dunand’s excavation labels—Temple of Baalat Gebal, L-shaped Temple, Obelisk Temple, Temple of Resheph—became influential enough to be mistaken for ancient names. Recent work re-examines the actual hieroglyphs and find contexts. The Obelisk Temple’s former assignment to Resheph is particularly doubtful because the relevant name is Herishef-Re. Archaeological labels should not manufacture divine ownership.

### Dating the Old Byblian inscriptions

Paleographic chronology, Egyptian royal statues, and royal genealogy do not align perfectly. Abibaal and Elibaal’s monuments incorporate statues of Sheshonq and Osorkon, but whether these were immediate royal gifts, heirlooms, or later reused objects affects dating. Current DEAPS ranges place:

- Yehimilk: 950–920 BCE;
- Abibaal: 940–930 BCE;
- Elibaal: 920–900 BCE;
- Shipitbaal: 900–880 BCE.

### The status of *Wenamun*

The text has been read as an official report, historical novella, literary fiction, or a fictionalized account preserving authentic institutional conditions. Its manuscript context and literary construction caution against treating every speech as a transcript. Conversely, its details of timber trade, Egyptian decline, and possession-prophecy are historically valuable. It is not, however, an explicit Baalat text.

### Ugarit and biblical comparison

Mark S. Smith, John Day, the *Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible*, Wyatt, Parker, and Smith–Pitard are indispensable for the wider West Semitic divine world. For this dossier their most important result is methodological: Ugaritic myths illuminate a related linguistic-cultural environment, but they do not supply missing Baalat myths. Similarly, the Hebrew Bible is a valuable, often hostile witness to Baal, Asherah, Astarte, and Phoenician cities, but it never names Baalat Gebal.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Baalat Gebal was certainly the wife of Baal or El.
- She appears in the Ugaritic Baal Cycle.
- The Obelisk Temple was unquestionably hers or unquestionably Resheph’s.
- *Wenamun* explicitly reports an oracle of Baalat.
- Carthaginian tophet sacrifice was performed for her.
- All Phoenician goddesses were one universal “Great Mother.”
- She was the ancient demon Beleth.
- Her cult survived continuously under the Virgin Mary.
- Modern correspondences involving crystals, fixed colors, planetary hours, or tarot are ancient.

None is established by the primary record.

## 10. Chronology

- **c. 2800–2700 BCE:** monumental establishment of the sanctuary conventionally called the Temple of Baalat Gebal.
- **Fourth–Sixth Dynasties, c. 2600–2200 BCE:** Egyptian royal objects deposited at Byblos; an Old Kingdom relief invokes Hathor, Lady of Byblos; Pepi I-related evidence records an endowment.
- **c. 2100–1900 BCE:** Middle Kingdom Coffin Texts attest Hathor as Lady of Byblos.
- **Early second millennium BCE:** rebuilding of Byblian sacred precincts; construction of the Obelisk Temple over the L-shaped sanctuary.
- **Reign of Thutmose III, c. 1479–1425 BCE:** Egyptian texts associate cedar, ships, construction, and the temple with Hathor, Lady of Byblos.
- **c. 1350–1330 BCE:** Rib-Addi’s Amarna letters repeatedly invoke *Bēltu ša Gubla*; EA 86 probably mentions her female servant Ummaḫnu.
- **Ramesside period, thirteenth century BCE:** Papyrus Anastasi I asks what Byblos and its goddess are like.
- **c. eleventh century BCE:** BM EA 9997 + 10309 invokes the Lady of Byblos in a healing spell.
- **Narrative setting c. 1075 BCE:** *Wenamun* visits Byblos; possession-prophecy occurs, but Baalat is not named.
- **c. 950–920 BCE:** KAI 4; Yehimilk restores sanctuaries and invokes Baalshamem, Baalat Gebal, and the divine assembly.
- **c. 940–930 BCE:** KAI 5; Abibaal dedicates an Egyptian royal statue to Baalat.
- **c. 920–900 BCE:** KAI 6; Elibaal dedicates an Osorkon bust.
- **c. 900–880 BCE:** KAI 7; Shipitbaal rebuilds a wall for her.
- **c. 450 BCE:** Yehawmilk erects KAI 10, portraying and naming Baalat as royal maker, benefactor, and temple owner.
- **Hellenistic period:** increasing equation with Astarte/Aphrodite.
- **Late first–early second century CE:** Philo of Byblos identifies Baaltis with Dione, distinguishes her from Astarte, and says Kronos grants her Byblos.
- **c. 100 CE:** Plutarch locates Isis’s search for Osiris at Byblos.
- **Second century CE:** *De Dea Syria* describes the sanctuary of Byblian Aphrodite and rites of Adonis/Osiris.
- **First–second centuries CE:** composite classical-Isiac-Astarte/Baalat bronze imagery appears.
- **Fourth century CE:** Eusebius preserves extensive fragments of Philo in *Praeparatio Evangelica*.
- **Late antiquity:** pseudo-Melito’s Balthi–Tammuz narrative circulates; Christianization displaces the old civic cult.
- **1860s–1870s:** Renan’s mission and the discovery/publication of the Yehawmilk stela bring the goddess into modern epigraphy.
- **1920s–1970s:** Montet and Dunand excavate and publish Byblos’s sacred architecture and deposits.
- **Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries:** scholarship increasingly distinguishes Baalat as an autonomous city-goddess from the foreign beings with whom she was identified.
- **Present:** archaeological, heritage, neopagan, occult, and internet receptions coexist; no continuous ancient cult organization is demonstrable.
