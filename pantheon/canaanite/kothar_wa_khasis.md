# Kothar-wa-Khasis: Research Dossier

**Class:** Male god; in the Late Bronze Age Ugaritic tradition, a divine craftsman, architect, metallurgist, maker of magical weapons and prestige objects, and possessor of specialized wisdom. Later Phoenician accounts recast his counterpart Chousor as a deified culture hero, inventor, magician, and primordial craftsman.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The securely identifiable evidence comes from Late Bronze Age Ugarit, principally thirteenth-century BCE alphabetic-cuneiform tablets from Ras Shamra. A proposed third-millennium Ebla attestation, *dKa-ša-lu*, is disputed and is rejected by Alfonso Archi as a different god, probably related to *dGa-ša-ru*. Consequently, Kothar should not presently be placed securely at Ebla. The Ugaritic tablets, ritual lists, and theophoric personal names constitute the first dependable dossier.

## Evidentiary labels

The following labels distinguish historical strata:

- **[Early—secure]** Direct Late Bronze Age Ugaritic or approximately contemporary Egyptian evidence.
- **[Early—reconstructed]** A scholarly restoration or interpretation of damaged Late Bronze Age evidence.
- **[Regional variant]** Evidence from Egypt, Phoenicia, Cyprus, or the Punic world that differs materially from the Ugaritic presentation.
- **[Learned systematization]** Ancient scribal equations or theological classifications.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A datable equation with another culture’s god.
- **[Late canonical reception]** A demonstrably later source transmitting or restructuring older material.
- **[Late accretion]** A Roman, late-antique, medieval, or early-modern addition not independently attested in the Bronze Age.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A modern inference from linguistic, comparative, or archaeological evidence.
- **[Modern invention/revival]** A claim or practice first documented in recent popular, occult, or reconstructionist settings.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

**[Early—secure]** At Ugarit, Kothar-wa-Khasis is the gods’ pre-eminent specialist in skilled manufacture. He fashions the two animate clubs with which Baal defeats Yam, makes gifts through which Baal seeks Athirat’s political assistance, constructs Baal’s palace, proposes and installs its window, and makes the extraordinary bow that becomes the central object in the *Tale of Aqhat*. He is not the pantheon’s sovereign and is not presented as a warrior acting on his own behalf. His importance lies in technical knowledge: divine power becomes materially effective through what Kothar knows how to make.

### Ugaritic name

**[Early—secure]** The alphabetic Ugaritic spelling is:

> 𐎋𐎘𐎗 𐎆 𐎃𐎒𐎒  
> *kṯr w ḫss*

The conventional vocalization is *Kôṯaru-wa-Ḫasīsu*, with modern variants including Kothar-wa-Khasis, Kothar-wa-Hasis, Koshar-wa-Hasis, and Kothar-and-Hasis. Because alphabetic Ugaritic normally omits vowels, the exact historical vocalization is reconstructed partly through syllabic spellings such as *Ku-ša-ru*.

**[Early—secure / philological interpretation]** The short form *Kothar*, *kṯr*, occurs independently. The second member, *ḫss*, does not securely occur as an independent divine name. The compound is usually translated “Skilled-and-Wise,” “Skillful-and-Wise,” or as a hendiadys, “the wise craftsman.”

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** *Kṯr* is normally connected with the West Semitic root *kšr/kṯr*, “to be successful, capable, skillful,” and with Akkadian *kašāru*, “to put in order, repair, accomplish.” Ugaritic *mkṯr* designates skilled workmanship. This is a strong linguistic explanation, although the precise semantic path from “success” to “craftsmanship” remains reconstructed.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** *Ḫasīsu* is usually explained as “wise, perceptive,” comparable to Akkadian *ḫasīsu*. Because wisdom and technical magic are characteristic of Mesopotamian Ea, some scholars suspect that this element entered Kothar’s titulary through contact with Ea traditions, perhaps through Hurrian mediation. It need not mean that Kothar originated as Ea.

### Other names and titles

- **Kothar, *kṯr*. [Early—secure]** The ordinary abbreviated name.
- **Kothar-wa-Khasis, *kṯr w ḫss*. [Early—secure]** The full literary name, especially prominent in myths; it also occurs in at least one prayer.
- **Hayyānu or Hayan, *hyn*. [Early—secure; etymology disputed]** A parallel or alternative name. It has been connected either with the Hurrian form Eyan/Eya, derived from Ea, or with an Arabic cognate meaning “easy, gentle,” hence possibly “dexterous.” Neither derivation is conclusive.
- **“The skillful craftsman.” [Early—secure]** A functional rendering of formulas employing his name and craft vocabulary.
- **“Lord of Memphis,” *bʿl ḥkpt*. [Early—secure]** Attested in the *Aqhat* text, KTU 1.17 V. Memphis is written with forms related to Egyptian *Ḥwt-kꜣ-Ptḥ*, “Estate of the ka of Ptah.”
- **Chousor/Khousor, Greek Χουσώρ or Χουσωρός. [Regional variant / late canonical reception]** The Phoenician-Greek form in Philo of Byblos and Damascius. The change from Ugaritic ṯ to Phoenician š is linguistically expected; Greek sigma represents the Phoenician consonant.
- **Khousarthis. [Late canonical reception]** A probable further spelling or expanded form in Philo’s fragments.
- **Kuthar, *kwtr*. [Late accretion]** The form in a Syriac apologetic work attributed to Melito of Sardis and preserved in a single manuscript in Hebrew script.
- **Keserty. [Regional variant—disputed]** An Egyptian god named on a Cairo Museum stela. László Kákosy and Izak Cornelius related him to Kothar, but the spelling is not identical to that in the “Budapest Kothar Papyrus”; his iconography resembles Resheph. Identification is plausible, not established.
- **Chousor-Hephaestus. [Syncretistic identification]** Explicit in Philo of Byblos.
- **Zeus Meilichios. [Syncretistic identification]** Philo says Chousor received this designation in association with his inventions and benefactions. This is not an Ugaritic title.
- **Ea/Eyan. [Learned systematization]** A trilingual Ugaritic god list aligns Kothar with Hurrian Eyan and Mesopotamian Ea. This is an ancient scribal equivalence, not proof that the three gods had always been one.
- **Ptah. [Syncretistic identification / modern reconstruction]** No surviving Ugaritic line simply says “Kothar is Ptah.” The association is inferred from Kothar’s craft function, his Memphis residence, and Egyptian reception. Later cosmogonic material strengthens but does not erase the distinction.

### Names that should not be accepted uncritically

- **Eblaite *dKa-ša-lu*. [Disputed]** Sometimes advertised as a third-millennium Kothar. Archi identifies the Ebla figure differently; this is not a secure attestation.
- **Baal-Malagê. [Disputed]** Known from Esarhaddon’s treaty with Baal of Tyre. Richard Clifford associated him with the craftsman god; Aaron Brody and more recent work regard him instead as an obscure maritime hypostasis of Baal. Identification with Kothar is unproved.
- **Taautos/Thoth. [Rejected reconstruction]** A proposed Kothar–Thoth fusion in Philo’s work is judged implausible by Albert Baumgarten; Taautos is much more directly derived from Egyptian Thoth.
- **Qurʾānic al-Kawthar, الكوثر. [Speculative comparison]** A relationship has occasionally been proposed on phonetic grounds. It is not a demonstrated historical survival of Kothar.
- **Hebrew *Cushan-rishathaim*. [Rejected reconstruction]** Derivation of the name in Judges 3 from Kothar is philologically weak.
- **Kabbalistic *Keter*. [Modern invention]** Internet claims connecting Kothar with Hebrew *keter*, “crown,” confuse different consonantal roots and lack an ancient chain of transmission.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Archaeological context

**[Early—secure]** Regular excavation at Ras Shamra began in 1929 under Claude Schaeffer. Tablets were recovered from buildings including the so-called House of the High Priest and other palace, temple, and private archives. The mythological tablets belong to Ugarit’s final Late Bronze Age phase, before the city’s destruction around 1190–1180 BCE. Several are associated through colophons with the scribe Ilimilku and the reign of Niqmaddu, conventionally placing their copying in the thirteenth century BCE. The stories may be older than the surviving copies, but their earlier age cannot be quantified from the tablets alone. The archaeological mission’s history and the 1929 discovery are documented by the [Syro-French Mission of Ras Shamra–Ugarit](https://www.mission-ougarit.fr/) and contemporary excavation reporting on [Persée](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bspf_0249-7638_1931_num_28_9_5596).

### Principal Ugaritic corpus

#### The Baal Cycle: KTU 1.1–1.6

**Language and support:** Ugaritic, alphabetic cuneiform on clay tablets.  
**Surviving copies:** Thirteenth century BCE, with extensive damage and lacunae.  
**Kothar’s role:** Weapon-maker, diplomatic artisan, architect, and technical adviser.

- **KTU 1.1–1.2. [Early—secure]** El’s council accepts Yam’s demand for Baal. Kothar becomes involved in the conflict and fashions two named clubs, Yagrush and Ayyamur. They spring or “dance” from Baal’s hand. The first blow does not defeat Yam; the second strikes his head, after which Yam collapses. Interpretations differ over whether Kothar is simply arming Baal or also pronouncing effective magical commands over animate weapons.
- **KTU 1.3–1.4. [Early—secure]** Because Baal lacks a palace, messengers are sent to Kothar’s distant residence, described with the paired geography of Caphtor and Memphis. Kothar makes precious-metal gifts for Athirat. After Athirat persuades El to authorize Baal’s house, Kothar constructs it.
- **KTU 1.4 V–VII. [Early—secure]** Kothar proposes a window or aperture in the palace. Baal initially refuses, apparently from concern connected with Yam or with the security of his daughters Pidray and Tallay. After the palace is finished, Baal authorizes the window. The opening is closely associated with Baal’s manifestation through thunder, cloud, and rain.
- **KTU 1.5–1.6. [Early—secure / absence finding]** Kothar is not a principal actor in Baal’s death-and-return conflict with Mot. Claims that he repairs or resurrects Baal are not supported by the surviving cycle.

Mark S. Smith and Wayne Pitard’s two-volume *The Ugaritic Baal Cycle* gives text, translation, and commentary; volume II’s discussion notes the uncertain relationship between real Memphis, Caphtor, and the poem’s mythic geography and warns against silently inserting Kothar’s name into damaged lines where the parallel structure does not require it. A searchable copy is available [here](https://www.ldsscriptureteachings.org/staging/4108/wp-content/uploads/2019/12/The-Ugaritic-Baal-Cycle-Volume-II.pdf).

#### The Tale of Aqhat: KTU 1.17–1.19

**Language and support:** Ugaritic alphabetic cuneiform on three badly damaged tablets.  
**Date:** Surviving copy, thirteenth century BCE.  
**Kothar’s role:** Maker and donor of the extraordinary bow.

**[Early—secure]** After major lacunae concerning Aqhat’s birth, Kothar approaches Danel carrying a bow and arrows. Danel suspends his judicial activity, welcomes him, and has a lamb prepared for him. Kothar gives Danel the weapon, which passes to Aqhat. Anat later covets it. Aqhat tells her to procure Lebanon wood, animal sinews and horns, reeds, and other materials and have Kothar make her another. His refusal leads indirectly to his killing and the loss or destruction of the bow.

**[Early—reconstructed]** The text does not preserve the bow’s manufacture. Saying that Kothar “forged” it is imprecise: its stated materials are organic, and “made” or “fashioned” is safer. Likewise, its exact magical properties are unclear. The surviving narrative establishes extraordinary craftsmanship and desirability, not a complete catalogue of powers.

A useful textual synopsis, including the damaged transition and Kothar’s hospitality scene, appears in the [Lexham Bible Dictionary entry](https://biblia.com/books/lbd/article/AQHAT%245FTALE.SUMMARY%245FOF%245FTHE%245FNARRATIVE.TABLET%245F1.28KTU%245F1.2E17.29).

#### The Legend of Kirta: KTU 1.14–1.16

**[Early—secure]** Kothar appears among the gods invited to the royal festivities celebrating Kirta’s acquisition of Huraya. He is named with Baal, Yarikh, Resheph, and Rahmay. He does not construct Kirta’s palace, make weapons, or otherwise drive the surviving plot. More expansive roles are unsupported.

#### Horon and the Mare / “Ridding the Land of Serpents”: KTU 1.100

**[Early—secure]** In this incantatory-mythological composition, Shapash is directed to seek divine aid against snake venom. Kothar is among the gods approached or invoked. The damaged, repetitive text does not securely preserve a successful individual intervention by him. Its importance is that technical or magical healing could fall within the orbit of his “wise” expertise.

#### KTU 1.92

**[Early—secure but fragmentary]** A fragmentary myth associates Ashtart with hunting and includes Kothar/Hayan in a divine meal scene. Its reconstruction is uncertain; it cannot safely bear a long narrative about a special relationship between Kothar and Ashtart.

### Ritual and administrative evidence

**[Early—secure]** Kothar was not merely a literary character:

- **RS 1.001:** a day-and-night sacrificial program lists a cow for Kothar.
- **RS 24.249:** a calendrical offering text prescribes two rams for him.
- **RS 24.246 / KTU 1.102:** a ritual or pantheon document includes him.
- Additional god lists place him within the recognized divine order.
- Approximately fifteen Ugaritic personal names have been identified as containing his name. One is *kṯrmlk*, “Kothar is king,” borne by a silversmith—particularly suggestive evidence for artisan devotion, though a single name cannot establish a guild cult.
- Syllabic writings include forms such as *Ku-ša-ru*; the divine logogram **dÉ.A** could represent him by learned equivalence with Ea.

Dennis Pardee’s editions remain fundamental for distinguishing sacrificial texts from myth; his *Ritual and Cult at Ugarit* is available through the [Society of Biblical Literature](https://www.sbl-site.org/wp-content/uploads/2024/11/Pardee.pdf).

### Egyptian material

#### Budapest Kothar Papyrus

**[Regional variant—secure text, uncertain date]** An Egyptian-language incantation invokes Kothar in connection with building a shrine or divine structure. It is no earlier than the Eighteenth Dynasty, but its precise date and provenance remain uncertain. It is important evidence for reception in Egypt, not proof that Kothar possessed a major Egyptian cult.

#### Keserty stela

**[Regional variant—disputed identification]** A Cairo Museum stela of unknown provenance shows the engraver Woše-seti praying before a seated god named Keserty. His crown has features comparable to Resheph iconography. Kákosy interpreted the name as an Egyptian form of Kothar; the divergent spelling and visual type prevent certainty.

### Phoenician and Greco-Roman corpus

#### Philo of Byblos, *Phoenician History*

**Date and transmission:** Greek, late first or early second century CE; survives mainly in Eusebius, *Praeparatio evangelica* 1.9–10, early fourth century CE. Philo claimed to translate the primordial Phoenician author Sanchuniathon. Modern scholarship accepts that the fragments preserve genuine Northwest Semitic material but regards their Bronze Age attribution and exact transmission history as unverified.

**[Late canonical reception / regional variant]** Philo’s Chousor and a brother discover iron and ironworking. Chousor separately invents the fishhook, bait, line, and raft, becomes the first sailor, and is credited with incantations and divination. Philo identifies him with Hephaestus and reports that he was honored as Zeus Meilichios.

Ironworking is not part of the surviving Ugaritic Kothar dossier: the Ugaritic god works chiefly with gold and silver and belongs to a Bronze Age textual world. Philo’s iron is therefore a historical updating, not evidence that the Baal Cycle originally portrayed an iron smith.

The underlying Greek and English presentation can be consulted through [ToposText’s Eusebius text](https://topostext.org/work/230). Albert I. Baumgarten’s commentary and Harold Attridge and Robert Oden’s critical edition are indispensable for the transmission problem.

#### Damascius, *De principiis* 125

**Date:** Early sixth century CE, Greek; attributed by Damascius to the Phoenician cosmogony of Mochos.

**[Late canonical reception / learned systematization]** Aither and Air produce Oulomos; from Oulomos’s self-union comes Chousoros, “the opener,” followed by the cosmic egg. Chousoros represents the force that differentiates undifferentiated nature, and the egg’s division generates heaven and earth.

This is the only source making the Kothar counterpart a primordial cosmogonic differentiator. It cannot simply be projected backward into Ugaritic religion. Damascius also supplies his own Neoplatonic interpretation. The Greek passage and its relation to Kothar, Ptah, and Philo are treated in Carolina López-Ruiz’s [“From Kothar to Kythereia”](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/Publications/SAOC/saoc73.pdf).

#### Pseudo-Melito, *Apology*

**[Late accretion]** A Syriac work attributed to the second-century bishop Melito, but preserved in a later and textually complicated tradition, calls Kuthar a Phoenician king and father of Tammuz. Balthi, queen of Cyprus, loves Tammuz; Hephaestus kills him from jealousy. The text dislocates Kothar and Hephaestus into separate figures even though Philo equates them. It is valuable evidence for late Cypro-Phoenician reception, not for Kothar’s Ugaritic genealogy.

### Sources that do not attest Kothar securely

- **Amarna letters. [Absence finding]** They illuminate Late Bronze Age Canaanite diplomacy, travel, and cultural contact but contain no secure appearance of Kothar-wa-Khasis.
- **Hebrew Bible. [Absence finding]** No uncontested biblical occurrence names him. Proposed emendations in Ezekiel 3:32 and Proverbs 31:19 are unnecessary; the attempt to find him in Cushan-rishathaim in Judges 3 is weak. Bezalel’s spirit-given wisdom and the Tabernacle-building formulas are comparative evidence for a shared craftsman ideology, not a disguised Kothar narrative.
- **Herodotus. [Indirect context]** His “Camp of the Tyrians” at Memphis provides context for Phoenician presence near Ptah’s city but does not name Kothar.
- **Josephus. [Absence finding]** No secure Kothar episode.
- **Lucian, *De Dea Syria*. [Absence finding]** Important for Roman-period Syrian religion, but it does not securely identify Kothar.
- **KAI inscriptions. [Limited evidence]** The standard monumental Phoenician corpus yields no unambiguous dedication to Kothar. Punic and Neo-Punic personal-name elements written *kšr/kyšr* may preserve his name, but lexical ambiguity prevents treating every such name as theophoric.
- **Carthage tophet. [Absence finding]** Excavation and inscriptions document vows and sacrifices to Baal Hammon and Tinnit. No offering there can securely be assigned to Kothar. The tophet debate therefore cannot be used to claim either infant sacrifice to Kothar or his opposition to it.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**[Early—secure]**

- Skilled manufacture and technical wisdom.
- Precious-metal work.
- Construction of divine buildings.
- Production of weapons and prestige objects.
- Knowledge expressed through naming, speech, and possibly incantation.
- Limited participation in anti-venom magic.
- Diplomatic craftsmanship: objects made by Kothar alter relations among gods.

**[Regional variant]**

- Temple construction in Egyptian reception.
- Ironworking, fishing technology, raft-building, navigation, divination, and generalized invention in Philo.
- Cosmic differentiation in Damascius.

Calling him simply a “blacksmith god” is too narrow. It imports the later emphasis on iron and the familiar Hephaestus analogy into texts where architecture, gold- and silverwork, woodworking, weapon-design, and wise speech are equally important.

### Characteristic objects

- **Yagrush**, usually interpreted “May [it] drive/chase out”: the first club made for Baal.
- **Ayyamur**, approximately “May [it] expel”: the second club, whose blow defeats Yam.
- **Athirat’s gifts:** elaborately fashioned silver and gold objects, probably furniture or serving vessels; the tablets are damaged and translations differ.
- **Baal’s palace:** constructed from precious materials with divine fire involved in the finishing process.
- **The palace window:** Kothar’s controversial architectural proposal, eventually adopted.
- **Aqhat’s bow and arrows:** made of elite organic materials; desired by Anat.
- **Fishing gear and raft:** only in Philo’s Roman-period account.
- **Cosmic egg:** opened or differentiated by Chousoros only in Damascius.

### Powers and limitations

**[Early—secure]** Kothar can make objects that move and strike with a will-like efficacy. Naming the clubs accompanies their activation. Whether this is “magic” in a distinct sense or the normal efficacy of divine craft is a modern analytical question.

He does not personally overthrow Yam, compel El, defeat Mot, or save Aqhat. His products empower other agents, and his palace work depends on political authorization from El. He is therefore powerful but institutionally subordinate.

### Iconography

**[Absence finding]** No Late Bronze Age image from Ugarit can be identified securely as Kothar by an accompanying inscription. Hammer-bearing, enthroned, or “smiting god” figures should not automatically be labeled Kothar; such motifs were widely shared.

**[Regional variant—disputed]** Keserty’s inscribed Egyptian stela is the closest proposed named image. He is seated, receives a prayer from an engraver, and wears a crown with a gazelle-head element. If Keserty is Kothar, this is an Egyptian adaptation using iconography closer to Resheph than to a distinctive “smith-god” type.

Consequently, the hammer, anvil, tongs, forge, volcanic fire, and lame body are not securely ancient Kothar attributes. They belong primarily to Hephaestus iconography or modern reconstruction.

### Sacred correspondences

**[Absence finding]** No securely attested sacred animal, plant, color, number, weekday, planet, annual festival date, or zodiacal sign is known for Kothar. Modern lists assigning bronze, blue, Wednesday, Mercury, fire, bulls, dolphins, or particular crystals are modern ritual inventions unless explicitly presented as contemporary symbolism.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Ugaritic genealogy

**[Absence finding]** The Ugaritic tablets do not securely name Kothar’s parents, consort, children, or siblings. He is a recognized member of the divine society but has no recoverable family tree comparable to Baal, Anat, or the children of Athirat.

### Later genealogies

**[Late canonical reception]** Philo makes Chousor and an unnamed brother descendants in a long euhemerized line from Hypsouranios, legendary founder of Tyre. Baumgarten suggests that Philo’s multiple inventor-brothers may reflect misunderstanding of paired divine names or Greek influence from traditions about Hephaestus and artisan descendants. This is conjectural.

**[Late canonical reception]** Damascius makes Chousoros an offspring or emanation of Oulomos’s self-union. This is a distinct cosmogonic genealogy.

**[Late accretion]** Pseudo-Melito makes Kuthar a Phoenician king and Tammuz’s father. No Ugaritic source corroborates this.

- **Baal — provides the clubs Yagrush and Ayyamur that enable Baal to defeat Yam.** KTU 1.2, thirteenth century BCE. **[Early—secure]**
- **Baal — receives Baal’s commission to make gifts for Athirat.** KTU 1.3 VI–1.4 I, thirteenth century BCE; connecting lines are damaged. **[Early—secure with reconstructed transition]**
- **Baal — builds Baal’s palace after El authorizes it.** KTU 1.4, thirteenth century BCE. **[Early—secure]**
- **Baal — disputes the inclusion of a palace window; Kothar’s proposal is initially rejected and later accepted.** KTU 1.4 V–VII, thirteenth century BCE. **[Early—secure; details disputed]**
- **Yam/Nahar — makes and commands the weapons by which Baal strikes and defeats Yam.** KTU 1.2 IV, thirteenth century BCE. Kothar does not personally fight Yam. **[Early—secure]**
- **Athirat — fashions precious-metal gifts intended to win her advocacy for Baal’s palace.** KTU 1.3–1.4, thirteenth century BCE. **[Early—secure]**
- **El — works within the order El authorizes when Baal is granted a palace; no direct personal dialogue between El and Kothar survives securely in this episode.** KTU 1.4, thirteenth century BCE. **[Early—secure / absence qualified]**
- **Gupan and Ugar — receive or carry Baal’s message to Kothar at Caphtor/Memphis.** KTU 1.3 VI, thirteenth century BCE. **[Early—secure]**
- **Anat — indirectly interacts through Aqhat’s bow: she desires Kothar’s work, and Aqhat tells her to commission another bow from him. No surviving scene shows Anat actually doing so.** KTU 1.17 VI, thirteenth century BCE. **[Early—secure / indirect]**
- **Shapash — Kothar is one of the gods whom Shapash is to approach in the anti-venom composition.** KTU 1.100, thirteenth century BCE. **[Early—secure but fragmentary]**
- **Horon — both belong to the divine network of KTU 1.100’s anti-venom narrative; the surviving text does not preserve a direct conversation between them.** KTU 1.100. **[Early—secure co-occurrence; direct interaction absent]**
- **Ashtart — Kothar/Hayan appears in a damaged divine meal or hunting composition involving Ashtart.** KTU 1.92, thirteenth century BCE. **[Early—secure co-occurrence; narrative uncertain]**
- **Yarikh — co-guest at Kirta’s celebration.** KTU 1.15, thirteenth century BCE. **[Early—secure joint appearance]**
- **Resheph — co-guest at Kirta’s celebration.** KTU 1.15. **[Early—secure joint appearance]**
- **Rahmay — co-guest at Kirta’s celebration.** KTU 1.15. **[Early—secure joint appearance]**
- **Ea — equated with Kothar in a multilingual god list.** Late Bronze Age Ugarit. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Eyan/Eya — Hurrian counterpart aligned with Kothar in the same scribal tradition.** Late Bronze Age Ugarit. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Ptah — connected through Memphis, common craft and architectural functions, and later “opener” speculation; no direct Ugaritic meeting occurs.** Second millennium BCE evidence interpreted in modern scholarship. **[Syncretistic identification / modern reconstruction]**
- **Hephaestus — explicitly identified with Chousor.** Philo of Byblos, late first–early second century CE, preserved by Eusebius. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Zeus Meilichios — Chousor receives or is interpreted through this divine title.** Philo of Byblos. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Oulomos — produces Chousoros by self-union in Mochos’s cosmogony.** Damascius, sixth century CE. **[Late canonical reception]**
- **Balthi/Baalat Gubal — in Pseudo-Melito, Balthi loves Kuthar’s son Tammuz; the connection is familial and political rather than a direct encounter.** Late-antique Syriac reception. **[Late accretion]**
- **Tammuz — identified as Kuthar’s son.** Pseudo-Melito. **[Late accretion]**
- **Ares — belongs to Pseudo-Melito’s entangled Balthi–Tammuz–Hephaestus story, but no direct Kuthar–Ares interaction is recorded.** **[Late accretion / absence qualified]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The making of Baal’s weapons

**[Early—secure]** In the opening movement of the Baal Cycle, Yam has gained El’s recognition and demands Baal’s surrender. Kothar fashions two clubs and gives each a performative name and command. Yagrush flies from Baal’s hand and strikes Yam’s shoulders or upper body, but Yam remains standing. Kothar then names Ayyamur, commands it to strike Yam’s crown between the eyes, and the second blow fells him.

The sequence prevents a flattened claim that Kothar simply made “one thunderbolt.” There are two distinct weapons, two named commands, and two outcomes. The clubs have often been compared with the paired weapons of other storm gods, but direct borrowing in any one direction is not demonstrable.

**Function:** The narrative legitimates Baal’s kingship while showing that royal force depends on expert knowledge. Kothar is neither claimant to the throne nor passive servant: his design and speech decide the combat.

### The palace of Baal

**[Early—secure]** After Yam’s defeat, Baal still lacks what marks a recognized king: a house. Through messengers, he commissions Kothar to make gifts for Athirat. These beautiful silver-and-gold objects help secure Athirat’s agreement to petition El. El authorizes the project, and Kothar constructs the palace.

Kothar proposes a window. Baal refuses, apparently fearing some threat associated with Yam or to his daughters. Construction proceeds; fire works upon the precious materials for several days. At the dedication feast Baal again considers the issue and ultimately accepts Kothar’s design. When the window opens, Baal’s voice goes forth in thunder and the earth responds.

**Variant interpretations:**

- The window may be a literal architectural aperture.
- It may be a smoke-hole, roof opening, or symbolic storm portal.
- It may explain how thunder and rain emerge from Baal’s celestial house.
- Some readings emphasize Baal’s fear that Yam could return through it.
- Others relate the concern to the security of Pidray and Tallay.

The text is too damaged to make one interpretation exclusively canonical.

### Aqhat’s bow

**[Early—secure]** Danel welcomes the approaching craftsman god with food. Kothar presents a bow and arrows, which Danel passes to Aqhat. Anat sees and covets the weapon. Aqhat refuses silver, gold, and the promise of extraordinary longevity. He tells her to gather raw materials and have Kothar construct another. Anat treats this and his gendered insult as intolerable. She arranges his killing; the bow is lost or broken, and drought and mourning follow.

Kothar does not participate in Aqhat’s killing and is not blamed by the text. The bow’s beauty and uniqueness are narratively essential, but its manufacture occupies a lacuna. Modern retellings that describe a named, invincible, metal “magic bow” go beyond the evidence.

### Kothar the primordial opener

**[Late canonical reception]** Damascius’s Mochos fragment places Chousoros immediately after Oulomos and before or alongside the cosmic egg. He is “the opener” and the differentiating power that makes ordered multiplicity possible. When the egg divides, heaven and earth result.

This narrative combines Phoenician nomenclature, possible Egyptian Ptah associations, cosmic-egg traditions, and Damascius’s Neoplatonic exegesis. Its relationship to genuine older Phoenician teaching is possible but unrecoverable in detail.

### Chousor the inventor

**[Late canonical reception]** Philo turns divine craft into a history of civilization: Chousor and his brother discover ironworking; Chousor develops fishing and rudimentary navigation and composes incantations and divinatory formulas. The god has become a *prōtos heuretēs*, a “first inventor,” and is described euhemeristically as a mortal honored after death.

This is not mere fiction imposed from outside: the craftsman core agrees strikingly with Ugaritic Kothar. But the individual inventions reflect Iron Age and Greco-Roman classifications of technology.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Ugarit

**[Early—secure]** Kothar received animal offerings in official ritual schedules. A cow in RS 1.001 and two rams in RS 24.249 demonstrate real cultic provision. God lists and personal names further show that he belonged to institutional religion.

**[Absence finding]**

- No independently identified Temple of Kothar has been excavated at Ugarit.
- No priesthood titled exclusively for Kothar is securely attested.
- No annual Kothar festival can be reconstructed.
- No initiation rite, artisan guild, oracle, or pilgrimage is documented.
- No evidence connects him with human sacrifice or cultic prostitution.
- No inscription demonstrates that metalworkers as a corporate group maintained his sanctuary, although the silversmith named *kṯrmlk* is suggestive of personal artisan devotion.

### Caphtor and Memphis

**[Early—secure literary geography]** The Baal Cycle gives Kothar two distant homes:

> “Caphtor, the throne of his dwelling; Memphis, the land of his inheritance.”

Caphtor is usually identified with Crete, though Cyprus or a wider Aegean zone has sometimes been proposed. Memphis is represented by a form derived from *Ḥwt-kꜣ-Ptḥ*. The paired locations connect Kothar with two prestigious horizons of manufacture and international exchange.

**[Absence finding]** A divine residence in a poem is not proof of a temple on Crete. No Minoan or Mycenaean inscription securely names Kothar, and no excavated Cretan sanctuary can presently be called his cult center.

**[Regional variant]** The Budapest incantation provides limited evidence that Kothar himself was invoked in Egypt. Herodotus’s later report of a Tyrian community in Memphis supplies historical context for continuing Levantine-Egyptian exchange, but not a Kothar temple.

### Phoenicia and Punic regions

**[Regional variant]** Chousor’s survival in Philo and possible *kšr* personal-name elements indicate continuing cultural memory. They do not establish a major public cult comparable to Melqart, Eshmun, Baal Hammon, or Tinnit.

A 2024 Carthage excavation reportedly found a fragmentary Punic altar inscription containing *kšr* as part of a personal name; as of the available preliminary conference abstract, this is evidence requiring full publication, not yet a direct dedication to Kothar. The preliminary report is represented by the [14th ICAANE abstract](https://frontoffice.inviteo.com/icaane2025/abstracts/export/exportAbstract-A17915FC.pdf).

### Sacrifice and disputed practices

**[Absence finding]** Nothing in Kothar’s surviving cult record documents human sacrifice, sexual rites, temple prostitution, ritual violence, or cultic warfare in his name. The violent use of his weapons occurs in myth, not in evidence for human ritual practice.

The Carthage tophet debate must not be transferred to him merely because he belongs broadly to a Phoenician-Punic religious world. The tophet inscriptions invoke other gods, and proposed Kothar-bearing personal names do not change the recipients of those dedications.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Possible third millennium BCE

**[Disputed]** The Ebla proposal would greatly extend Kothar’s history, but the reading does not command scholarly agreement. The responsible chronology begins with Ugarit.

### Fourteenth–thirteenth centuries BCE: emergence in the record

**[Early—secure]** Ugaritic myths, rituals, god lists, and personal names reveal a fully developed god, not an experimental newcomer. He belongs simultaneously to royal mythology and operating sacrificial religion. His foreign residences reflect Ugarit’s maritime and Egyptian connections.

### Late Bronze Age Egyptian reception

**[Regional variant]** Kothar entered or was recognized in Egyptian magical-architectural practice, but on a modest scale. He never approached the Egyptian popularity of Anat, Ashtart, Resheph, or Baal. His association with Memphis encouraged comparison with Ptah.

### After Ugarit’s destruction

**[Historical inference]** The destruction of Ugarit ended the archive that preserves most evidence. It does not prove the immediate extinction of his cult. Phoenician linguistic developments plausibly changed *Kṯr* to *Kšr/Chousor*, while the god’s functions were reframed.

### First millennium BCE Phoenicia and Punic expansion

**[Regional variant / sparse evidence]** Direct dedications remain elusive. Personal-name elements and the later Chousor tradition imply survival, but the scale, temples, festivals, and priesthood are unknown. Baal-Malagê should not be used to fill the gap.

### Hellenistic and Roman periods

**[Syncretistic identification]** Kothar’s recognizable craft identity was rendered through Hephaestus. Philo’s culture-hero system assigned him ironworking, navigation, magic, and invention. “Zeus Meilichios” introduced a second, more puzzling Greek equation, perhaps connected with benefaction, wealth, or maritime protection.

### Late antiquity

**[Late canonical reception]** Damascius preserved a cosmogonic Chousoros. Pseudo-Melito transmitted an unstable Cypro-Phoenician genealogy involving Kuthar, Tammuz, Balthi, Ares, and Hephaestus. Christian authors preserved these reports for apologetic, historical, or philosophical purposes after traditional temples had declined.

### Medieval to early modern period

**[Absence finding]** There is no demonstrated continuous medieval cult of Kothar under his own name. He does not have an independently traceable role in Arabic folklore, Islamic ritual, Jewish magic, European grimoires, Renaissance Hermeticism, or the standard demonological catalogues.

### Modern rediscovery

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Before the Ugaritic discoveries, Chousor was known primarily through Philo and Damascius. Decipherment of the Ras Shamra tablets after 1929 allowed scholars to identify Chousor as the later Phoenician reflex of Kothar. This was an important vindication of the presence of genuine Phoenician material inside Philo’s heavily Hellenized account.

### Contemporary revival

**[Modern invention/revival]** Small modern Canaanite, Phoenician, Semitic-polytheist, and eclectic pagan communities invoke Kothar as patron of smiths, engineers, artists, programmers, inventors, and craftspeople. Their prayers and offerings are contemporary religious creations informed by Ugaritic texts. No reliable demographic data demonstrate a large organized cult, continuous lineage, inherited priesthood, or common liturgical calendar.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

Kothar remains far less prominent in modern literature and popular media than Baal, Anat, or Moloch. His principal modern afterlife has been scholarly and reconstructionist.

### Scholarship and comparative mythology

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** He is regularly described as the “Canaanite Hephaestus,” an efficient orientation but a potentially distorting one. Unlike the Homeric Hephaestus, Kothar is not described as lame, rejected by his mother, married to Aphrodite, trapped in a net, or resident beneath a volcano. Those are Greek stories.

Comparisons with Ptah are stronger historically because Kothar’s own texts locate him at Memphis. Even so, the evidence supports contact and equivalence rather than simple identity.

Comparisons with Ea emphasize technical wisdom, magic, and learned god-list equations. These are stronger than casual resemblance but still represent ancient theological translation.

### Occultism and esotericism

**[Absence finding]** Kothar has no substantial documented role in Renaissance magic, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Thelema, traditional Solomonic grimoires, or classical demonology. Modern websites occasionally assign him planetary, elemental, angelic, or Qabalistic correspondences; these are modern inventions.

### Neopagan practice

**[Modern invention/revival]** Current practitioners sometimes:

- celebrate him as patron of crafts and technology;
- use hammers, tongs, anvils, blueprints, bronze, or model tools on altars;
- offer beer, bread, incense, crafted objects, or the first product of a workshop;
- equate him devotionally with Ptah or Hephaestus;
- create feast days from personal or seasonal calendars.

None of these particulars is recoverable as an ancient Kothar rite unless independently tied to the Ugaritic animal offerings. They may be legitimate modern practices for their adherents, but they are not evidence of continuity.

### Political or extremist appropriation

**[Absence finding]** No significant nationalist, racist, or extremist appropriation centered on Kothar was located. General Phoenician identity politics should not be attributed specifically to this god without evidence.

### Commercial and popular representation

**[Modern invention]** Contemporary art usually depicts a muscular human smith with a hammer, forge, and Minoan, Egyptian, or generic “Canaanite” costume. Since no secure Ugaritic image exists, such portrayals are imaginative composites.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Discovery and decipherment

The 1929–1931 discoveries transformed Chousor from an obscure figure in a late Greek fragment into a god documented in Bronze Age archives. Early editions by Charles Virolleaud and the development of the Ugaritic script’s decipherment made the Baal, Kirta, and Aqhat texts available. KTU—*Die keilalphabetischen Texte aus Ugarit, Ras Ibn Hani und anderen Orten*—became the standard catalogue.

### Principal controversies

#### Was Kothar already at Ebla?

The proposed *dKa-ša-lu* resembles Kothar imperfectly. Archi’s objection—that it represents another deity—means the proposal cannot establish a third-millennium cult.

#### Was Kothar Cretan?

Caphtor probably denotes Crete, but a divine residence is not an ethnic birth certificate. The text may associate superior craftsmanship with western trade, foreignness, or mythic distance. No Linear A or Linear B form securely names him.

#### Was Kothar Egyptian Ptah?

The shared craft domain and Memphis residence make some association highly probable. Direct identity is not stated in surviving Ugaritic narrative. The safest formulation is that Ugaritic tradition located Kothar in Ptah’s city and ancient or later interpreters could consequently align them.

#### Was Kothar Hephaestus?

In Philo, yes: this is an explicit Roman-period Phoenician-Greek equation. For the Bronze Age, “Hephaestus” is comparative shorthand, not Kothar’s Greek name retrojected backward.

#### Is Chousor’s ironwork ancient?

Not as far as the surviving Bronze Age texts show. Ironworking is a later technological update. Kothar’s earlier materials include gold, silver, wood, horn, sinew, and reeds.

#### Does the Hebrew Bible name him?

No secure occurrence. Literary comparison with Bezalel and divine wisdom traditions is possible; proposed textual emendations have not gained general acceptance.

#### Does Kothar have underworld powers?

Some scholars place his workshop in or near the underworld by analogy with Hephaestus and because of mythic geography. The texts themselves locate it in Caphtor/Memphis and do not explicitly make Kothar ruler of the dead. Claims that he rules the underworld with Shapash are not securely established.

#### Is Kothar the same as the Kotharat?

No. The Kotharat are a plurality of birth and marriage goddesses whose name is etymologically related to skill, success, or good fortune. Grammatical number, gender, and function distinguish them from Kothar.

### State of the evidence

The primary Ugaritic evidence is concentrated in tablets divided principally between collections in Syria and the Louvre. Individual objects retain RS excavation numbers and KTU text numbers; the [Louvre catalogue](https://collections.louvre.fr/ark%3A/53355/cl010143580) illustrates the museum documentation system. Many tablets remain physically fragmentary, and modern narratives must not disguise their lacunae.

The [Ras Shamra Tablet Inventory](https://ochre.lib.uchicago.edu/) provides a digital research framework for tablets from later excavation seasons. Political instability and damage to Syrian heritage institutions complicate access, conservation, and verification, though it would be misleading to claim that the whole Ugaritic corpus has been destroyed.
