# Astarte: Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess. In the relevant ancient traditions she is a Northwest Semitic female deity, not originally a demon, spirit, or collective. Her principal historically documented spheres are royal and military power, hunting, sexuality and generative force, celestial/astral sovereignty, horses and chariots, maritime protection, and the protection of cities and dynasties. Which of these is prominent varies sharply by place and period.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The divine name appears at third-millennium BCE Ebla, probably in the twenty-fourth century BCE, in syllabic cuneiform forms conventionally read *Aš-dar-tu/Aš-tar-tu*. These records securely establish a named deity but do not supply a narrative personality comparable to the later goddess. The earliest substantial mythological and ritual documentation is the thirteenth-century BCE Ugaritic corpus, where she is **ʿAṯtartu** (𐎓𐎘𐎚𐎗𐎚, consonantally *ʿṯtrt*). The equation of the Eblaitic deity with later Astarte is a strong scholarly reconstruction grounded in name continuity, not an explicit ancient genealogy.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EARLY]** Securely attested Bronze Age material.
- **[IA]** Securely attested Iron Age Phoenician, Punic, biblical, or neighboring evidence.
- **[REGIONAL]** A geographically restricted form or interpretation.
- **[LATE]** Hellenistic, Roman, or late-antique elaboration.
- **[LEARNED]** Ancient scholarly, priestly, antiquarian, or mythographic systematization.
- **[SYNCRETISM]** A datable equation or fusion with another deity.
- **[MEDIEVAL]** Medieval or early-modern reception.
- **[MODERN-RECONSTRUCTION]** A modern scholarly reconstruction rather than an explicit ancient statement.
- **[MODERN-INVENTION]** A recent assertion unsupported as an ancient tradition.
- **[DISPUTED]** Evidence or interpretation remains materially contested.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

Astarte is the conventional Greek-derived English name for a goddess whose West Semitic forms include Eblaitic *Ašdartu/Aštartu*, Ugaritic **ʿAṯtartu**, Phoenician **ʿAštart**, and Hebrew **ʿAštōret/Ashtoreth**. Egyptian writes her foreign name approximately *ʿsṯrt*; Greek authors use **Ἀστάρτη, Astartē**. The historical evidence does not support a single timeless “Astarte mythology.” At Ugarit she is a comparatively infrequent but potent royal, hunting, and apotropaic goddess; in New Kingdom Egypt she is conspicuously martial and equestrian; in Iron Age Sidon and Tyre she is a major civic and dynastic goddess; in Cyprus and the Mediterranean colonies she enters locally specific relationships with Aphrodite, Uni, Hera, Juno, and other goddesses.

### Name forms

- **Eblaite:** *Aš-dar-tu*, *Aš-tar-tu*, written syllabically in cuneiform. **[EARLY; reading and historical continuity partly reconstructed]**
- **Ugaritic:** 𐎓𐎘𐎚𐎗𐎚, *ʿṯtrt*, vocalized **ʿAṯtartu/Athtart**. **[EARLY]**
- **Akkadian syllabic West Semitic:** *Aštartu/Aštartum*; local writing may overlap with or be translated by **Ištar**. **[EARLY; context-dependent]**
- **Phoenician:** 𐤏𐤔𐤕𐤓𐤕, *ʿštrt*, conventionally **ʿAštart/Ashtart**. **[IA]**
- **Punic:** the same consonantal spelling, with later phonological developments imperfectly recoverable. **[IA]**
- **Biblical Hebrew:** עַשְׁתֹּרֶת, **ʿAštōret/Ashtoreth**; plural עַשְׁתָּרוֹת, **ʿAštārōt/Ashtaroth**. **[IA]**
- **Egyptian:** several consonantal spellings approximating *ʿAštart/ʿAstart*, normally rendered Astarte. **[EARLY/REGIONAL]**
- **Greek:** Ἀστάρτη, **Astartē**. **[IA–LATE]**
- **Latin:** *Astarte*; Roman provincial cult could instead employ an interpretive name such as *Venus*. **[LATE/SYNCRETISM]**
- **Etruscan:** **Uni**, not a cognate name but the deity with whom Astarte was equated in the Pyrgi bilingual. **[SYNCRETISM, ca. 500 BCE]**
- **Medieval Latin and vernacular demonology:** **Astaroth**, generally masculine. This is a reception-history descendant of biblical *Ashtaroth/Ashtoreth*, not the ancient goddess’s original classification. **[MEDIEVAL]**

### Etymology

**[MODERN-RECONSTRUCTION; DISPUTED]** The names Astarte, Akkadian **Ištar**, Ugaritic male **ʿAṯtar**, and South Arabian **ʿAṯtar** are generally assigned to a common Semitic divine-name complex reconstructed approximately as *ʿAṯtar-*. The phonetic relationship between Ištar and the West Semitic forms is strong. That does not make every bearer of the name the same deity in every period.

Two principal explanations remain in discussion:

1. The name was originally Semitic and associated very early with Venus, especially its morning/evening-star manifestations.
2. The form may be a prehistoric borrowing connected with Indo-European *h₂ster-* “star.” This proposal is ingenious but unproved; Mark S. Smith’s assessment that no certain Semitic etymology has been established remains prudent.

**Ashtoreth and “shame.”** A long-standing explanation holds that Hebrew scribes retained the consonants of *ʿAštart* but supplied vowels recalling **bōšet**, “shame,” producing the polemical form *ʿAštōret*. This is plausible and widely repeated but not demonstrable in every occurrence. Alternative phonological explanations invoke native Canaanite vowel shifts. The consonantal biblical text alone does not settle the question.

### Attested titles and local forms

- **“Astarte of the name of Baal”** (*ʿštrt šm bʿl*): Sidonian royal inscription, especially the Eshmunazar inscription, fifth century BCE. Whether this means “Astarte who bears the name/title of Baal,” “Astarte of the temple/domain called Name-of-Baal,” or a special manifestation remains disputed. **[IA; DISPUTED]**
- **“Astarte of Sidon” / “goddess of the Sidonians”:** Hebrew Kings; Phoenician royal cult. **[IA]**
- **“Astarte of Tyre”:** attested by Tyrian cult traditions, inscriptions, and the transmission through Menander quoted by Josephus. **[IA/LATE testimony to earlier tradition]**
- **“Astarte of Mari”:** invoked in Ugaritic serpent-incantation material as a localized manifestation. **[EARLY/REGIONAL]**
- **“Astarte the Hurrian”:** a localized form in Ugaritic ritual material. **[EARLY/REGIONAL]**
- **“Astarte the Field”:** an Egyptian epithet/form associated with martial and equestrian settings. **[REGIONAL]**
- **“Mistress of horses,” “lady of the chariot,” “lady of heaven,” and royal-protective titles:** Egyptian evidence, especially New Kingdom and later Memphis traditions. Exact wording varies by monument. **[REGIONAL]**
- **“Queen” or “mistress” language:** historically plausible in several corpora, but the globally popular title “Queen of Heaven” must not automatically be assigned to Astarte whenever it occurs. Jeremiah’s “Queen of Heaven” is not named as Astarte, and identifications with her, Ishtar, or another goddess are scholarly proposals. **[DISPUTED]**
- **Astarte Paphia/Aphrodite Paphia:** bilingual or interpretive Cypriot contexts, especially Hellenistic evidence. **[SYNCRETISM]**
- **Astarte–Uni:** Pyrgi, ca. 500 BCE. **[SYNCRETISM]**
- **Venus Heliopolitana:** Roman Baalbek’s goddess has strong Phoenician/Astarte iconographic ancestry, but “Astarte” is not simply her invariant name. **[LATE/SYNCRETISM]**

### Necessary distinctions

Astarte is not Asherah/Athirat. Their names, textual roles, and cult histories differ, although later writers and modern popular accounts frequently conflate them. She is not simply Anat either, although Ugaritic and Egyptian texts pair Astarte and Anat and sometimes blur their functional boundaries. She is cognate with Ištar, but “Astarte is Ishtar” is only safe when describing an explicit ancient translation or syncretism. She is not automatically Atargatis: the latter’s name and cult emerged through a different Syrian history, despite overlapping traits and later identifications.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Ebla

**[EARLY]** Third-millennium BCE Ebla tablets provide the earliest recognized divine-name forms. They establish antiquity but yield little securely recoverable narrative. Claims that the full later profile—war, sex, Venus, lions, or a particular consort—already existed at Ebla exceed the evidence.

### Mari and Middle/Late Bronze Age Syria

**[EARLY]** Second-millennium evidence from Mari and related Syrian archives documents local forms of the goddess and interaction between the names Aštart and Ištar. The evidence is administrative, ritual, and onomastic more often than narrative. Ugaritic incantations later remember an “Astarte of Mari,” suggesting the city retained exemplary cultic authority.

### Ugarit: thirteenth century BCE tablets

The tablets from Ras Shamra/Ugarit are the richest early West Semitic corpus. The principal poetic tablets were copied in the late thirteenth century BCE, shortly before Ugarit’s destruction around 1190 BCE. Their myths may preserve older compositions, but assigning precise earlier dates to the stories is speculative. Modern critical access is through **KTU/CAT**, the editions and commentaries of Mark S. Smith and Wayne Pitard, Nicolas Wyatt, Simon Parker and others, and the Göttingen electronic edition of the Ugaritic poetic corpus.

Important Astarte passages include:

- **KTU 1.2, Baal Cycle:** Anat and Astarte restrain Baal when he threatens Yam’s heralds, preserving the inviolability of messengers. Astarte rebukes or cautions Baal. **[EARLY]**
- **KTU 1.14–1.16, Kirta/Keret Epic:** Astarte is invoked as a standard of extraordinary beauty; Hurriya’s beauty is compared with that of Anat and Astarte. In the concluding curse, Horon is asked to break Kirta’s head and Astarte “the Name of Baal” his skull. **[EARLY]**
- **KTU 1.92:** highly fragmentary hunt narrative centered on Astarte. She hunts, encounters or pursues game, and appears as a forceful independent protagonist. Reconstructions of a continuous plot differ because substantial text is missing. **[EARLY; fragmentary]**
- **KTU 1.100 and 1.107:** serpent/venom incantations in which divine powers, including localized Astartes, are engaged against poison. **[EARLY; ritual-magical]**
- **KTU 1.43, 1.91, 1.148:** ritual texts involving Astarte’s ceremonial “entry,” probably into palace or royal space. The proposed comparison with Mesopotamian *kispu* observance is scholarly reconstruction. **[EARLY]**
- **KTU 4.168:** administrative reference to a **“singer of Astarte”** (*šr ʿṯtrt*), direct evidence for specialized cult personnel. **[EARLY]**
- **KTU 1.86:** a dream or omen text referring to Astarte’s horses, though interpretation is difficult. **[EARLY; DISPUTED context]**
- **RIH 98/02:** a subsequently discovered hymn/song presents her overwhelming hostile forces. **[EARLY; text and restoration specialized]**
- **God lists and sacrificial lists:** place Astarte within Ugarit’s cultic order and sometimes coordinate her with Anat or Mesopotamian Ishara/Ištar. Such equations are scribal correspondences, not proof that worshippers regarded all names as interchangeable. **[EARLY/LEARNED]**

Astarte is absent or marginal in much of the surviving Baal Cycle and Aqhat Epic. That absence is significant: Anat, not Astarte, performs many of the violent martial and fertility-adjacent narrative actions that later summaries indiscriminately assign to “the Canaanite goddess.”

### Egyptian texts

- **The “Astarte and the Sea” papyrus, pAmherst IX:** New Kingdom hieratic literary text, commonly placed in the Nineteenth Dynasty or broadly the thirteenth–twelfth centuries BCE. The beginning and large portions are lost. Sea/Yam demands tribute; the divine assembly sends Astarte bearing offerings; she weeps, negotiates, and becomes implicated in the demand that she be given to Sea. Seth/Baal appears to have been important in the missing resolution. Any complete modern ending is reconstruction. **[EARLY/REGIONAL; fragmentary]**
- **The Contendings of Horus and Seth, Papyrus Chester Beatty I:** Ramesside manuscript, probably twelfth century BCE. Neith recommends compensating Seth with Anat and Astarte, described as daughters of Re. This is Egyptian divine diplomacy, not a Ugaritic genealogy. **[REGIONAL]**
- **Royal and monumental texts:** Amenhotep II and Ramesside material associate Astarte with royal warfare; Ramesses II’s martial rhetoric and later monuments place her among deities protecting the king. **[REGIONAL]**
- **Memphis evidence:** priests and monuments attest a sustained cult associated with Ptah’s precinct and with a Semitic population. Some Egyptian texts call Astarte a daughter of Ptah; others make her a daughter of Re. **[REGIONAL; competing genealogies]**
- **Iconographic ostraca and stelae:** depict a mounted or armed foreign goddess, sometimes securely named Astarte, sometimes identified by modern scholars from attributes. The Fitzwilliam ostracon E.GA.4290.1943 shows a goddess riding side-saddle on a stallion and is catalogued as Astarte. **[REGIONAL; object identification partly curatorial]**

### Hebrew Bible

The biblical passages are primarily polemical witnesses produced or edited by Yahwistic writers, not neutral descriptions of Astarte’s cult.

- **Judges 2:13; 10:6:** Israelites serve “the Baals and the Ashtaroth.” The plural may mean goddesses collectively, local manifestations, or cult images. **[IA; hostile witness]**
- **1 Samuel 7:3–4; 12:10:** Samuelic narratives describe putting away “the Baals and the Ashtaroth.” **[IA; hostile witness]**
- **1 Samuel 31:10:** the Philistines place Saul’s armor in a temple of “Ashtaroth”; the textual tradition varies, and the parallel 1 Chronicles 10:10 says “the house of their gods.” Beth-Shean is a possible setting, but singular Astarte versus plural remains textually debated. **[IA; hostile witness/textual variant]**
- **1 Kings 11:5, 33:** Solomon follows “Ashtoreth, goddess of the Sidonians.” These verses belong to the Deuteronomistic evaluation of Solomon and probably reached their present form centuries after the purported tenth-century events. They establish what later Judahite writers remembered or alleged, not an excavated Solomonic foundation. **[IA; later canonical elaboration]**
- **2 Kings 23:13:** Josiah defiles the high place allegedly built by Solomon for Ashtoreth east/south of Jerusalem. This is evidence for a remembered or existing cult site in the late seventh century BCE, filtered through reform ideology. **[IA; hostile witness]**

The Bible never supplies a full Astarte myth. It classifies her as a foreign goddess and “abomination,” reflecting the authors’ theological program. Mark S. Smith and John Day situate these passages within Israel’s gradual differentiation from the wider West Semitic religious environment, rather than treating “Canaanite religion” and Israelite religion as originally sealed compartments.

### Phoenician inscriptions

- **Tabnit sarcophagus, KAI 13, ca. 500 BCE:** Tabnit identifies himself first as “priest of Astarte,” then king of the Sidonians; his father bears the same titles. This is direct evidence that Astarte’s priesthood stood at the heart of Sidonian kingship. **[IA]**
- **Eshmunazar II sarcophagus, KAI 14, fifth century BCE:** credits the Sidonian dynasty with building temples, including a temple of “Astarte, Name of Baal,” and names the king’s mother Amoashtart as priestess of Astarte. **[IA]**
- **Bodashtart inscriptions, fifth century BCE:** record Sidonian royal building activity for Eshmun and, in a debated text, for Astarte. **[IA]**
- **“Throne of Astarte” inscription, KAI 17, near Tyre, Hellenistic period:** dedicates a sphinx-flanked empty throne or cult seat to Astarte. **[LATE IA/HELLENISTIC]**
- **KAI 19, Tyre:** a portico dedication relating to Astarte and a local Tyrian era. **[HELLENISTIC]**
- **Kition tariff, CIS I 86, fifth–fourth century BCE:** temple financial/administrative account mentioning Astarte’s temple and personnel or payments. Earlier claims that its twenty-two girls were “sacred prostitutes” are not established by the Phoenician wording. **[IA; contested interpretation]**
- **Pyrgi gold tablets, ca. 500 BCE:** Phoenician and Etruscan dedicatory texts installed by Thefarie Velianas, ruler of Caere, at Pyrgi. The Phoenician invokes “the Lady Astarte”; the Etruscan addresses Uni. They are parallel but not word-for-word translations. **[IA/SYNCRETISM]**

### Classical and late-antique authors

- **Herodotus, *Histories* 1.105, fifth century BCE:** describes the temple of Aphrodite Urania at Ascalon and reports that Scythians plundered it. He does not use “Astarte” in the passage; its equation with Astarte is Greek interpretive geography and later commentary. **[IA/SYNCRETISM]**
- **Menander of Ephesus, preserved by Josephus, *Against Apion* 1.117–126:** says Hiram of Tyre rebuilt temples of Heracles/Melqart and Astarte; later names Ithobaal as priest of Astarte before becoming king. Menander’s Phoenician history is lost; Josephus, writing in the first century CE, is the transmitter. **[LATE testimony claiming Iron Age records]**
- **Philo of Byblos, preserved by Eusebius, *Preparation for the Gospel* 1.10:** Astarte, Zeus Demarous, and Adodos rule with Kronos’s consent; Astarte places a bull’s head on her head as royal insignia and consecrates a fallen star at Tyre. Philo claimed to translate the ancient Phoenician author Sanchuniathon, but the surviving material is a Roman-period learned synthesis transmitted by a Christian apologist. **[LEARNED/LATE]**
- **Lucian, *De Dea Syria* 4, second century CE:** reports a great Sidonian temple said to belong to Astarte; Lucian identifies her with Selene, while local priests identify the temple’s goddess as Europa, sister of Cadmus. This is valuable precisely because the interpretations conflict. **[LATE/LEARNED]**
- **Lucian, *De Dea Syria* 6–9:** describes Byblian mourning for Adonis and a goddess usually called Aphrodite. Calling that goddess straightforwardly Astarte is plausible in some ancient and modern interpretations, but Lucian does not give a simple one-name equation. **[LATE/SYNCRETISM; DISPUTED]**

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains by period

**Ugarit. [EARLY]** Hunting, beauty, royal protection, apotropaic power against venom or hostile forces, and a subordinate but meaningful role in divine diplomacy. Astarte is not the principal heroine of the Baal Cycle. Assertions that she was chiefly a “fertility goddess” at Ugarit are too broad.

**Egypt. [REGIONAL]** Warfare, royal victory, shield-like protection, horses, chariots, and foreign military potency. Egyptian adoption sharpened these characteristics and placed her within the pharaoh’s divine armory.

**Phoenician cities. [IA]** Civic sovereignty, dynastic legitimacy, protection of Sidon and Tyre, royal priesthood, and probably sexuality, reproduction, and celestial power. The direct inscriptions are much more explicit about temples, offices, dedications, and local manifestations than about narrative mythology.

**Cyprus and the Mediterranean. [IA–LATE/SYNCRETISM]** Sexuality, reproduction, maritime protection, royal and civic power, and increasing overlap with Aphrodite. At Pyrgi she underwrites the ruler’s sanctuary and political authority through identification with Uni.

### Astral identity

The common Semitic name-complex and ancient comparisons strongly associate Astarte with Venus. Philo’s story of the fallen star consecrated at Tyre is a late explicit astral narrative. Lucian’s equation with Selene shows that Roman-period observers could instead interpret her as lunar.

**[DISPUTED]** Modern handbooks frequently call the crescent her defining ancient emblem. Horns may be bovine, royal, astral, or iconographically borrowed; not every horned female figure is Astarte, and a crescent does not by itself prove lunar identity.

### Animals and objects

- **Horse and chariot:** securely prominent in Egypt; suggested at Ugarit by the “horses of Astarte.” **[EARLY/REGIONAL]**
- **Lion:** appears within the broad iconographic repertoire of powerful Near Eastern goddesses, but attribution of anonymous lion-associated females specifically to Astarte must be contextual. **[DISPUTED]**
- **Dove:** later Phoenician and Aphrodite-associated imagery; a British Museum bronze from Byblos identified as Astarte has a dove incorporated into the headdress. **[IA/LATE; individual identification]**
- **Bull horns or bull’s head:** explicit in Philo’s late mythographic account as a royal sign. **[LATE/LEARNED]**
- **Weapons, shield, spear, bow:** especially Egyptian martial iconography. **[REGIONAL]**
- **Nudity and frontal sexuality:** nude female plaques are often called “Astarte plaques,” but most lack inscriptions. They may represent a goddess, worshipper, protective figure, or generalized divine sexuality. **[DISPUTED]**
- **Sphinx throne:** securely associated with Astarte through inscriptions on several Phoenician cult seats. The empty seat could support a now-lost image, serve as a symbolic throne, or materialize divine presence aniconically. **[IA–LATE]**
- **Ship’s prow and aphlaston:** Hellenistic and Roman civic coinage from Phoenician ports depicts Astarte as maritime patron, standing on or beside a prow. **[LATE/REGIONAL]**

### Recognizable surviving images

1. **New Kingdom Egyptian mounted-goddess ostraca**, including Fitzwilliam E.GA.4290.1943: female rider side-saddle on a stallion. Identification with Astarte is plausible and museum-supported but should not be generalized to every mounted goddess.
2. **Memphis stelae:** named Astarte armed beside Ptah or receiving worship, anchoring iconography more securely than anonymous plaques.
3. **Phoenician “Astarte plaques”:** widespread nude frontal females, especially first millennium BCE. The conventional label is typological, not proof of identity.
4. **Astarte throne from the Eshmun sanctuary near Sidon:** sphinx-flanked stone throne within a cultic installation, fourth century BCE or later phases.
5. **KAI 17 throne from near Tyre:** inscribed dedication to Astarte, now in the Louvre.
6. **Byblos bronze statuette:** goddess with dove-bearing headdress, garland, and round fruit-like object, now in the British Museum.
7. **Cypriot terracottas:** clothed, crowned female figures influenced by Phoenician types; some are plausibly Astarte, but Cyprus’s local goddess and Aphrodite complicate identification.
8. **Phoenician and Roman coins:** turreted, enthroned, armed, or maritime goddess, sometimes identifiable through the issuing city’s cult and inscriptions.

### Unsupported modern correspondences

Fixed lists assigning Astarte a universal sacred color, number, weekday, crystal, herb, tarot card, or “chakra” are **[MODERN-INVENTION]** unless explicitly presented as the rules of a named modern occult system. No ancient pan-Phoenician dossier supplies such a standardized table.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogies

No single genealogy is canonical.

- **Ugarit:** Astarte’s parents are not securely specified in the surviving myths. She belongs to the divine world and is associated with Baal and Anat, but describing her simply as “Baal’s wife” overstates ambiguous evidence. **[EARLY]**
- **Egypt, Contendings of Horus and Seth:** Astarte and Anat are daughters of Re and are offered to Seth as wives/companions. **[REGIONAL]**
- **Egyptian Memphis theology:** Astarte may be called daughter of Ptah. **[REGIONAL]**
- **Philo of Byblos:** Astarte is a daughter of Ouranos and sister of Rhea and Dione; she rules alongside Zeus Demarous and Adodos under Kronos. This is a Roman-period Phoenician-Greek synthesis. **[LATE/LEARNED]**
- **Sidon:** cultic proximity and later evidence link Astarte and Eshmun, sometimes treated as a divine couple. The inscriptions document shared civic space more securely than a developed marriage myth. **[IA/REGIONAL]**
- **Tyre:** Astarte and Melqart are major civic deities. Some reconstructions make them consorts, but direct early narrative evidence for a marriage is thin. **[MODERN-RECONSTRUCTION]**

No securely attested ancient source makes Astarte the mother of all gods, the wife of Yahweh, or simply another name for Asherah. Those are conflations or modern systematizations.

### Every specifically documented divine interaction

The following list separates actual episodes or joint scenes from mere membership in a pantheon.

- **Astarte — Baal:** With Anat, restrains Baal from attacking Yam’s envoys and admonishes him; *KTU* 1.2 I, thirteenth century BCE. **[EARLY]**
- **Astarte — Anat:** Act jointly to restrain Baal; *KTU* 1.2 I. They are also paired in Egyptian and Ugaritic cultic material. **[EARLY]**
- **Astarte — Yam/Sea:** Carries or negotiates tribute to the demanding Sea and becomes the object of his demand; pAmherst IX, New Kingdom Egypt. **[EARLY/REGIONAL; fragmentary]**
- **Astarte — the Ennead/divine council:** The council sends her to Sea after earlier tribute fails; pAmherst IX. **[EARLY/REGIONAL]**
- **Astarte — Seth:** The likely champion against Sea in the damaged Egyptian story; elsewhere Neith recommends giving Astarte and Anat to Seth; pAmherst IX and *Contendings of Horus and Seth*, Ramesside. The first interaction’s details are incomplete. **[REGIONAL]**
- **Astarte — Anat — Seth:** Neith proposes Astarte and Anat as compensation/partners for Seth; *Contendings of Horus and Seth*. **[REGIONAL]**
- **Astarte — Re:** Astarte and Anat are called daughters of Re; *Contendings of Horus and Seth*. This is genealogy rather than an enacted meeting. **[REGIONAL]**
- **Astarte — Neith:** Neith assigns or recommends Astarte and Anat to Seth; *Contendings of Horus and Seth*. **[REGIONAL]**
- **Astarte — Ptah:** Egyptian Memphis tradition makes her Ptah’s daughter and installs her cult in or near his temple complex. **[REGIONAL]**
- **Astarte — Horon:** Invoked jointly in the curse against King Kirta: Horon is to smash his head, Astarte his skull; *KTU* 1.16 VI, thirteenth century BCE. **[EARLY]**
- **Astarte — serpent/venom deities:** Local manifestations of Astarte participate in sequences of divine aid against snakebite; *KTU* 1.100 and 1.107. Identification of every damaged divine name is uncertain. **[EARLY]**
- **Astarte — Kronos:** Rules with his consent in Philo’s Phoenician history; Philo via Eusebius, second–fourth centuries CE transmission. **[LATE/LEARNED]**
- **Astarte — Zeus Demarous:** Joint ruler in Philo’s account. **[LATE/LEARNED]**
- **Astarte — Adodos:** Joint ruler in Philo’s account; Adodos is a Greek rendering of Hadad. **[LATE/LEARNED]**
- **Astarte — Ouranos:** Philo makes her Ouranos’s daughter. **[LATE/LEARNED]**
- **Astarte — Rhea and Dione:** Sisters in Philo’s genealogy. **[LATE/LEARNED]**
- **Astarte — Eshmun:** Jointly embedded in Sidonian sanctuary and civic theology; direct narrative interaction is not preserved. **[IA; cultic association, no surviving episode]**
- **Astarte — Melqart:** Temples jointly rebuilt by Hiram according to Menander preserved by Josephus; this is coordinated royal cult, not a surviving divine encounter. **[IA tradition/LATE transmission]**
- **Astarte — Aphrodite:** Explicit or practical cultic identification in Cyprus and Greek interpretation of Levantine sanctuaries; no single myth narrates their meeting. **[SYNCRETISM]**
- **Astarte — Uni:** Bilingual equivalence at Pyrgi, ca. 500 BCE; no narrative meeting. **[SYNCRETISM]**
- **Astarte — Selene:** Lucian’s interpretive identification, second century CE. Sidonian priests reportedly disagreed. **[LATE/LEARNED]**
- **Astarte — Europa:** Sidonian priests told Lucian that the temple he thought Astarte’s belonged to Europa. This documents competing identification, not a mythic encounter. **[LATE/REGIONAL]**
- **Astarte — Ishtar:** Cognate and sometimes translated/equated in scholarly god lists; no preserved myth stages two separate goddesses meeting. **[EARLY scribal equation/MODERN-RECONSTRUCTION]**
- **Astarte — Yahweh:** The Hebrew Bible opposes their cults but preserves no scene in which the two deities meet, fight, marry, or speak. **Absence of documented interaction.**
- **Astarte — Adonis:** Byblian cult and later interpretation associate a local goddess with Adonis mourning, but Lucian calls her Aphrodite; an explicit early Astarte–Adonis myth is not preserved. **[LATE/SYNCRETISM; DISPUTED]**
- **Astarte — Tanit and Baal Hammon:** Punic evidence does not preserve a narrative encounter. Claims that Tanit is merely Astarte under a new name are unsupported; they are distinct in the epigraphic record even where functions overlap. **Absence of documented interaction; modern conflation rejected.**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Astarte restrains Baal

**[EARLY]** In the Baal Cycle, Yam’s messengers arrive before the divine assembly. Baal reacts violently, but Anat and Astarte seize his hands or otherwise restrain him. Astarte addresses him and invokes the protected status of messengers. The scene shows her as disciplined divine power and guardian of political protocol.

Later summaries sometimes turn this into a generic claim that Astarte “fights beside Baal.” The surviving episode instead has her prevent violence. It also demonstrates that Anat and Astarte can act as a coordinated pair without being identical.

### Astarte the huntress, KTU 1.92

**[EARLY; fragmentary]** This is the only surviving Ugaritic myth in which Astarte is clearly the central figure. She goes hunting and encounters animal prey; descriptions suggest beauty, vigor, and hunting expertise. New fragments have improved the reading but not restored a continuous plot.

Reconstructions in which she slays a monstrous bull, feeds it to Athirat’s sons, or performs a complete ritual cycle depend on restorations and parallels. They should be labeled literary hypotheses, not translated as continuous surviving text.

### Kirta/Keret material

**[EARLY]** Astarte operates as a standard of beauty in the epic’s praise of Hurriya. At the poem’s end Horon and Astarte are invoked as destructive powers against the king. The epic therefore preserves both attractive and violent valences without explaining them through a unified biography.

### Snakebite and anti-venom traditions

**[EARLY; ritual narrative]** The incantations send appeals through a geography of divine residences. Astarte appears in localized forms, including association with Mari, as a power capable of countering venom. These texts are both mythic and performative: the divine itinerary is part of an actual healing or protective rite.

### Astarte and the insatiable Sea

**[EARLY/REGIONAL]** In the Egyptian tale, Sea demands tribute from the gods. Initial offerings prove insufficient. Astarte is selected to carry further tribute; she weeps, adorns herself, and approaches Sea, who demands her as wife or possession. The papyrus breaks off before the full confrontation and resolution.

The tale is related structurally to Levantine storm-god-versus-Sea traditions, including Baal versus Yam, but it is not simply a translation of the extant Ugaritic Baal Cycle. Astarte’s diplomatic and endangered position is specific to the Egyptian composition as preserved. Modern retellings that supply a decisive rescue by Seth or a wedding ending must disclose that the original conclusion is missing.

### Philo’s astral Astarte

**[LATE/LEARNED]** Philo’s Astarte rules with other gods, places a bull’s head on her own head as a royal emblem, travels the inhabited world, finds a star fallen from heaven, and consecrates it on Tyre’s sacred island. This is the fullest surviving Astarte-centered narrative, but it is approximately 1,300 years later than Ugarit and mediated through Greek intellectual categories and Eusebius’s Christian quotation.

The fallen-star episode supports an astral interpretation in Philo’s era; it cannot establish that every Bronze Age image of Astarte was explicitly Venus.

### Astarte, Europa, and the Sidonian temple

**[LATE/REGIONAL]** Lucian believed the Sidonian temple’s goddess was Astarte and identified her with Selene. Priests instead told him it belonged to Europa, whose disappearance prompted Sidonian temple honors. Rather than choosing one as “the real myth,” the dossier records a second-century CE contest among outside interpretation, local priestly explanation, and Greek mythic translation.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Ugarit

Ritual lists record offerings and ceremonial entries; an administrative text records a professional “singer of Astarte.” The palace-entry rites imply royal patronage. Snakebite texts indicate practical ritual invocation. The surviving evidence does not disclose a complete priestly hierarchy or a freestanding monumental temple securely identified as exclusively hers.

### Sidon

Astarte’s cult was inseparable from royal government:

- Tabnit and his father were both “priest of Astarte” and king.
- Eshmunazar’s mother Amoashtart was priestess of Astarte.
- Royal inscriptions credit the dynasty with building Astarte’s temple.
- The Eshmun sanctuary at Bostan esh-Sheikh contained an Astarte installation with pool and sphinx throne, reflecting the intertwined cults of Astarte and Eshmun.
- The “two Sidons” language in Eshmunazar’s inscription suggests differentiated urban districts or communities, each with its sacred geography.

Astarte’s priesthood could therefore be held by both men and women and was a source of dynastic legitimacy. Recruitment rules beyond the royal examples are unknown.

### Tyre

Menander’s history, quoted by Josephus, claims that King Hiram demolished or renewed ancient temples and rebuilt those of Melqart and Astarte. Ithobaal is called a priest of Astarte before becoming king. These notices again join temple office and sovereignty.

Tyrian and neighboring inscriptions document dedications, architectural work, and special manifestations of the goddess. Hellenistic coins later portray a maritime goddess on a ship’s prow, reflecting Tyre’s civic and naval identity.

### Cyprus

At Kition, Phoenician settlement established a monumental Astarte cult by the late ninth century BCE. Temple accounts document organized sacrifices, payments, crafts, and personnel. Phoenician, local Cypriot, and Greek divine forms interacted over centuries; Astarte’s assimilation to Aphrodite was gradual, not an instantaneous importation.

The older interpretation of the Kition tariff as evidence for “sacred prostitution” is methodologically weak. The text mentions women or girls in a cultic-accounting context but does not call them prostitutes or describe sexual rites.

### Pyrgi and Etruria

The gold tablets from Pyrgi record Thefarie Velianas’s dedication of a sacred place to Astarte/Uni. The dedication served cult, kingship, and international prestige. Because the Phoenician and Etruscan texts are not exact translations, they reveal negotiated equivalence rather than simple identity.

### Phoenician colonies and Punic world

Astarte is epigraphically present in Sicily, Sardinia, Malta, North Africa, and Iberia, but her relative prominence varies. Tanit became the dominant named goddess in much Punic votive religion. Treating every Punic female image or every Tanit symbol as Astarte erases this development.

### Offerings and economic organization

Attested practices include animal sacrifice, incense, libation, votive objects, temple building, textile or garment provision, music, royal dedications, financial accounts, and named priestly offices. The evidence for cult sexuality is far weaker than older scholarship assumed.

### Temple prostitution

**[CONTESTED-MATERIAL FINDING]** No Phoenician inscription securely describes an institution of women compelled or appointed to have ritual sex for Astarte. Classical authors’ broader claims about “Babylonian” or Cypriot customs, ambiguous terms such as *qdš/qdšt*, and nude female images were formerly assembled into a universal “sacred prostitution” model. Current scholarship treats that construction skeptically. Sexuality may be part of Astarte’s divine domain; that is not evidence for temple prostitution.

### Human and child sacrifice

**[CONTESTED-MATERIAL FINDING]** The Carthage tophet contains urns with cremated remains of infants, young children, and animals, accompanied by votive inscriptions principally to **Baal Hammon and Tanit**, not Astarte. Greek and Roman writers accuse Carthaginians of child sacrifice. Archaeologists disagree over whether all, some, or none of the human remains represent sacrificial killings rather than special burial of children who died naturally.

One bioarchaeological study argued that age profiles fit natural infant mortality; subsequent specialists challenged its aging methods and argued that the combined osteological, epigraphic, iconographic, and literary evidence supports sacrifice at least in some cases. The defensible conclusion is:

1. Cremated infant and animal deposits in a special votive precinct are secure.
2. Sacrificial interpretation is serious and cannot be dismissed as merely Roman propaganda.
3. Frequency, selection, and whether every child was killed remain disputed.
4. The principal named recipients at Carthage were Tanit and Baal Hammon.
5. Assigning “Astarte’s child sacrifices” to this evidence is unsupported.

There is likewise no secure evidence that Astarte required human sacrifice at Ugarit, Sidon, Tyre, or Cyprus merely because later Punic tophets existed.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Third millennium BCE: name and cultic ancestry

**[EARLY]** Ebla supplies the earliest likely Astarte-name. The material establishes a deity but not a full later persona. Any reconstruction of a Proto-Semitic Venus deity behind both Astarte and Ishtar remains comparative.

### Early and Middle second millennium BCE: Syrian localization

**[EARLY]** Mari and other Syrian evidence show local manifestations and scribal interaction with Ištar. The goddess participated in networks of divine translation across Akkadian- and West-Semitic-speaking courts.

### Late Bronze Age, ca. 1500–1190 BCE: Ugarit and Egypt

**[EARLY]** At Ugarit, Athtart is a huntress, beauty-standard, royal/apotropaic power, ritual visitor, and partner of Anat in restraining Baal. Her relative narrative marginality is genuine.

**[REGIONAL]** Egypt adopted Astarte amid imperial contact with Syria-Palestine. She became especially prominent under the Eighteenth and Nineteenth Dynasties as a martial, equestrian, royal-protective goddess. Egyptian theologians naturalized her as daughter of Re or Ptah and associated her with Seth and Anat.

### Early Iron Age, ca. 1200–800 BCE: coastal city-states

The collapse of Ugarit did not end Astarte’s cult. Phoenician-speaking polities made her a major civic deity. Tyrian traditions connected her sanctuary with Hiram; biblical writers remembered her as the Sidonians’ goddess. Kition received an organized Phoenician cult by the late ninth century BCE.

### Neo-Assyrian period

Astarte appears among the divine witnesses of Esarhaddon’s treaty with Baal of Tyre in the seventh century BCE. This is direct evidence of her standing in Tyrian state religion and international oath enforcement. Phoenician trade and settlement carried her name and imagery westward.

### Seventh–sixth centuries BCE: Israel/Judah and Mediterranean expansion

Biblical traditions oppose Baals and Ashtaroth and attribute a Jerusalem-area shrine to Solomon. Josiah’s reform narrative places its defilement in the late seventh century BCE. Regardless of the historicity of every Solomonic detail, the polemic shows that Astarte worship was intelligible and threatening to Deuteronomistic writers.

At the same time, Phoenician iconographic forms spread through Cyprus, the Aegean, Sicily, Sardinia, North Africa, Iberia, and Etruria.

### Fifth century BCE: Sidonian royal apex and Pyrgi

The Tabnit and Eshmunazar inscriptions show Astarte’s priesthood embedded in the royal dynasty. At Pyrgi, a ruler dedicated a sanctuary to Astarte/Uni, materializing diplomatic and cultic translation between Phoenician and Etruscan worlds.

### Hellenistic period

Astarte increasingly appears under Greek interpretive names, especially Aphrodite. In Cyprus, bilingual and institutional evidence makes the Astarte–Aphrodite relationship particularly strong. Phoenician cult seats, inscriptions, and coins nevertheless preserve Astarte’s own name and local forms. Syncretism did not simply erase her.

### Roman period

Roman civic religion translated related local goddesses as Venus, Juno, or other imperial forms. Lucian still knew a Sidonian temple attributed to Astarte in the second century CE. Philo of Byblos produced a learned Phoenician cosmogony in which she remained a royal and astral goddess. At Baalbek, Venus Heliopolitana retained throne-and-sphinx imagery associated with Phoenician goddess traditions.

### Christianization and disappearance of public cult

From the fourth century CE, imperial restrictions on sacrifice and temple cult, episcopal expansion, and conversion transformed the sacred landscape. There is no securely documented continuous institutional priesthood of Astarte from late antiquity into the medieval period. Temples were abandoned, repurposed, quarried, or absorbed into later settlement. Specific claims that a particular Astarte sanctuary was violently destroyed require site-specific evidence; a universal single “suppression event” did not occur.

### Medieval transformation into Astaroth

Biblical *Ashtoreth/Ashtaroth* entered Christian demonological name-lists. Medieval and early-modern grimoires turned **Astaroth** into a male demon or duke/prince of hell. The gender change arose within demonological reception, not an ancient Phoenician myth. It is misleading to present Astaroth’s grimoire offices—teaching sciences, revealing secrets, commanding legions—as forgotten Astarte attributes.

### Modern revival

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century polytheists, goddess-spirituality practitioners, occultists, and reconstructionists have revived Astarte worship. This is a genuine modern religious phenomenon but not demonstrably an unbroken survival. Reliable numbers are unavailable because practice is decentralized and often private.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship, archaeology, and nationalism

The discovery of Ugarit in 1928 and publication of its tablets radically changed Astarte studies. She could no longer be reconstructed mainly from hostile biblical passages, Greek writers, and comparison with Ishtar. Lebanese and broader Phoenician cultural movements subsequently adopted Astarte as a symbol of ancient heritage, femininity, or Mediterranean identity. Such uses are modern political reception, not evidence of ancient pan-Phoenician nationalism.

### Literature and art

Astarte’s name appears in Romantic, Symbolist, Decadent, and modern fantasy writing as shorthand for an exotic goddess of sex, cruelty, or the “Orient.” Much of this image descends from nineteenth-century biblical and colonial scholarship rather than primary Phoenician texts.

Gustave Flaubert’s *Salammbô* shaped popular images of Carthaginian religion, sacrifice, and erotic priesthood, although its goddess is chiefly Tanit and its ritual world is literary invention. Later fantasy, comics, games, and television often merge Astarte, Ishtar, Asherah, Lilith, and Astaroth.

### Occult traditions

- **Astaroth in grimoires:** The *Book of Abramelin*, later demonological catalogues, Johann Weyer’s *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*, and the *Lesser Key of Solomon* transmit a male demon. **[MEDIEVAL/EARLY-MODERN]**
- **Ceremonial magic:** Nineteenth- and twentieth-century occultists reinterpreted ancient gods through planetary, qabalah-derived, and initiatory tables. Any fixed occult correspondence belongs to the specific modern system.
- **Goddess spirituality and neopaganism:** Astarte is invoked as a love, war, sovereignty, or “divine feminine” goddess, often through synthesis with Ishtar and Aphrodite. **[MODERN REVIVAL]**
- **Demonolatry:** Some practitioners treat Astaroth as Astarte’s demonized form or as a distinct masculine spirit. These are current theological positions, not historically demonstrable ancient continuity.

### Common modern inventions

- “Astarte was the wife of Yahweh”: unsupported; usually confusion with Asherah.
- “Astarte, Asherah, Anat, Ishtar, Isis, and Aphrodite were one universal goddess”: modern comparativist flattening.
- “Her priestesses universally practiced sacred prostitution”: unsupported.
- “The Carthage tophet was Astarte’s temple”: false as stated; inscriptions principally name Tanit and Baal Hammon.
- “Her universal sacred number was eight,” or fixed correspondences to Friday, emerald, rose, a tarot card, or a chakra: modern occult systematization unless a named modern source is specified.
- “Astaroth preserves an unchanged secret cult of Astarte”: unsupported.
- “Every nude female Levantine figurine depicts Astarte”: unsupported iconographic overidentification.

No substantial modern extremist appropriation comparable to the use of some Germanic or classical symbols is securely documented for Astarte. Individual nationalist uses exist, but claims of an organized Astarte-centered extremist movement cannot be established.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### From biblical goddess to archaeological figure

Early European scholarship began with the Hebrew Bible and classical testimony and therefore characterized Astarte as an alien, sexually immoral “fertility goddess.” Assyriology then linked her with Ishtar; Egyptology revealed a warrior and equestrian Astarte; Ugaritic discoveries exposed a distinct Athtart alongside Anat and Athirat; Phoenician epigraphy made her a dynastic and civic institution.

### Ishtar–Astarte relationship

The names are cognate and ancient scribes sometimes equated them, but modern scholarship increasingly distinguishes:

- linguistic relationship;
- scribal translation;
- functional borrowing;
- local cult identity;
- later interpretive fusion.

The volume *Transformation of a Goddess: Ishtar–Astarte–Aphrodite* usefully frames these as historical transformations rather than a single goddess traveling unchanged.

### Astarte versus Anat at Ugarit

Older scholarship sometimes transferred Ishtar’s entire love-and-war profile to Astarte. Ugaritic narrative gives Anat the more spectacular martial role. Astarte nevertheless hunts, restrains Baal, counteracts hostile forces, participates in curses, and has a specialist singer. Calling her either “the supreme Ugaritic goddess” or a completely “colorless deity” is too simple.

### Sexuality and “fertility goddess”

The category “fertility goddess” is often so broad that it explains nothing. Nude imagery, reproductive concerns, royal continuity, and Aphrodite identification support sexual and generative dimensions, but the securely named textual evidence emphasizes different functions in different places. Astarte’s sexual sphere should not be erased, yet neither should it swallow her political, military, astral, healing, and maritime roles.

### Aphrodite’s origins

Phoenician and Cypriot cults demonstrably contributed to the historical environment in which Greek Aphrodite developed, and ancient Greeks equated Aphrodite with Levantine goddesses. A simple linear formula “Ishtar became Astarte became Aphrodite” is inadequate. Greek, Cypriot, Aegean, and Near Eastern traditions interacted over a long period.

### Ashtoreth’s vowels

The “vowels of *bōšet*, shame” explanation remains influential. Because consonantal Hebrew writing and later vocalization are separated in time, absolute certainty is unavailable. Scholars must distinguish the ancient consonantal divine name from the medieval Masoretic pointing.

### Astarte and the Queen of Heaven

Jeremiah does not name the Queen of Heaven. Identifying her with Astarte, Ishtar, Anat, or another astral goddess is comparative reconstruction. The title may also have been applicable to more than one goddess. It cannot safely be entered as a uniquely Astarte cult title in Judah.

### Iconographic attribution

Elizabeth Bloch-Smith, Izak Cornelius, and others have emphasized how rarely an image is accompanied by an identifying inscription. “Astarte figurine” is often an archaeological convention. Secure identifications should privilege named monuments, cult context, and repeated local types.

### Child sacrifice

The tophet debate is not solved by choosing either “Roman propaganda” or “mass systematic sacrifice.” Competing osteological age estimates, the meaning of *mlk* inscriptions, the mixture of animal and human deposits, and biased classical witnesses must be evaluated together. Astarte’s dossier must also resist transferring evidence from Tanit and Baal Hammon to her.

### Sacred prostitution

The once-dominant model is now widely criticized. Ambiguous vocabulary, classical ethnography, and sexualized assumptions about Near Eastern goddesses were mutually used to confirm one another. The Kition tariff does not establish prostitution.

### Primary-evidence condition

- Ugaritic tablets are chiefly held in Syrian collections associated with the Damascus and Latakia/Ras Shamra excavations; war and access conditions have complicated continued study.
- The Tabnit sarcophagus is in Istanbul; the Eshmunazar sarcophagus and an Astarte throne are in the Louvre.
- The Fitzwilliam Museum holds the mounted-goddess ostracon.
- Pyrgi’s tablets are held in the National Etruscan Museum, Villa Giulia, Rome.
- Phoenician and Punic stelae are dispersed among Beirut, Tunis, Carthage, Paris, London, Istanbul, and other collections.
- Philo’s claimed Phoenician source is lost; only fragments survive through Eusebius and other authors.
- Menander’s Phoenician history is lost; Josephus preserves excerpts.
- pAmherst IX is physically fragmentary, so a major Astarte myth has no recoverable ending.

### Principal modern works

- Mark S. Smith, *The Early History of God*, for Israelite religion’s relationship to its West Semitic setting.
- John Day, *Yahweh and the Gods and Goddesses of Canaan*, especially the chapter on Astarte and Anat.

### Critical synthesis

The evidence supports an enduring name and a chain of historically related goddesses, but not a single invariant personality. The earliest records disclose little beyond cultic existence; Ugarit reveals a huntress and royal-apotropaic goddess; Egypt makes her a mounted protector of kings; Sidon and Tyre make her priesthood an institution of sovereignty; Cyprus and Pyrgi document negotiated translation into Aphrodite and Uni; Roman antiquarians reconstruct a royal astral myth; medieval Christianity converts the name into Astaroth; modern religion and popular culture rebuild Astarte from fragments. Those transformations—not an artificially harmonized biography—are her documented history.
