# The Kotharat: Research Dossier

**Class:** Collective of female deities—normally a heptad—associated with conception, marriage, pregnancy, and childbirth. “Divine midwives” is convenient modern shorthand, but the preserved narratives explicitly show them enabling conception and blessing marriage; their presence at an actual delivery is reconstructed, not preserved.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Old Babylonian administrative and offering texts from Mari and its Middle Euphrates religious sphere, approximately the eighteenth century BCE, naming cognate collectives as **Kawašurātum/Kuwasūratum** and later **Kûšarātum/Kušarātum**. The best-documented narrative attestations are the thirteenth-century BCE tablets from Ugarit, especially the *Aqhat* story, KTU 1.17, and the *Marriage of Yarikh and Nikkal*, KTU 1.24.

**Scope warning:** The Kotharat are securely Bronze Age Syrian and Ugaritic deities. No Phoenician or Punic inscription presently establishes their continued worship under this name. Connections with Philo of Byblos’s divine heptads, the Cabeiri, or later Phoenician religion are scholarly comparisons or reconstructions—not evidence for an unbroken Phoenician-Punic Kotharat cult.

## Evidentiary labels

- **Securely attested early material:** Direct Bronze Age textual or administrative evidence.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** A later but established form within an identifiable textual tradition.
- **Regional variant:** A local cognate, translation, or functionally comparable divine group.
- **Learned systematization:** Scribal god-list equations or theological ordering.
- **Syncretistic identification:** An explicit or inferred equation between traditions.
- **Late accretion:** An ancient or medieval claim substantially later than the Bronze Age evidence.
- **Folklore:** Vernacular survival supported by ethnographic or comparable documentation.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** A hypothesis built from damaged texts, comparison, or inference.
- **Modern invention:** A claim without ancient documentation or traceable to recent popular or occult writing.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

The Ugaritic **Kotharat** are not one goddess but a plural collective. Their principal preserved functions are to overcome childlessness, apportion or prepare the “delights” of conception and childbirth, and participate in a divine marriage composition. In KTU 1.17 they enter the household of the childless Danel, are feasted for six days, depart on the seventh, and are causally connected with the conception of Aqhat. In KTU 1.24 they appear in a wedding poem involving the moon god Yarikh and Nikkal and receive a concluding hymn.

Their collective nature is explicit; that they numbered precisely seven is highly probable but not equally explicit in every occurrence. The seven-day hospitality sequence in *Aqhat*, the apparent enumeration in KTU 1.24, and their equation with other divine heptads support the conventional number seven. Some analyses have instead produced four or six members because the final section of KTU 1.24 is damaged and its grammar uncertain.

### Names and scripts

- **Ugaritic:** 𐎋𐎘𐎗𐎚, alphabetic **kṯrt**. Because the script ordinarily records consonants, **Kôṯarātu**, **Kotharat**, **Kathirat**, **Katiratu**, and **Kôṯarātu** are modern vocalizations or spelling conventions, not variant vocalizations written by Ugaritic scribes. **Securely attested early material.**
- **Mari Akkadian spellings:** forms conventionally read **Kawašurātum/Kuwasūratum** and **Kûšarātum/Kušarātum**, written with the divine determinative. ARM 24 no. 263 preserves a later spelling approximately *dKu-šu-[ra-tum]*. **Securely attested early material/regional forms.**
- **Emar:** **Ilū kašarāti**, written *DINGIR.MEŠ ka-ša-ra-ti*, “the kašarātu gods/deities.” **Securely attested early regional form.**
- **Akkadian translation at Ugarit:** **Šassūrātu**, a collective associated with the womb and birth. In a multilingual god list the Ugaritic Kotharat are aligned with Mesopotamian Šassūrātu and Hurrian birth/fate goddesses. This is a **learned scribal equation**, not proof that the names designated historically identical beings everywhere.
- **Hurrian correspondence:** **Ḫutena and Ḫutellura**. Their equation with the Kotharat is documented in scholarly god-list tradition; whether the Hurrian goddesses were partly modeled on Syrian birth-goddess collectives remains a **modern historical reconstruction**. Alfonso Archi argues for Syrian influence; Piotr Taracha stresses that the Hurrian evidence can also describe a pair rather than seven individuals.

### Etymology

The prevailing derivation connects the name with the Semitic root represented by Akkadian **kašāru**, “to achieve, put in order, repair,” and Ugaritic **kṯr**, “skilled, clever, wise.” The collective can therefore be rendered “the Skillful Ones,” “the Accomplishers,” or “the Successful Ones.” Hebrew *kôšārāh*, “success/prosperity,” is a possible lexical comparison.

The masculine craftsman god **Kothar-wa-Ḫasis** has a cognate name. That linguistic relationship does **not** establish that he was their brother, father, consort, or master. No surviving Ugaritic narrative stages an interaction between Kothar-wa-Ḫasis and the Kotharat. Any presentation of “Kothar and his seven female assistants” as an attested family is a **modern harmonization**.

### Epithets

#### *bnt hll*

KTU 1.24 and related poetic language call the Kotharat **bnt hll**, literally “daughters of hll.” Interpretations include:

- “daughters of **Hulelu**,” treating *hll* as a divine name;
- “daughters of the crescent,” comparing Arabic *hilāl*, “new/crescent moon”;
- “daughters of brightness/radiance”;
- “daughters of purity.”

The existence of an Emar deity Hulelu makes a personal name possible, while the lunar setting of KTU 1.24 favors the crescent interpretation for some scholars. Neither the vocalization nor the meaning is certain. Consequently “Hulelu is their father” is best labeled **possible early genealogy**, not settled fact.

#### *snnt*: “swallows” or “radiant ones”?

Older translations commonly joined *bnt hll snnt* as “daughters of the crescent, the swallows,” comparing Akkadian *sinuntu*, “swallow.” This produced the memorable “swallows of Aqhat’s conception.”

Dennis Pardee and Aicha Rahmouni instead connect *snnt* with brightness or radiance, comparing Arabic *sanā*, “shine, gleam,” and West Semitic vocabulary for glittering or refining. Rahmouni renders the phrase approximately “the luminaries, the radiant ones.” She observes that luminous divine epithets have good ancient Near Eastern parallels, whereas a class of swallow-shaped birth goddesses does not.

Accordingly:

- **The word *snnt* is securely attested.**
- **“Swallows” is a disputed older philological interpretation.**
- **Actual transformation into birds, swallow iconography, and flight into Danel’s house are not stated in KTU 1.17.**
- **“Radiant/shining ones” is influential modern philological interpretation, not an unquestionably established translation.**

Other expressions applied to them in *Aqhat* concern the bed of conception and childbirth. Wyatt translates them as those who apportion “the delights of the bed of conception” and “the joys of the bed of childbirth”; exact English wording varies because the Ugaritic is difficult.

### Alleged individual names

The final hymn in KTU 1.24 may enumerate individual Kotharat. A commonly reconstructed list is:

1. **Ṯlḫh**
2. **Mlgh**
3. **Yṯtqt**
4. **Bqʿt**
5. **Tqʿt**
6. **Prbḫṯ**
7. **Dmqt**, apparently called the youngest

Watson omits *Yṯtqt* and therefore obtains a different count. Suggested interpretations connect these words with bridal property, separation or extraction, splitting, clapping, and goodness. Theuer, for example, associated *Yṯtqt* and *Bqʿt* with separation or opening at delivery, but these are speculative etymologies.

A competing reading treats some or all of the sequence not as personal names but as ordinary nouns, ceremonial designations, or language addressed to a mortal bride. *Prbḫṯ* may be a woman blessed by the Kotharat rather than one of them; *Dmqt* can be read as an epithet, “the beautiful/fair one,” or as referring collectively to the goddesses.

Thus the “seven named wise women” are a **plausible but contested reading of an early text**, not an uncontested Ugaritic roster.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Mari and the Middle Euphrates

The oldest secure witnesses are administrative or cultic rather than narrative. Old Babylonian Mari records birth goddesses called Kawašurātum/Kûšarātum among offerings and pantheon lists. They belong to the cultic sphere of Terqa and the Middle Euphrates and occur near other mother, palace, and birth deities. These documents establish that a cognate divine collective existed by the early second millennium BCE; they do not supply a myth, iconography, genealogy, or individual names.

The spelling variation may reflect chronological, dialectal, or scribal developments. It should not be converted into a sequence of separate goddesses without evidence.

### Ugarit: archaeological context and dating

Ugarit’s alphabetic cuneiform tablets were excavated at Ras Shamra on the Syrian coast beginning in 1929. Most relevant literary and ritual manuscripts belong to the city’s Late Bronze Age archives, principally the thirteenth century BCE, before Ugarit’s destruction around 1190–1185 BCE. Composition could precede the surviving copies, but precise earlier dates are usually unrecoverable.

### KTU 1.17–1.19: the *Aqhat* tablets

**Language:** Ugaritic.  
**Support:** Clay tablets in alphabetic cuneiform.  
**Surviving manuscript:** Late Bronze Age, generally thirteenth century BCE.  
**Kotharat passage:** KTU 1.17 II.

Danel has no son. Baal appeals to El on his behalf, and El blesses him with the prospect of an heir. Danel returns home and sees or receives the Kotharat. He sacrifices and supplies food and drink to them:

- day one and a second;
- a third and fourth;
- a fifth and sixth;
- on the seventh, the Kotharat leave.

The text associates them with the bed of conception and childbirth. The following two columns are lost. When the text resumes, Aqhat exists and Kothar-wa-Ḫasis brings the bow that will propel the later tragedy.

What is secure:

- the Kotharat enter Danel’s conception narrative;
- Danel hosts them for six days;
- they depart on the seventh;
- their action is connected with obtaining Aqhat.

What is reconstructed:

- that they return for the actual delivery;
- that the missing columns narrated them functioning as midwives;
- that their seven-day visit proves there were exactly seven;
- that they were literally swallows.

Michaela Budin has proposed that the missing columns contained Aqhat’s birth and the return of the Kotharat, possibly accompanied by Danel’s wife Danataya. This is a coherent **modern reconstruction**, but no surviving line describes that event.

### KTU 1.24 = RS 5.194: *Marriage of Yarikh and Nikkal*

**Language:** Ugaritic, with strong Hurrian and Mesopotamian cultural connections.  
**Support:** Clay tablet in alphabetic cuneiform.  
**Date:** Late Bronze Age, probably thirteenth century BCE.  
**Genre:** Disputed—wedding myth, hymnic composition, ritual recitation, or a composite of these.

The opening invokes Nikkal-wa-Ib, daughter of Ḫarḫab. Yarikh seeks her in marriage, negotiates through Ḫiriḫibi, and offers silver, gold, lapis lazuli, and agricultural abundance as bride-price. The composition repeatedly invokes the Kotharat and ends with a hymn to them.

The closing lines call them daughters of *hll* and *snnt*, then present the difficult sequence sometimes read as seven names. The text links them to marriage, sexuality, conception, and possibly the expected birth of a divine child.

Calling them “the seven wise women of the marriage text” is broadly defensible if carefully qualified:

- “wise/skilled” reflects the probable etymology of *kṯrt*;
- “seven” is the conventional reconstruction;
- “women” means goddesses, not human officiants;
- whether the closing words are seven divine names remains disputed.

A Hurrian Vorlage has sometimes been proposed because Nikkal derives from Mesopotamian Ningal and because the text’s divine relationships have northern Syrian/Hurrian features. Other scholars prefer an Ugaritic adaptation of Hurrian motifs rather than direct translation.

### KTU 1.10 II 30 and KTU 1.11: fragmentary mythic references

The Kotharat appear in fragmentary mythological passages, including the collective term **hbl** or a similarly read expression. Damage and lack of narrative context prevent a secure reconstruction. These witnesses establish their circulation beyond the two best-known compositions but do not sustain a separate myth.

### Ritual and god-list texts

The collective is attested or restored in:

- **KTU 1.47:13**
- **KTU 1.118:12**
- **KTU 1.130:20–21**, though the reading/restoration is disputed
- **KTU 1.148:5, 25**
- **KTU 4.412 II 5**, an administrative text
- **RS 20.24**, a multilingual pantheon list
- **RS 24.643**, another list or ritual witness

KTU 1.47 and 1.118 are parallel divine lists in different scribal settings. Their order places the Kotharat among major cosmic and cultic powers, although the theological meaning of list order is debated. RS 20.24 gives an Akkadian equivalent, **Šassūrātu**, “birth/womb goddesses.” Such lists document official scribal knowledge and cultic inclusion; they do not necessarily describe popular belief.

KTU 1.130 is particularly cautionary: some restorations supply *kṯrt* from expected divine nomenclature rather than clearly preserved signs. It must be labeled **uncertain attestation**.

### Emar

Late Bronze Age Emar texts record **Ilū kašarāti**. Their presence confirms that the collective was not unique to Ugarit and supports a broader northern Syrian/Middle Euphrates tradition. No identified temple or securely Kotharat-derived theophoric name is known from Emar. The evidence is cult-list evidence, not narrative mythology.

### Trilingual god-list equation

A Ugaritic multilingual god list aligns:

- Ugaritic **Kotharat**
- Akkadian **Šassūrātu**
- Hurrian **Ḫutena and Ḫutellura**

This is the clearest ancient comparative-theological statement about them. It shows what learned Late Bronze Age scribes considered corresponding divine offices. It does not prove common origin, identical numbers, or identical myths.

### Texts named in the broader Canaanite brief but not actually witnesses

- **The Baal Cycle:** Does not contain a preserved episode involving the Kotharat as actors. Baal’s role in Danel’s fertility belongs to *Aqhat*, not the Baal Cycle.
- **Kirta:** No secure Kotharat episode survives. Kirta’s marriages and fertility problems provide cultural comparison, not direct attestation.
- **Amarna letters:** No secure Kotharat occurrence.
- **Hebrew Bible:** No accepted occurrence. Albright’s proposed Kotharat in Psalm 68:7 is rejected; the Hebrew is not a disguised divine name. Biblical prohibitions or polemics against “Canaanite” religion are therefore not direct testimony about this collective.
- **Phoenician and Punic inscriptions, including KAI:** No secure inscription naming the Kotharat has been identified.
- **Philo of Byblos via Eusebius:** Contains divine groups that have been compared with the Kotharat, but not a transparent preservation of their name.
- **Lucian, *De Dea Syria*:** No Kotharat.
- **Herodotus and Josephus:** No identifiable Kotharat episode or cult.
- **Carthage tophet evidence:** No dedication names them. The tophet debate cannot be attached to them.
- **Beth Shemesh tablet:** Albright’s proposed Kotharat reading is no longer accepted.

These absences materially limit any claim that they belonged continuously to the Iron Age Phoenician and Punic pantheons.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

The documented domains form a connected reproductive sequence:

- marriage and wedding blessing;
- sexual union;
- conception;
- successful pregnancy or formation of the fetus;
- childbirth;
- perhaps neonatal fate and prosperity.

The strongest direct evidence is for conception and marital fertility. “Midwives” and “birth goddesses” are supported by comparative lists and the language of childbirth but should not erase Pardee’s objection: in the intact narrative they intervene before pregnancy, while the presumed delivery scene is missing.

The equation with Šassūrātu, whose name is related to Akkadian *šassūru*, “womb,” strengthens the uterine/birth interpretation. The comparison with Hurrian fate goddesses raises the possibility that they also determined a newborn’s fate, but no surviving Ugaritic narrative explicitly has them pronounce Aqhat’s destiny.

### Powers and limitations

They can:

- remedy a ruler’s childlessness;
- sanctify or enable conception;
- receive sacrificial hospitality;
- participate in or bless a wedding;
- possibly form the embryo and assist delivery.

They are not shown:

- ruling a city;
- commanding war or weather;
- receiving a monumental temple;
- judging the dead;
- opposing another deity;
- acting independently in a sustained narrative.

Their collective agency is ritual and reproductive rather than sovereign.

### Iconography

No surviving image is securely inscribed or otherwise identified as the Kotharat. Consequently they have no securely established:

- physical appearance;
- dress;
- emblem;
- weapon;
- animal;
- plant;
- color;
- sacred number beyond the inferred seven;
- day of the week;
- planet or constellation.

A Tell Chuera relief showing seven female figures has been tentatively connected with the Kotharat or analogous birth goddesses. Without an identifying inscription, this remains **modern iconographic conjecture**.

The “swallow” translation of *snnt* has encouraged modern swallow imagery, but no ancient artifact depicts seven swallows and labels them Kotharat. Swallows should therefore not be presented as their established sacred animals.

### Astral associations

The expressions *bnt hll* and *snnt* can be read in luminous or lunar terms, and KTU 1.24 concerns a moon god. This may give them an astral complexion—daughters of a crescent, brightness, or luminaries—but there is no securely identified star group or constellation called Kotharat. “Pleiades,” “seven stars,” and similar identifications are speculative unless a separate source is produced.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to divine beings

### Genealogical possibilities

#### Daughters of *hll*

KTU 1.24 calls them *bnt hll*. If *hll* is a god’s name, he is their father. If it means crescent, brightness, or purity, the phrase is an epithet rather than genealogy. **Contested early evidence.**

#### Daughters or partners of El?

Some god-list arrangements place the Kotharat after El, and Pardee has entertained the possibility that list order preserves a theogonic scheme. A proposed comparison with Philo’s seven daughters of Elos/Kronos would then make them daughters of El.

Against this, Lluís Feliu argues that a list may contain a scribal error, **kṯrt** for **aṯrt** (Athirat), and notes that the corresponding syllabic entry uses the singular mother-goddess logogram *dNIN.MAH*. The evidence therefore does not securely establish El as their father or sexual partner.

#### Relationship to Kothar-wa-Ḫasis

Their names share the “skill/achievement” root. No preserved genealogy relates them. Cognation is not kinship. **Absence of documented interaction.**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Danel’s childlessness and the conception of Aqhat

The *Aqhat* story opens with Danel performing the duties of an exemplary ruler while lacking a son. Baal notices or responds to his condition and approaches El. El blesses Danel: he will have a son who can perform filial duties, erect his ancestral stele, and maintain household and mortuary obligations.

Danel returns to his palace. The Kotharat appear, described with language variously translated as daughters of brightness, crescent daughters, radiant ones, or swallows. Danel provides sacrificial animals, food, and drink. The poem counts the days formulaically. For six days he feeds them; on the seventh they leave. Their titles associate them with the bed of conception and the bed of childbirth.

Two columns are lost. The narrative resumes with Aqhat already present and Kothar-wa-Ḫasis delivering an extraordinary bow. Anat later covets it, Aqhat refuses her, and Yatpan kills him through Anat’s plot. The Kotharat never re-enter the preserved story.

Interpretive history:

- **Early core:** divine authorization by Baal and El, ritual hospitality to the Kotharat, and the production of an heir.
- **Secure function:** facilitation of conception and dynastic continuity.
- **Probable but unpreserved development:** pregnancy and Aqhat’s birth occupied the missing columns.
- **Speculative detail:** the Kotharat returned as midwives and worked with Danataya.
- **Rejected embellishment:** seven literal swallows flew into the palace and transformed into women. That is not preserved in the tablet.

The episode integrates household fertility with royal and ancestral continuity. Danel needs not merely a child but a son capable of funerary, social, and patrimonial duties. The Kotharat therefore operate at the junction of sexuality, inheritance, and the maintenance of the lineage.

### The marriage of Yarikh and Nikkal

KTU 1.24 begins hymnicly with Nikkal-wa-Ib and introduces Yarikh’s desire to marry her. Through Ḫiriḫibi he negotiates a bride-price of silver, gold, lapis lazuli, and agricultural fertility. Alternative brides are mentioned, but Yarikh insists on Nikkal.

The Kotharat appear in invocations distributed through the poem and dominate its closing hymn. The final section may call upon them to bless a bride and future child; alternatively, it may enumerate the goddesses themselves. Their presence makes the poem not merely a divine romance but a reproductive sequence: courtship, marriage exchange, union, conception, and expected issue.

The composition may have been recited at human weddings, but no rubric says so. Its wedding-liturgical function is a **scholarly inference** from content and form.

Variants are primarily philological:

- whether the poem is native Ugaritic, translated Hurrian, or an Ugaritic composition using Hurrian material;
- whether Nikkal is “born of” the Kotharat;
- whether *Prbḫṯ* is a bride or a goddess;
- whether the final words are seven names;
- whether *snnt* means swallows or radiant ones.

### Fragmentary Kotharat material

KTU 1.10 and 1.11 preserve too little context for a complete narrative. Modern reconstructions sometimes attach them to reproductive myth or ritual performance, but their damaged state forbids a separate continuous story.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Mari and Terqa

Administrative offering texts are the strongest evidence for an institutional cult outside Ugarit. The Kûšarātum occur among deities receiving provisions within the palace-temple economy of Mari and Terqa. This establishes scheduled or official offerings but does not identify a dedicated temple, priesthood, or festival.

Their association with Šalaš, Nikkal, palace goddesses, and other female reproductive powers suggests a cultic cluster concerned with maternity and dynastic fertility.

### Ugarit

#### Offerings

In *Aqhat*, Danel slaughters or supplies animals and provides food and drink over six days. Although embedded in myth, the detailed hospitality probably reflects recognizable sacrificial practice: divine guests are sustained by repeated offerings, and the seventh day completes the sequence.

Ritual lists name the Kotharat among recipients. Administrative KTU 4.412 may record allocations involving them. These data establish cultic recognition beyond literary imagination.

#### Priesthood and sanctuary

No temple has been securely identified as a “Temple of the Kotharat.” No named priest, priestess, or guild is securely assigned to them. Their rites may have been conducted in domestic, palace, or general temple settings, but this is inference.

Their reproductive function makes participation by women likely; the texts do not tell us whether human midwives served them, whether women controlled their rites, or whether special birth rooms existed.

#### Festivals and calendar

The seven-day feast in KTU 1.17 is mythic narrative, not an explicitly calendrical festival. It may encode a ritual pattern, but no annual “Festival of the Kotharat” is documented.

#### Theophoric names

A possible Ugaritic personal name **bn kṯrt**, “son of the Kotharat,” has been proposed. If correct, it shows personal devotion or acknowledgment of divine assistance in birth. Because the reading is isolated, it cannot establish how common their popular cult was.

### Emar

The *Ilū kašarāti* occur in cultic documentation, but there is no securely identified temple or priestly office and no robust body of theophoric names. Their place appears integrated into larger civic ritual systems.

### Human sacrifice, temple prostitution, and cult violence

No source connects the Kotharat with:

- human or infant sacrifice;
- the Carthage tophet;
- temple prostitution or “sacred marriage” enacted by cult personnel;
- cultic violence;
- military rites;
- coerced sexual activity.

The Carthage tophet controversy concerns dedications principally to Baal Hammon and Tanit and is chronologically, geographically, and epigraphically separate. Attaching it to the Kotharat merely because they are “Canaanite fertility goddesses” would be unsupported.

Likewise, KTU 1.24’s sexual and nuptial language is not evidence for institutional “temple prostitution,” a category whose indiscriminate use in ancient Near Eastern studies has itself been heavily criticized.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Early second millennium BCE: Middle Euphrates emergence

The Mari/Terqa forms provide the earliest secure horizon. Archi reconstructs an inland Syrian origin followed by diffusion along the Middle Euphrates. They were already a collective of birth-related goddesses rather than a late Ugaritic literary invention.

### Late Bronze Age: regional integration

At Ugarit they became part of:

- courtly epic;
- wedding poetry;
- ritual and offering lists;
- multilingual scribal theology.

At Emar they appear under an Akkadian designation. The Ugaritic equivalence with Šassūrātu and Ḫutena/Ḫutellura demonstrates international theological translation among West Semitic, Akkadian, and Hurrian traditions.

This is the evidentiary peak: numerous texts of different genres, not necessarily the historical peak of worship.

### Around 1190 BCE: destruction of Ugarit

The destruction of Ugarit ended the archive and the particular literate institution that preserved the Kotharat. No source describes a formal suppression of their cult. Their disappearance from documentation is better explained by the collapse of Late Bronze Age palace-temple and scribal systems than by a recorded campaign against them.

### Iron Age Phoenicia

No secure Phoenician inscription names them. It is possible that birth-goddess functions continued under other divine names or in domestic practice, but possible continuity is not evidence for survival of the Kotharat as a named collective.

Philo of Byblos, writing in Greek in the late first or early second century CE and surviving principally through Eusebius, gives Phoenician divine heptads:

- seven daughters of Elos/Kronos;
- the Cabeiri/Dioscuri/Corybantes/Samothracians, sons of Sydyk.

Pardee has compared Philo’s seven daughters with the Kotharat and an Ugaritic list. The comparison is structurally suggestive but cannot demonstrate identity. Philo does not preserve the name *Kotharat*, and his text combines Phoenician material, Greek translation, antiquarian systematization, and euhemerism.

The male maritime Cabeiri are an even weaker identification: their sex, genealogy, and functions differ. Treating them as the Kotharat transformed into Phoenician sailor gods is a **modern speculative synthesis**.

### Punic world and Carthage

Neither the Punic inscriptional corpus nor tophet stelae securely preserve the Kotharat. They have no demonstrated temple, priesthood, cult title, image, or offering tariff in Carthage. “Punic Kotharat” should therefore be understood as a broad cultural classification sometimes imposed by modern summaries, not as a documented cult.

### Jewish, Christian, and Islamic periods

The Hebrew Bible never names them securely. There is no historical report of their temple being destroyed by Israelites, Christians, or Muslims. Eusebius preserved Philo’s Phoenician material for Christian apologetic purposes, but not a recognizable Kotharat cult.

No continuous folk practice under their name is documented in medieval Syria, Lebanon, Palestine, or North Africa.

### Modern rediscovery

Their effective modern reappearance began with the discovery of Ugarit in 1929 and the publication and decipherment of its alphabetic tablets. Before those finds, no continuous community is known to have remembered or worshiped “the Kotharat” by that name.

---

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

The Kotharat have had a much smaller modern afterlife than Baal, Anat, Astarte, or Asherah.

### Scholarship and reference literature

Translations popularized several durable images:

- “seven divine midwives”;
- “daughters of the crescent”;
- “swallows”;
- “seven wise women.”

All have a basis in scholarly readings, but none should be repeated without qualification. In particular, the swallow imagery often appears as if it were an illustrated myth rather than one disputed translation of *snnt*.

### Neopaganism and reconstructionism

Individual modern practitioners sometimes invoke the Kotharat in Canaanite or Levantine reconstructionist religion, especially in connection with fertility, pregnancy, and childbirth. No centralized organization, standardized liturgy, or reliable membership count has been documented. Any current ritual is necessarily reconstructed or newly composed because no complete ancient Kotharat liturgy survives.

Claims of an unbroken lineage of Kotharat priestesses from Bronze Age Ugarit are **modern inventions unless supported by evidence not presently known**.

### Occultism and ceremonial magic

The Kotharat do not have a documented role in Renaissance grimoires, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Crowley’s corpus, classical Theosophy, or standard early-modern demonologies. Modern lists that assign them planets, tarot cards, sigils, chakras, colors, or angelic correspondences are recent occult constructions.

They are not demons in the Ugaritic sources. Describing them as “seven demons,” succubi, or infernal wives is **modern invention** unless explicitly framed as creative adaptation.

### Literature, games, and commercial culture

They remain obscure and have not acquired a stable mass-media image. Modern fantasy treatments tend to extrapolate from “seven,” “midwives,” or “swallows.” Such representations are modern retellings rather than transmitted mythology.

### Political and extremist appropriation

No substantial nationalist, racialist, or extremist cult of the Kotharat is documented. General Phoenicianist or “Canaanite revival” discourse sometimes incorporates reconstructed pantheons, but the Kotharat have not independently become a major political emblem.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Discovery and editions

The decipherment of Ugaritic after 1929 transformed knowledge of Late Bronze Age Syrian religion. The principal reference system is **KTU/CAT**, *Die keilalphabetischen Texte aus Ugarit*, which assigns the familiar numbers 1.17 and 1.24. Earlier literature may cite CTA or UT numbers.

Major English presentations include:

- Mark S. Smith and Wayne Pitard’s work on Ugaritic literature and the Baal Cycle;
- Simon B. Parker, ed., *Ugaritic Narrative Poetry*;
- Nicolas Wyatt, *Religious Texts from Ugarit*;
- Dennis Pardee’s editions and studies of Ugaritic ritual texts;
- the *Context of Scripture* translations.

The Kotharat do not play a substantive role in the Baal Cycle itself, so Baal Cycle commentaries are contextual rather than their principal textual editions.

### Number and individuality

The traditional seven rests on converging evidence rather than one simple statement. KTU 1.24 may enumerate them, *Aqhat* uses seven-day structure, and comparative birth-goddess groups are heptadic. Damage to the text and uncertainty over whether the closing terms are names allow alternative counts.

### “Midwives” versus agents of conception

Older and popular literature calls them divine midwives. Pardee emphasizes that the intact *Aqhat* passage situates their decisive work before pregnancy. Budin reconstructs their return during the missing birth scene. The responsible conclusion is that they are reproductive goddesses whose portfolio includes conception and is likely, but not fully demonstrably, extended to delivery.

### *Snnt*: swallows versus shining ones

This is the most visible lexical dispute. The Akkadian “swallow” comparison produced an attractive image but lacks iconographic corroboration. Rahmouni’s luminosity interpretation fits broader divine epithet traditions and Arabic/Aramaic comparisons. Neither reading should be silently treated as fact.

### Who is *hll*?

Proposals include:

- a personal god Hulelu;
- the crescent moon;
- brilliance;
- purity;
- an association with Enlil.

The Kotharat’s lunar environment in KTU 1.24 supports but does not prove “crescent.” Calling them “daughters of the moon god” overstates the evidence.

### Individual names in KTU 1.24

Theuer’s seven-name reconstruction creates a coherent body of birth functions. Watson and other translators divide the syntax differently. The tablet’s state prevents final certainty. Consequently lists of seven neatly translated goddess names are editorial reconstructions, not a transparent ancient catalogue.

### Origin and cultural transmission

Archi sees the Syrian Kotharat tradition as influential on the Hurrian Ḫutena and Ḫutellura. The multilingual lists prove perceived equivalence by Ugaritic scribes but not the direction of borrowing. KTU 1.24’s Hurrian features further complicate any simple “Canaanite original versus foreign borrowing” scheme.

### Proposed Phoenician survival

Comparison between Ugaritic list structure and Philo’s seven daughters of El is suggestive. However:

- there is a gap of more than a millennium between the Ugaritic tablets and Philo;
- Philo writes Greek under the Roman Empire;
- his claimed Sanchuniathon source cannot be independently recovered;
- the relevant group is unnamed as Kotharat;
- the Ugaritic list itself may contain a scribal problem.

The proposal belongs to **comparative historiography**, not the secure corpus.

### Discredited identifications

- Kotharat in Psalm 68: rejected.
- Kotharat on a Beth Shemesh tablet: rejected.
- Direct identity with Kothar-wa-Ḫasis’s female entourage: unattested.
- Secure Punic cult at Carthage: unattested.
- Association with tophet sacrifice: unattested.
- Literal swallow-form iconography: unattested.
- A continuous occult tradition: unattested.

### State of the evidence

The corpus is small but unusually varied: narrative tablets, a marriage hymn, ritual lists, multilingual god lists, and administrative records. Its chief limitations are physical damage, uncertain vocalization, and the disappearance of most everyday domestic practice. Excavated archives privilege palace and scribal institutions; absence from them does not prove absence from ordinary childbirth rites, but such rites cannot be reconstructed in detail.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Event in the record |
|---|---|
| Early second millennium BCE | Cognate Kawašurātum/Kuwasūratum appear in the Mari–Terqa religious sphere. Earliest secure attestation. |
| Eighteenth century BCE | Mari pantheon and offering documents record Kûšarātum/Kušarātum among birth-related deities. |
| Fourteenth–thirteenth centuries BCE | The collective circulates in Late Bronze Age northern Syrian religion; Emar knows *Ilū kašarāti*. |
| Thirteenth century BCE | Ugaritic scribes copy KTU 1.17: Danel hosts the Kotharat in the conception of Aqhat. |
| Thirteenth century BCE | KTU 1.24 invokes them in the marriage of Yarikh and Nikkal and may enumerate seven members. |
| Thirteenth century BCE | Ritual and deity lists—KTU 1.47, 1.118, 1.148, RS 20.24, and related texts—record them in official and learned religion. |
| Thirteenth century BCE | Multilingual scribal theology equates them with Šassūrātu and Ḫutena/Ḫutellura. |
| c. 1190–1185 BCE | Ugarit is destroyed. Its archive closes; no text documents deliberate suppression of the Kotharat. |
| Iron Age | No secure Phoenician inscription names them. Continuity under another name remains unproved. |
| First–second century CE | Philo of Byblos composes a Greek *Phoenician History* containing divine heptads later compared with the Kotharat. |
| Early fourth century CE | Eusebius preserves substantial fragments of Philo in *Praeparatio Evangelica*. |
| Medieval–early modern periods | No documented continuous Kotharat cult, folklore, grimoire tradition, or ecclesiastical suppression. |
| 1929 onward | Excavation of Ras Shamra/Ugarit recovers the tablets that make modern reconstruction possible. |
| 1930s–1970s | Successive editions and translations identify them as a collective of birth and marriage goddesses. |
| Late twentieth century | Comparative work links Mari, Emar, Ugaritic, Mesopotamian, and Hurrian witnesses; “seven divine midwives” becomes standard shorthand. |
| 1990s–2000s | Pardee and others refine the ritual corpus and question whether “midwives” overstates the preserved narrative evidence. |
| 2012 | Aicha Rahmouni argues extensively that *snnt* means radiant or luminous beings rather than swallows. |
| 2010s–present | Gender-oriented and comparative studies revisit the missing birth scene, the supposed individual names, and Syrian-Hurrian transmission. |
| Present | Small-scale reconstructionist invocation exists, but no institutional continuity, established ancient liturgy, or measurable organized cult is documented. |
