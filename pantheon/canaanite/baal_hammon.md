# Baal Hammon: Research Dossier

**Class:** male god; an Iron Age Syro-Phoenician *baʿal* (“lord”) who became the principal male recipient of Punic worship at Carthage and across much of the Punic western Mediterranean. Under Roman rule, related African cults increasingly addressed their god as Saturn, conventionally “Saturnus Africanus.”

**Earliest secure attestation:** the Phoenician-language inscription of King Kilamuwa from Samʾal/Zincirli, southeastern Anatolia, conventionally dated about **825 BCE** (KAI 24). Baal Hammon appears in its closing curse beside Baal Ṣemed and Rakabʾel. The inscription proves that a deity called *BʿL ḤMN* was invoked in ninth-century BCE Syro-Anatolia; it does **not** establish that all his later Carthaginian functions, iconography, relationship with Tanit, or sacrificial rites already existed there.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** contemporary inscription, artifact, archaeological context, or near-contemporary documentary evidence.
- **[LE] Later ancient evidence:** Greek, Latin, Christian, or other testimony substantially later than the practices described.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a local form, title, image, or practice not safely generalizable.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** a historically situated equation or translation between gods.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** ancient or modern harmonization that creates a genealogy or theological identity not stated by the primary cult evidence.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference from incomplete evidence.
- **[MI] Modern invention or reception:** post-antique literary, occult, political, or popular material.
- **[UE] Unestablished:** often repeated but not demonstrated by surviving evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Name

**[EA]** Phoenician and Punic inscriptions write the name without vowels:

> 𐤁𐤏𐤋 𐤇𐤌𐤍 — **BʿL ḤMN**

The first element, *baʿl*, securely means “lord,” “master,” or “owner.” It is a title used in many Northwest Semitic divine names and does not by itself identify Baal Hammon with the storm-god Baal/Hadad of Ugarit.

Greek inscriptions from El-Hofra render the name **ΒΑΛ ΑΜΟΥΝ** and, in the dative, **ΒΑΛ ΑΜΟΥΝΙ**; Latin evidence has dative **BALAMONI**. These transcriptions support the conventional vocalization *Baʿal Ḥammōn/Hammon*, although the exact Punic vowels remain unrecoverable.

### The disputed meaning of *ḤMN*

No derivation commands consensus.

- **“Lord of the brazier” or “burning lord.” [MSR]** This connects *ḥmn* with Hebrew *ḥammānîm*, cultic installations variously interpreted as incense altars, solar pillars, or fire stands. It became especially attractive when Baal Hammon was associated with cremated offerings. The argument risks circularity: the supposed fire cult explains the name, while the name is then used to prove a fire cult.
- **“Lord of the incense burner.” [MSR]** A related cult-object interpretation, again philologically possible but not demonstrated by an ancient gloss.
- **“Lord of the sanctuary/cult installation.” [MSR]** Proposed from possible cultic meanings of *ḥmn*.
- **“Our protector.” [MSR]** A more recent title-based interpretation; it remains debated.
- **A geographic title: “Lord of Ḥammon.” [MSR]** One proposal connects the western god with a locality near Tyre, conventionally identified with Umm el-ʿAmed.
- **“Lord of Mount Ḥamānu/Amanus.” [MSR]** Frank Moore Cross and others connected the Zincirli god with the Amanus mountain range. Ugaritic and Akkadian evidence contains *ḥmn/Ḫamānu* as a mountain name and divine or personal-name element. This may suit the Zincirli occurrence particularly well.
- **Contextually different homonyms. [MSR]** André Caquot and subsequent discussion allow that the “Baal Hammon” of Zincirli might be “lord of the Amanus,” while a Levantine or western *BʿL ḤMN* arose from another local referent. Orthographic identity need not prove an unchanged trans-Mediterranean personality.
- **“Lord of the multitude/abundance.” [UE]** This popular etymology usually confuses Phoenician **ḥmn** with a different Semitic root or with Hebrew *hāmôn*. It lacks decisive epigraphic support.
- **A derivation from Egyptian Amun. [UE]** The resemblance between *Hammon* and Greek *Ammon* encouraged ancient and modern comparisons, but no inscription establishes that Baal Hammon’s name means “Baal-Amun.” Zeus-Ammon and Jupiter-Ammon have their own Egyptian-Libyan history and must not automatically be collapsed into Baal Hammon.

The safest translation is therefore **“Lord Ḥammon”**, leaving the second element untranslated.

### Titles and descriptions

- **ʾDN, “Lord.” [EA]** Frequent in Punic dedicatory formulas: *lʾdn lbʿl ḥmn*, “to the Lord, to Baal Hammon.”
- **QDŠ, “holy/sacred.” [EA]** Attested on several inscriptions.
- **BL BT, “master/lord of the house.” [EA/RV]** An El-Hofra inscription presents him in this capacity, plausibly as guardian of a household or extended family.
- **Baal Qarnaim, “Lord of the Two Horns.” [UE/MSR]** Frequently connected in modern literature with Jebel Boukornine, the double-peaked mountain near Carthage, and with ram imagery. The particular title and a securely identified Baal Hammon sanctuary on the mountain require more caution than popular accounts normally show.
- **“Chief god” or “supreme god of Carthage.” [MSR]** His enormous inscriptional presence justifies calling him Carthage’s principal male cult recipient. It does not prove a formally codified monarchic theology. From approximately the fifth century BCE, Tanit often precedes him in dedicatory formulas.
- **Kronos. [SI/LE]** Greek authors translated the recipient of Carthaginian child sacrifice as Kronos. This is an ancient interpretive equivalence, not his Punic name.
- **Saturnus/Saturn. [SI]** Roman African worship adopted Saturn’s name and imagery through a gradual, regionally uneven transformation rather than a single official renaming.
- **Zeus. [SI/uncertain]** Polybius’ Greek version of Hannibal’s treaty with Philip V invokes Zeus, Hera, and Apollo first. Identifying these individually as Baal Hammon, Tanit, and Eshmun or Resheph is plausible but reconstructed; Polybius does not supply the Punic original.
- **El, Dagan, Baal/Hadad, Amun. [LS/MSR]** These are scholarly identity proposals based on function, seniority, etymology, or interpretatio, not names securely equated with Baal Hammon in Punic narrative texts.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Zincirli: KAI 24

**[EA, c. 825 BCE]** The Kilamuwa stele is a monumental Phoenician inscription from Samʾal. Its curse says, in substance, that if a successor damages the monument, Baal Ṣemed, Baal Hammon, and Rakabʾel will smash his head. Baal Hammon is associated with a named dynastic or household context rendered *Bamah/BMH* in modern translations.

What it establishes:

- the divine name existed in ninth-century BCE Syro-Anatolia;
- the god could enforce an inscriptional curse;
- he belonged to a group of locally invoked divine protectors.

What it does not establish:

- a connection with Tanit;
- supremacy over a pantheon;
- agricultural or solar functions;
- a tophet or *mlk* rite;
- identity with the Ugaritic storm-god;
- direct institutional continuity to Carthage.

### Levantine and Mediterranean inscriptions

**[EA]** A lapis-lazuli amulet from the Tyrian region names **BʿL ḤMN** with **BʿL ṢPN**, Baal Saphon. The joint invocation documents proximity, not a surviving myth or family relationship.

**[EA]** A painted amphora from Cyprus preserves **BLḤMN**. Other attestations follow Phoenician and Punic settlement networks to Malta, Motya, Palermo, Lilybaeum, Grotta Regina, Sulcis, Tharros, perhaps Monte Sirai, and Olbia.

**[EA]** North African evidence occurs at Carthage, Hadrumetum/Sousse, Dougga, Téboursouk, Maktar, Mididi, and Cirta/Constantine, among other sites. At Carthage alone, thousands of stelae or fragments belong to the tophet corpus.

A standard formula is:

> *lrbt ltnt pn bʿl wlʾdn lbʿl ḥmn … kšmʿ qlʾ brkʾ*  
> “To the Lady Tanit, Face of Baal, and to the Lord Baal Hammon … because he heard his voice; may he bless him.”

**[EA]** The formulas describe a vow, gift, deed, or offering and divine response—“he heard his voice” and “blessed him.” Most do not name the substance of the offering.

### KAI, CIS, and museum corpora

The essential inscriptional repositories include:

- *Kanaanäische und Aramäische Inschriften* (**KAI**), especially KAI 24 and Punic texts;
- *Corpus Inscriptionum Semiticarum*, Part I (**CIS I**);
- regional collections from Carthage, El-Hofra, Malta, Sicily, Sardinia, and North Africa;
- the Louvre’s 2024 scholarly catalogue of **1,383 Carthaginian stelae**, integrating epigraphy, iconography, collection history, and technical study.

The fourth-century BCE **Mitsri genealogy inscription** (KAI 78/CIS I 3778) was reportedly associated with a statue dedicated to Baal Hammon and gives a dedicant’s genealogy over sixteen generations. It is evidence of elite memory and votive patronage, not of the god’s genealogy.

### What the named comparative corpora do—and do not—contain

- **Ugaritic texts: Baal Cycle, Kirta, Aqhat. [EA/comparative]** These Late Bronze Age Ugaritic works illuminate Northwest Semitic divine vocabulary and the storm-god Baal/Hadad’s battles with Yamm and Mot. They do **not** name Baal Hammon as the protagonist. Mark S. Smith and Wayne Pitard, Wyatt, and Parker provide critical editions or translations; their narratives must not be imported wholesale into Baal Hammon’s dossier.
- **Amarna letters. [EA/comparative]** Fourteenth-century BCE Akkadian correspondence documents Canaanite rulers, cities, and divine/theophoric vocabulary, but supplies no secure dossier for Baal Hammon.
- **Hebrew Bible. [EA/hostile comparative witness]** Biblical denunciations of “the Baals,” *Topheth*, and passing children “through the fire” to *Molech* are crucial for West Semitic sacrificial comparison. Song of Songs 8:11 mentions a place called **Baal-hamon**, not securely this god. No biblical passage unambiguously narrates Baal Hammon’s cult.
- **Philo of Byblos via Eusebius. [LE/LS]** Philo’s Greek *Phoenician History*, known chiefly through Eusebius’ fourth-century CE *Praeparatio Evangelica*, says that Elos/Kronos sacrificed his only son in a national crisis. This is important comparative Phoenician learned mythology. Identifying Philo’s El-Kronos directly with Baal Hammon is a reconstruction, not Philo’s explicit statement.
- **Lucian, *De Dea Syria*. [LE/comparative]** Describes Syrian cult at Hierapolis and wider Phoenician ritual analogies, but does not provide a securely attributable Baal Hammon narrative.
- **Herodotus. [LE/comparative]** His western Phoenician and Libyan ethnography helps contextualize Carthage but contains no clear named Baal Hammon episode.
- **Josephus. [LE/comparative]** Preserves Phoenician historical traditions and biblical antiquarian argument but no independent Baal Hammon myth.
- **Polybius 7.9. [LE/SI]** Preserves in Greek the oath of Hannibal’s treaty with Philip V, dated 215 BCE. The first triad is Zeus, Hera, Apollo, followed by the daimon of Carthage, Heracles, Iolaus, and others. Proposed Punic identities remain reconstructions.
- **Diodorus Siculus 20.14. [LE]** First century BCE, describing the crisis during Agathocles’ invasion in 310 BCE. He calls the god Kronos and gives the fullest ancient child-sacrifice account.
- **Plutarch, *De superstitione* 13/171C–D. [LE]** Late first or early second century CE; alleges voluntary parental participation, purchased poor children, throat-cutting, and music drowning lamentation.
- **Tertullian, *Apologeticum* 9. [LE]** About 197 CE; says infants were publicly sacrificed to Saturn in Africa until a proconsul punished the priests and claims clandestine continuation.
- **Augustine. [LE]** Late fourth–early fifth century CE; his discussions attest the continuing cultural prominence of African Saturn, but are Christian polemic rather than neutral Punic ethnography.

### Missing corpus

No surviving Punic hymn, theology, epic, prayer book, calendar, or priestly commentary tells Baal Hammon’s biography. The immense votive corpus records transactional devotion—vows, offerings, hearing, blessing—rather than narrative mythology. Any full “myth of Baal Hammon” assembled from Ugaritic Baal, Philo’s El-Kronos, Greek Kronos, biblical Molech, and Egyptian Amun is a modern composite.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Securely documented functions

- **Receiver of vows and offerings. [EA]** This is his best-attested function.
- **God who hears petitioners and blesses them. [EA]** Explicit in repeated tophet formulas.
- **Enforcer of curses. [EA/RV]** Kilamuwa’s inscription.
- **Household protector. [EA/RV]** Suggested by *BL BT* at El-Hofra.
- **Senior civic or supra-household power. [MSR]** Inferred from the scale and distribution of dedications and his position in divine lists.

### Reconstructed functions

- **Agriculture, vegetative fertility, rain, and abundance. [MSR/SI]** Strongly represented in Roman African Saturn’s imagery and rural cult, but only indirectly recoverable for Punic Baal Hammon.
- **Sky god. [MSR/SI]** Plausible through Baal terminology and later interpretatio, but not directly defined in Punic prose.
- **Solar god. [MSR]** Proposed from disk-and-crescent symbols, *ḥammānîm*, and Roman African iconography. The evidence is not decisive.
- **Chthonic or funerary god. [MSR]** Inferred from cremated deposits and later Saturn. Tophet dedications are votive rather than ordinary funerary inscriptions, so “god of the dead” is too strong.
- **Fertility god. [MSR/SI]** Common and plausible, especially under Saturn, but not stated in an extant Punic hymn.

### Images

**[EA/RV]** The most secure anthropomorphic image comes from **Thinissut/Tnsmt** near modern Bir Bou Regba, Tunisia. A terracotta statuette approximately 38.5–40 cm high, dated broadly to the second–first centuries BCE, depicts a mature bearded god:

- seated frontally on a throne;
- wearing a high *kalathos*-like headdress;
- flanked by sphinxes;
- raising one hand;
- projecting age, authority, and beneficent presence.

Its sanctuary was active from Punic times, perhaps the fifth century BCE, into the Roman imperial period. Inscriptions from the sanctuary name Baal Hammon and Tanit, making the identification substantially firmer than free-standing “horned god” identifications.

**[EA/RV]** A fifth–fourth-century BCE Hadrumetum stele shows a bearded, long-haired god seated on a sphinx-flanked throne, holding a long staff or sceptre and raising his right hand toward a much smaller worshipper.

**[MSR]** Ram horns, ram-supported thrones, and horned heads are frequently attributed to Baal Hammon. Some objects may instead represent Zeus-Ammon, Amun, or a local Cypriot god using shared iconography. Horns are not a universal diagnostic.

### Symbols

- **Disk and crescent. [EA/iconographic attribution]** Common on stelae dedicated to Baal Hammon and to Baal Hammon with Tanit. Its precise celestial meaning is disputed.
- **“Sign of Tanit.” [EA]** A trapezoidal or triangular body, horizontal bar, and circular head appears extensively in the same cultic environment. It should not automatically be called Baal Hammon’s personal symbol.
- **Raised right hand. [EA/RV]** A gesture of presence, greeting, authority, or blessing; meaning is context-dependent.
- **Sceptre or staff. [EA/RV]** Marks enthroned authority.
- **Sphinx throne. [EA/RV]** Links his imagery to broader eastern Mediterranean traditions of divine and royal enthronement.
- **Ram. [EA/SI/RV]** Important in some western and Roman-African images, but not a universal exclusive emblem.
- **Agricultural tools, grain, fruit, bulls, and sheep. [SI/RV]** Especially characteristic of African Saturn stelae. Their projection backward onto Punic Baal Hammon is probable in some contexts but not automatic.

No securely ancient color, sacred number, weekday, or canonical festival calendar for Baal Hammon survives. Saturday, the planet Saturn, and Saturnalia belong first to Roman Saturn and only secondarily to Baal Hammon through interpretatio.

## 4. Genealogy and relations with divine beings

### Genealogy

**[UE]** No securely identified Punic text names Baal Hammon’s parents, siblings, birth, offspring, or cosmic succession. No ancient Punic family tree survives.

**Tanit. [EA/MSR]** Tanit is his consistent female cult partner at Carthage. The formula “Lady Tanit, Face of Baal” commonly places her before “Lord Baal Hammon.” Calling her his **consort** is reasonable shorthand for cult partnership, but no surviving myth describes their marriage or sexuality.

**Astarte. [EA/uncertain]** A Mididi inscription reportedly gives Astarte a title interpreted as “wife of Baal.” Whether “Baal” there means Baal Hammon specifically is uncertain. It cannot simply be merged with the Tanit evidence.

**El/Kronos. [LS/MSR]** Philo’s Elos/Kronos is often treated as a theological ancestor or identity behind Baal Hammon. No Punic inscription says “Baal Hammon is El.”

**Dagan. [MSR]** Edward Lipiński’s proposed identification emphasizes the senior agricultural god Dagan. It remains a scholarly hypothesis.

**Baal/Hadad. [MSR/UE]** Baal Hammon’s first element is the title *Baal*, but the Ugaritic storm-god’s genealogy and myths cannot be assigned to him without independent evidence.

### Every documented god–god interaction or joint appearance

Because no surviving Baal Hammon narrative cycle exists, these are principally co-invocations and historical equations.

- **Baal Ṣemed — co-enforcer of Kilamuwa’s curse.** KAI 24, c. 825 BCE. **[EA]**
- **Rakabʾel — co-enforcer of the curse and dynastic “lord of the house.”** KAI 24, c. 825 BCE. **[EA]**
- **Baal Ṣapon/Baal Saphon — jointly named on a Tyrian-region lapis-lazuli amulet.** Iron Age date according to its editor; exact chronology remains debated. **[EA]**
- **Tanit — receives joint dedications with Baal Hammon; usually styled “Face of Baal.”** Carthaginian and western Punic inscriptions, especially fifth–second centuries BCE. **[EA]**
- **Baal Shamem — precedes Tanit and Baal Hammon in a Carthaginian divine list.** Punic inscription. **[EA]**
- **Baal Magnim/BʿL MGNM — follows Baal Hammon in the same list.** Punic inscription. **[EA; reading/meaning uncertain]**
- **Astarte — possibly titled “wife of Baal” at Mididi.** Punic/Neo-Punic inscription; reference to Baal Hammon is not certain. **[EA/uncertain]**
- **Kronos — Greek authors identify the Carthaginian sacrificial recipient as Kronos.** Cleitarchus tradition, Diodorus 20.14, Plutarch *De superstitione*. **[SI/LE]**
- **Heracles/Melqart — receives renewed Carthaginian gifts to Tyre in the same crisis in which Kronos is propitiated.** Diodorus 20.14, describing 310 BCE. This is coordinated crisis worship, not a mythic meeting. **[LE]**
- **Zeus, Hera, Apollo — first triad in the Greek text of Hannibal’s oath with Philip V.** Polybius 7.9, treaty of 215 BCE. Identification of Zeus as Baal Hammon and Hera as Tanit is likely but unproved without the Punic original. **[SI/LE]**
- **Saturn — Roman and African worshippers progressively use Saturn’s name, images, and Latin cult language for a god historically related to Baal Hammon.** Late Punic through imperial centuries. **[SI]**
- **Juno Caelestis — Roman-African cult partner commonly related to Tanit, appearing beside African Saturn in the transformed divine pair.** Imperial period. **[SI]**
- **Elos/El — Philo calls Elos “Kronos,” but does not explicitly name Baal Hammon.** Philo of Byblos via Eusebius, first–fourth centuries CE transmission. **[LS/LE]**
- **Amun/Zeus-Ammon/Jupiter-Ammon — iconographic and onomastic comparison.** Hellenistic and Roman contexts; direct identity with Baal Hammon is not securely demonstrated. **[SI/MSR]**
- **Molech/Moloch — later interpretive conflation through child-sacrifice traditions and the consonants *mlk*.** Biblical interpretation, scholarship after Eissfeldt, and especially modern popular culture. *Mlk* in Punic is normally treated as a rite or offering category, not securely as another god. **[LS/MI]**

There is no securely attested episode in which Baal Hammon fights, marries, deceives, rescues, or judges another named god.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The absence of a Baal Hammon epic

**[EA]** No indigenous narrative myth centered on Baal Hammon survives. His “complete mythology” cannot be reconstructed because the relevant Punic literary corpus is lost.

### The crisis sacrifice of 310 BCE

**[LE]** Diodorus Siculus, writing in the first century BCE, describes Carthage during Agathocles’ invasion of Africa in 310 BCE. The Carthaginians concluded that Kronos was hostile because elite families had formerly sacrificed their own best sons but had lately purchased and raised substitute children.

Diodorus says:

- an investigation exposed the substitutions;
- two hundred elite children were publicly offered;
- as many as three hundred others offered themselves;
- a bronze image of Kronos held its hands sloping downward;
- children placed on the hands rolled into a fiery pit.

This is the nearest thing to a surviving Baal Hammon narrative, but it is historical-sacrificial exemplum, not Punic myth. Diodorus does not use the name Baal Hammon in the passage; the identification follows the Greek interpretatio and the Punic cult context.

### Plutarch’s ritual tableau

**[LE]** Plutarch alleges that Carthaginians knowingly offered their children, that childless people purchased children from the poor, and that mothers were forbidden to weep. Flutes and drums supposedly covered the cries.

The account is rhetorically embedded in an argument that superstition is worse than atheism. It may preserve sacrificial tradition, moralizing elaboration, or both. Its throat-cutting differs from the popular image of children burned alive inside a hollow idol.

### El-Kronos sacrifices his only son

**[LE/LS]** Philo of Byblos says Elos/Kronos, threatened by war, dressed his “only-begotten” son by the nymph Anobret in royal clothing and sacrificed him on an altar. The son is called *Iedoud/Jeoud*, explained through the Phoenician word for “only one.”

This is a Phoenician learned myth of royal substitution or exemplary sacrifice. It belongs directly to Philo’s El-Kronos, not demonstrably to Baal Hammon. Its frequent inclusion in Baal Hammon biographies results from the prior equation Baal Hammon = Kronos = El.

### Ugaritic Baal’s battles

**[EA but not Baal Hammon]** In the Baal Cycle, Baal defeats Yamm, builds a palace, confronts Mot, disappears, and returns. Anat attacks Mot. Athirat negotiates with El; Kothar-wa-Hasis makes weapons and constructs Baal’s palace. These are securely Ugaritic myths of Baal/Hadad, copied in the thirteenth century BCE from older traditions. No extant tablet calls their hero Baal Hammon.

Assigning Yamm, Mot, Anat, Athirat, Dagan, or Kothar as Baal Hammon’s narrative relations is therefore **modern harmonization**, not a documented interaction.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Carthage and the tophet

**[EA]** The so-called Tophet of Salammbô was an open-air sacred precinct containing superimposed deposits of urns with cremated human infants, young children, and animals, accompanied by votive monuments. Its use extended broadly from the eighth or seventh century until Carthage’s destruction in 146 BCE; detailed phase boundaries remain debated.

“Tophet” is a modern archaeological label borrowed from the Hebrew Bible, not a securely recovered Punic name for the precinct.

The material assemblage includes:

- urns with cremated remains;
- limestone stelae and cippi;
- dedications chiefly to Baal Hammon, later often jointly to Tanit;
- symbols including raised hands, disks and crescents, and the sign conventionally associated with Tanit;
- formulas recording a vow or gift and divine hearing;
- animal remains, especially sheep or goats, sometimes alone and sometimes commingled with human bone.

The precinct was discovered in 1921 by François Icard and Paul Gielly. Later campaigns included Kelsey and Harden, Chabot and Lapeyre, Pierre Cintas, and the 1970s ASOR/UNESCO expedition directed by Lawrence Stager. Poor early excavation records and the separation of stelae from urn contexts permanently limit interpretation.

### Did worshippers sacrifice children?

The answer must distinguish propositions.

#### What is secure

- **[EA]** Thousands of votive inscriptions were erected in a specialized sacred precinct.
- **[EA]** Urns contain cremated remains of fetuses or perinates, neonates, older infants, some children, and animals.
- **[EA]** The inscriptions differ from ordinary Carthaginian funerary epitaphs. They speak of vows, gifts, and offerings, not merely “the tomb of” a deceased person.
- **[EA]** The term **MLK** occurs in the sacrificial corpus and is best treated as a ritual or offering term; its exact vocalization and scope remain disputed.
- **[EA]** Expressions including *mlk ʾmr* are associated with an ovine offering and have been interpreted as an animal substitute.
- **[LE]** Multiple Greek and Latin authors accuse Carthaginians or Africans of child sacrifice.
- **[EA]** Cremation itself prevents osteology from determining cause of death. Burned bone can establish age and treatment, not whether a child died naturally before cremation.

#### Schwartz’s cemetery or mixed-mortuary interpretation

Jeffrey H. Schwartz, Frank Houghton, Roberto Macchiarelli, Luca Bondioli, and collaborators analyzed material from 348 urns representing a minimum of approximately 540 individuals.

Their 2010 and later studies argued:

- many individuals were prenatal, perinatal, neonatal, or under one month;
- even generous corrections for heat shrinkage left a substantial prenatal component;
- the age profile resembles high natural infant mortality;
- the tophet could have served as a special cremation cemetery for those excluded from ordinary cemeteries;
- the evidence does not support **systematic** sacrifice of all or most infants;
- ancient hostile testimony and modern expectations may bias interpretation.

Their estimates included approximately 24 percent prenatal, 15 percent peri-/neonatal, and 17 percent younger than one month in one analytical presentation. Critics dispute the aging procedures and especially the interpretation of dental neonatal lines.

#### Smith, Avishai, Greene, and Stager

Patricia Smith, Gal Avishai, Joseph Greene, and Lawrence Stager reanalyzed aging criteria and argued in 2011 and 2013 that:

- most infants died around one to one-and-a-half months;
- the distribution did not resemble an ordinary natural-mortality cemetery;
- deliberate selection is more probable;
- animal victims and human infants occupied parallel ritual contexts;
- inscriptions and ancient testimony strengthen the sacrificial reading.

They concluded that infant sacrifice was very probable, though osteology alone cannot demonstrate the manner of death.

#### Xella, Quinn, Melchiorri, and van Dommelen

Paolo Xella, Josephine Quinn, Valentina Melchiorri, and Peter van Dommelen emphasized that:

- the inscriptions are explicitly votive, not funerary;
- their formulas name acts of giving, vowing, doing, or offering;
- the combination of inscriptions, selected young remains, animal alternatives, specialized precincts, and ancient testimony is more economically explained as sacrifice;
- prenatal remains do not disprove sacrifice, since a child promised before birth might still be ritually offered after stillbirth.

#### Responsible conclusion

**[Contested/MSR]** The evidence strongly supports a sacrificial institution in which cremated animals and at least some young humans were deposited as votive offerings. It does not permit a secure count of live child killings, prove that every human deposit was a sacrifice, or show that the practice was constant across six centuries and every Punic site. A mixed practice—live sacrifice in some circumstances, deposition of naturally dead infants in others, and animal substitution—is possible but cannot presently be quantified.

The famous image of an enormous hollow bronze idol into which screaming children were continuously dropped is chiefly a combination of Diodorus, Plutarch, later Moloch imagery, and Flaubert. No such machine has been excavated.

### Tertullian and suppression

**[LE]** Tertullian states that infants were openly sacrificed to Saturn in Africa until a proconsul—his text’s wording has generated chronological confusion—had participating priests punished and exposed on trees. He says the rite continued secretly.

This is evidence that a North African Christian around 197 CE expected readers to recognize an association among Saturn, Africa, and child sacrifice. It is not contemporary proof that Baal Hammon’s Punic rite persisted unchanged into Tertullian’s day.

### Other sanctuaries

- **Thinissut. [EA]** Punic through Roman-period sanctuary with inscriptions naming Baal Hammon and Tanit and unusually secure terracotta divine images.
- **Hadrumetum/Sousse. [EA]** Tophet and stelae, including the enthroned god and worshipper.
- **El-Hofra at Cirta/Constantine. [EA]** Major Punic/Neo-Punic votive deposit with Phoenician, Greek, and Latin evidence; includes *ΒΑΛ ΑΜΟΥΝ* and household-protector language.
- **Dougga/Thugga. [EA/SI]** Sacred area shows movement from Punic or Numidian Baal Hammon cult to Roman Saturn worship.
- **Maktar, Téboursouk, Mididi, Sulcis, Tharros, Motya, Malta, and Lilybaeum. [EA/RV]** Local inscriptional and tophet traditions demonstrate the breadth of western cult, but practices varied.

### Priesthood, economics, and worshippers

**[EA]** Punic inscriptions preserve priestly titles elsewhere in the religious system, but the tophet corpus is dominated by individual and family dedications rather than a surviving institutional rulebook. Men and women could dedicate; genealogies, professions, and patronymics show participation across social strata.

**[MSR]** Diodorus depicts sacrifice as an elite civic obligation in crisis. The archaeological inscriptions more commonly present individual vows. Roman African stelae increasingly foreground the worshipper, civic rank, dress, tools, and sacrificial implements.

No securely reconstructable annual Baal Hammon festival calendar survives. Claims about a fixed “Baal Hammon day,” sacred number, temple prostitution, or universal priestly costume are unestablished.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 825 BCE: possible antecedents

The element *ḤMN* occurs in earlier Northwest Semitic personal names and perhaps divine-geographic vocabulary. An eleventh- or tenth-century BCE Byblian clay cone reportedly names a person **ʿBDḤMN**, “servant of ḤMN.” **[EA/uncertain]** This may preserve a divine name or name element, but it is not an unambiguous full attestation of Baal Hammon.

### Ninth–seventh centuries BCE: eastern attestations and transmission

KAI 24 attests the god at Zincirli around 825 BCE. The Tyrian-region amulet and Cypriot evidence indicate a wider eastern Mediterranean distribution. Whether Carthage inherited precisely the Zincirli god or developed a western homonymous form remains debated.

### Eighth/sixth centuries–fifth century BCE: establishment in the Punic west

Baal Hammon becomes a major recipient at Carthage and other western Phoenician settlements. The earliest tophet chronology is disputed, partly because of disturbed deposits and revised dating of the “Cintas chapel.”

### Fifth–third centuries BCE: Tanit’s rise and cultic pairing

Tanit increasingly appears as **Tanit Face of Baal** and is often named before Baal Hammon. This does not show Baal Hammon’s disappearance; it marks a transformed divine pairing and formulaic hierarchy. Joint dedications spread with Punic networks through North Africa and the central Mediterranean.

### 310 BCE: Agathoclean crisis

Diodorus’ later account places a mass propitiatory sacrifice to Kronos during Agathocles’ invasion. The episode is historically possible but known only through later Greek historiography.

### 215 BCE: diplomatic interpretatio

Hannibal’s treaty with Philip V, preserved by Polybius, translates or represents the Carthaginian divine witnesses in Greek. It demonstrates active cross-cultural divine interpretation but does not securely map every Greek name onto a Punic one.

### 146 BCE and after: destruction without immediate religious extinction

Rome destroyed Punic Carthage in 146 BCE, ending the old city’s political institutions and the main tophet sequence. Punic-speaking communities and regional cults nevertheless persisted. Baal Hammon-related worship continued outside Carthage and was progressively reformulated through Saturn.

### First century BCE–third century CE: Saturnus Africanus

**[SI]** In Roman Africa, Saturn becomes one of the most widely attested gods, especially in rural and inland sanctuaries. Older scholarship, above all Marcel Le Glay, stressed deep continuity: Baal Hammon essentially lived on under Saturn’s Latin name.

Recent scholarship complicates that model:

- bilingual and Neo-Punic/Latin dedications demonstrate continuity and translation;
- cult sites and votive forms persist;
- yet imagery, social display, sacrificial practice, and institutional setting change substantially;
- regional Saturns adopt local agricultural symbols—pruning hooks in grain country, forestry tools in the Aurès—and should not be treated as a frozen Punic survival.

The best description is **continuity through transformation**, uneven by locality.

### Third–fifth centuries CE: Christian contest and decline

Tertullian and Augustine show that African Saturn remained rhetorically and socially important. By the 360s, cities such as Timgad still had traditional pontiffs and augurs. Imperial restrictions on sacrifice, Christianization of civic institutions, closure or abandonment of sanctuaries, and conversion of landscapes progressively ended public cult.

There is no documented continuous Baal Hammon priesthood through the medieval Islamic period. Claims of uninterrupted secret survival are **[UE]**.

### Medieval to early modern period

Baal Hammon largely disappears as a living named cult figure. Classical and Christian accounts of Carthaginian sacrifice remain available through manuscript traditions, while “Moloch,” Kronos, Saturn, and generic “Baal” accumulate demonological associations. These later constructions are not evidence for Punic belief.

### Nineteenth century to present

Archaeology, colonial collection, Flaubert’s fiction, and modern debates resurrect Baal Hammon as a named historical figure. Small contemporary polytheist communities discuss or worship him, but no reliable census or continuous inherited liturgy exists. Their rites are reconstructionist or innovative.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Flaubert’s *Salammbô*

**[MI, 1862]** Gustave Flaubert’s novel set during the Mercenary War decisively shaped the modern image of Carthage. Its mass child-sacrifice scene synthesizes classical accusations, biblical Moloch traditions, nineteenth-century Orientalism, and imaginative staging.

Flaubert wrote before excavation of the Carthage tophet in 1921. Subsequent discoveries made the novel appear prophetic, but archaeological evidence does not validate every detail of its colossal brass idol, mechanical arms, continuous holocaust, or the conflation Baal Hammon = Moloch.

The novel generated paintings, sculpture, opera, cinema, comics, and games. Modern visual culture often reproduces Flaubert more faithfully than Punic material culture.

### “Moloch,” demons, and occultism

**[MI]** Early-modern and modern demonologies frequently treat “Baal” as a demon, but their Baal normally derives from biblical polemic, Beelzebub, or grimoire traditions—not from documented worship of Baal Hammon.

Specific modern conflations include:

- Baal Hammon as the literal biblical god Moloch;
- Baal Hammon as a demon of Saturn;
- identity with the grimoire spirits Bael or Aamon;
- continuity with Baphomet because both can be horned;
- a secret Saturn cult governing finance, Freemasonry, entertainment, or political elites.

None is established by Punic evidence. The Baphomet image created by Éliphas Lévi in the nineteenth century has no demonstrated descent from the Thinissut statuette.

### Conspiracy and extremist appropriation

**[MI]** Online “Saturn-cube,” antisemitic, anti-Masonic, and “occult elite” conspiracy milieus use Baal Hammon or Moloch as labels for alleged elite child abuse. These narratives fuse Carthaginian sacrifice accusations with medieval blood libel, modern Satanic-panic imagery, and partisan rhetoric. They are modern ideological products, not survivals of Punic religion.

No evidence found establishes an organized modern extremist cult actually worshipping Baal Hammon.

### Reconstructionist worship

**[MI]** Contemporary Canaanite, Phoenician, Punic, and broader polytheist practitioners sometimes honor Baal Hammon and Tanit. Online communities explicitly distinguish historical reconstruction from personal gnosis, though individual practice often combines Punic formulas with modern devotional forms.

Because no Baal Hammon liturgy survives, every complete modern ritual necessarily reconstructs or invents:

- pronunciation;
- hymnody;
- calendars;
- ethical theology;
- domestic altar practice;
- divine personality and private revelation.

### Civic and commercial memory

Baal Hammon’s name appears in museums, exhibitions, streets, cultural associations, historical festivals, fiction, and tourist interpretation. A “Baal-Hammon” contingent remains part of Cartagena’s modern Carthaginians and Romans festival. This is civic historical performance, not evidence of revived ancient sacrifice or institutional cult.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Major interpretive phases

1. **Classical and Christian polemic.** Diodorus, Plutarch, Tertullian, and later Christian authors fixed the association of Carthage with child sacrifice.
2. **Nineteenth-century comparative religion.** Biblical “Moloch,” Phoenician religion, Kronos, Saturn, solar worship, and “Semitic sacrifice” were frequently harmonized into one system.
3. **Flaubertian reception.** *Salammbô* supplied a visual and emotional template before archaeological excavation.
4. **Tophet discoveries from 1921.** Cremated infant and animal bones appeared to confirm classical accounts, though skepticism arose immediately.
5. **Corpus-based epigraphy.** KAI, CIS, and regional publications clarified that the monuments are votive and that *mlk* is a ritual term.
6. **Le Glay’s continuity model.** *Saturne africain* presented African Saturn as the Roman continuation of Baal Hammon, with agrarian, celestial, funerary, and universal functions.
7. **Bioarchaeological dispute.** Schwartz and collaborators argued for natural mortality and special infant burial; Smith, Stager, Greene, and Avishai defended sacrificial selection.
8. **Integrated ritual interpretation.** Xella, Quinn, Melchiorri, and van Dommelen insisted that bones, animals, inscriptions, precinct form, and literary testimony be interpreted together.
9. **Postcolonial and material-semiotic revision.** Recent work challenges an overly simple “Semitic god unchanged beneath a Roman name,” emphasizing regional remaking, imperial social structures, and changes in how stelae presented gods and worshippers.

### The sacrifice dispute: strongest arguments

| Question | Sacrificial reading | Cemetery/mixed reading |
|---|---|---|
| Inscriptions | Votive verbs, vows, gifts, *mlk*, divine hearing | A vow could commemorate or ritually dedicate a naturally dead child |
| Animal remains | Animals function as victims or substitutes | Animal and infant burial together does not establish killing |
| Age profile | Selection around early infancy differs from ordinary mortality | High prenatal/neonatal mortality explains concentration |
| Ancient texts | Multiple writers independently allege sacrifice | Writers are late, hostile, rhetorical, and sometimes mutually dependent |
| Precinct character | Specialized sanctuary unlike ordinary cemetery | Specialized cemetery for infants excluded from adult necropolises |
| Cause of death | Ritual assemblage makes sacrifice probable | Cremation prevents direct proof of ante-mortem killing |
| Best limited conclusion | At least some human sacrifice likely | “All tophet children were sacrificed” is not demonstrated |

### Identity debate

- **Baal Hammon as El.** Supported by seniority, Kronos identification, and comparative sacrifice traditions; not inscriptionally explicit.
- **Baal Hammon as Dagan.** Lipiński’s functional and historical proposal; contested.
- **Baal Hammon as Baal/Hadad.** Tempting because of the name *Baal*, weather/fertility associations, and mountain etymology; weakened by the absence of storm-god narratives and distinct Carthaginian profile.
- **Baal Hammon as Amun/Zeus-Ammon.** Encouraged by Greek spelling and ram imagery; no decisive identity statement.
- **Indigenous Libyan substrate.** Likely relevant to Roman African regional development, but simplistic claims that Baal Hammon was purely Libyan conflict with the eastern Zincirli and Levantine evidence.
- **One god or several homonymous Baals?** A serious possibility: Zincirli’s “lord of Amanus,” the Tyrian-region god, and Punic Baal Hammon may reflect linked but not identical local formations.

### Primary evidence and its custody

- KAI 24 is held in Berlin’s Vorderasiatisches Museum.
- Important Carthaginian stelae are in Tunisian collections, especially the Bardo and Carthage museums.
- The Louvre holds 1,383 stelae studied in its recent catalogue.
- Thinissut’s enthroned terracotta god is in the Bardo National Museum.
- Other material is dispersed among the British Museum, archaeological museums in Sicily and Sardinia, and regional North African collections.

The original Punic libraries of Carthage are lost. The surviving record is therefore structurally biased toward durable votive stone, cremation deposits, hostile outsiders, and Roman-period reinterpretation.

- **Eighth/seventh century BCE:** earliest phases often assigned to the Carthage tophet; exact beginning disputed. **[EA/disputed dating]**

- **Fifth century BCE onward:** Tanit increasingly appears with and before Baal Hammon in Carthaginian formulas. **[EA]**

- **Fourth century BCE:** KAI 78/CIS I 3778 commemorates a statue for Baal Hammon and an unusually long dedicant genealogy. **[EA]**
- **310 BCE:** crisis during Agathocles’ invasion; Diodorus later reports mass sacrifice to Kronos. **[LE]**
- **215 BCE:** Hannibal–Philip treaty invokes a Greek-named divine hierarchy, probably translating Carthaginian gods. **[LE/SI]**
- **Second–first centuries BCE:** Thinissut enthroned terracotta image; sanctuary bridges Punic and Roman phases. **[EA]**
- **146 BCE:** Roman destruction of Carthage ends the principal tophet sequence. **[EA]**
- **First century BCE–first century CE:** Baal Hammon/Saturn translation becomes increasingly visible in North Africa. **[SI]**
- **First century BCE:** Diodorus writes the principal surviving account of Carthaginian sacrifice. **[LE]**
- **Late first–early second century CE:** Plutarch gives his moralizing sacrifice description. **[LE]**
- **c. 197 CE:** Tertullian says African infant sacrifice to Saturn had been suppressed publicly but persisted secretly. **[LE]**
- **Second–third centuries CE:** African Saturn cult flourishes in rural and urban sanctuaries; regional iconographies proliferate. **[EA/SI]**
- **Fourth century CE:** traditional African priesthoods and Saturn devotion remain visible while Christian imperial restriction intensifies. **[EA/LE]**
- **Late fourth–early fifth century CE:** Augustine polemicizes in an environment where Saturn remains culturally intelligible. **[LE]**

- **1920s–1930s:** major excavations recover thousands of urns and monuments; sacrificial and cemetery interpretations compete. **[modern archaeological event]**
- **1966:** Marcel Le Glay’s *Saturne africain* systematizes the continuity model. **[historiography]**
- **1970s:** UNESCO rescue campaign and ASOR excavations under Stager produce the major modern osteological sample. **[modern archaeological event]**
- **2010:** Schwartz and collaborators publish the large-sample argument against systematic infant sacrifice. **[historiography]**
- **2011:** Smith, Avishai, Greene, and Stager argue that revised aging supports sacrifice. **[historiography]**
- **2013:** competing responses sharpen the dispute; Xella and collaborators stress the integrated epigraphic and ritual evidence. **[historiography]**
- **2017:** Schwartz and collaborators defend their methods and reject an all-sacrifice interpretation. **[historiography]**
- **1979–present:** the archaeological site of Carthage, including the tophet, is protected as part of a UNESCO World Heritage property.
- **2024:** the Louvre publishes its multidisciplinary catalogue of 1,383 Punic stelae dedicated principally to Tanit and Baal Hammon.
- **2026:** Baal Hammon remains a subject of museum work, academic debate, limited reconstructionist devotion, online conspiracy reinvention, and civic historical performance; no continuous ancient cult has been demonstrated.
