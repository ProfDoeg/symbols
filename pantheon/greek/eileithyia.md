# Eileithyia: Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess; in Homer occasionally a plural collective of birth-goddesses, the **Eileithyiai**.  
**Tradition:** Mycenaean and ancient Greek religion; subsequently identified in Greek and Roman literature with Roman **Lucina**, especially Juno Lucina and sometimes Diana Lucina.  
**Earliest secure attestation:** Mycenaean Greek **𐀁𐀩𐀄𐀴 / e-re-u-ti-ja**, conventionally reconstructed as *Eleuthia*, on the Linear B tablet **KN Gg(3) 705** from Knossos, approximately the fourteenth–thirteenth century BCE. The tablet records one amphora of honey “at Amnisos, to Eleuthia.” This establishes a named divine recipient, an Amnisos association, and a honey offering; it does **not** itself identify her as a childbirth goddess or prove that the excavated Amnisos cave was the precise sanctuary intended.

## Evidentiary labels

- **Securely attested early material:** Mycenaean Linear B, Homeric epic, early Greek poetry, or securely identified Archaic material evidence.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** Hellenistic or Roman-period mythography developing older stories.
- **Regional variant:** A locally attested genealogy, epithet, image, ritual, or narrative.
- **Learned systematization:** Ancient antiquarian, philosophical, etymological, or mythographic ordering of inconsistent traditions.
- **Syncretistic identification:** A historically situated equation with Hera, Artemis, Lucina, or another divine figure.
- **Medieval or early-modern accretion:** Post-antique interpretation without demonstrable ancient cultic continuity.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** An inference from linguistic, archaeological, or comparative evidence.
- **Modern invention:** A claim first demonstrable in modern occult, neopagan, popular, or commercial material.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

Eileithyia is the Greek divine power whose presence permits, governs, eases, or obstructs childbirth. In Homer the name can be singular or plural. The singular Eileithyia “brings the child into the light” in *Iliad* 16.187 and is expected to do so in 19.103; the plural Eileithyiai inflict the “bitter pangs” of childbirth in 11.269–272 and are called daughters of Hera in 19.118–119. This variation is securely early and should not be converted into a firm doctrine that there were originally exactly two goddesses. It demonstrates that birth-power could be personified either as one named goddess or as a functional plurality.

By the Hesiodic *Theogony* 921–923, Eileithyia is one individual goddess, daughter of Zeus and Hera and sister of Ares and Hebe. Her cult nevertheless remained highly local: Cretan cave goddess, Delian helper of Leto, Athenian goddess represented by three concealed wooden images, Achaean torch-bearer, Olympian partner or mother of Sosipolis, and Tegean divinity attached to Auge’s labor.

### Documented names and spellings

- **𐀁𐀩𐀄𐀴, e-re-u-ti-ja** — Mycenaean Linear B spelling on KN Gg(3) 705. Linear B does not distinguish *r* from *l* and cannot fully represent consonant clusters; *Eleuthia* is therefore a scholarly phonological reconstruction. **Securely attested early material.**
- **Εἰλείθυια, Eileíthyia** — standard Ionic-Attic literary spelling.
- **Εἰλήθυια / Εἰλείθεια / Ἐλείθυα** — literary or dialectal variants.
- **Ἐλεύθυια, Eleúthyia; Ἐλευθία, Eleuthía** — especially associated with Crete and close to the reconstructed Mycenaean form.
- **Ἐλυσία, Elysía / Eleusía** — Laconian or Messenian forms reported in lexicographic and epigraphic discussion.
- **Ἐλευθώ, Eleuthṓ** — poetic short form.
- **Εἰλειθυῖαι, Eileithyiai** — plural “Eileithyias,” used in Homer.
- **Ilithyia, Ilithyiae, Eilethyia, Lucina** — Latin literary forms or translations/equations.
- **Natio** — Latin personification of birth occasionally offered by learned sources as a Roman counterpart, but not simply another ancient name for the Greek goddess.

The numerous dialectal forms are documented in literary and epigraphic evidence, including Cretan inscriptions. R. F. Willetts catalogued forms from Lato, Aptera, Gortyn, Paros, Astypalaea, Laconia, and Boeotia and emphasized how widespread the cult was beyond Crete. His further claim of continuity from a Neolithic “prototype” is a historical theory, not something the inscriptions themselves say.

### Etymology

The etymology is unresolved.

1. **Ancient and older modern Greek derivation:** Ancient etymologizing connected the name with forms of **ἐλεύθειν**, “to come,” producing the attractive interpretation “she who comes [to aid]” or “the coming/helper goddess.” Later scholars also associated her with bringing a child forth or into the light. This is a **learned systematization**, not a demonstrable Bronze Age etymology.

2. **Greek derivation defended:** Walter Burkert regarded the name as Greek and the goddess as the divine power of birth. A semantic connection with coming or arrival fits her function, but semantic appropriateness is not proof of linguistic descent.

3. **Pre-Greek derivation:** Martin P. Nilsson and R. S. P. Beekes considered the name probably pre-Greek or non-Indo-European. The unusual phonological variants and parallels with pre-Greek place-name formations support caution. The Oxford Classical Dictionary accordingly calls the name obscure and the proposed connection with Eleusis conjectural. **Modern scholarly reconstruction.**

4. **Eleusis/Eleusinia connection:** Similarities among *Eleuthia*, *Elysia*, the month *Eleusinios*, and *Eleusis* have prompted theories of relationship. No surviving ancient text establishes their historical derivation from one another. **Disputed modern reconstruction.**

### Epithets and cult titles

- **Μογόστοκος / Mogostókos, “of painful birth” or “bringing travail”:** Homeric description of Eileithyia’s harsh labor pains; an early functional characterization.
- **Olympia / Olympian Eileithyia:** Pausanias 6.20.2, at Olympia, where her sanctuary contained the inner cult of Sosipolis. **Regional cult title.**
- **Εὐλινός / Eulinos, approximately “of good flax/thread”:** reported in cultic and antiquarian literature and connected with spinning or fate; its exact local application and interpretation remain uncertain.
- **“Clever spinner”:** Pausanias 8.21.3 reports a lost Delian hymn by Olen that called her a skillful spinner and made her older than Cronus. Because the hymn is lost and survives only through Pausanias, its wording and date cannot be independently checked. **Reported archaic tradition through a Roman-period witness.**
- **Genetyllis / Genetyllides:** Divine power or powers of generation and childbirth, associated with Aphrodite in Aristophanes, *Clouds* 52 and *Thesmophoriazusae* 130–131, and with Artemis in later lexicography. Calling Genetyllis simply “another name of Eileithyia” overstates the evidence: this is a functional association or partial identification. **Classical association; later learned equation.**
- **Prothyraia, “before the door”:** The deity of Orphic Hymn 2, invoked at the threshold of delivery and addressed both as Eileithyia and Artemis Eileithyia. The collection is normally dated to the second or third century CE. **Late syncretistic identification.**
- **Artemis Eileithyia / Artemis Lochia:** An identification arising from overlapping responsibility for childbirth, youth, and transition. It is historically attested but never erased independent cults of Eileithyia.
- **Hera Eileithyia:** A cultic-functional merging of Eileithyia with Hera, marriage goddess and her literary mother. Farnell found evidence for Hera Eileithyia in Attica and possibly Argos. **Syncretistic cult title.**
- **Lucina:** Latin “she of light” or “she who brings into light,” primarily an epithet of Juno and secondarily Diana. Greek and Roman writers used Lucina as Eileithyia’s functional equivalent, but Roman Lucina possessed an independent Italian history. **Graeco-Roman identification, not primordial identity.**

No securely ancient evidence assigns Eileithyia a distinctive sacred color, weekday, number, planet, zodiacal rulership, crystal, or modern-style correspondence table.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Linear B and Amnisos

**KN Gg(3) 705** reads, in conventional transliteration:

> ] a-mi-ni-so / e-re-u-ti-ja  ME+RI  amphora 1  
> ] pa-si-te-o-i  ME+RI  amphora 1  
> ] perhaps po-se-da-o-ne  ME+RI  amphora 1

That is: at or from **Amnisos**, one amphora of honey for **Eleuthia**; one for “all the gods”; and probably one for Poseidon. The tablet belongs to the Knossian palace’s accounting system. A neighboring text, **KN Gg(1) 702**, records honey for all the gods and for the Mistress of the Labyrinth; it does **not** name Eileithyia and is sometimes mistakenly cited as her tablet. The correct primary record is KN Gg(3) 705.

What it establishes:

- the divine name existed in Mycenaean Greek;
- the recipient was associated administratively with Amnisos;
- honey was offered;
- she appeared within a larger offering distribution that included all gods and probably Poseidon.

What it does not establish:

- her genealogy;
- childbirth as her domain;
- whether the name was inherited from Minoan language;
- whether Poseidon and Eileithyia shared a myth or sanctuary;
- whether the precise excavated cave was meant.

The surviving Knossos tablet is the earliest secure named attestation. Earlier cave deposits at Amnisos or Inatos cannot be assigned to “Eileithyia” merely because later worship occurred there.

### Homer

- **Iliad 11.269–272:** the pains of wounded Agamemnon are compared to the sharp, bitter pains sent by the plural Eileithyiai, daughters of Hera. **Securely attested early material.**
- **Iliad 16.187–188:** singular Eileithyia brings Polydorus into the light. This is a simile or genealogical notice, not an extended myth.
- **Iliad 19.103–133:** Zeus announces that Eileithyia will bring forth a descendant of his who will rule; Hera hastens Eurystheus’s premature birth and arrests Alcmene’s labor. The poem does not explicitly show Eileithyia carrying out Hera’s obstruction, although 19.118–119 again names the Eileithyiai. Later authors make her role explicit.
- **Odyssey 19.188–189:** in Odysseus’s Cretan lying tale, a storm drives him to Amnisos, “where is the cave of Eileithyia.” This is the earliest literary testimony to an Amnisian cave bearing her name, though the episode’s voyage is fictional within the poem.

The *Iliad* and *Odyssey* reached their monumental forms around the late eighth or early seventh century BCE, while the surviving medieval manuscripts are vastly later. Individual verses may preserve older formulas, but precise Bronze Age dating of a particular Homeric detail cannot be proved.

### Hesiod

- **Theogony 921–923:** Hera, after union with Zeus, bears Hebe, Ares, and Eileithyia. This is the earliest preserved explicit Zeus-Hera genealogy.
- **Works and Days:** no substantive narrative of Eileithyia. Her frequent inclusion in generic source lists should not be mistaken for an important role in that poem.

### Homeric Hymn to Apollo

In the Delian portion, usually dated to the seventh–sixth century BCE, Leto labors for nine days and nights while goddesses attend her. Eileithyia alone remains on Olympus, kept unaware by jealous Hera. The goddesses send Iris, promising Eileithyia a necklace of gold and amber. When Eileithyia reaches Delos, labor grips Leto; Leto embraces a palm, kneels, and gives birth to Apollo. The hymn thus makes Eileithyia’s physical arrival the condition that releases birth.

The poem names Artemis elsewhere and in one traditional reading distinguishes her birth on Ortygia from Apollo’s on Delos. Later summaries sometimes say Eileithyia assisted at the birth of both twins, but the surviving Delian episode concentrates on Apollo.

### Lyric poetry

- **Pindar, Nemean 7.1–4:** invokes Eileithyia, attendant or assessor beside the deep-thinking Fates and child of powerful Hera; without her mortals do not see daylight or dark night. **Early fifth century BCE.**
- **Pindar, Olympian 6.39–46:** Apollo sends kindly Eileithyia and the Fates to assist Evadne, who gives birth to Iamus. **Early fifth century BCE.**
- **Pindar, Pythian 3:** birth, death, and Asclepius dominate, but references to childbirth powers should not be expanded into an independent Eileithyia narrative absent from the text.
- **Delian hymn of Olen:** known only from Pausanias. It reportedly made Eileithyia a clever spinner, older than Cronus, and elsewhere the mother of Eros. Its actual date is uncertain; “Olen” belongs to Delian antiquarian constructions of primordial hymnody.

### Drama and comedy

No surviving tragedy provides a major Eileithyia-centered plot. Tragic invocations and childbirth language attest her function but not a separate narrative cycle. Aristophanic references to Genetyllis or Genetyllides concern generation and sexuality and document the overlap among Eileithyia, Artemis, and Aphrodite rather than an exact identity.

### Hellenistic poetry

Callimachus repeatedly uses Eileithyia as the power invoked in pregnancy or easy delivery:

- *Hymn to Zeus* 12–15: the Arcadian birth-place of Zeus is closed to pregnant women and animals “in need of Eileithyia.”
- *Hymn to Demeter* 130: a pregnant woman stretches her hand toward Eileithyia.
- *Epigram* 54 / *Greek Anthology* 6.146: Kykainis dedicates an offering and asks Eileithyia to return with easy birth.
- *Aetia* fr. 7: “with Eileithyia’s goodwill” a person emerges naked from the womb.

These passages attest ordinary invocation more directly than most mythographic stories.

### Mythographers and historians

- **Diodorus Siculus 4.9.4, first century BCE:** Hera uses her daughter Eileithyia to arrest Alcmene’s labor and bring Eurystheus prematurely to light.
- **Diodorus 5.72.5:** Zeus and Hera assign Eileithyia care of pregnant women and relief of labor; Artemis is called her helper. This is explicit **learned systematization** of divine offices.
- **Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 1.3.1 and 2.4.5:** gives the Zeus-Hera genealogy and says Hera persuaded Eileithyia to delay Heracles’ birth.
- **Antoninus Liberalis, *Metamorphoses* 29, second century CE:** Eileithyia and the Fates sit with crossed limbs to obstruct Alcmene; Galinthias deceives them into releasing their posture. They transform her into a weasel; Hecate makes her a sacred attendant.
- **Pausanias, *Description of Greece*, second century CE:** the richest literary dossier for local sanctuaries, images, priesthoods, foundation legends, and conflicting Cretan and Delian origin stories.
- **Aelian, *On Animals* 7.15 and 10.47:** calls the Eileithyiai daughters of Hera and reports that ichneumons were sacred to Leto and the Eileithyiai at Egyptian Heracleopolis.

### Latin texts

- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 9.281–323:** develops the Galanthis episode. Lucina, bribed or controlled by Juno, sits with legs and fingers crossed, chants spells, and blocks Alcmene’s delivery; Galanthis’s false announcement makes her spring up and release the magical closure. Lucina then changes Galanthis into a weasel.
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 5.303; 10.504–513:** Lucina is invoked in ordinary and extraordinary births, including Myrrha’s delivery of Adonis from a tree.
- **Ovid, *Amores* 2.13–14:** prays to Ilithyia/Lucina for Corinna’s survival after an abortion.
- **Horace, *Carmen Saeculare* 13–16:** invokes “Ilithyia, Lucina, or Genitalis,” explicitly permitting multiple names for the birth goddess in the Roman Secular Games.
- **Catullus 34.13–14:** Diana is addressed as Juno Lucina in childbirth.
- **Hyginus, *Fabulae* and preface:** gives Romanized genealogy and birth narratives, sometimes translating or confusing Greek divine names.

“Lucina” in these authors must be read contextually. Sometimes it represents Juno; sometimes Diana; sometimes a poetic Latin rendering of Eileithyia.

### Orphic Hymns and magical material

**Orphic Hymn 2 to Prothyraia**, probably second–third century CE, asks the threshold goddess to attend birth, calls her Eileithyia, and assimilates her to Artemis. It is evidence for late-antique ritual syncretism, not for a prehistoric “Orphic Eileithyia.”

The Greek Magical Papyri contain many invocations of Artemis-Hecate-Selene and numerous birth, contraception, and gynecological procedures. A dedicated search does not establish a major independent Eileithyia cult or mythology within the PGM comparable to Hecate’s. Claims that elaborate PGM rites were specifically “rites of Eileithyia” require an exact papyrus and line number; none should be inferred from generic childbirth magic.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain and characteristic action

Eileithyia governs the transition from pregnancy into delivered life:

- labor pains;
- opening or obstructing the womb;
- safe delivery;
- the dangerous interval in which mother and child may die;
- the child’s appearance “into the light”;
- in some local evidence, postnatal care, children, fertility, and healing.

Her central power is ambivalent. She does not merely soothe labor. Homeric and later narratives make the same power capable of imposing pain, prolonging labor, releasing it, or denying birth altogether. Crossed limbs, locked fingers, and absence from the birthing place magically close delivery; arrival, uncrossing, or raised arms release it.

### Form

Ancient texts do not supply a single stable physiognomy. She is usually a fully clothed woman, maiden or matron, without the unique weapon or animal that makes Athena, Artemis, or Aphrodite immediately recognizable. Identification consequently depends on inscriptions, narrative context, gestures, and attributes.

### Principal iconographic types

1. **Birth of Athena:** Archaic and early Classical vase-painting often places one or two Eileithyiai beside enthroned Zeus as Athena springs from his head. They raise, lower, or extend their hands in an assisting gesture. A British Museum black-figure neck-amphora, museum no. **1837,0609.27**, shows two Eileithyiai flanking Zeus, with Iris below and Hephaestus departing with his axe. The object demonstrates the plural visual type but does not prove that every birth scene with two women represents two independently worshipped goddesses.

2. **Inscribed Eileithyia in Athena’s birth:** British Museum amphora **1839,1109.1** preserves part of the goddess and her name, strengthening identification of the general type.

3. **Late Archaic red-figure assembly:** British Museum pelike **1849,0620.14**, attributed to the Painter of the Birth of Athena, includes Eileithyia departing from the scene among numerous gods.

4. **Torch-bearing cult image:** Pausanias 7.23.5–6 describes the Aegium image, attributed to Damophon of Messene, as a wooden-bodied acrolith with Pentelic-marble face, hands, and feet, covered in woven clothing. One hand extended forward; the other held a torch. Pausanias proposed two interpretations: labor pains burn like fire, or Eileithyia brings children into light. These are Pausanias’s interpretations, not an inscription explaining the statue.

5. **Concealed images:** Pausanias 1.18.5 reports three Athenian wooden images clothed to their feet. At Mases the image was concealed entirely from everyone but priestesses. Concealment was therefore local ritual treatment, not universal iconography.

6. **Direct birth scene from Inatos:** A small clay bowl from the Inatos cave, dated approximately **850–750 BCE**, contains two seated figures: one pregnant or parturient, the other embracing and touching her abdomen. The Heraklion Archaeological Museum interprets the helper as probably Eileithyia. This is a strong contextual interpretation, but the figures are not inscribed.

### Symbols and animals

- **Torch:** securely attested at Aegium and in later iconographic identifications.
- **Raised or outstretched hands:** common in Athena-birth scenes; interpreted as aiding delivery.
- **Spindle or thread:** associated with Olen’s “clever spinner” and with proximity to the Fates, but no surviving securely identified early statue makes it her universal emblem.
- **Honey:** securely attested as a Mycenaean offering, not necessarily a personal symbol.
- **Incense and sacrifice:** documented at Mases and elsewhere as ritual offerings.
- **Weasel/polecat:** belongs primarily to the Galinthias/Galanthis transformation myth. Aelian’s ichneumon is sacred to Leto and the Eileithyiai in Egypt. It is too broad to call “the sacred animal of Eileithyia” without qualification.
- **Serpent:** the serpent-child Sosipolis occupied the inner room of her Olympian sanctuary. The serpent belonged to Sosipolis’s local manifestation; it was not a general Eileithyian attribute.
- **Palm tree:** Leto grasps the Delian palm during Apollo’s birth, but the palm is chiefly Delian and Apolline rather than Eileithyia’s universal plant.
- **Colors, celestial bodies, numbers, days:** no secure ancient system.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical versions

| Version | Relationship | Source and date | Classification |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eileithyiai are daughters of Hera | Mother: Hera; father unstated | *Iliad* 11.270, 19.118–119, late eighth/early seventh century BCE | Securely attested early |
| Eileithyia is daughter of Zeus and Hera | Parents: Zeus and Hera; siblings Ares and Hebe | Hesiod, *Theogony* 921–923 | Early systematization |
| Born at Amnisos; Hera her mother | Cretan local origin | Pausanias 1.18.5, reporting Cretan belief | Regional tradition, Roman-period witness |
| Older than Cronus | Primordial goddess/Fate | Lost hymn of Olen reported by Pausanias 8.21.3 | Reported archaizing regional theology |
| Mother of Eros | Eros as child | Lost hymn of Olen reported at Pausanias 9.27.2 | Regional/antiquarian variant |
| Sosipolis treated as her child or closely paired cult-child | Local child/partner | Pausanias 6.20.2–6 | Elean regional cult |
| No spouse or ordinary offspring | Maiden goddess in common literary presentation | Negative result from the principal corpus | Absence of evidence |

The Hesiodic Zeus-Hera genealogy became the standard mythographic version because Hesiod supplied the most influential organized divine family tree. It did not eliminate the older plural daughters of Hera, the Delian primordial spinner, or local cult-child relationships.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Hera — daughter and subordinate:** Homer calls the Eileithyiai Hera’s daughters; later mythographers make Eileithyia obey Hera by obstructing Alcmene. *Iliad* 11.270, 19.118–119; Diodorus 4.9.4; Apollodorus 2.4.5. **Early relationship; later explicit elaboration.**
- **Zeus — father in the Hesiodic genealogy:** Zeus and Hera beget Eileithyia. Hesiod, *Theogony* 921–923. **Early systematization.**
- **Zeus — executor of his birth decree:** Zeus announces that Eileithyia will bring his Perseid descendant into light; Hera manipulates the timing. *Iliad* 19.103–133. **Securely attested early.**
- **Ares — sibling:** both are children of Zeus and Hera. Hesiod, *Theogony* 921–923. No independent narrative interaction is preserved. **Early genealogy only.**
- **Hebe — sibling:** same passage and qualification. No securely preserved episode shows the sisters acting together. **Early genealogy only.**
- **Leto — delayed helper in Apollo’s birth:** Eileithyia’s arrival at Delos releases Leto’s nine-day labor. *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* 91–126. **Archaic canonical narrative.**
- **Apollo — permits his birth:** she arrives immediately before Apollo is born; later Pindar also makes Apollo send her to Evadne. *Homeric Hymn to Apollo*; Pindar, *Olympian* 6. **Archaic/early Classical.**
- **Apollo — receives his command to assist Evadne:** Apollo sends Eileithyia and the Fates to Evadne’s delivery of Iamus. Pindar, *Olympian* 6.39–46. **Early Classical.**
- **Iris — messenger who fetches her:** the assembled goddesses send Iris secretly from Hera, promising Eileithyia a necklace. *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* 102–119. **Archaic.**
- **Artemis — functional colleague:** Diodorus calls Artemis Eileithyia’s helper; cult and hymn traditions form Artemis Eileithyia or Prothyraia. Diodorus 5.72.5; Orphic Hymn 2. **Later systematization and syncretism.**
- **Artemis — possible Delian identification:** Herodotus 4.35 records Hyperborean offerings to Eileithyia for easing childbirth in a Delian complex strongly associated with Artemis. Exact identity remains debated. **Regional association, not proven complete equation.**
- **The Moirai/Fates — joint attendants at birth:** they accompany Eileithyia at Evadne’s delivery and jointly obstruct Alcmene in Antoninus Liberalis. Pindar, *Olympian* 6; Antoninus Liberalis 29. **Early association; later narrative elaboration.**
- **Athena — attends Athena’s emergence from Zeus:** widely depicted in sixth–fifth-century BCE vase-painting, including inscribed examples. No early surviving literary narrative assigns her this role. **Secure iconographic interaction.**
- **Hephaestus — joint participant in Athena’s birth scene:** he opens Zeus’s head while Eileithyia assists, on Archaic vases. **Iconographic, not an independent literary encounter.**
- **Eros — mother in Olen’s Delian hymn:** Pausanias 9.27.2. **Regional variant preserved second-hand.**
- **Sosipolis — joint cult and possible mother-child relation:** her outer sanctuary at Olympia encloses his restricted inner chamber; the local story has a woman present the savior child to the Eleans. Pausanias 6.20.2–6. **Regional cult relationship.**
- **Hecate — connected indirectly through Galinthias:** after Eileithyia and the Fates transform Galinthias, Hecate pities the animal and makes her an attendant. Antoninus Liberalis 29. Eileithyia and Hecate do not converse or act jointly. **Late mythographic connection.**
- **Aphrodite — associated through Genetyllis:** Aristophanes’ generative goddess Genetyllis appears beside Aphrodite Kolias; later lexicographers associate the name also with Artemis and childbirth. This is functional proximity, not a securely narrated meeting. **Classical cultic association; later equation.**
- **Poseidon — co-recipient in a Mycenaean offering list:** KN Gg(3) 705 probably assigns honey separately to Eileithyia and Poseidon. This documents administrative juxtaposition, **not a mythic interaction or proven shared cult**.
- **“All the gods” — co-recipients:** the same tablet lists *pa-si-te-o-i*, “to all the gods.” Again, this is offering context, not narrative interaction.
- **Rhea — implied by exclusion:** Callimachus’s Arcadian place of Zeus’s birth excludes beings needing Eileithyia. She does not personally appear. **Hellenistic learned allusion.**
- **Demeter — cultic proximity:** her invocation appears in Callimachus’s *Hymn to Demeter*; at Cleitor Eileithyia’s sanctuary ranked with those of Demeter and Asclepius. No discrete mythic encounter is attested.
- **Asclepius — cultic proximity:** paired institutionally at Cleitor and possibly in debated iconography, but no securely attested narrative interaction.
- **Enesidaon/Poseidon and a divine child at Amnisos:** theories that Eileithyia participated in an annual Minoan divine-child cycle with a chthonic Poseidon depend on comparative reconstruction, not a surviving Mycenaean or Classical narrative. **Modern scholarly reconstruction; unestablished.**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The Delian birth of Apollo

**Earliest full source:** *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* 91–126, seventh–sixth century BCE.

Leto, unable to obtain a birthplace for Zeus’s child, reaches Delos. In the labor scene she suffers for nine days and nights. Dione, Rhea, Themis, Amphitrite, and other goddesses attend; Hera is absent. Eileithyia remains on Olympus because Hera, out of jealousy, has kept her uninformed. The goddesses dispatch Iris, promising Eileithyia a necklace nine cubits long strung with golden threads and instructing Iris to summon her apart from Hera. Iris persuades her. When Eileithyia sets foot on Delos, labor takes Leto; Leto grips the palm, kneels, and Apollo is born.

**Development:**

- Eileithyia’s indispensable physical arrival is the story’s early core.
- Hera’s detention of her exemplifies Eileithyia’s subordinate position within Hera’s sphere.
- Her promised necklace is persuasion/payment, not necessarily a permanent cult attribute.
- Pausanias 1.18.5 gives a later Delian history: Eileithyia came from the Hyperboreans to assist Leto, after which her name spread.
- Herodotus 4.35 records Hyperborean maidens bringing tribute to Eileithyia in thanks for easy childbirth. This is a distinct Delian sacred-history tradition.
- Cretans, according to Pausanias, instead said she was born at Amnisos. Delian and Cretan origin claims are competing regional traditions.
- Later summaries sometimes make her assist both Artemis and Apollo. That is plausible within the twin-birth tradition but is not the exact focus of the surviving hymn.

### The manipulated births of Heracles and Eurystheus

**Earliest source:** *Iliad* 19.95–133.

Zeus boasts that a descendant of his born that day will rule surrounding peoples. Hera extracts an oath, hastens the seven-month birth of Eurystheus, and delays Alcmene’s delivery of Heracles. The Homeric text invokes Eileithyia as the birth power but does not explicitly narrate Hera instructing her.

**Later canonical elaboration:**

- Diodorus 4.9.4 names Eileithyia as Hera’s helper.
- Apollodorus 2.4.5 says Hera persuaded her to hold back labor.
- Antoninus Liberalis 29 adds the Fates, seated with crossed arms, and Galinthias’s deception.
- Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 9, turns Eileithyia into Lucina, emphasizes the magical crossed limbs and fingers, spells, seven days of suffering, and Galanthis’s transformation.

These are not interchangeable versions. Homer has divine manipulation of birth order; Hellenistic/Roman mythography develops a magical-obstruction scene; Ovid supplies an elaborate first-person account by Alcmene.

### Galinthias/Galanthis and the weasel

**Source:** Antoninus Liberalis 29 and Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 9.281–323.

Galinthias/Galanthis falsely announces that Alcmene has delivered. Eileithyia and, in Antoninus, the Fates relax their magically closed posture in shock, unintentionally releasing the birth. They punish the deceiver by converting her into a weasel or polecat, whose supposed oral conception or birth mirrors the lie told by her mouth. Antoninus adds Hecate’s pity and adoption of the transformed woman as a sacred servant.

This episode explains an ancient erroneous zoological belief and a cultic relationship between weasels, childbirth, and Hecate. It is a **later canonical elaboration**, not securely present in early epic.

### Birth of Iamus

**Source:** Pindar, *Olympian* 6.39–57, 472 BCE.

Evadne, pregnant by Apollo, lays aside her girdle and gives birth. Apollo sends Eileithyia and the Fates to attend. Iamus is then nourished by serpents with honey and becomes the ancestor of the Iamidae, hereditary seers at Olympia. The story integrates Eileithyia into a prestigious genealogical and political charter for a prophetic family.

### Birth of Athena

**Source type:** sixth- and fifth-century BCE vase-painting rather than a surviving early text explicitly naming her as midwife.

Zeus sits enthroned while Athena emerges armed from his head; Hephaestus departs with the axe, and one or two Eileithyiai gesture beside Zeus. The episode visually universalizes her power: even a male divine cranial birth requires the birth goddess. Because some figures are inscribed and the composition recurs, the identification is secure. Literary retellings of Athena’s birth should not automatically insert her unless they say so.

### Sosipolis at Olympia

**Source:** Pausanias 6.20.2–6.

Before a battle with Arcadians, an Elean woman appeared with an infant, reportedly following a dream. Placed before the army, the child became a serpent; the enemy fled. The child was worshipped as Sosipolis, “Savior of the City,” in an inner chamber of Eileithyia’s sanctuary. Eileithyia’s relation may be construed maternally, protectively, or spatially; Pausanias does not provide a conventional divine conception-and-birth genealogy.

### Auge at Tegea

**Source:** Pausanias 8.48.7.

Aleus ordered Nauplius to drown his pregnant daughter Auge. While being taken away, she fell to her knees and delivered Telephus on the spot. The Tegean sanctuary and image of Eileithyia were called **Auge on her Knees**. This is a regional foundation legend tying the goddess to the bodily posture and location of a threatened birth.

### Helen’s Argive sanctuary

**Source:** Pausanias 2.22.6.

After the Dioscuri recovered Helen, she supposedly gave birth at Argos and founded a sanctuary of Eileithyia, entrusting the child to Clytemnestra. Pausanias reports the Argive story but elsewhere expresses skepticism toward competing local claims. **Regional sacred history, not panhellenic canon.**

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Amnisos, Crete

The Odyssey locates Eileithyia’s cave at Amnisos, Knossos’s harbor. A cave about one kilometer inland from ancient Amnisos has been identified with it, but the identification is not epigraphically certain.

Excavations by Joseph Hazzidakis and Federico Halbherr in 1884 and by Spyridon Marinatos in 1929–1930 recovered material ranging from the Neolithic or Early Minoan periods through Roman and later periods. The cave includes prominent stalagmites and a low enclosure. The finds demonstrate long use of the cavity, but early burials and pottery are not automatically evidence for a named Eileithyia cult. Indeed, reassessment notes Final Neolithic and Early Minoan burial use, possible cult from later prepalatial phases, weak Archaic–Hellenistic representation, and substantial Roman activity.

The conjunction of:

- KN Gg(3) 705 naming Eleuthia at Amnisos;
- *Odyssey* 19 naming her cave there;
- later Cretan birth-place tradition;
- and long-lived cave use

makes Amnisos exceptionally important. It still does not prove uninterrupted, unchanged worship from the Neolithic.

### Inatos/Tsoutsouros, Crete

The Inatos cave in south-central Crete is more richly documented as a birth-related sanctuary. It was used from the Protopalatial period through Roman times, with its peak from approximately the late eleventh through early sixth century BCE. Finds include:

- embracing couples;
- pregnant women;
- kourotrophoi carrying or nursing children;
- explicit childbirth scenes;
- Egyptian imports and Egyptianizing objects;
- terracottas and vessels.

The clay childbirth group dated 850–750 BCE is among the clearest material representations of divine assistance in delivery. Later Cretan inscriptions identify an important Eileithyia of Inatos; interstate oaths invoked her, showing that the sanctuary had civic and diplomatic importance beyond private obstetric devotion.

### Lato, Crete

Eileithyia was a principal civic goddess at Lato. Hellenistic treaties were deposited in her sanctuary, an institutional function that shows her temple operating as a protected public archive and guarantor. Terracottas include pregnant women, mothers, and other reproductive imagery. Calling every female figurine Eileithyia would nevertheless be circular; identification depends on find context and comparison.

### Athens

Pausanias 1.18.5 saw a temple near the Prytaneion/Olympieion area containing three wooden images covered to their toes. One was said to have come from Delos with Erysichthon; two were Cretan offerings of Phaedra. These provenance stories symbolically reconcile competing Delian and Cretan claims and should not be treated as secure histories of the physical statues.

Plato, *Laws* 783e, imagines officials supervising married couples from a temple of Eileithyia. This is evidence for the goddess’s intelligibility as patron of lawful reproduction, not direct evidence that historical Athens ran Plato’s proposed institution.

### Megara and Corinth

Pausanias reports a sanctuary of the plural **Eileithyiai** at Megara (1.44.2–3) and a sanctuary of Eileithyia by the Teneatic gate near Corinth (2.5.4). The Megarian plural preserves the Homeric capacity to imagine multiple birth powers.

### Argos, Mycenae, Mases, and Hermione

- **Argos:** sanctuary attributed to Helen after giving birth there, Pausanias 2.22.6.
- **Mycenae:** a gate named from a nearby sanctuary, 2.18.3.
- **Mases:** sanctuary within the wall; daily sacrifice and incense, many votives, and an image visible only to priestesses, 2.35.11.
- **Hermione:** sanctuary/cult evidence reported by Pausanias in his Argolid itinerary.

Mases supplies particularly valuable evidence for regular institutional worship: daily rites, an accumulated votive economy, priestesses, and restricted viewing.

### Sparta and Laconia

Pausanias 3.17.1 reports a sanctuary near Artemis Orthia, founded after a Delphic oracle. Other Laconian evidence includes regional spellings of the goddess’s name. The proximity to Artemis Orthia reflects overlapping concern with female maturation and reproduction but does not prove identity.

### Messene

Pausanias 4.31.7 saw a temple with a stone image. Epigraphic dialect forms and local calendrical terms add evidence, though proposed connections between *Elysia* and Eleusinian terminology remain disputed.

### Aegium, Bura, and Pellene in Achaea

- **Aegium:** ancient sanctuary and Damophon’s acrolithic, draped, torch-bearing image; head, hands, and feet in Pentelic marble.
- **Bura:** temple with Pentelic-marble images.
- **Pellene:** sanctuary in the smaller division of the city.

The concentration demonstrates strong Achaean cult. Pausanias’s description also anchors the torch as a genuine cult-image attribute.

### Cleitor and Tegea in Arcadia

At **Cleitor**, Eileithyia’s sanctuary ranked after those of Demeter and Asclepius among the city’s most celebrated sanctuaries. Pausanias uses the occasion to report Olen’s primordial spinner theology.

At **Tegea**, the agora sanctuary commemorated Auge’s delivery and bore the local title “Auge on her Knees.”

### Olympia

The sanctuary had two rooms:

- a public front room with Eileithyia’s altar;
- a restricted inner chamber for Sosipolis.

An annually selected priestess served Eileithyia. An elderly woman serving Sosipolis lived chastely, bathed the god, brought honeyed barley cakes, and entered veiled in white. Maidens and matrons waited outside singing a hymn while incense was burned. This is the fullest account of an Eileithyian priesthood and segregated ritual space.

### Delos and the Cyclades

Delos possessed old traditions of Eileithyia’s arrival, Hyperborean tribute, and Olen’s hymns. Inscriptions attest cult across Delos, Tenos, Paros, Naxos, Thera, and Astypalaea. At Paros, anatomical votives and a sacred well have encouraged interpretation of her as a broader healing goddess, though childbirth and female health need not have been mutually exclusive.

### Offerings and worshippers

Documented practices include:

- honey in the Mycenaean palace offering system;
- incense and animal sacrifice;
- votive figurines of pregnant women, birthing women, infants, and kourotrophoi;
- anatomical votives;
- garments or coverings for images;
- hymns by women and girls;
- prayer during labor;
- dedications after a successful birth;
- civic deposition of treaties;
- annual priestly appointment;
- restricted access to cult images and inner rooms.

No reliable evidence associates Eileithyia’s cult with temple prostitution or human sacrifice. Sosipolis’s military foundation legend and serpent transformation are not evidence of human sacrifice. No ancient source documents cult violence conducted in her name.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Pre-Mycenaean material background

The caves at Amnisos and Inatos preserve occupation and deposit histories earlier than readable Greek evidence. Some objects concern burial, fertility, pregnancy, or motherhood. Scholars including Nilsson and Willetts proposed an indigenous Minoan or even Neolithic ancestor for Eileithyia.

The restrained conclusion is:

- prehistoric Cretan communities used the relevant caves;
- some early imagery concerns reproduction;
- the name *Eleuthia* is first readable only in Mycenaean Greek;
- continuity of place or function does not prove uninterrupted identity, theology, or ritual.

### Mycenaean palace religion, c. fourteenth–thirteenth century BCE

KN Gg(3) 705 places *Eleuthia* within Knossian palace accounting and connects her with Amnisos. Honey offerings and juxtaposition with “all the gods” show recognized divine status. Nothing in the tablet subordinates her to Hera or names childbirth.

### Early Iron Age and Archaic period

Homer supplies both singular and plural forms and links her with Hera and Amnisos. The Homeric Hymn makes her indispensable to Apollo’s birth. At Inatos, the late eleventh–sixth-century flourishing produced explicit reproductive votives. Archaic vase-painting inserted one or two Eileithyiai into Athena’s birth.

This is the best-attested period for the emergence of her familiar Greek identity: a specialized but widely comprehensible birth power, capable of both pain and release.

### Classical period

Pindar aligns her with the Fates and Apollo. Sanctuaries spread or remained active throughout mainland Greece and the islands. Eileithyia overlapped increasingly with:

- Hera as marriage and legitimate maternity;
- Artemis as protector of girls, mothers, and young life;
- Aphrodite/Genetyllis in generation;
- the Moirai as controllers of life’s allotted transition.

This was not simply “demotion” from an earlier great goddess. The claim that patriarchal Olympian religion reduced a once-supreme Minoan mother goddess is a modern grand narrative not demonstrated by the surviving evidence.

### Hellenistic period

Civic institutions at Lato placed treaties in her sanctuary. Callimachus made invocation of Eileithyia a conventional sign of pregnancy and childbirth. Local sculpture developed monumental cult images, including Damophon’s work at Aegium. Mythographers began systematizing her under Zeus and Hera’s administrative assignment.

### Roman imperial period

Pausanias documents an exceptionally broad living sanctuary network in the second century CE. Greek Eileithyia coexisted with the Roman vocabulary of Lucina, Juno Lucina, Diana Lucina, and Genitalis. The Orphic Hymn to Prothyraia fused Eileithyia and Artemis in ritual poetry.

Roman-period finds are substantial at Amnisos and continue at Inatos. This is evidence of continued or revived sanctuary use, though not necessarily unchanged ritual.

### Christianization and disappearance

No surviving source records a specific imperial order closing “the cult of Eileithyia” or a documented martyr-style destruction of one of her temples. The cult disappeared as a public institution during the broader suppression, abandonment, conversion, and economic dissolution of traditional cults in Late Antiquity. Any more specific claim requires site-level archaeological evidence.

In Greek Christian popular religion, **Saint Eleutherios** became associated with pregnancy and childbirth, encouraged by the saint’s name and the verb *eleutheroō*, “to free.” This is a functional succession or folk analogy, not evidence that Eileithyia secretly survived under a Christian name.

### Medieval and early-modern reception

Eileithyia persisted in Byzantine lexica and manuscript transmission of Homer, Hesiod, Pausanias, Ovid, and mythographers. Renaissance and early-modern classicists knew her primarily as Ilithyia/Lucina. Unlike Hecate, Hermes, or Isis, she did not become a major autonomous deity in Renaissance magic, Hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, or ceremonial grimoires.

### Modern revival

Modern Hellenic polytheists may honor Eileithyia in prayers concerning pregnancy, delivery, midwifery, fertility, or infants. This belongs to the wider twentieth- and twenty-first-century revival of Hellenic religion. No reliable census isolates her devotees, and no evidence supports an unbroken initiatory lineage from an ancient Eileithyian priesthood.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and art

Modern archaeological publication has made Eileithyia unusually important to the study of childbirth religion, women’s votives, and possible Bronze Age–Classical continuity. Her ancient visual record remains specialized, however, and she rarely acquired an independent modern artistic personality comparable to Athena or Artemis.

### Kerényi

Karl Kerényi emphasized the duality of the Amnisos cave’s formations and Eileithyia’s power both to open and close birth. His phenomenological treatment is influential reception and interpretation, not a recovered Minoan doctrine. Claims that the stalagmites objectively portray her “double form” exceed the archaeological evidence.

### Graves

Robert Graves incorporated Eileithyia into his comparative reconstructions of pre-Hellenic goddess religion. His narratives frequently combine late sources, ritual speculation, etymology, and a hypothesized matriarchal past. They should be used as twentieth-century literary-scholar reception, not as a critical source for Bronze Age belief.

### Neopaganism

Modern reconstructionists employ ancient hymns, archaeological descriptions, incense, libation, and adapted prayer. Eclectic pagan and witchcraft sources often add:

- crystals;
- moon phases;
- assigned colors;
- modern altar correspondences;
- generalized “womb healing”;
- abortion, fertility-treatment, doula, or neonatal patronage;
- personal associations with Hecate, Selene, or a universal Mother Goddess.

Some applications are intelligible contemporary extensions of her ancient sphere. They are nevertheless **modern practice or personal gnosis** unless tied to a cited ancient source.

### Occultism

The dedicated contested-material search finds no strong historical role for Eileithyia in:

- Renaissance Hermetic planetary magic;
- the Golden Dawn;
- Theosophy;
- standard Solomonic grimoires;
- chaos-magic canons.

Modern claims of an ancient “Eileithyian mystery order,” lunar initiation, chakra system, crystal correspondence, or hereditary witch-cult are unsupported unless accompanied by independently verifiable documentation.

### Popular culture

Eileithyia appears chiefly in mythological reference works, historical fiction, online deity guides, games that require large pantheon rosters, and modern devotional writing. These appearances have not generated one dominant modern image. Online summaries often repeat four simplifications:

1. her name certainly means “the helper who comes”;
2. she was definitively a Minoan Great Mother;
3. she had exactly two forms, one helpful and one harmful;
4. Lucina was merely her Roman renaming.

All four require qualification.

### Political or extremist appropriation

No substantial Eileithyia-specific nationalist, fascist, or extremist appropriation is documented in the sources examined. Modern Hellenic religion can intersect with debates over Greek ethnicity and cultural ownership, but attributing those movements specifically to Eileithyia would be unsupported.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Foundational scholarship

- **L. R. Farnell, *The Cults of the Greek States*, vol. II:** assembled literary, cultic, and iconographic evidence and emphasized how specialized Eileithyia existed alongside Hera and Artemis.
- **Richard Baur, *Eileithyia*:** early monographic collection of sources and images, still useful historiographically but superseded in chronology and method.
- **Martin P. Nilsson, *The Minoan-Mycenaean Religion and Its Survival in Greek Religion*:** argued for Minoan/Mycenaean continuity; influential but often more confident about survival than current evidentiary standards permit.
- **R. F. Willetts, “Cretan Eileithyia,” 1958:** argued that continuity rested on the stable function of the divine midwife and assembled Cretan epigraphy.
- **Vasiliki Pingiatoglou, *Eileithyia*:** major modern synthesis of literary, epigraphic, and archaeological evidence.
- **LIMC, “Eileithyia,” by Ricardo Olmos:** principal iconographic catalogue.
- **Walter Burkert, *Greek Religion*:** situates her within Mycenaean continuity, birth ritual, and Greek polytheism.
- **Timothy Gantz, *Early Greek Myth*:** separates early sources from later mythographic elaboration.
- **Oxford Classical Dictionary / Brill’s New Pauly:** concise critical orientation, particularly useful for etymology and cult geography.
- **Davaras and Masson, “Cretica: Amnisos et ses inscriptions”:** archaeological and epigraphic reassessment of Amnisos.
- **Recent Inatos publications:** catalogue Egyptian imports, Egyptianizing objects, and reproductive votives from the sanctuary.

### Principal debates

#### Greek or pre-Greek name?

The Greek “coming/helper” derivation is semantically persuasive but linguistically insecure. Beekes, Nilsson, and the OCD favor or allow a non-Greek substrate; Burkert was more receptive to a Greek derivation. The Linear B form establishes antiquity, not ultimate linguistic origin.

#### Minoan goddess or Mycenaean Greek goddess?

Because the name occurs in a Greek Linear B document from Crete, she unquestionably belonged to Mycenaean-period religion at Knossos. Whether Greek speakers adopted a preexisting Minoan goddess, translated a local title, or brought a Greek goddess to Crete cannot be determined from the tablet alone.

#### Continuous cult from the Neolithic?

Material exists from Neolithic to Roman or later phases at Amnisos, but the uses changed: habitation, burial, cult, and later visitation. “Objects from many periods” is not the same as continuous worship of the same named goddess. Claims of direct Neolithic-to-Classical continuity are **modern reconstruction**.

#### One goddess or many?

Homer’s singular and plural coexist. Older scholarship sometimes posited two original Eileithyiai, one helpful and one obstructive. The sources establish dual capacity and grammatical plurality, not a securely institutionalized binary pair across Greece.

#### Independent goddess or epithet?

She had independent sanctuaries, priestesses, offerings, and images, so she cannot be reduced to an epithet. Yet Hera and Artemis could assume Eileithyian functions and titles. Greek polytheism allowed both independent personality and functional fusion.

#### Amnisos cave identification

Topography, the Odyssey, and the Linear B mention make the conventional identification plausible. Early excavation records and the absence of a decisive in-situ dedication prevent certainty. The archaeological cave should therefore be called the **probable or conventionally identified** Eileithyia cave.

#### Healing goddess at Paros

Anatomical votives and a sacred well have been interpreted as evidence for broader healing. This need not contradict childbirth specialization; reproductive health generated anatomical dedications. The degree of independent healing function remains debated.

### Discredited or unsupported ideas still circulating

- Eileithyia is proven to be a universal Neolithic Mother Goddess.
- The Amnisos stalagmites are demonstrably male and female cult statues.
- Her cult enacted an annual sacred marriage with Poseidon.
- She ruled the Moon, a zodiac sign, or a fixed weekday.
- Her worship involved human sacrifice or temple prostitution.
- Saint Eleutherios is simply Eileithyia renamed.
- A continuous secret Eileithyian order survived Christianization.
- Every Greek image of a woman holding torches represents Eileithyia.
- Genetyllis, Hecate, Artemis, Hera, Lucina, and Eileithyia were everywhere the same goddess.

None is established by the available primary record.
