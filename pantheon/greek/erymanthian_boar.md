# The Erymanthian Boar: Research Dossier

**Class:** Monster; more precisely, a singular monstrous wild boar. No ancient source classifies it as a god, *daimōn*, sacred animal, transformed deity, or cult recipient.

**Earliest secure attestation:** An established Greek iconographic scene—Heracles carrying the living boar toward Eurystheus, who hides in a partly buried *pithos*—by approximately **560 BCE**. A fragment dated around 600 BCE may belong to the same composition, but its identification is uncertain. The earliest identifiable literary witness is **Hecataeus of Miletus, *Genealogies* 2, FGrHist/BNJ 1 F6**, about 500 BCE, preserved only through Stephanus of Byzantium. It says that a boar on the mountain inflicted great harm on the people of Psophis. The earliest surviving literary text to name the exploit directly is probably Sophocles, *Trachiniae* 1095–1097, produced in the later fifth century BCE.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EARLY]** Secure Archaic or Classical evidence.
- **[LATER CANONICAL]** Hellenistic or Roman-period formulation that became standard.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** Localized or geographically divergent tradition.
- **[LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]** Mythographic ordering or reconciliation.
- **[LATE ACCRETION]** Late-antique, Byzantine, medieval, or early-modern expansion.
- **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** Scholarly inference not stated by an ancient source.
- **[MODERN INVENTION]** Recent attribution, symbolism, narrative, or practice unsupported by ancient evidence.

The labels describe the history of attestation, not the truth or falsity of the story.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

The Erymanthian Boar is the destructive wild animal whom Eurystheus required Heracles to capture alive. Its identity is relational and geographical: it is not given a personal name but is “the boar of Erymanthus.” It ranges from Mount Erymanthus or nearby Mount Lampeia into the territory of Psophis in northwestern Arcadia. Heracles drives, wrestles, snares, or otherwise subdues it and carries it toward Mycenae. The image of Eurystheus cowering in a storage jar became the episode’s most durable visual formulation.

### Names

- **Greek:** ὁ Ἐρυμάνθιος κάπρος, *ho Erymánthios kápros*, “the Erymanthian boar.”
- **Alternative Greek noun:** ὗς, *hŷs*, “pig, swine”; modern indexes consequently use **Ὕς Ἐρυμάνθιος**, *Hŷs Erymánthios*.
- **Latin:** *aper Erymanthius*, “Erymanthian wild boar”; Latin poets also identify it descriptively as the Arcadian, Maenalian, or Erymanthian beast.
- **English:** Erymanthian Boar; older spelling **Erymanthean Boar**.
- **French:** *sanglier d’Érymanthe*.
- **German:** *Erymanthischer Eber*.
- **Italian:** *cinghiale di Erimanto*.
- **Modern Greek:** Ερυμάνθιος Κάπρος.

*Erymanthios* is an adjectival formation from Ἐρύμανθος, *Erymanthos*, the name of both a mountain complex and river. No secure ancient etymology of *Erymanthos* survives. Its structure has encouraged speculation that it is pre-Greek, but this is a linguistic possibility, not an established derivation. Pausanias records a genealogical Erymanthus and a cult image of the river Erymanthus at Psophis; those facts show local personification and etiology, not that the boar itself was the river-god or his offspring.

### Descriptions rather than epithets

Ancient authors call the animal:

- destructive of Psophidian or Arcadian land—Hecataeus F6; Apollodorus 2.5.4;
- exceptionally large and strong—Pausanias 8.24.5;
- “untamable,” alive, and foam-jawed—Quintus Smyrnaeus 6.220–248;
- shaggy and a ravager of Arcadian woodland—Seneca, *Hercules Furens* 228–235;
- enormously heavy—Ovid, *Heroides* 9.87–90;
- dark, hard-bristled, rock-shouldered, and armed with crescent tusks—pseudo-Claudian, *Laus Herculis* 103–117.

These are poetic descriptions, not cult titles.

### Classification and false identifications

Ancient evidence consistently treats the being as an extraordinary animal or monster. No primary source makes it:

- a child of Typhon and Echidna;
- a child of the Crommyonian Sow Phaea;
- the Calydonian Boar;
- a servant or sacred guardian of Artemis;
- Apollo in animal form;
- a constellation;
- a god or demon.

Claims of descent from Phaea circulate in modern compilations but lack an identified ancient source. The notion that Artemis sent the boar, or that Heracles’ labor was designed to provoke Artemis, appears to be modern contamination from the Ceryneian Hind and Calydonian Boar narratives. A study of Greek animal representations explicitly notes that no evidence makes the Erymanthian Boar the emissary of a particular deity.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Archaic visual evidence

**[EARLY, with qualification] c. 600 BCE:** A badly preserved fragment catalogued in the LIMC may show the delivery scene. Its identification is not secure enough to serve as the earliest certain attestation.

**[EARLY] c. 560 BCE onward:** The recognizable delivery schema is established: Heracles carries the boar alive and upside-down on his shoulder, bends it toward Eurystheus, and sometimes places one foot on the rim of the king’s partly buried *pithos*. Eurystheus raises his hands defensively or in supplication. This predates every complete surviving literary narration.

A mid-sixth-century metope from the Heraion at Foce del Sele near Paestum apparently showed Eurystheus peering from beneath a jar lid as Heracles approached with the boar. Attic black-figure painters subsequently made the scene common.

### Literary corpus

**Homer, *Iliad* 15.639–640, eighth or seventh century BCE — [POSSIBLE BACKGROUND, NOT DIRECT ATTESTATION].** Homer says Eurystheus sent the herald Copreus to convey orders to Heracles. The boar and jar are not mentioned. Daniel Ogden proposes that the audience might have known Eurystheus’ concealment, but this is inference.

**Homer, *Odyssey* 6.102–109 — [BACKGROUND ONLY].** Artemis hunts boars and deer on Erymanthus or Taygetus. This establishes Erymanthus as an imagined hunting landscape, not a relationship between Artemis and the particular boar.

**Stesichorus, fr. 181 PMG / S19 Campbell / F22 Finglass, early sixth century BCE — [EARLY, ADJACENT EPISODE].** The fragment indicates that Pholus gave Heracles wine. It does not securely mention the boar, but it proves the antiquity of the Pholus episode later attached to the journey to Erymanthus.

**Hecataeus of Miletus, *Genealogies* 2, FGrHist/BNJ 1 F6, c. 500 BCE — [EARLY].** Preserved by Stephanus of Byzantium under Ψωφίς: “There was a boar on the mountain, and it did much harm to the Psophidians.” This is the earliest identifiable literary notice. Because the work is lost and the quotation short, it does not prove that Hecataeus included the snow, noose, pithos, or Centaur battle.

**Panyassis, *Heraclea*, fragment 4 Davies / 9 West / 7 Bernabé, fifth century BCE — [EARLY, ADJACENT].** Apparently described Heracles or Pholus drinking deeply from the Centaurs’ wine.

**Pindar, *Pythian* 3.1–7, 462 BCE — [EARLY, ADJACENT].** Knows Chiron as dead but does not connect his death explicitly with this labor.

**Sophocles, *Trachiniae* 714–715 and 1095–1097, later fifth century BCE — [EARLY].** Heracles recalls the poison with which he wounded Chiron and is addressed as the hero who “tamed the beast in Erymanthia.” The play proves that both elements were familiar, although it does not narrate their chronological connection.

**Euripides, *Heracles* 181–184, 364–374, 416 BCE — [EARLY/REGIONAL VARIANTS].** The chorus mentions Heracles’ Centaur battles at Pholoe and in Thessaly. In the labor catalogue the boar is associated with Thessalian or Maenalian geography in some interpretations. This helps explain later reference-book claims that the hunt occurred in Thessaly, but it does not relocate the fully developed Psophis narrative with certainty.

**Herodorus of Heraclea, FGrHist/BNJ 31 F24, c. 400 BCE — [EARLY VARIANT].** According to scholia on Apollonius, Heracles set the boar down before the gates of Mycenae rather than in its marketplace.

**Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 1.122–132, c. 270 BCE — [HELLENISTIC].** Heracles passes through Lyrceian Argos carrying the living, chained boar from the Lampeian glen by the Erymanthian marsh. He shakes it from his shoulders at the entrance to Mycenae’s agora and proceeds toward the assembling Argonauts with Hylas. Eurystheus and the pithos do not appear.

**Diodorus Siculus, *Library of History* 4.12.1–8, c. 60–30 BCE — [LATER CANONICAL].** Makes the capture the third labor, places the animal on Mount Lampeia, and emphasizes the difficulty of subduing it without either being gored or accidentally killing it. Heracles returns with it alive; Eurystheus hides in a bronze *pithos*. Diodorus then narrates the Pholus episode after the capture, reversing the order found in Apollodorus.

Diodorus adds distinctive Centaur material: Dionysus had entrusted a jar of wine to a Centaur ancestor four generations earlier, ordering that it be opened when Heracles came; its odor maddens the Centaurs; and their mother Nephele aids them with rain, which favors four-legged fighters.

**Tabula Albana, IG XIV 1293, first century BCE–second century CE — [LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION].** Lists the boar as the third labor and apparently says it was killed rather than captured.

**Ovid, *Heroides* 9.87–90; *Metamorphoses* 9.191, c. 20 BCE–8 CE — [ROMAN RECEPTION].** Refers to the massive boar that burdened Erymanthus and devastated Arcadia but provides no full narrative.

**Hyginus, *Fabulae* 30, probably first or second century CE — [LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION].** Places the boar among the labors but tersely says Heracles killed it. His account of Chiron’s death preserves alternatives: Heracles accidentally dropped an arrow on Chiron’s foot, or Chiron dropped one while examining it. This differs substantially from Apollodorus’ arrow passing through Elatus into Chiron’s knee.

**Seneca, *Hercules Furens* 228–235; *Agamemnon* 833–834, first century CE — [ROMAN RECEPTION].** Treats the shaggy boar as a celebrated Arcadian labor.

**Statius, *Thebaid* 4.296–298; 8.746–751, c. 92 CE — [ROMAN ELABORATION].** Calls Erymanthus a home of monsters and compares a carried warrior to the still-breathing boar borne on Heracles’ left shoulder, with blood staining the hero’s back. The wound is a poetic addition not required by the live-capture tradition.

**Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 2.5.4, conventionally dated first or second century CE — [LATER CANONICAL].** This is the fullest standard account. Eurystheus assigns the fourth labor: the boar devastates Psophis from Mount Erymanthus. On the way Heracles stays with Pholus; opening the communal wine attracts the Centaurs; Heracles fights them with brands and poisoned arrows; an arrow passes through Elatus’ arm into Chiron’s knee; Chiron cannot die despite his agony because he is immortal; Pholus accidentally drops an arrow onto his foot and dies. Heracles buries Pholus, routs the boar from a thicket by shouting, exhausts it in deep snow, catches it with a noose, and carries it to Mycenae.

Apollodorus earlier says that, after the Nemean Lion, Eurystheus ordered a bronze jar made as a refuge and thereafter sent commands through Copreus. Thus the jar is not logically exclusive to the boar episode, even though Archaic art overwhelmingly associates the two.

**Pausanias, *Description of Greece* 5.10.9; 8.24.3–5, c. 150–180 CE — [ANTIQUARIAN/REGIONAL].** Locates the hunt beside the Erymanthus River and calls the boar unsurpassed in size and strength. At Cumae in Opician Italy, tusks displayed in Apollo’s sanctuary were claimed as the animal’s; Pausanias judges the claim implausible. He also identifies a representation of the “Arcadian boar” among the labors on the Temple of Zeus at Olympia.

**Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica* 6.220–248, third or fourth century CE — [LATE ANTIQUE].** Describes an image of the foam-jawed living boar carried to Eurystheus on Eurypylus’ shield.

**Pseudo-Claudian, *Laus Herculis* 103–117, probably fifth century CE — [LATE ANTIQUE ELABORATION].** Supplies the most extravagant physical description: black, bristling, massive, destructive of trees and crops. Heracles captures it barehanded rather than with arrows or club.

**Dracontius, *Romulea* 2.96, later fifth century CE — [LATE VARIANT].** Gives Hylas the boar’s hide, implying that it was killed, contrary to the canonical live-capture version.

**Suda, s.v. Δρύοπες, tenth century — [BYZANTINE ACCRETION].** Adds that while fetching the boar Heracles asked the Dryopes for food and punished or resettled them when they refused.

**Tzetzes, *Chiliades* 2, twelfth century — [BYZANTINE SYSTEMATIZATION].** Retells and orders the labor from earlier mythographic material.

### Negative corpus findings

The Erymanthian Boar is not securely present in Hesiod’s *Theogony* or *Works and Days*, the surviving Homeric Hymns, the Orphic Hymns, surviving Orphic fragments, or the Greek Magical Papyri. Those corpora therefore supply no genealogy, hymn, ritual, spell, sacred correspondence, or cult title for it.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain and behavior

The animal’s effective “domain” is not divine jurisdiction but dangerous wilderness intruding upon cultivated land. Hecataeus and Apollodorus make it a ravager of Psophis; Roman poets generalize the devastation to Arcadia. Its defining actions are foraging, uprooting or damaging vegetation, destroying fields, threatening hunters with its tusks, and resisting capture.

Its limitations are physical. It can be frightened from cover, exhausted, bogged down in snow, snared, chained, carried, wounded, or—in minority variants—killed. It speaks no words, gives no prophecy, and displays no supernatural intelligence.

### Standard iconographic types

1. **Delivery to Eurystheus:** Heracles carries the boar upside-down on his shoulder and lowers or threatens to throw it toward Eurystheus in the *pithos*. This is the earliest and most common type.
2. **Heracles carrying the boar:** The return journey without Eurystheus.
3. **Combat or capture:** Heracles grapples the animal, strikes or threatens it with a club, or pushes it “wheelbarrow-fashion.”
4. **Dodekathlos sequence:** A compressed emblem among the twelve labors.
5. **Roman decorative survival:** Reliefs, mosaics, sarcophagi, coins, and shield descriptions preserve the carrying or delivery type.

The *pithos* is often partly buried. Ogden argues that the protrusion is an artistic device allowing the viewer to see Eurystheus; Apollodorus describes the king’s refuge as underground. Its repetition alongside the Centaurs’ buried wine jar may have encouraged narrative linkage between the episodes.

### Named objects

- **Foce del Sele metope**, approximately mid-sixth century BCE: among the earliest recognizable monumental versions.
- **Temple of Zeus at Olympia metope**, c. 470–456 BCE: the Arcadian-boar labor, later described by Pausanias 5.10.9.
- **British Museum cup E44 / 1836,0224.25**, attributed to Onesimos, c. 510–500 BCE: Heracles approaches Eurystheus with the upside-down boar; the king’s name, ΕΥΡΥΣΘΕΥΣ, appears on the jar.
- **Walters Art Museum 48.253**, Attic black-figure neck amphora, c. 520 BCE: Heracles holds the living animal above the hiding king.
- **Metropolitan Museum 13.60**, Roman marble relief, 27 BCE–68 CE: Heracles carrying the boar in an archaizing idiom.
- **Llíria mosaic**, third century CE: continuation of the Heracles–boar–Eurystheus scheme.

Some vases place Athena, Hermes, Iolaus, or a supplicating woman near the scene. Such figures may frame or assist Heracles iconographically; they do not prove narrative intervention unless the image makes an action clear.

### Unattested correspondences

No ancient evidence assigns the boar a sacred color, number, weekday, planet, plant, festival date, zodiac sign, or magical substance. Modern claims linking it to Aries, Mars, January, an alchemical stage, or *prima materia* are unsupported modern constructions.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations

### Genealogy

No ancient source supplies parents, mate, offspring, siblings, or species genealogy for the Erymanthian Boar. The creature is never securely made a descendant of Typhon, Echidna, Phaea, or the Calydonian Boar. Its geographical adjective is not a patronymic.

Pausanias’ Erymanthus—an Arcadian ancestor in local genealogies and a personified river with a statue at Psophis—is distinct from the animal. No surviving source calls that Erymanthus the boar’s father.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

Because the subject is a monster rather than a god, this record is very short.

- **Heracles — capture and transport alive.** Hecataeus F6, c. 500 BCE, gives only the destructive boar; fully visualized by c. 560 BCE and directly mentioned in Sophocles, *Trachiniae* 1095–1097; narrated by Apollonius, Diodorus, and Apollodorus. **[EARLY → LATER CANONICAL].** Heracles is a divine hero in cult and later an Olympian, although the labor belongs to his mortal career.
- **Athena — attendant or witnessing figure in some vase paintings.** Sixth–fifth centuries BCE. **[EARLY ICONOGRAPHIC].** No surviving text says she helped capture this boar; the images may signify her general patronage of Heracles.
- **Hermes — appears to assist or attend Heracles in a black-figure capture scene, including Harvard 1960.314.** c. 510–500 BCE. **[EARLY ICONOGRAPHIC/INTERPRETIVELY UNCERTAIN].** No matching literary episode survives.

### Named divine beings connected only indirectly

- **Dionysus:** In Diodorus 4.12.3–8, Dionysus arranged the Centaurs’ wine for Heracles. He does not meet or control the boar. **[LATER CANONICAL ADJACENT EPISODE].**
- **Nephele:** Diodorus makes the mother of the Centaurs send rain during their fight with Heracles. She has no interaction with the boar. **[LATER VARIANT].**
- **Artemis:** Homer pictures Artemis hunting boars on Erymanthus, but no ancient text identifies this animal as hers or says she sent it. **Absence of evidence is decisive against presenting a direct relationship.**
- **Apollo:** Cumae displayed alleged tusks in Apollo’s sanctuary; Pausanias rejects their authenticity. This is a relic claim, not an encounter between Apollo and the boar. **[REGIONAL CULTIC CLAIM].**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The capture

**Early reconstructable core:** By the mid-sixth century BCE, viewers knew that Heracles returned with a living boar and confronted Eurystheus, who hid in a jar. By Hecataeus’ time, the animal was remembered as a destructive boar associated with a mountain and Psophis. The surviving evidence does not establish that the earliest form already contained snow, a net, or a numbered sequence of twelve labors.

**Apollonian version:** Heracles carries the chained animal from Lampeia and the Erymanthian marsh. He drops it at Mycenae’s agora entrance and leaves to join the Argonauts. Hylas accompanies him.

**Diodoran version:** The third labor. Heracles must balance force carefully: insufficient force leaves him vulnerable to the tusks; excessive force would kill the required living prize. Eurystheus retreats into a bronze jar.

**Apollodoran version:** The fourth labor. After the Pholus disaster, Heracles finds the boar, shouts it from a thicket, drives the exhausted animal into deep snow, snares it, and brings it to Mycenae.

**Pseudo-Claudian version:** Heracles rejects weapons and seizes the foam-mouthed animal directly, carrying it inverted into Eurystheus’ house.

**Killing variant:** Hyginus, the Tabula Albana, later catalogues, Martial’s reference to a boar skin, and Dracontius’ hide on Hylas imply or state death. This conflicts with the dominant live-capture tradition.

The animal’s fate after delivery is nowhere securely narrated. Modern stories that Eurystheus released it, sacrificed it, or kept it are conjecture.

### Eurystheus in the *pithos*

The jar motif is older in art than in surviving prose. Its principal meaning within the images is Eurystheus’ fear and the reversal of authority: the commanding king is reduced to a supplicant beneath the servant he sought to humiliate. Whether the scene is straightforwardly comic remains disputed. Some scholars accept deliberate visual humor; Frank Brommer rejected humor as the governing intention.

Apollodorus rationalizes the recurring jar by saying Eurystheus had a bronze refuge constructed after the Nemean Lion. A Caeretan vase transfers the jar to Cerberus’ delivery, confirming that it was not invariably exclusive to the boar.

### Pholus and the Centaurs’ wine

The Pholus material is ancient in its own right: Stesichorus already knew Pholus giving Heracles wine. What cannot be proved is when it became fixed specifically “on the way” to the boar.

In Apollodorus, Pholus serves Heracles roasted meat while eating his own raw. He hesitates to open a communal wine jar belonging to all the Centaurs, but Heracles insists. Its aroma draws the Centaurs, armed with rocks and fir trees. Heracles initially repels them with firebrands and then uses Hydra-poisoned arrows. Survivors flee toward Chiron at Malea. This disaster delays but does not prevent the hunt.

Diodorus places the Centaur episode after the capture and gives it a providential Dionysiac framework: Dionysus had designated the ancestral wine for Heracles generations earlier. The smell causes madness; Nephele’s storm assists the Centaurs. Thus “Pholus on the way” is canonical chiefly through Apollodorus, not uniform ancient chronology.

### Chiron’s wounding

Apollodorus gives the best-known form: an arrow shot at the Centaurs passes through Elatus’ arm and lodges in Chiron’s knee. Chiron’s immortality prevents death but not incurable pain. Prometheus ultimately receives or exchanges immortality in the wider Chiron tradition, allowing Chiron to die, though Apollodorus does not develop the full exchange at this point.

Sophocles proves that Heracles’ wounding of Chiron was known in the fifth century BCE but does not preserve the Apollodoran mechanics. Hyginus gives different accidents involving a dropped arrow and Chiron’s foot. These are competing versions, not reconcilable details.

### Pholus’ death

In Apollodorus, Pholus pulls an arrow from a dead Centaur and wonders how so small a weapon killed so great a creature. It slips, pierces his foot, and kills him. Heracles returns, finds him dead, and buries him before continuing the hunt. The parallel accidental deaths of the two “good” Centaurs may be a mythic doublet; that is modern structural analysis, not an ancient explanation.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

There is **no documented cult of the Erymanthian Boar**.

No surviving inscription records:

- a dedication to it;
- a priest or priestess;
- a sacrifice or offering made to it;
- a festival, oracle, procession, prayer, or mystery;
- a temple or altar belonging to it;
- a theophoric personal name derived from it;
- magical invocation in the Greek Magical Papyri.

### Material claims near the myth

**Cumae:** Pausanias 8.24.5 records tusks in Apollo’s sanctuary that local people identified as those of the Erymanthian Boar. Pausanias expressly doubts the claim. This is evidence for a Roman-period local relic tradition, not proof of the tusks’ identity or of worship of the animal. A modern paleontological proposal suggests such “heroic” relics could sometimes have been fossils, but the Cumaean specimens are lost or unidentified, so the explanation cannot be tested.

**Psophis:** Pausanias records a temple and statue of the river Erymanthus. That was worship of a river personification, not the boar.

**Olympia:** The Temple of Zeus displayed the labor among Heracles’ deeds. This is sanctuary decoration and Panhellenic heroic ideology, not a cult of the monster.

No evidence links the boar to human sacrifice, temple prostitution, ritual violence, or political suppression. General Greek boar sacrifices must not be reassigned to this individual creature.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before c. 560 BCE: formation not recoverable

Boar hunting was a real aristocratic and dangerous activity, and destructive boars were familiar in Greek epic. The Calydonian hunt already appears in the *Iliad*. These contexts may explain the plausibility of the Erymanthian episode, but no Mycenaean tablet or securely identified Geometric image names this boar.

### c. 560–480 BCE: iconographic consolidation

Artists establish the delivery scene, the living inverted boar, and Eurystheus’ *pithos*. The theme becomes especially popular in Attic black-figure painting. Hecataeus localizes the threat at Psophis; early lyric and art attest the neighboring Pholus complex.

### Fifth–fourth centuries BCE: literary recognition and geographical variation

Sophocles treats the exploit as proverbial enough for allusion. Euripides knows Centaur battles at Pholoe and Thessaly. Herodorus preserves a variant location for delivery. The labor’s numbering remains fluid.

### Hellenistic period

Apollonius integrates the captured boar into Argonautic chronology and shifts emphasis from Eurystheus’ terror to Heracles’ passage toward the Argo. Hellenistic cataloguing encourages a fixed series but does not establish a single order.

### Roman Republic and Empire

Diodorus and Apollodorus provide the narratives that dominate modern retellings. Latin poets expand the creature’s physical enormity. Pausanias connects the story with Arcadian topography, monumental art, and Cumaean relic display. Reliefs and mosaics reproduce the canonical visual type across the Roman world.

### Late antiquity and Byzantium

Quintus and pseudo-Claudian elaborate spectacle; Dracontius and several lists permit the boar to be killed. Byzantine compilers preserve the episode while adding marginal adventures such as the Dryopian refusal of food. There is no evidence for continuing worship requiring suppression or Christian conversion.

### Medieval and early-modern reception

The labor survives through mythographic manuscripts, Ovidian education, emblematic art, prints, sculpture, and cycles of Hercules’ labors. Renaissance and Baroque artists favor muscular capture and the comic-terror confrontation with Eurystheus. No securely documented medieval occult cult of the animal is known.

### Modern period

Classical dictionaries and handbooks increasingly standardize “fourth labor,” although Diodorus, Hyginus, inscriptions, and poetic catalogues preserve “third.” Archaeological publication restored the importance of the pre-literary visual record.

No continuous folk cult or demonstrably organized modern worship of the individual boar is documented. Contemporary Hellenic polytheism chiefly honors deities and heroes; internet references to the boar do not establish a cult community.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and popular mythography

Karl Kerényi treats the episode within the heroic career of Heracles, while Robert Graves incorporates it into a highly synthetic interpretive system. Both are modern retellings or interpretations, not primary evidence. Graves’ ritualist and comparativist deductions require independent verification and should not be presented as surviving ancient doctrine.

Nineteenth-century solar mythologists sometimes interpreted Heracles’ labors as Indo-European meteorological or solar allegories and compared the boar with clouds, winter, or darkness. These theories belong to the history of scholarship and are not accepted as recoverable ancient belief without independent evidence.

### Art and literature

François Lespingola’s bronze *Hercules Delivering the Erymanthean Boar to Eurystheus*, made in the last quarter of the seventeenth century, intensifies the moment before the animal is thrown. The ancient delivery type thereby continued in European court art.

Agatha Christie’s **“The Erymanthian Boar”** appeared in *The Labours of Hercules* (1947). Hercule Poirot’s adversary is a dangerous human criminal, Marrascaud; the title converts the mythic beast into a metaphor within a modern twelve-case cycle. It was adapted in the 2013 television episode *The Labours of Hercules*.

### Games

Ubisoft’s *Assassin’s Creed Odyssey* (2018) makes the Erymanthian Boar a legendary animal that the player kills for the Daughters of Artemis. Its poisonous flatulence, pelt quest, direct association with an Artemis cult, and death at the hands of Kassandra or Alexios are **[MODERN INVENTION]**, not ancient variants.

*Hades II* incorporated an Erymanthian Boar into its developing modern mythological game world by 2025. Such appearances show continued recognition of the name but do not transmit ancient ritual belief.

### Contested modern occult claims

Web pages now claim that:

- the boar represents alchemical *prima materia* or a fourth stage of the Great Work;
- the *Rosarium Philosophorum* associates it with *nigredo*;
- the *Key of Solomon* gives it a Mars-hour sigil;
- PGM IV uses a captured Erymanthian-boar image to bind an enemy;
- Austin Osman Spare used it as a specific symbol of the captured self;
- it corresponds to Aries, Mars, or January.

No reliable edition or academic study located in the dedicated search substantiates these claims. They conflict with the absence of the named creature from the PGM and appear on recent automatically assembled “grimoire” pages. They should be classified as **[MODERN INVENTION]** unless an exact primary-text passage and manuscript can be produced.

No documented nationalist, extremist, or commercial religious appropriation specific to the Erymanthian Boar was found.

- Daniel Ogden, “Labor IV: The Erymanthian Boar (and Pholus),” in *The Oxford Handbook of Heracles* (2021).

### Principal disputes

**Earliest image:** A c. 600 BCE fragment may represent the scene, but full identification is uncertain. Secure examples begin around 560 BCE. Different summaries sometimes give c. 510 BCE because they count only a narrower or fully preserved image class.

**Third or fourth labor:** The Tabula Albana, Diodorus, Quintus, Ausonius, and other catalogues make it third; Apollodorus and later influential retellings make it fourth. “Fourth” is conventional, not universally ancient.

**Where was the beast?** Sources variously emphasize Psophis, Mount Erymanthus, Mount Lampeia, the Erymanthus River or marsh, a wood, Maenalus, Thessaly, and—in later lexicographic summaries—Phrygia. These reflect changing geography and poetic compression, not one precise map.

**Captured or killed?** Archaic art, Apollonius, Diodorus, Apollodorus, and Quintus emphasize capture alive. Hyginus, the Tabula Albana, Martial, Dracontius, and later catalogues imply killing. The live-capture version is earlier and visually dominant but not exclusive.

**When did the Centaur episode occur?** Apollodorus places it before the hunt; Diodorus places it after. Early evidence proves the antiquity of Pholus, wine, and Centaur combat but not their original position within the boar narrative.

**Meaning of the jar:** It can dramatize cowardice, supplication, comic reversal, or Heracles’ hostility toward Eurystheus. It should not automatically be treated as a ritual vessel, womb symbol, initiation chamber, or chthonic portal; ancient testimony does not give those interpretations.

**Relic tusks:** Pausanias’ skepticism is itself primary evidence. Identifying the objects as fossils is an attractive modern hypothesis but cannot be verified because the specimens are unavailable.

**Artemis theory:** Erymanthus is Artemis’ hunting terrain in Homer, and Artemis sends the Calydonian Boar. That does not establish that she sent, owned, or protected the Erymanthian animal. Conflation remains common in popular retellings.

### State of the evidence

The literary tradition is dispersed across fragments, allusions, mythographic summaries, scholia, Latin poetry, and Byzantine lexica. Hecataeus and Herodorus survive only by quotation. Numerous vases are distributed among European and American collections; the LIMC and WebLIMC provide the most systematic iconographic cataloguing. The Foce del Sele and Olympia metopes preserve the monumental tradition. The alleged Cumaean tusks have not been securely identified.

## 11. Sources

### Primary sources and editions

The principal primary witnesses are Homer, Stesichorus, Hecataeus F6, Panyassis, Pindar, Sophocles, Euripides, Herodorus F24, Apollonius Rhodius 1.122–132, Diodorus 4.12, the Tabula Albana/IG XIV 1293, Ovid, Hyginus 30, Seneca, Statius, Apollodorus 2.5.4, Pausanias 5.10.9 and 8.24.3–5, Quintus Smyrnaeus 6, pseudo-Claudian’s *Laus Herculis*, Dracontius, the Suda, and Tzetzes.

The most important methodological conclusion is negative: the boar has no ancient personal name, genealogy, cult, prayer, priesthood, festival, magical invocation, or documented status as Artemis’ animal. Its historical “life” is the transmission of one heroic adversary through Archaic images, fragmentary Classical references, Hellenistic and Roman mythography, monumental art, learned catalogues, and modern popular reinvention.
