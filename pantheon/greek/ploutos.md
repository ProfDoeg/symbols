# Ploutos: Research Dossier

**Class:** Male Greek god (*theos*) and personification of wealth, especially agricultural abundance; in later literature an allegorical divine character. He is not originally identical with Plouton/Hades.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Hesiod, *Theogony* 969–974, composed in Greek probably around 700 BCE: Demeter bears Ploutos to Iasion after their union in a thrice-ploughed field in Crete. No securely identified earlier Mycenaean Linear B attestation or image is known.

**Evidentiary warning:** Three figures are often collapsed but must initially be separated:

1. **Πλοῦτος, Ploutos**, “Wealth,” Demeter and Iasion’s son.
2. **Πλούτων, Ploutōn**, “the Wealthy One,” a Classical-period name of Hades.
3. The ordinary noun **πλοῦτος**, “wealth, abundance.”

Ancient writers and artists sometimes brought the first two into deliberate proximity, but similarity of name does not make every Plouton text, Ploutonion sanctuary, or underworld image evidence for Ploutos.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identity

**[Securely attested early material]** Hesiod calls Ploutos a “kindly” or “noble” god, **ἐσθλὸς θεός**, who travels over land and sea and enriches whoever encounters him (*Theogony* 969–974). His earliest identity is consequently not “money” in the narrow sense. He embodies transferable prosperity grounded in agricultural production but capable of circulating through the whole inhabited world.

**[Archaic elaboration]** The *Homeric Hymn to Demeter* 486–489, probably composed in the later seventh or sixth century BCE, makes Ploutos an agent whom Demeter and Persephone send to the household of a mortal they favor. This locates him within the Eleusinian sphere without making him an underworld king.

**[Classical literary development]** By the fifth and fourth centuries BCE, moralizing poetry and comedy treated wealth as indiscriminate or “blind.” Aristophanes’ extant *Ploutos* of 388 BCE dramatized this metaphor as a myth: Zeus blinded the young god because Ploutos proposed to enrich only decent people.

### Names and scripts

- **Ancient Greek:** Πλοῦτος, *Ploûtos* in conventional scholarly transliteration; reconstructed Classical Attic pronunciation approximately /plûː.tos/. The same spelling is the common noun “wealth.”
- **Latin:** *Plutus*, the regular Latinized form. Latin writers could also use *Pluto* ambiguously, especially once Ploutos and Plouton/Hades were being assimilated.
- **Modern Greek:** Πλούτος, *Ploútos*, also still the common noun “wealth.”
- **English:** Ploutos preserves the Greek form; Plutus is the conventional Latinized form.
- **French:** Ploutos or Plutus.
- **German:** Plutos.
- **Italian and Spanish:** Pluto can be dangerously ambiguous because it may denote either Ploutos or Plouton/Hades.

### Etymology

**[Secure linguistic fact]** The divine name is transparently identical to Greek **πλοῦτος**, “wealth, riches, abundance.” Homeric poetry uses the noun non-theologically, sometimes alongside **ὄλβος**, prosperity or blessed fortune. The distinction is not absolute, but *ploutos* tends toward material abundance while *olbos* can emphasize divinely sanctioned flourishing.

**[Disputed reconstruction]** The deeper Indo-European etymology is uncertain. Proposals connect *ploutos* with notions of fullness, abundance, flowing, or “having much”; popular derivations from **πολύς**, “much,” are not a substitute for a demonstrated historical derivation. The safely recoverable ancient fact is the divine name’s identity with the Greek noun.

### Ploutos versus Plouton

**[Classical syncretistic development]** Πλούτων, *Ploutōn*, is morphologically “the rich one” or “wealth-giver” and becomes a euphemistic or beneficent title/name for Hades during the Classical period. Plato, *Cratylus* 403a, explains Plouton’s name from the wealth that rises from beneath the earth. This creates a structural connection—grain, metals, buried treasure, and the dead all belong below the surface—but does not erase the archaic genealogy that makes Ploutos Demeter and Iasion’s son.

Modern scholarship divides broadly as follows:

- Martin P. Nilsson proposed historical convergence between Ploutos and Plouton and treated Plouton as, in part, a development from the wealth-god.
- The more cautious position begins with two distinct formations: personified Wealth and the underworld ruler’s euphemistic title, which subsequently overlap in Eleusinian and agrarian settings.
- Timocreon’s fifth-century BCE fragment already exploits the proximity: blind Ploutos is told that he ought to have remained in Tartarus and Acheron. This is poetic association, not proof that every Greek regarded the gods as identical.
- Aristophanes appears to call his wealth-god **Plouton** at *Ploutos* 727, another sign that the boundary could be rhetorically porous.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Karl Kerényi inferred from Eleusinian images that a divine child Ploutos could be the child of Persephone and Plouton. No surviving primary text explicitly gives Ploutos that parentage. It must not be reported as an attested ancient genealogy.

### Epithets and descriptions

Ploutos has no securely documented, stable catalogue of cult epithets comparable to Zeus or Athena. Attested descriptions include:

- **ἐσθλὸς θεός**, “kindly/noble god”: Hesiod, *Theogony* 971.
- **ἀφνειὸν … δίδωσι**, “gives wealth”: Hesiod, *Theogony* 974, a function rather than a formal cult title.
- “Blind Ploutos”: Timocreon fr. 731 and Aristophanes’ *Ploutos*; later proverbial.
- “Giver of wealth to mortal people”: *Homeric Hymn to Demeter* 489.
- Winged, golden, and sighted: Philostratus, *Imagines* 2.27, describing a purported Rhodian image.

Claims that Ploutos had fixed sacred colors, numbers, weekdays, planets, gemstones, or modern “correspondences” are **not established by ancient evidence**.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

#### Hesiod, *Theogony* 969–974

**Date and language:** Archaic Greek hexameter, normally dated around 700 BCE, with debate extending into the early seventh century.

**Textual support:** The work survives through medieval manuscripts, supplemented by ancient quotations and papyri; the surviving copies are much later than its composition. The Ploutos passage is nevertheless part of the securely transmitted text.

**What it says:** Demeter has intercourse with Iasion in a thrice-ploughed fallow in rich Crete and gives birth to Ploutos. He moves across earth and sea and makes rich the mortal who encounters him.

**What it does not say:** It does not make Ploutos blind, call Zeus his father, give him a cornucopia, identify him with Hades, describe a temple, or connect him explicitly with an Eleusinian secret birth.

### Archaic corpus

- **Homer, *Odyssey* 5.125–128**, probably late eighth or seventh century BCE: Calypso recalls Demeter’s union with Iasion in a thrice-ploughed field and Zeus’s killing of Iasion with a thunderbolt. Ploutos is not named. This is early evidence for his parents’ liaison, not independently for the child.
- **Hesiod, *Theogony* 969–974**, ca. 700 BCE: earliest explicit Ploutos.
- **Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, fragments 177 and 185 Merkelbach–West**, archaic but fragmentary: preserves traditions about Iasion/Eetion’s union with Demeter and Zeus’s thunderbolt; Ploutos’ role in the surviving fragments is limited.
- **Homeric Hymn 2 to Demeter 486–489**, later seventh or sixth century BCE: Demeter and Persephone send Ploutos to a favored mortal’s house.
- **Theognidean corpus 523–526**, material assembled between the sixth and fifth centuries BCE: mortals honor Ploutos most because he tolerates baseness—that is, wealth accepts bad possessors.
- **Hipponactean or comic proverbial material:** fragments about blind Wealth reflect the emerging topos, but individual attributions and dates are not always secure.
- **Aesopic tradition, “Heracles and Ploutos,” preserved much later in Phaedrus 4.12:** Heracles refuses to greet Ploutos because wealth befriends the wicked and corrupts people. The story’s extant Latin form is Imperial; assigning its exact wording to sixth-century Aesop would be unsafe.

### Classical corpus

- **Timocreon fr. 731**, early fifth century BCE: curses blind Ploutos and associates him rhetorically with Tartarus and Acheron.
- **Aristophanes, *Thesmophoriazusae* 295–311**, 411 BCE: the women’s herald invokes Demeter, Kore, Ploutos, Kalligeneia, Kourotrophos, Ge, Hermes, and the Charites for the welfare of Athens and its women. This is literary evidence modeling festival prayer, not an inscriptional ritual rubric.
- **Aristophanes, first *Ploutos***, produced 408 BCE: known from ancient notices but lost or extensively reworked. Its precise plot cannot be reconstructed.
- **Aristophanes, extant *Ploutos***, 388 BCE: the fullest ancient narrative about the god. The transmitted play is the 388 production; ancient scholarship sometimes mistakenly treated it as the 408 version. Modern study of the scholia confirms that two Aristophanic productions existed.
- **Plato, *Laws* 631c**, ca. 350s BCE: contrasts foolish “blind Ploutos” with wealth that possesses sight because wisdom accompanies it.
- **Attic drama generally:** tragedies often personify or morally qualify *ploutos*, but a capitalized translation can create a god where Greek syntax only means “wealth.” No major surviving tragedy contains a narrative cycle centered on Ploutos.

### Hellenistic and Roman-period corpus

- **Diodorus Siculus, *Library of History* 5.48.2 and 5.77.1**, first century BCE: rationalizes the genealogy. One explanation says “Ploutos” meant the abundance produced when Iasion cultivated the earth; another makes Ploutos a historical culture-bringer who taught acquisition and preservation of property.
- **Cicero, *On the Nature of the Gods* 2.61**, 45 BCE: includes abstract benefits such as Wealth among entities deified because of their utility. This is Roman philosophical classification, not evidence of a Ploutos temple.
- **Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 3.12.1/3.138**, probably first or second century CE: Iasion assaults or desires Demeter and is struck by Zeus. Ploutos is not central in the surviving wording.
- **Pausanias, *Description of Greece* 1.8.2, 9.16.2, 9.26.8**, ca. 150–175 CE: records the Athenian Eirene carrying Ploutos, Theban Tyche carrying him, and Ploutos beside Athena Ergane at Thespiai.
- **Pseudo-Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.4**, probably second century CE in its transmitted form: Plutus and Philomelus are brothers, sons of Ceres and Iasion. Rich Plutus refuses to share with poor Philomelus, who invents the plough-wagon and becomes Boötes.
- **Lucian, *Timon or the Misanthrope***, second century CE: Zeus orders Hermes to escort blind Ploutos back to Timon. Poverty, Labor, Wisdom, and Courage oppose the transfer; Ploutos reveals buried treasure and Timon becomes a wealthy misanthrope.
- **Philostratus the Elder, *Imagines* 2.27**, early third century CE: describes Ploutos on the Rhodian acropolis as golden, winged, descending from a cloud, and deliberately sighted.
- **Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 11.390 and related passages**, fifth century CE: recalls Demeter’s fruitful union with Iasion but does not add an independent Ploutos cycle.

### Orphic hymns and magical papyri

The surviving **Orphic Hymns**, probably composed in Asia Minor during the second or third century CE, contain a hymn **to Plouton**, not to Demeter’s son Ploutos. Hymn 18 calls the underworld ruler “Chthonic Zeus,” keeper of earth’s keys and giver of yearly fruits. It is valuable for studying convergence between subterranean rule and abundance, but cataloguing it simply as a hymn to Ploutos is philologically wrong.

The **Greek Magical Papyri**, principally manuscripts of the second century BCE through fifth century CE from Egypt, invoke Plouton, Hades, Kore, Demeter, chthonic powers, and wealth-producing or prosperity powers in several syncretistic rites. No well-attested PGM ritual has been demonstrated to invoke Demeter-and-Iasion’s Ploutos unambiguously as a separate magician’s god. The absence is significant: claims for an ancient “Ploutos money spell” commonly arise by mistranslating *ploutos* (“wealth”) or *Plouton*.

### Negative findings in the requested corpus

- **Homer:** knows the Demeter–Iasion liaison but does not name Ploutos.
- **Hesiod’s *Works and Days*:** contains extensive teaching about work and prosperity but no comparably secure narrative appearance by the god.
- **Pindar:** repeatedly moralizes about wealth and excellence; no major surviving Pindaric myth of Ploutos is securely established.
- **The tragedians:** use *ploutos* extensively as wealth and sometimes personify it rhetorically, but preserve no full Ploutos myth.
- **Orphic Hymns:** hymn Plouton, not securely Ploutos.
- **PGM:** no securely isolated cult or spell of the distinct Hesiodic god has been identified.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**[Early core]** Agricultural surplus: grain, stored produce, and the prosperity generated by cultivated land. The genealogy itself joins cultivated Earth’s goddess, Demeter, to a mortal culture-hero in a ploughed field.

**[Archaic expansion]** Household prosperity: Demeter and Persephone send him into a favored home.

**[Classical moral and economic abstraction]** Wealth as transferable property, money, and social power. Aristophanes makes Ploutos responsible for acquiring slaves, houses, silver, political influence, sacrifices, and nearly every human craft. Penia’s rebuttal is that universal wealth would eliminate labor and production.

**[Later rationalization]** Diodorus makes Ploutos either a name for crop abundance or the inventor of accumulating and guarding property.

### Forms

1. **Mobile adult or unspecified god:** implied by Hesiod’s god who travels land and sea.
2. **Blind elderly beggar:** Aristophanes, 388 BCE, and Lucian, second century CE. Blindness signifies indiscriminate distribution.
3. **Infant or small naked boy:** dominant in Classical and Roman visual art; embodies prosperity as something generated, nurtured, and protected.
4. **Winged golden figure:** Philostratus’ Rhodian description; a regional/literary variant in which his rapid arrival from heaven and precious substance embody sudden wealth.
5. **Sighted god:** Philostratus and the healed Ploutos of Aristophanes, each for a particular narrative purpose.

Claims that Ploutos was canonically lame and winged derive chiefly from proverbial allegory and later summaries; they are not uniformly early physical attributes.

### Diagnostic attributes

- **Cornucopia:** the principal later attribute, filled with fruit or grain.
- **Wheat or ears of grain:** links wealth to Demeter and harvest.
- **Infancy/nudity:** frequent when held by Eirene or Tyche.
- **Blind eyes, staff, beggar’s clothing:** literary rather than the dominant surviving sculptural type.
- **Gold and wings:** specifically Philostratus’ Rhodian image.
- **No secure ancient sacred animal, fixed color, number, or celestial body** is documented.

### Major images

#### The Great Eleusinian Relief

A monumental Attic marble relief, conventionally dated ca. 440–430 BCE, shows Demeter, Kore, and a youthful figure. The youth is often identified as Triptolemos. Other proposed identifications include Ploutos, Eubouleus, Iakchos, a child initiate, or a composite Eleusinian initiate-hero.

**[Contested]** It is therefore unsafe to call this the earliest certain portrait of Ploutos. It is, however, crucial evidence for the iconographic environment in which an Eleusinian sacred child could be visualized.

#### Eleusinian child imagery

Late fifth- and fourth-century BCE vases and reliefs show a small boy with Demeter and Kore, sometimes holding or associated with a cornucopia. Kevin Clinton’s iconographic work identifies Ploutos in a number of these scenes but emphasizes the problem created by uninscribed children and overlapping iconographic types. One analogous figure is inscribed **Eniautos**, “Year” or “the year’s produce,” suggesting that several child personifications could represent agricultural renewal.

#### Kephisodotos’ Eirene and Ploutos

**[Secure Classical monument through literary testimony and copies]** Pausanias credits Kephisodotos the Elder with the bronze group of Eirene holding infant Ploutos, erected in the Athenian Agora. It is normally dated ca. 375–370 BCE, often connected with Athens’ establishment or renewal of Eirene’s cult following peace with Sparta in 375/4 or the peace settlement of 371.

The original is lost. Its design is reconstructed from Roman marble copies and images on Panathenaic amphorae, including securely dated examples of 360/59 BCE. Eirene stands in peplos and mantle, holding a scepter while cradling the boy; Ploutos reaches toward her and is associated with the cornucopia. The political proposition is visual and public: peace nurses civic prosperity.

Important copies or fragments include:

- The near-complete Roman copy in the Munich Glyptothek.
- A first-century CE infant Ploutos from Piraeus, National Archaeological Museum, Athens, inv. Γ 175.
- Additional Roman torsos, heads, and adaptations.

#### Tyche and Ploutos

Pausanias saw a sanctuary at Thebes whose cult image showed Tyche carrying the child Ploutos, made by Xenophon of Athens and Kallistonikos of Thebes. He interprets Tyche as mother or nurse: fortune controls wealth. Roman reliefs and coins repeat the type; a third-century CE relief from Melos shows Tyche with scepter and child.

This is symbolic fosterage, not necessarily a literal alternative birth myth. The late Aesopic/Phaedran fable that calls Plutus the son of Fortuna goes further and turns the visual relation into genealogy.

#### Athena Ergane and Ploutos at Thespiai

Pausanias 9.26.8 reports Ploutos standing beside Athena Ergane, “Athena the Worker.” The sculptor’s name is lost in a textual lacuna. The juxtaposition presents skilled work as a producer or guardian of prosperity; no narrative between them survives.

#### Apulian and South Italian images

Fourth-century BCE South Italian red-figure pottery sometimes depicts a child with cornucopia before Demeter or emerging in an Eleusinian divine assembly. Identification as Ploutos is often plausible but must be object-by-object; a cornucopia-bearing child is not automatically him.

## 4. Genealogy and Divine Relations

### Genealogical versions

#### Version A: Demeter and Iasion

**[Securely attested early material; dominant literary genealogy]**

- **Mother:** Demeter.
- **Father:** Iasion, a mortal or heroic son of Zeus and the Pleiad Electra in many genealogies.
- **Birth setting:** a thrice-ploughed field in rich Crete, Hesiod *Theogony* 969–974.
- **Related event:** Zeus kills Iasion with a thunderbolt after the union, Homer *Odyssey* 5.125–128. Hesiod’s Ploutos passage itself does not narrate the killing.

The thrice-ploughed field is both an erotic setting and an agricultural image. Attempts to reconstruct an otherwise unattested ritual of literal human sexual intercourse in a field remain speculative.

#### Version B: Son of Tyche/Fortuna

**[Late allegorical genealogy]** Phaedrus 4.12 calls Plutus a son of Fortuna in the transmitted Latin fable. Pausanias says the Theban sculpture merely suggested Tyche was his mother or nurse. The late genealogy likely rationalizes the familiar visual pairing.

#### Version C: Brother of Philomelus

**[Late mythographic elaboration]** Pseudo-Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.4, citing Hermippus and the Cretan writer Petellides, makes Plutus and Philomelus sons of Ceres and Iasion. Plutus is rich but refuses to help his poor brother. Philomelus invents the wagon or plough-team and becomes the constellation Boötes.

#### Version D: Child of Persephone and Plouton/Hades

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction, not primary-source genealogy]** Kerényi and some later popular writers interpret Eleusinian images as implying this parentage. No surviving Greek or Latin source explicitly states that Ploutos is the son of Hades/Plouton and Persephone. Online family trees presenting it as canonical reproduce an inference as fact.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

The following list separates an actual episode or visual conjunction from mere pantheon membership.

- **Demeter — conception and birth:** Demeter bears Ploutos after union with Iasion in Crete; Hesiod, *Theogony* 969–974, ca. 700 BCE. **Secure early attestation.**
- **Demeter — dispatch to favored households:** Demeter and Persephone send Ploutos to people they love; *Homeric Hymn to Demeter* 486–489, later seventh/sixth century BCE. **Archaic Eleusinian elaboration.**
- **Persephone/Kore — joint dispatch:** With Demeter, she sends Ploutos as a household guest; same source and date. **Archaic elaboration.**
- **Zeus — blinding:** Ploutos says Zeus blinded him because he intended to enrich only the just; Aristophanes, *Ploutos* 87–92, 388 BCE. **Classical comic myth, not in Hesiod.**
- **Apollo — oracle initiating the cure:** Apollo’s Delphic oracle tells Chremylos to follow the first person he meets, leading him to Ploutos; Aristophanes, *Ploutos* 33–49. **Classical comic narrative.**
- **Penia — confrontation:** Poverty argues that curing Ploutos and enriching everyone would destroy craft and labor; Aristophanes, *Ploutos* approximately 415–618. **Classical comic agon.**
- **Asklepios — healing:** During temple incubation Asklepios wipes Ploutos’ eyelids and sends sacred snakes to restore his sight; Aristophanes, *Ploutos* approximately 653–747. **Classical comic narrative drawing on incubation ritual.**
- **Panakeia — assistance in healing:** She covers Ploutos’ face with a purple cloth while Asklepios’ snakes lick his eyes; Aristophanes, *Ploutos* 730s. **Classical comic narrative.**
- **Hephaistos — receives the sacrificial flame/cake:** Offerings for Ploutos’ incubation are given to the altar fire conventionally called Hephaistos; Aristophanes, *Ploutos* 660s. **Ritual personification within comic narrative; not a separate mythic alliance.**
- **Athena — reception in Athens:** After recovering sight, Ploutos salutes Athens as the city beloved of Pallas; Aristophanes, *Ploutos* 770s. **Classical comic joint reference, not a developed episode.**
- **Athena — enthronement:** At the play’s conclusion Ploutos is installed in the opisthodomos of Athena’s temple, where the civic treasury was imagined; Aristophanes, *Ploutos* 1191–1209. **Comic civic-religious finale.**
- **Eirene — nurtures or carries infant Ploutos:** Kephisodotos’ sculptural group, ca. 375–370 BCE; reported by Pausanias 1.8.2 and 9.16.2 and preserved in Roman copies. **Secure Classical iconographic interaction.**
- **Tyche — carries infant Ploutos:** Theban cult image reported by Pausanias 9.16.2; later reliefs and coins. **Regional Classical/early Imperial iconographic relation.**
- **Athena Ergane — stands beside Ploutos:** Thespian statues reported by Pausanias 9.26.8. **Regional cult-image association; no narrative survives.**
- **Heracles — rejects Ploutos:** Deified Heracles turns away from Ploutos because wealth befriends and corrupts the wicked; Phaedrus 4.12, first century CE, preserving an Aesopic fable. **Imperial literary attestation, conventionally attributed to the Aesopic tradition.**
- **Zeus — sends Ploutos to Timon:** Zeus orders that Timon be enriched again; Lucian, *Timon*, second century CE. **Imperial satirical elaboration.**
- **Hermes — escorts blind Ploutos:** Hermes leads him by the cloak to Timon and compels him to obey Zeus; Lucian, *Timon*. **Imperial satirical elaboration.**
- **Penia — opposes his return to Timon:** Poverty argues that she, Labor, Wisdom, Courage, and Perseverance made Timon virtuous; Lucian, *Timon*. **Imperial satirical elaboration.**
- **Labor, Wisdom, Courage, and Perseverance — antagonistic entourage:** These personified powers accompany Penia and intimidate Ploutos; Lucian, *Timon*. **Imperial allegory.**
- **Ge, Kalligeneia, Kourotrophos, Hermes, and the Charites — joint invocation:** Named alongside Ploutos, Demeter, and Kore in the women’s prayer; Aristophanes, *Thesmophoriazusae* 295–311, 411 BCE. **Classical festival representation; co-invocation, not narrative interaction.**
- **Plouton/Hades — no explicit surviving meeting:** Association and partial assimilation are documented, but no secure ancient text narrates the two as separate characters meeting.
- **Dionysos/Iakchos — no secure direct episode:** Both occur in the wider Eleusinian religious field, but no surviving source establishes a discrete Ploutos–Dionysos encounter.
- **Triptolemos and Eubouleus — no secure direct episode:** Their images may share Eleusinian scenes with a child identified as Ploutos, but uninscribed figures and competing identifications prevent a confidently narrated interaction.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. Birth in the thrice-ploughed field

**Earliest source:** Hesiod, *Theogony* 969–974, ca. 700 BCE.

Demeter and Iasion make love in a thrice-ploughed fallow in Crete. Their child Ploutos becomes a wandering distributor of wealth. The poem’s agricultural logic is transparent: cultivated land and Demeter’s fertility produce abundance.

Homer’s *Odyssey* supplies a related but darker version. Zeus discovers Demeter and Iasion and kills Iasion with lightning. Homer does not name their child. Pseudo-Apollodorus later makes Iasion’s approach violent or attempted rape; Diodorus makes Demeter enamored of him and associates their union with the wedding of Kadmos and Harmonia.

**Development:** The earliest sources preserve a divine–heroic coupling and Zeus’s punitive jealousy but do not say Zeus blinds the resulting child. The blindness belongs to a separate later explanatory myth.

### B. Ploutos as Demeter and Persephone’s guest-gift

**Earliest source:** *Homeric Hymn to Demeter* 486–489.

At the hymn’s conclusion, the initiated and those favored by the goddesses are blessed; Demeter and Persephone quickly send Ploutos to dwell in the favored mortal’s house. Ploutos here is not an underworld judge. He is the concrete household benefit of the restored agricultural and divine order.

**Function:** The passage joins agrarian fertility, divine favor, and household prosperity to the Eleusinian goddesses. It does not prove that initiates were promised cash or that Ploutos was revealed as the Mysteries’ central secret.

### C. Zeus blinds Wealth

**Earliest secure narrative:** Aristophanes, *Ploutos* 87–92, 388 BCE, though the blindness trope is older and appears in Timocreon.

Ploutos explains that when young he intended to visit only just, wise, and orderly people. Zeus, jealous of morally good humans, blinded him so that he could no longer distinguish them. Consequently wicked politicians, informers, and temple-robbers become rich while decent people remain poor.

This reverses ordinary theology: the chief god, rather than guaranteeing justice, preserves an unjust economic distribution. Since it appears in comedy, it is evidence for an available mythic conceit and social critique, not necessarily a universally believed doctrine.

### D. The cure at Asklepios’ sanctuary

Chremylos takes Ploutos for incubation at an Asklepieion. After purification, sacrifice, and bedding down in the sanctuary, Asklepios arrives with Panakeia. Sacred snakes lick Ploutos’ eyelids; his sight returns. He then directs wealth toward the just.

The cure disrupts the entire economy. A just man prospers; a dishonest informer is ruined; an old woman discovers that her youthful lover cared for her gifts; Hermes complains that sacrifices to the Olympians have ceased because everyone is rich; Zeus’s priest defects to Ploutos. The god is finally installed near Athena’s treasury.

**Function:** The play tests a utopian redistribution of wealth and exposes its contradictions. Penia’s argument—that need produces work and civilization—is not defeated philosophically merely because comedy expels her.

### E. Ploutos and Philomelus

**Source:** Pseudo-Hyginus, *Astronomica* 2.4, probably second century CE.

Rich Plutus refuses assistance to his poor brother Philomelus. Philomelus uses what little he has to acquire oxen and invents the wagon or improved ploughing. Demeter rewards him by placing him among the stars as Boötes.

**Tier:** Late mythographic and astronomical elaboration. It turns the tension between inherited wealth and productive labor into a family conflict.

### F. Timon and the reluctant god

**Source:** Lucian, *Timon or the Misanthrope*, second century CE.

Having squandered his fortune on flatterers, Timon becomes a laboring recluse protected by Poverty and the virtues. Zeus nevertheless sends blind Ploutos back with Hermes. Ploutos objects that Timon’s former prodigality made him as leaky as a Danaid’s jar. Poverty protests, but divine command prevails. Ploutos reveals buried treasure, and Timon becomes wealthy and even more hostile to humanity.

**Tier:** Imperial satirical reinvention. Lucian combines Aristophanic blindness, buried subterranean treasure, philosophical allegory, and the Timon legend.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Was there an independent cult of Ploutos?

Evidence is thin. No securely documented pan-Hellenic priesthood, festival, oracle, or major freestanding temple can be assigned to Ploutos as confidently as those of Demeter, Athena, or Asklepios. His religious presence is normally embedded in larger cults and civic ensembles.

This does not make him “merely literary.” Ancient Greek religion accommodated personifications through hymns, prayers, images, and joint cult without requiring each to possess a temple and priesthood.

### Thesmophoria

Aristophanes’ *Thesmophoriazusae* includes Ploutos in a formal prayer addressed with Demeter, Kore, Kalligeneia, Kourotrophos, Ge, Hermes, and the Charites. This is the strongest literary evidence for invocation within a women’s agricultural festival.

It does not establish:

- a separate sacrifice to Ploutos;
- a Ploutos priest;
- a uniform prayer used throughout Greece;
- a fixed modern calendar date independent of each polis’s lunisolar calendar.

### Eleusis

Ploutos belongs to the symbolic and iconographic world of Demeter and Kore. The hymn’s closing blessing and Classical art both support that connection.

The sanctuary called the **Ploutonion** at Eleusis, however, belonged to **Plouton**, the underworld god, not automatically to the child Ploutos. Building accounts of 329/8 BCE mention work associated with the Ploutonion, and archaeological remains at the natural cave southeast of the acropolis are conventionally identified with it. Votive reliefs of Plouton and Persephone reinforce the identification.

The child Ploutos may appear in Eleusinian reliefs, but individual identifications remain contested. The famous late report in Hippolytus, *Refutation of All Heresies* 5.8, says the hierophant proclaimed, “The Mistress has borne a holy child, Brimo [has borne] Brimos.” The child is unnamed. Identifying him specifically as Ploutos, Iakchos, Dionysos, or another mystery-child is interpretation, not the wording of the evidence.

### Athens

The Eirene–Ploutos group in the Agora was a public civic image. It likely functioned within Eirene’s cult and Athenian peace ideology. Ploutos’ placement in her arms made prosperity the dependent child of peace.

Aristophanes’ concluding installation of Ploutos in Athena’s opisthodomos alludes to the city’s treasury. It is comic theatre, not documentary proof that an actual cult image of Ploutos was permanently installed there after 388 BCE.

### Thebes and Thespiai

- **Thebes:** sanctuary of Tyche with Ploutos child; Pausanias 9.16.2.
- **Thespiai:** image of Ploutos beside Athena Ergane; Pausanias 9.26.8.

These are regional visual theologies: fortune mothers wealth; skilled labor stands beside it.

### Sacrifice and offerings

No distinctive sacrificial animal or offering regimen is securely documented for Ploutos. In Aristophanes, the rites concern Asklepios’ incubation and later Ploutos’ installation; they are comic representations of recognizable Greek ritual.

### Human sacrifice, temple prostitution, and cult violence

**Dedicated contested-material finding:** No ancient source, inscription, or archaeological report securely connects Ploutos’ cult with human sacrifice, ritual killing, temple prostitution, sexual servitude, or organized cult violence. Greek traditions of human sacrifice elsewhere must not be transferred to him by analogy.

The Demeter–Iasion union in a ploughed field has sometimes encouraged speculative “fertility ritual” reconstruction. No evidence establishes temple prostitution or an enacted sexual rite for Ploutos.

### Economics and politics

The most securely political use is the Eirene–Ploutos monument: fourth-century Athens publicly represented peace as the nurse of prosperity. Aristophanes’ play places the god at the center of arguments about redistribution, corruption, labor, sacrifice, civic finance, slavery, and economic inequality. In the Roman period, Tyche-and-Ploutos images and provincial coins could connect urban fortune, imperial prosperity, fertility, and dynastic children.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### ca. 700 BCE: agrarian genealogy

Ploutos enters the record as Demeter and Iasion’s child, the mobile bestower of abundance. His identity is rooted in cultivation and surplus.

### Seventh–sixth centuries BCE: household and Eleusinian integration

The *Homeric Hymn to Demeter* makes him the goddesses’ gift to favored households. Elegy begins criticizing wealth’s indifference to moral worth.

### Fifth century BCE: blindness and moral critique

Timocreon calls him blind and associates him with evils. Aristophanes includes him in a Thesmophorian prayer. Eleusinian art develops divine-child imagery, though not every child is securely identifiable.

### 408 and 388 BCE: comic personhood

Aristophanes produces two plays called *Ploutos*. Only the 388 version survives substantially. The god becomes an adult speaking character with a biography—blinded by Zeus, healed by Asklepios, and installed within civic Athens.

### ca. 375–350 BCE: civic infant

Kephisodotos’ Eirene group turns Ploutos into a nurtured infant and monumentalizes peace-produced prosperity. Plato makes the contrast between blind and sighted wealth philosophical.

### Hellenistic period: abstraction and convergence

Ploutos is increasingly available as an allegorical child with cornucopia. Plouton/Hades’ identity as a giver of subterranean fertility and wealth grows prominent. The figures overlap without becoming consistently indistinguishable.

### First century BCE–third century CE: rationalization and regional multiplication

Diodorus historicizes Ploutos as crop abundance or a culture-hero of property. Roman and Greek art pair him with Tyche/Fortuna and other civic personifications. Lucian expands the blind god’s satirical characterization. Philostratus supplies a distinct winged, golden, sighted Rhodian form.

### Fourth–fifth centuries CE: displacement

Imperial anti-sacrifice legislation and Christianization ended public state-supported polytheistic ritual. The Eleusinian sanctuary was damaged and ceased functioning around the end of the fourth century, traditionally connected with Alaric’s incursion in 395/396, though the exact agents, sequence, and religious motives behind the destruction remain debated. This marks the end of the institutional matrix most closely associated with Ploutos, not a documented edict against his individual cult.

### Medieval period: demonization and confusion

Greek *ploutos* remained an ordinary word, while Latin and vernacular “Pluto” blurred Ploutos with the underworld ruler. Christian literature could turn the wealth/underworld figure into a demon of avarice.

### Renaissance to nineteenth century: classical allegory

Humanist editions of Aristophanes and Lucian restored literary Plutus to European readers. Plutus became a stock personification of riches in emblem books, court pageants, satire, political economy, and theatre. Archaeology and Roman copies reconstructed the Eirene group, while nineteenth-century scholarship attempted to separate Ploutos from Pluto/Hades.

### Twentieth century to present

LIMC cataloguing and Kevin Clinton’s work refined the iconographic distinctions among Ploutos, Plouton, Eubouleus, Iakchos, Triptolemos, and anonymous Eleusinian children. Modern Hellenic polytheists sometimes honor Ploutos through libations, grain, coins, charitable giving, or prayers derived from Hesiod and Aristophanes. These practices are modern reconstructions or innovations unless directly tied to an ancient rite; no reliable census isolates the number of Ploutos devotees.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Dante

In *Inferno* VII, composed in the early fourteenth century, “Pluto” guards the circle of hoarders and squanderers and utters the famously obscure “Pape Satàn, pape Satàn aleppe.” Medieval commentators disagreed whether Dante meant Hades/Pluto, Plutus the wealth-god, or a deliberate fusion. His placement with avarice strongly activates Plutus, while his infernal monstrosity activates Pluto. This is a medieval Christian conflation, not ancient Ploutos theology.

### Goethe

In *Faust II*, published in 1832, Plutus appears during the imperial masquerade as the personification of wealth, surrounded by allegories of luxury, fear, avarice, and paper-money economics. Goethe’s Plutus belongs to early-modern/classicizing political allegory.

### Theatre and economic thought

Aristophanes’ *Ploutos* has repeatedly been translated, adapted, and staged as a satire of inequality. Modern productions sometimes turn the cure of blindness into a critique of capitalism, communism, welfare policy, corruption, or utopian redistribution. These are receptions of the 388 BCE comedy, not recovered cult traditions.

### Psychology and archetypal writing

Jungian and post-Jungian writers sometimes treat Ploutos as an archetype of inner abundance, shadow wealth, blocked value, or the unconscious treasury. Such readings are twentieth-century psychological allegories. They are not Eleusinian doctrines.

### Occultism and ceremonial magic

A dedicated search finds no important role for the distinct Ploutos in the canonical Renaissance planetary-magic systems, the *Key of Solomon*, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, or early Thelema. Modern prosperity-magic pages occasionally invoke “Plutus” using candles, planetary hours, colors, crystals, or sigils. These correspondences are modern inventions or eclectic borrowings unless independently sourced.

The Orphic Hymn to Plouton is frequently relabeled online as a “hymn to Plutus.” That substitution creates a modern ritual text for the wrong deity.

### Neopagan worship

Modern Hellenic polytheism became publicly organized especially from the 1990s onward; Greek legal recognition of Hellenic religious practice came in the 2010s. Individual worship of Ploutos is documented online, but neither its scale nor continuous descent from antiquity can be established. Historically responsible reconstruction can draw on grain, household prosperity, Demeter, the *Hymn to Demeter*, and the Thesmophorian prayer; fixed “Ploutos feast days” circulated online are generally modern calendrical choices.

### Popular culture and commerce

“Plutus/Ploutos” is now used for financial companies, investment products, cryptocurrencies, software, artificial-intelligence projects, and economic journals. This is commercial naming based on “wealth,” not cultic survival. Fiction and games frequently make Plutus a demon, treasurer, dragon, plutocrat, or son of Hades. Such representations usually descend from Dante, the Pluto/Ploutos confusion, or generic fantasy conventions.

No substantial evidence was found for a distinctive modern extremist appropriation of Ploutos. Generic nationalist use of Greek antiquity should not be attributed specifically to him without documentation.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### God or abstraction?

Older reference works sometimes call Ploutos “merely” an abstraction. That describes his transparent name but risks imposing a modern binary. Hesiod explicitly calls him a god; Greeks could worship or represent personifications without deciding whether they were “real gods” by later theological criteria.

### Agricultural origin versus generalized money

The early genealogy and hymn favor agricultural prosperity. Classical comedy expands the domain to silver, trade, property, slavery, and political power. It is therefore inaccurate both to restrict him forever to grain and to project the modern financial god backward into Hesiod.

### Ploutos and Plouton

This remains the central dispute:

- **Separation model:** Ploutos is an archaic personification and son of Demeter; Plouton is a Classical euphemistic name of Hades.
- **Convergence model:** their shared relationship to earth’s wealth and Eleusinian religion encouraged actual assimilation.
- **Strong derivation model:** associated especially with Nilsson, Plouton developed from Ploutos or absorbed his cultic identity.
- **Cautious current practice:** distinguish them at first occurrence, then document each ancient overlap.

Farnell’s treatment of “Hades-Plouton” is important but largely concerns the underworld god, not automatically Ploutos. Modern database searches that merge *Ploutos*, *Plouton*, Latin *Pluto*, and the common noun produce many false positives.

### The Eleusinian child

The Great Eleusinian Relief and related vases generated identifications as Triptolemos, Ploutos, Eubouleus, Iakchos, an initiate, or other sacred youths. Clinton’s *Myth and Cult* remains fundamental because it treats attributes, inscriptions, scene structure, and find context rather than assuming every Eleusinian boy is Ploutos.

### The two Aristophanic plays

Ancient catalogues record *Ploutos* twice. The extant play belongs to 388 BCE, but its scholia sometimes treat it as the 408 production. Recent study attributes this confusion to the history of ancient commentary and transmission. It is unsafe to reconstruct the lost first play simply by moving the extant plot twenty years earlier.

### Kerényi and Graves

- **Karl Kerényi:** valuable as a major twentieth-century interpreter of Eleusinian symbolism; his claim that Persephone bore little Ploutos to Plouton is a reconstruction from images, not an attested genealogy.
- **Robert Graves:** influential modern mythographic synthesis, but his ritual hypotheses and harmonized family structures require verification claim by claim. He is reception and interpretation, not primary evidence.

### State of the evidence

The main limitations are:

- no Mycenaean attestation;
- no securely identified Archaic cult image;
- no independent temple archive for Ploutos;
- ambiguous uninscribed divine children;
- lost first version of Aristophanes’ play;
- medieval manuscripts substantially later than the archaic poems;
- frequent lexical ambiguity between the noun “wealth” and the god;
- frequent confusion of Ploutos with Plouton/Hades in Latin and modern languages.

Major evidence is held or documented through the National Archaeological Museum in Athens, the Munich Glyptothek, the Archaeological Museum and site at Eleusis, the *Inscriptiones Graecae*, the LIMC, Perseus, the Thesaurus Linguae Graecae, and modern archaeological publications.

## 10. Chronology

- **Late eighth–early seventh century BCE:** *Odyssey* 5.125–128 records Demeter and Iasion’s union and Zeus’s killing of Iasion, but not Ploutos.
- **ca. 700 BCE:** Hesiod, *Theogony* 969–974, first securely names Ploutos as their son and a wandering giver of wealth.
- **Later seventh/sixth century BCE:** *Homeric Hymn to Demeter* makes Demeter and Persephone send Ploutos to favored households.
- **Sixth–fifth centuries BCE:** Theognidean poetry criticizes Wealth’s tolerance of bad people.
- **Early fifth century BCE:** Timocreon calls Ploutos blind and wishes him confined to Tartarus and Acheron.
- **ca. 440–430 BCE:** Great Eleusinian Relief; the youthful figure’s identity remains disputed.
- **411 BCE:** Aristophanes’ *Thesmophoriazusae* includes Ploutos in the women’s festival prayer.
- **408 BCE:** first Aristophanic *Ploutos*, now lost or unrecoverable as a distinct text.
- **388 BCE:** extant *Ploutos* produced; Zeus’s blinding, Asklepian cure, debate with Penia, and civic installation receive their fullest form.
- **ca. 375–370 BCE:** Kephisodotos creates Eirene carrying infant Ploutos for Athens.
- **360/59 BCE:** Panathenaic amphora imagery documents the Eirene–Ploutos group.
- **Mid-fourth century BCE:** Plato, *Laws* 631c, contrasts blind wealth with sighted wealth accompanied by wisdom.
- **Fourth century BCE:** South Italian and Attic art disseminates cornucopia-bearing child imagery.
- **329/8 BCE:** Eleusinian building accounts document the Ploutonion—evidence for Plouton, requiring distinction from Ploutos.
- **First century BCE:** Diodorus rationalizes Ploutos as agricultural surplus or an early teacher of property management.
- **45 BCE:** Cicero includes Wealth among beneficial abstractions treated as divine.
- **First century CE:** Phaedrus preserves the fable of Heracles rejecting Plutus.
- **First/second century CE:** Pseudo-Hyginus gives the Plutus–Philomelus brother story.
- **ca. 150–175 CE:** Pausanias records Ploutos with Eirene, Tyche, and Athena Ergane.
- **Second century CE:** Lucian’s *Timon* makes blind Ploutos a reluctant servant of Zeus escorted by Hermes.
- **Second/third century CE:** Orphic Hymn 18 celebrates Plouton as giver of annual fruits; important convergence evidence but not a secure hymn to Ploutos.
- **Early third century CE:** Philostratus describes a winged, golden, sighted Ploutos at Rhodes.
- **Fourth century CE:** public pagan cult loses imperial support; anti-sacrifice laws weaken institutional practice.
- **395/396 CE:** destruction and abandonment at Eleusis end its mystery institution; details of responsibility remain debated.
- **ca. 1308–1321:** Dante transforms or conflates Pluto/Plutus into the infernal guardian of avarice.
- **Renaissance–eighteenth century:** printed Aristophanes, Lucian, emblem books, and court allegory restore Plutus as a literary personification.
- **1832:** Goethe’s *Faust II* uses Plutus in a masquerade of wealth and paper finance.
- **Late nineteenth–twentieth century:** systematic archaeology, Farnell, Nilsson, Kerényi, LIMC, and Clinton reshape debate over cult, iconography, and Ploutos/Plouton convergence.
- **1990s–present:** organized Hellenic reconstructionism provides a setting for renewed individual devotion; Ploutos-specific practice remains small and poorly quantified.
- **Twenty-first century:** the name proliferates commercially in finance and technology, while online mythology often repeats the unattested “son of Hades and Persephone” genealogy.
