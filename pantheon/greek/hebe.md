# Hebe: Research Dossier

**Class:** goddess; more precisely, a personified Olympian goddess of youthful maturity, the prime of life, and—in later narrative—rejuvenation.  
**Greek:** Ἥβη, *Hḗbē*.  
**Roman equation:** Iuventas/Juventas, “Youth,” although the Roman goddess had an independent civic history before being assimilated to Hebe.  
**Earliest secure attestation:** the *Iliad*, especially 4.2–3, conventionally dated to the late eighth or early seventh century BCE. There Hebe pours nectar for the gods. No securely identified Linear B occurrence of her name is known.  
**Evidence labels used below:** **[Early]** securely attested archaic or early-classical evidence; **[Classical]** fifth–fourth-century BCE evidence; **[Hellenistic/Roman]** later ancient elaboration; **[Regional]** geographically restricted cult or myth; **[Syncretistic]** historical identification with another deity; **[Late]** late-antique or medieval development; **[Early-modern]** Renaissance–eighteenth-century reception; **[Modern reconstruction]** scholarly inference rather than an explicit ancient statement; **[Modern invention]** a demonstrably post-antique story or attribution.

---

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

**[Early]** Hebe is the divine embodiment of **ἥβη**, the condition of youth at or approaching sexual and civic maturity—not infancy and not merely childishness. In Homer she belongs to the Olympian household and performs the work of an unmarried divine daughter: pouring nectar, assisting Hera with her chariot, and washing and dressing Ares. Hesiod makes her explicitly a daughter of Zeus and Hera and later the immortal bride of Heracles. Her marriage converts Heracles’ admission to Olympus into a dynastic reconciliation with Hera and a permanent possession of divine youth.

Her surviving mythology is small but unusually coherent around three connected ideas:

1. youthful vigor;
2. service within the Olympian household;
3. the immortalization or renewed youth of Heracles and his circle.

She was not merely a literary abstraction. Cult is securely reported at Phlius and Sicyon, while inscriptions attest a sanctuary and priestly establishment associated with Hebe and the Heraclids at Aixone in Attica.

### Name

**Ἥβη — Hḗbē.** The theonym is identical with the common noun **ἥβη**, “youth,” “youthful vigor,” “prime of life,” or “sexual maturity.” The word could denote puberty, the age of marriageability, the bodily vigor of youth, and in some contexts the pubic region or sexual maturity itself.

The goddess’s name should therefore be translated cautiously. “Youth” is correct but can misleadingly suggest childhood. “Youthful prime” or “mature youth” more closely conveys much of the Greek semantic range.

### Etymology

**[Secure linguistic fact]** The immediate derivation from Greek ἥβη is certain.

**[Disputed reconstruction]** An Indo-European comparison has connected ἥβη with forms denoting strength or vigor, including Lithuanian *jėga* and Latvian *jēga*, and reconstructed a root approximately *\*(H)iēgʷ-* “youthful strength.” This remains debated. Greek dialectal forms with initial aspiration or differing vowels complicate a single reconstruction. Pierre Chantraine considered attempts at a deeper etymology unproductive; R. S. P. Beekes reviews the forms without establishing a universally accepted derivation. Claims that the name is demonstrably “pre-Greek” or demonstrably derived from a specific Near Eastern deity exceed the evidence.

### Principal names and titles

- **Ἥβη, Hḗbē — “Youth/Youthful Prime.”** The ordinary literary name from Homer onward.
- **Γανυμήδα, Ganymḗda — “Ganymeda.”** **[Regional]** Pausanias 2.13.3 says that the earliest Phliasians called the goddess of the sacred cypress grove Ganymeda, whereas later authorities called her Hebe. This is not proof that the male Ganymede and Hebe were once universally a single deity. It is evidence for a local Phliasian name and for later interpretive identification.
- **Δία, Día.** **[Regional; disputed sense]** Strabo 8.6.24 says the goddess worshipped at Phlius and Sicyon was called Dia and identifies her as Hebe. The adjective may mean “of Zeus,” “daughter of Zeus,” or “divine/heavenly.” Because *Dia* was used elsewhere of other female divinities, it is not a unique Hebe-name and cannot by itself establish a universal Hebe–Aphrodite equation.
- **χρυσοστέφανος, chrysostéphanos — “golden-crowned.”** Applied to Hebe in the opening divine catalogue of Hesiod’s *Theogony*.
- **καλλίσφυρος, kallísphyros — “fair-ankled.”** Homeric/Hesiodic poetic description.
- **ἀγλαόγυιος, aglaógyios — “bright-limbed” or “splendid of limb.”** A poetic epithet associated with youthful beauty.
- **καλλίστα θεῶν, kallísta theôn — “fairest of the goddesses.”** Pindar, *Nemean* 10.17, in a description of Hebe beside Hera.
- **Ἥβη Ἰουνωνία / Hebe Iunonia — “Junonian Hebe,” “Hebe daughter of Juno.”** **[Roman literary usage]** Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 9.400, marking her maternal association.
- **Iuventas/Juventas — “Youth.”** **[Syncretistic]** Latin divine name historically equated with Hebe. The spelling *Juventas* reflects later orthography; Classical Latin normally wrote *Iuventas*.

### Classification and its changes

- **Archaic Greek poetry:** an Olympian goddess and divine household member.
- **Hesiodic genealogy:** daughter of Zeus and Hera; sister of Ares and Eileithyia; bride of the apotheosized Heracles.
- **Classical cult:** a goddess capable of receiving sanctuary cult, priestly service, festivals, and asylum-seekers.
- **Phliasian local religion:** Ganymeda/Hebe, a highly sacred but aniconic sanctuary goddess connected with release from bonds.
- **Roman religion:** identified with Iuventas, an older civic personification protecting entry into adult male citizenship.
- **Allegorical and artistic reception:** personification of youth, grace, beauty, springlike vitality, and immortality.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest securely datable evidence

**[Early]** The oldest secure textual attestations are Homeric:

- *Iliad* 4.2–3: Hebe pours nectar while the gods sit with Zeus and look toward Troy.
- *Iliad* 5.720–723: she helps Hera prepare and harness the divine chariot.
- *Iliad* 5.905–906: after Ares is wounded by Diomedes and healed by Paeon, Hebe washes him and dresses him in fine clothing.

The *Iliad* reached substantially its transmitted monumental form around the late eighth or early seventh century BCE, although its diction preserves older oral material. The earliest complete manuscripts are medieval; papyri and quotations establish the poem’s circulation long before them. The Homeric evidence does **not** state Hebe’s parentage or say that Ganymede displaced her. It simply places her in the Olympian household.

### Homeric and Hesiodic corpus

#### *Odyssey* 11.601–604

**[Early text; passage textually disputed]** During Odysseus’ vision of the dead, the poem distinguishes Heracles’ phantom from Heracles himself: the divine Heracles feasts among the immortals and possesses “fair-ankled Hebe,” daughter of Zeus and Hera, as wife. Ancient and modern critics have suspected that all or part of this passage is interpolated. Even if secondary within the poem, it is ancient and important evidence for the early reception of Heracles’ apotheosis and marriage.

#### Hesiod, *Theogony* 17 and 921–955

**[Early]** The proem includes “golden-crowned Hebe” among the gods. Lines 921–923 say that Hera, after union with Zeus, bore Hebe, Ares, and Eileithyia. Lines 950–955 say that Heracles, after completing his labors, made Hebe his “revered wife” on snowy Olympus and lives among the immortals free from suffering and old age.

These lines supply the oldest explicit genealogy and connect Hebe directly with the abolition of Heracles’ age and mortality.

#### Hesiod, *Works and Days*

Hebe does not have a narrative role in the securely transmitted poem. General descriptions of the human life-cycle and youthful vigor should not be converted into references to the goddess without her name.

### Homeric Hymns

**[Early corpus; limited evidence]** Hebe has no surviving Homeric Hymn addressed to her. In the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* she belongs to the Olympian company around Apollo’s music and dance, but is not the center of an independent episode. Statements that an ancient “Homeric Hymn to Hebe” survives are unsupported.

### Olen’s lost hymn to Hera

**[Lost early-looking tradition, date indeterminate]** Pausanias 2.13.3 cites a hymn attributed to Olen in which the Horae rear Hera and Hera has Ares and Hebe. Olen was treated as a primordial hymn-poet, but the quoted composition cannot be securely dated from the attribution. It is evidence for a tradition known to Pausanias, not proof of an actual pre-Homeric written hymn.

### Pindar

- **Pindar, *Isthmian* 4.55–60, c. 474 BCE. [Classical]** Heracles lives beside Zeus, honored by the immortals, married to Hebe, possessing a golden home, and reconciled as Hera’s son-in-law.
- **Pindar, *Nemean* 10.17–18, fifth century BCE. [Classical]** Hebe, “fairest of goddesses,” stands beside Hera, guardian of marriage. The passage emphasizes mother–daughter proximity and nuptial order.

### Euripides, *Heracleidae* 849–858

**[Classical, c. 430 BCE]** A messenger reports that two stars appeared over Iolaus’ chariot; interpreters identified them as Heracles and Hebe. From the divinely caused darkness, the aged Iolaus emerged young and strong enough to overtake Eurystheus. Euripides does not have Hebe speak, but this is the earliest surviving dramatic presentation of her as an agent in restored youth.

### Diodorus Siculus, *Library of History* 4.39

**[Hellenistic/Roman, first century BCE]** After Heracles’ pyre and apotheosis, Zeus persuades Hera to adopt Heracles as her son and give him Hebe in marriage. The adoption is dramatized by a simulated rebirth: Hera takes Heracles under her clothing and lets him fall to the ground. Diodorus presents this as current mythic tradition and uses the marriage to seal Heracles’ reconciliation with Hera.

### Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca*

**[Roman-era mythographic systematization, usually first–second century CE]**

- 1.3: Hebe is a child of Zeus and Hera.
- 2.7.7: after Heracles is deified, he reconciles with Hera, marries Hebe, and fathers Alexiares and Anicetus/Aniketos.
- The mythographer’s orderly genealogy is influential but later than Homer, Hesiod, Pindar, and Euripides.

### Pausanias, *Description of Greece*

**[Roman Imperial documentary testimony, c. 150–175 CE]**

- 1.19.3: in the Athenian Cynosarges complex, altars or cults of Heracles and his circle included Hebe.
- 2.13.3–4: the Phliasian cypress grove and sanctuary of Ganymeda/Hebe; asylum and release of prisoners; dedicated fetters; the annual Kissotomoi; no cult image.
- 5.17.5: on the Chest of Cypselus at Olympia, Heracles is shown beside a seated woman interpreted as Hebe; an inscription identifies the scene.
- 8.9.2: at Mantinea, Praxiteles’ sculptural group placed Hebe and Athena standing beside the seated Hera.
- 9.11.2: Theban religious monuments connected Heracles’ Olympian marriage with Hebe.

Pausanias is indispensable because he reports things seen, inscriptions read, and local explanations heard; nevertheless, his “ancient” traditions are second-century reports and must not automatically be projected into the Bronze Age.

### Strabo, *Geography* 8.6.24

**[Hellenistic/Roman, composed late first century BCE–early first century CE]** Strabo says temples of Dia were honored at both Phlius and Sicyon and explicitly glosses Dia as the local name for Hebe.

### Hyginus

**[Roman mythography; transmitted in late manuscripts]** The *Fabulae* and prefatory genealogies Latinize Hebe as Juventas and place her among the children of Jupiter and Juno. Hyginus is useful for Roman school mythography but cannot independently establish an archaic Greek variant.

### Ovid

- *Metamorphoses* 9.394–417: Hebe restores Iolaus to youth for Heracles’ sake, then swears not to repeat the favor. Themis explains that other miraculous changes of age will occur through different divine dispensations.
- *Fasti* 6.65–88: Juventas appears in a contest over the patronage of June and stresses her antiquity and her place in Jupiter’s Capitoline temple.

**[Roman literary elaboration]** Ovid makes Hebe’s control of age unusually explicit. This should not be retrojected unchanged into Homer, where pouring nectar does not itself amount to an articulated power of rejuvenation.

### Philostratus the Elder, *Imagines* 2.20

**[Late antique rhetoric, third century CE]** In an ekphrasis of Heracles’ Olympian reception, Hebe is characterized as the youngest and most honored of the gods because through her they remain young. This is an interpretive theological expansion of her personified domain.

### Nonnus, *Dionysiaca*

**[Late, fifth century CE]** Nonnus repeatedly uses Hebe as the exemplary Olympian bride and cupbearer. At 35.333–348 Hera momentarily imagines Dionysus as a possible bridegroom for Hebe, but Zeus’ ordinance reserves her for Heracles. Nonnus’ florid epic should be treated as late-antique literary recomposition, not evidence for a classical cult doctrine.

### Orphic material

**[Negative finding]** No independent Hymn to Hebe occurs in the surviving collection of eighty-seven *Orphic Hymns*, usually dated to the Roman Imperial period. She can appear in Orphic genealogical or cosmogonic material transmitted through fragments and late mythographers, but the evidence is indirect.

**[Late variant]** The story that Hera conceived Hebe without Zeus after eating lettuce is preserved in the medieval *First Vatican Mythographer* 1.204 and has sometimes been called “Orphic.” Its attribution to a lost Orphic source is uncertain. It is not in Hesiod, where Zeus is explicitly the father.

### Greek Magical Papyri

**[Negative finding]** Hebe is not a significant independent deity in the standard indexed corpus of the Greek Magical Papyri. No securely identified Hebe spell, voces magicae formula, or distinctive magical cult emerges comparable to those of Hecate, Selene, Helios, Hermes, Aphrodite, or the syncretistic supreme gods. Claims that she possessed an extensive “ancient magical tradition” are modern extrapolations.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

#### Youth and the prime of life

**[Early]** Hebe’s central domain follows directly from her name. She embodies the vigorous, sexually mature, socially transitional stage called *hēbē*. She is not originally documented as a goddess of babies, education, beauty cosmetics, or general healing.

#### Olympian service

**[Early]** The *Iliad* presents her as:

- pourer of nectar;
- assistant to Hera’s chariot;
- attendant who bathes and clothes Ares.

These resemble the high-status domestic duties of an unmarried elite daughter. They need not imply humiliation or servitude.

#### Immortality and rejuvenation

**[Early association; Classical and Roman elaboration]** Marriage to Hebe marks Heracles’ freedom from old age. Euripides associates her with Iolaus’ renewed youth. Ovid makes the rejuvenation her personal gift. Philostratus makes her the reason the gods remain young. The progression is traceable: personified youth becomes an active distributor of youth.

#### Release and asylum

**[Regional]** At Phlius her sanctuary protected suppliants. Freed prisoners hung their fetters on trees. This may express liberation from bonds and renewed civic existence, but that interpretation is modern; Pausanias reports the acts, not a developed theology connecting chains with rejuvenation.

### Physical form

Hebe is normally represented as a youthful woman. Ancient artists did not give her an absolutely exclusive anatomy or costume. Identification therefore depends heavily on context, inscriptions, and objects:

- proximity to Hera, Zeus, Ares, or Heracles;
- a cup, bowl, oinochoe, or jug;
- participation in an Olympian banquet;
- wedding procession with Heracles;
- inscription naming her.

### Earliest and important representations

#### Sophilos’ dinos, c. 580–570 BCE

**[Early iconographic evidence]** An Attic black-figure dinos attributed to Sophilos, now in the British Museum, shows the procession of gods to the wedding of Peleus and Thetis. Hebe is identified by inscription among the Olympians. This is among the earliest secure visual attestations.

#### Chest of Cypselus

**[Archaic object known from Roman testimony]** Pausanias describes the elaborately decorated Chest of Cypselus at Olympia, conventionally dated to the early sixth century BCE. Heracles appears with Hebe, whose identity is clarified by an inscription. The object is lost; Pausanias’ description is the evidence.

#### Heracles’ apotheosis and wedding

From the sixth and especially fifth centuries BCE, Attic vase painters show:

- Heracles entering Olympus;
- Hera welcoming or reconciling with him;
- Hebe presented as his bride;
- the wedding procession of Heracles and Hebe.

A red-figure pyxis now in the University of Pennsylvania Museum is a prominent example of the wedding. Some compositions place Aphrodite, Eros, Hera, Zeus, Athena, or other Olympians around the pair. Because an unlabeled young woman beside Heracles might also be Athena or another goddess, individual identifications must follow inscriptions and catalogue arguments, not merely resemblance.

#### Hebe driving Heracles to Olympus

**[Iconographic variant]** A hydria generally dated around 525 BCE, often called the Ricci hydria, has been interpreted as showing Hebe driving Heracles’ chariot toward Olympus—a role more commonly given to Athena. The interpretation is noteworthy but depends on the object’s inscriptions and iconographic reconstruction; it should not be generalized into a narrative known from surviving literature.

#### Mantinean cult group

**[Classical art reported later]** Pausanias 8.9.2 says Praxiteles made a group in Hera’s Mantinean temple with seated Hera flanked by Athena and Hebe. This locates Hebe in her mother’s cultic and familial orbit.

### Symbols and objects

Securely associated objects are:

- cup or shallow drinking vessel;
- jug or oinochoe;
- nectar;
- occasionally an Olympian chariot;
- bridal dress or wedding attributes in scenes with Heracles.

A later European iconographic convention adds:

- an eagle, primarily as a sign of Zeus and as a visual contrast with Ganymede;
- clouds as a base;
- exposed shoulder or diaphanous classical dress;
- amphora and goblet.

### Sacred animals, plants, colors, planets, and numbers

- **Cypress:** securely present in the Phliasian sanctuary grove, but not necessarily Hebe’s universal “sacred tree.”
- **Ivy:** present in the name *Kissotomoi*, “Ivy-cutters,” the Phliasian annual festival. The ritual meaning is lost.
- **Eagle:** strongly associated with her in modern art, but not documented as her exclusive ancient sacred animal.
- **Doves:** Servian commentary preserves a story in which Zeus gave Hebe two speaking doves, one associated with Dodona. This is a late scholastic tradition, not a central early myth.
- **Colors, weekdays, crystals, herbs, tarot cards, or planets:** no ancient canonical system assigns these to Hebe.
- **Asteroid:** (6) Hebe, discovered in 1847, is a modern astronomical namesake, not an ancient celestial identification.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Principal genealogy

**[Early dominant genealogy]**

- **Father:** Zeus.
- **Mother:** Hera.
- **Siblings explicitly joined with her by Hesiod:** Ares and Eileithyia.
- **Husband:** the deified Heracles.
- **Children:** Alexiares and Anicetus/Aniketos in Pseudo-Apollodorus; they are absent from Homer and Hesiod.

### Competing birth traditions

| Version | Source | Date and tier |
|---|---|---|
| Hera bears Hebe after union with Zeus | Hesiod, *Theogony* 921–923 | Eighth–seventh century BCE; **[Early dominant]** |
| Zeus and Hera are her parents | *Odyssey* 11.603–604; if passage secondary, still archaic | **[Early, textually disputed passage]** |
| Olen’s hymn names Hebe and Ares as Hera’s children | Quoted by Pausanias 2.13.3 | Lost and undatable; **[Local/antiquarian testimony]** |
| Hera conceives Hebe parthenogenetically after eating lettuce | *First Vatican Mythographer* 1.204 | Medieval manuscript tradition; **[Late accretion]** |
| Hebe/Juventas is simply the daughter of Jupiter and Juno | Hyginus and Roman mythography | **[Roman systematization]** |

The lettuce-conception story did not displace the Zeus-and-Hera genealogy in antiquity. Modern retellings that present both as equally early conceal the chronology.

### Marriage and children

**[Early]** Hesiod and the *Odyssey* already know Hebe as Heracles’ divine wife. Pindar makes the marriage the culmination of the hero’s suffering and his reconciliation with Hera. Diodorus dramatizes Hera’s adoption of Heracles before the wedding. Pseudo-Apollodorus supplies two sons:

- **Alexiares**, perhaps “warder-off of war” or “protector”;
- **Anicetus/Aniketos**, “unconquered.”

These children have no substantial independent narrative in the surviving early corpus.

### Every documented god–god interaction

The following list separates actual interactions or joint episodes from mere membership in the same pantheon.

- **Zeus — father and dynastic authority.** Zeus fathers Hebe with Hera in Hesiod; in later accounts he arranges or sanctions her marriage to the deified Heracles. *Hesiod, Theogony* 921–923, 950–955; Diodorus 4.39. **[Early; later elaboration]**
- **Hera — mother, mistress of the divine household, and companion in cult and art.** Hebe helps harness Hera’s chariot; Pindar places her beside Hera; Praxiteles’ Mantinean group similarly juxtaposed them. *Iliad* 5.720–723; Pindar, *Nemean* 10.17–18; Pausanias 8.9.2. **[Early/Classical]**
- **Ares — sisterly attendant.** Hebe washes and clothes the wounded Ares after Paeon heals him. *Iliad* 5.899–906. **[Early]**
- **Paeon — sequential joint care of Ares.** Paeon heals the wound and Hebe washes and dresses Ares; Paeon and Hebe do not converse. *Iliad* 5.899–906. **[Early]**
- **Heracles — husband and beneficiary of immortal youth.** Hebe marries him after his apotheosis; later traditions make her rejuvenate Iolaus at his request. *Odyssey* 11.601–604; Hesiod, *Theogony* 950–955; Pindar, *Isthmian* 4; Euripides, *Heracleidae* 849–858; Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 9.394–417. **[Early core; Classical/Roman elaboration]**
- **Iolaus — rejuvenated mortal/hero.** Hebe and Heracles appear as two stars, after which the aged Iolaus becomes young; Ovid makes Hebe the direct giver of the transformation. Euripides, *Heracleidae* 849–858; Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 9.394–417. **[Classical; Roman explicit elaboration]**
- **Themis — limits or explains Hebe’s dispensation.** In Ovid, Themis prevents Hebe from promising universal rejuvenation and predicts other age transformations. *Metamorphoses* 9.403–417. **[Roman literary elaboration]**
- **Ganymede — parallel or rival cupbearer.** Homer assigns Ganymede to Zeus and Hebe to the divine company without narrating a replacement; Cicero says poetic banquets employ “Juventas or Ganymede.” A direct rivalry becomes prominent in later ancient scholia, medieval debate poetry, and modern mythography. *Iliad* 4.2–3 and 20.231–235; Cicero, *De natura deorum* 1.40; medieval *Altercatio Ganimedis et Hebe*. **[Early parallel roles; Late rivalry]**
- **Athena — joint divine presence and iconographic partner.** Athena and Hebe flank Hera in the Mantinean group; in vase painting each can conduct Heracles toward Olympus, though not necessarily in the same scene. Pausanias 8.9.2 and vase evidence. **[Classical/iconographic]**
- **Aphrodite — attendant at Hebe’s wedding in some vase scenes.** Aphrodite and sometimes Eros accompany the marriage of Heracles and Hebe. Attic red-figure vase painting, fifth century BCE. **[Classical iconographic]**
- **Eros — wedding attendant.** Appears in some representations of Heracles and Hebe’s marriage. **[Classical iconographic]**
- **Dionysus — proposed but unrealized bridegroom.** Nonnus says Hera would have given Hebe to Dionysus had Zeus not reserved her for Heracles. *Dionysiaca* 35.333–348. **[Late literary counterfactual]**
- **Horae — indirect familial interaction.** In Olen’s hymn as reported by Pausanias, the Horae rear Hera, Hebe’s mother. No direct Hebe–Horae action is preserved. **[Undatable lost hymn; absence noted]**
- **Eileithyia — sibling relationship only.** Hesiod makes both daughters of Zeus and Hera; no surviving early narrative interaction is documented. **[Early genealogy; no episode]**
- **Hephaestus — possible sibling only in some broader genealogical systems.** No securely attested Hebe–Hephaestus encounter is known. **[Absence of interaction]**
- **Apollo — shared Olympian celebration.** Hebe participates in the company responding to Apollo’s music in the *Homeric Hymn to Apollo*, but no individual exchange is narrated. **[Early joint scene]**
- **All Olympian gods collectively — cupbearer.** Hebe pours nectar for the assembled gods. *Iliad* 4.2–3. **[Early]**

No surviving source documents Hebe fighting another god, conducting an affair, issuing an oracle, or presiding over a divine judgment.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. Hebe as cupbearer

**[Early core]** At the opening of *Iliad* 4, the gods sit with Zeus while Hebe pours nectar. They drink from golden cups and watch Troy. The scene establishes the Olympian banquet as a stable divine society set against mortal war.

The drink is nectar in the passage. Later summaries often say that she serves both nectar and ambrosia, but Homer does not here describe ambrosia as the liquid in her vessel.

#### Was she replaced by Ganymede?

The familiar sequential story—Hebe served first, retired or was dismissed, and Ganymede replaced her—is not narrated by Homer. The *Iliad* can accommodate both simultaneously:

- Hebe pours for the assembled gods at 4.2–3.
- Ganymede was taken by the gods to pour for Zeus at 20.231–235.
- Hephaestus also serves the Olympians in the comic banquet at 1.597–600.

These offices are not exclusive in the early epic.

**[Later interpretation]** By Roman and medieval reception, Hebe and Ganymede became alternatives or rivals. Cicero’s wording—poets place “Juventas or Ganymede” at the banquet—shows the alternative already recognizable in the first century BCE.

**[Modern invention/early-modern moralization]** The story that Hebe slipped while serving, exposed her body, and was dismissed is unattested in surviving classical literature or art. It appears in early-modern lexicographical and moralizing traditions and was popularized in Thomas Bulfinch’s *Age of Fable*. It should not be reported as an ancient Greek myth. Neither is there an ancient source saying she retired simply because she married Heracles.

### B. Heracles’ apotheosis and marriage

**[Early core]** In the *Odyssey* passage and Hesiod’s *Theogony*, Heracles resides among the immortal gods and marries Hebe. Hesiod emphasizes his release from age and suffering.

**[Classical interpretation]** Pindar treats the marriage as Heracles’ final reward: he becomes Zeus’ neighbor, Hera’s son-in-law, and owner of a golden Olympian home. This is more than romance. It resolves the entire conflict in which Hera persecuted Zeus’ mortal son. Marriage to Hera’s legitimate daughter incorporates him into Hera’s household.

**[Hellenistic/Roman elaboration]** Diodorus makes the reconciliation formal. Hera adopts Heracles through a ritualized simulated birth before giving him Hebe. Pseudo-Apollodorus adds the sons Alexiares and Anicetus.

**[Iconography]** Archaic and classical artists made the arrival, presentation, or wedding visible. Athena, Hera, Zeus, Aphrodite, and Eros can participate. The literary sources say little about Hebe’s consent, emotions, or courtship; elaborate modern romantic narratives fill a genuine ancient silence.

### C. Rejuvenation of Iolaus

**[Classical]** In Euripides’ *Heracleidae*, the elderly Iolaus insists on joining battle to defend Heracles’ descendants. Two stars descend over his chariot; the messenger says knowledgeable observers identified them as Heracles and Hebe. Within the darkness Iolaus recovers youthful strength and captures Eurystheus.

The miracle is politically charged. Athens protects suppliants descended from Heracles, and the rejuvenated hero helps defeat Argive aggression. Hebe’s action thus belongs to a civic drama about asylum, kinship, and Athens’ claimed benefaction to the Heraclids.

**[Roman elaboration]** Ovid explicitly says that Hebe renewed Iolaus’ years to gratify Hercules. She intends the gift as exceptional. The gods then debate demands for comparable transformations, and Themis insists that fate will allocate them through other mechanisms.

### D. Phliasian Ganymeda and liberation

**[Regional cult narrative]** Pausanias reports a sanctuary in a cypress grove on the acropolis of Phlius. The earliest inhabitants called its goddess Ganymeda, later people called her Hebe, and no image existed. Suppliants obtained pardon; released prisoners hung their shackles on the grove’s trees; the annual festival was called Kissotomoi, “Ivy-cutters.”

The sanctuary’s lost *hieros logos* explained its aniconism, but Pausanias does not reproduce the explanation. Modern reconstructions tying each feature to Heracles, puberty, vegetation, or initiation are possible but not demonstrable.

### E. Hera’s lettuce conception

**[Late accretion]** The *First Vatican Mythographer* says Juno/Hera conceived Juventas/Hebe by eating lettuce, parallel to Jupiter’s production of Minerva without a mother. This belongs to medieval mythographic transmission and perhaps draws upon lost antique material, but no extant early Greek source supports it. Calling it “the Orphic version” without qualification is unwarranted.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Phlius

**[Regional; securely literary-attested]** This is the best-documented Greek cult center.

Pausanias records:

- a cypress grove on the citadel;
- a sanctuary regarded as exceptionally holy;
- the names Ganymeda and later Hebe;
- universal protection or pardon for suppliants;
- freed prisoners dedicating their chains on trees;
- an annual festival called **Kissotomoi**, “Ivy-cutters”;
- absence of any public or hidden cult image;
- a neighboring temple of Hera with a Parian-marble image.

The ritual presents Hebe not simply as a decorative cupbearer but as a powerful civic sanctuary deity. What counted legally as “pardon,” who administered asylum, how prisoners were freed, and why ivy was cut are not preserved.

There is no documented human sacrifice, temple prostitution, ritual sexual service, or cult violence associated with this sanctuary.

### Sicyon

**[Regional]** Strabo states that Phlius and Sicyon honored temples of **Dia**, whom he identifies as Hebe. Pausanias says Sicyonian accounts substantially agreed with those of Phlius, but does not supply an equally full Sicyonian ritual description.

The evidence does not establish that Hebe’s Phliasian asylum customs were practiced identically at Sicyon. Nor does the title Dia prove that all cults named Dia belonged to Hebe.

### Aixone in Attica

**[Epigraphic]** Inscriptions conventionally cited as IG II² 1199 and 2492 attest a sanctuary or cult complex involving Hebe, Heracles, Alcmene, and the Heraclids in the deme Aixone. The association is significant: Hebe appears inside the cultic family created by Heracles’ apotheosis.

The inscriptions are stronger evidence for actual institutional worship than literary genealogy alone. They do not support a large autonomous “mystery cult of Hebe.”

### Cynosarges and Athens

Pausanias’ account of Cynosarges associates Hebe with Heracles and his family. The complex was connected with Heracles and with questions of civic status, though it is unsafe to infer that Hebe herself universally supervised Athenian citizenship transitions.

### Mantinea

Pausanias reports the Praxitelean group of Hera, Athena, and Hebe in Hera’s temple. This constitutes cult-image evidence for Hebe as Hera’s daughter and attendant but does not prove a separate Mantinean priesthood devoted exclusively to her.

### Thebes

Pindar and Pausanias connect Heracles’ divine marriage and honors to his Theban setting. The evidence concerns the Heraclean complex more than an independent major temple of Hebe.

### Priesthood

A dedicated priesthood is epigraphically indicated in Attic material and may be implied wherever a maintained sanctuary and annual festival existed. No surviving source supplies a general rule for appointment, hereditary succession, gender, costume, privileges, or initiation of Hebe’s priests.

### Offerings and ritual objects

Secure or probable offerings include:

- fetters dedicated after liberation at Phlius;
- ordinary sacrifice and festival offerings implied by temple cult;
- ivy cut at the Kissotomoi;
- cups and vessels in iconography, though artistic attributes are not automatically cult offerings.

### Political and social functions

- **Asylum and reintegration:** explicit at Phlius.
- **Heraclean dynastic identity:** evident at Aixone, Thebes, and in myth.
- **Youthful civic transition:** especially explicit in Roman Iuventas worship.
- **Imperial succession:** Roman imperial coinage could use Iuventas to advertise the vigor and future of an emperor or heir.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### 1. Pre-Homeric background: unestablished

No Mycenaean tablet securely names Hebe. The common noun *hēbē* and the personification may be old, but a Bronze Age goddess Hebe cannot presently be documented. The Phliasian claim to an ancient Ganymeda is valuable local memory recorded in the second century CE, not a datable Bronze Age attestation.

### 2. Archaic period, c. 750–480 BCE

Hebe enters the documentary record as an Olympian attendant in the *Iliad*. Hesiod gives her genealogy and marriage. The Sophilos vase and Chest of Cypselus establish her in early visual culture. By this stage the principal complex—youth, Hera’s daughter, Olympian service, Heracles’ bride—already exists.

### 3. Classical period, c. 480–323 BCE

Pindar makes the marriage a theological conclusion to Heracles’ career. Euripides develops her power to restore youth. Vase painters elaborate the apotheosis and wedding. Local cults in Attica and the northeastern Peloponnese place her inside civic and Heraclean religious institutions.

This is not evidence that Hebe ranked among the most widely worshipped Olympians. Her appearances are substantial but commonly relational: daughter of Hera, attendant of the gods, or wife of Heracles.

### 4. Hellenistic and early Roman period, 323 BCE–100 CE

Mythographers systematize genealogies. Strabo records Dia/Hebe at Phlius and Sicyon. Diodorus develops Hera’s ritual adoption of Heracles. Roman authors equate Hebe with Iuventas and explore rejuvenation more explicitly.

### 5. Roman Imperial period, c. 100–400 CE

Pausanias preserves the fullest cult description. Philostratus and Nonnus transform Hebe into an increasingly allegorical guarantor of divine youth. Roman Iuventas becomes a figure of civic continuity, military vigor, princely youth, and imperial succession.

### 6. Roman Iuventas: independent origin and assimilation

**[Syncretistic]** Iuventas was not simply “Hebe under another spelling.” Roman tradition located a shrine of Iuventas on the Capitoline and claimed that she and Terminus refused removal when the great temple of Jupiter was built. Dionysius of Halicarnassus also attributes to Servius Tullius a contribution associated with youths entering adult status.

Historically firmer Republican evidence includes:

- sacrifice to Iuventas during the crisis of 218 BCE;
- Marcus Livius Salinator’s vow of a temple during the Battle of the Metaurus in 207 BCE;
- commencement while he was censor in 204;
- dedication by Gaius Licinius Lucullus in 193 BCE, according to Livy 36.36.5–6;
- destruction by fire in 16 BCE;
- restoration by Augustus.

The temple stood near or within the Circus Maximus zone. Roman youths assuming the *toga virilis* were associated with offerings to Iuventas. Greek Hebe supplied a developed genealogy and iconography; Roman Iuventas supplied a civic rite of passage and political application. Their identification was a historical fusion, not proof of original identity.

### 7. Christianization and disappearance of public cult

No edict or destruction account specifically targeting Hebe’s Phliasian sanctuary survives. Her cult disappeared within the wider suppression, abandonment, conversion, and economic dissolution of polytheistic institutions in late antiquity. Claims that Christians violently destroyed a named “Temple of Hebe” at Phlius require evidence not presently available.

Hebe remained available in copied mythography, poetry, school texts, and allegory after sacrifice to her ceased. This is literary survival rather than continuous cult.

### 8. Medieval transmission

The *First Vatican Mythographer* preserved and altered her genealogy. A medieval Latin debate between Hebe and Ganymede turned the two cupbearers into disputants. The poem belongs to learned medieval engagement with classical sexuality and mythology; it is not an ancient Greek cult text.

### 9. Renaissance to Enlightenment

Humanist editions restored Homeric, Hesiodic, Ovidian, and Pausanian evidence to wider circulation. Painters and decorators favored Hebe because a youthful woman with a cup and eagle could personify youth while allowing fashionable portraiture in classical guise.

The early-modern story of her indecent fall and dismissal belongs to this reception history. Its moral about female modesty reflects Christian European social instruction, not known archaic Greek religion.

### 10. Nineteenth century to the present

Neoclassical sculpture, myth handbooks, astronomy, advertising, fiction, comics, and modern polytheist revival made Hebe more visually recognizable than the scale of her ancient cult might suggest.

Present-day Hellenic polytheists sometimes honor her with libations, prayers, youth-related petitions, athletic symbolism, or rites of personal renewal. These practices are real modern religion but vary by practitioner. There is no evidence of an unbroken institutional Hebe cult from antiquity to the present.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Canova’s *Hebe*

Antonio Canova’s standing, airborne-seeming *Hebe* became the decisive modern image of the goddess. She advances lightly, holding a cup and a raised vessel, her drapery appearing to float. Canova produced four principal marble versions in addition to the plaster model:

1. **1796**, commissioned by Giuseppe Giacomo/Vivante Albrizzi; now Berlin, Alte Nationalgalerie.
2. **1800–1805**, commissioned for Joséphine de Beauharnais; exhibited at the Paris Salon in 1808; later entered the Russian imperial collections and is now in the Hermitage.
3. **c. 1814–1816**, made for Lord Cawdor; associated with Chatsworth.
4. **1816–1817**, commissioned by Countess Veronica Zauli Naldi Guarini; now Forlì.

Early versions use a cloud support; later versions employ a tree trunk. Canova also experimented with polychromatic and metallic effects in the vessel and accessories. The composition’s poised movement, partly exposed torso, and gravity-defying balance made it a model of Neoclassical grace.

The Prado’s marble *Hebe*, dated 1825, is by Canova’s workshop pupil Adamo Tadolini after Canova rather than one of Canova’s four autograph versions. The Prado documentation traces it through the ducal House of Osuna and the Museo de Arte Moderno.

### Thorvaldsen

Bertel Thorvaldsen produced versions of *Hebe* in 1806 and 1816. His later version covers the chest more fully. The comparison between Canova’s sensuous movement and Thorvaldsen’s restrained classicism became part of nineteenth-century debates about Neoclassical sculpture.

### Portraiture “as Hebe”

European and American portraitists portrayed aristocratic or celebrated women as Hebe, usually with cup, pitcher, clouds, or Jupiter’s eagle. The convention functioned as an assertion of youth and ideal beauty. These images are modern allegorical portraits, not copies of a known ancient cult statue.

### Literature and mythography

From Renaissance mythographic dictionaries through Bulfinch, Hebe’s short ancient dossier was expanded into a sequential biography: cupbearer, accidental fall, dismissal, replacement, marriage, retirement. This smooth narrative is largely a product of harmonization.

Robert Graves treats myth through ritualist and poetic hypotheses that are influential in popular culture but often conflate evidence of very different dates. Karl Kerényi offers psychologically and religiously suggestive syntheses, useful as twentieth-century reception and interpretation rather than as primary documentation.

### Medieval Hebe–Ganymede debate

The Latin *Altercatio* or debate between Hebe and Ganymede, studied by Tina Chronopoulos, belongs to the medieval debate tradition. It makes the classical cupbearers vehicles for argument over gendered and sexual forms of love. It is not a missing classical myth and should not be inserted into Greek narrative chronology.

### Astronomy

Karl Ludwig Hencke discovered **6 Hebe** on 1 July 1847. The asteroid’s naming translates mythological antiquity into nineteenth-century scientific nomenclature. Later speculation that it was a principal source of H chondrite meteorites has been questioned by modern shape and crater studies.

### Modern esoteric and neopagan material

**[Negative/qualified finding]**

- Hebe has no substantial role in the Renaissance planetary magic of Ficino, Agrippa, or the standard grimoire tradition.
- She is not a major Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or modern ceremonial-magic deity.
- No ancient set of Hebe-specific planetary hours, crystals, chakras, sigils, tarot correspondences, or colors is documented.
- Modern lists assigning rose quartz, youthful herbs, particular moon phases, or a tarot card to Hebe are contemporary devotional or commercial constructions.
- Modern Hellenic practitioners may nevertheless create historically informed worship from Homer, Hesiod, Pausanias, and general Greek sacrificial forms. Such reconstruction is modern religion using ancient evidence, not a continuous unchanged survival.

### Popular entertainment and commerce

Hebe appears intermittently in classical dictionaries, young-adult mythology, fantasy games, comics, and branded statuary. Modern fiction often gives her explicit “youth magic,” healing, age manipulation, or children with inherited superpowers. Only the broad rejuvenation motif has ancient precedent; elaborate power systems are modern inventions.

### Political appropriation

Roman Iuventas could express the vigor of the state and the promise of a youthful emperor or heir. Modern political organizations have used cognates of *iuventus* for youth movements, but the lexical descent of “juvenile,” “youth,” or an organization named *Juventus* does not demonstrate worship of Hebe. No major modern extremist appropriation centered specifically on Hebe was found.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Hebe merely an abstraction?

One older approach treated her as a transparent personification invented from the noun *hēbē*. Her archaic appearance, local cult, and differentiated family role show that “personification” does not mean “pure literary fiction without worship.” Greek religion regularly allowed an abstract quality and a personal goddess to coexist.

### How old is the Phliasian cult?

Pausanias calls the sanctuary ancient and records the earlier name Ganymeda. Scholars have proposed that:

- Ganymeda was an older local goddess later identified with Panhellenic Hebe;
- Ganymeda was a feminine formation parallel to Ganymede;
- Hebe and the male Ganymede differentiated from a shared cupbearing or youth complex;
- *Dia* connected the cult with Zeus, Hera, or an Aphrodite-like local goddess.

None can be conclusively proved from the surviving evidence. The secure facts are the names, places, rituals, and Pausanias’ second-century testimony.

### Did Ganymede replace Hebe?

This must be divided into three claims:

1. **Both are cupbearers:** secure and early.
2. **Their offices could be imagined as alternatives:** secure by Cicero and later literature.
3. **A narrated dismissal in which Hebe falls or exposes herself:** post-classical and apparently early-modern, not securely ancient.

The older scholarly explanation that female cupbearers became socially obsolete and therefore mythology replaced Hebe with a boy is an anthropological conjecture, not an ancient account.

### Is Hebe responsible for the gods’ immortality?

Homer shows her pouring nectar but does not say that she generates immortality. Philostratus explicitly states that through Hebe the gods remain young. That theological formulation is late. Modern summaries commonly merge the two.

### Was the *Odyssey* marriage passage interpolated?

Ancient and modern editors have questioned *Odyssey* 11.601–604 because it distinguishes Heracles’ image in Hades from the real Heracles feasting on Olympus and disrupts the surrounding underworld catalogue. Even if interpolated into the monumental poem, the passage remains early evidence for a developed apotheosis tradition. “Interpolation” does not mean modern forgery.

### Did Hebe have an Orphic cult or hymn?

The surviving *Orphic Hymns* contain no hymn to her. Orphic genealogical fragments are difficult to date and reconstruct. The lettuce conception’s frequent designation as “Orphic” rests on uncertain source attribution through late mythography. An autonomous Hebe mystery cult is not documented.

### Hebe and Aphrodite

Farnell and other comparativist scholars explored whether Dia/Hebe had once been identical with an Aphrodite associated with Zeus and Dione. Similar titles and themes of youthful beauty prompted the proposal. The evidence establishes overlap and possible local assimilation, not identity throughout Greece.

### Iconographic caution

The *Lexicon Iconographicum Mythologiae Classicae* assembles candidate images, but Hebe’s appearance is generic enough that uninscribed figures remain contestable. Cups, pitchers, and eagles are not independently decisive. Context and inscriptions matter more than later visual familiarity with Canova.

### Primary-evidence condition

The surviving dossier is asymmetrical:

- epic poems survive through manuscript traditions and papyri;
- the Phliasian sanctuary is known chiefly through Pausanias;
- local sacred stories and ritual explanations are lost;
- the Chest of Cypselus is lost and known through description;
- many vase scenes survive but their identifications vary in security;
- Attic cult is partially recoverable from inscriptions;
- Roman Iuventas has stronger historical and institutional chronology than Greek Hebe.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Hebe was dismissed because she exposed herself: **early-modern invention**.
- She retired automatically on marrying Heracles: **harmonizing modern narrative**, not stated by early sources.
- Ganymede universally replaced her in a single canonical event: **unsupported simplification**.
- She had a surviving Homeric or Orphic Hymn: **false**.
- She possessed a standard ancient zodiac sign, crystal, weekday, or tarot card: **modern invention**.
- She was universally worshipped as Aphrodite Dia: **overstatement of a disputed local comparison**.
- Her Phliasian rites involved human sacrifice or sacred prostitution: **no evidence**.
- A continuous secret cult survived from antiquity into modern witchcraft: **no evidence**.
