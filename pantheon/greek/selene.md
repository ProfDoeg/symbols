# Selene: Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess; more exactly, the divine personification of the Moon in Greek poetic and religious language. Hesiod places her among the second-generation Titans, but “Titaness” is a genealogical category rather than a uniformly attested cultic title.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Hesiod’s *Theogony*, probably composed about 700 BCE, names “bright Selene” (λαμπρὰ Σελήνη) among the gods and makes her the daughter of Hyperion and Theia and sister of Helios and Eos (*Theogony* 19, 371–374). Homeric descriptions of the moon may be older in oral formation, but they do not securely establish a separately acting goddess. No accepted Linear B occurrence of the divine name *Selene* is known.

**Evidence labels used below:** **[Early]** securely attested archaic or early-classical evidence; **[Later canonical]** influential Hellenistic or Roman literary elaboration; **[Regional]** local or competing tradition; **[Learned]** antiquarian, philosophical, allegorical, or priestly systematization; **[Syncretistic]** historical equation with another deity; **[Late antique]** imperial or late-antique magical/theological development; **[Medieval/early modern]** post-antique reception; **[Modern reconstruction]** scholarly inference; **[Modern invention]** a recent claim not documented in ancient evidence.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Selene is simultaneously the visible Moon, the divine power manifested in it, and—when narrative requires—a female anthropomorphic deity who travels across the night sky. Greek texts do not consistently separate these senses. A poet may write *selēnē* as the ordinary noun “moon,” invoke it as a goddess, or move between the two without explanation.

### Names

- **Σελήνη, Selḗnē** — Ionic-Attic Greek, literally “Moon.” Dialect forms include Doric **Σελάνα, Selána**, and Aeolic/Lesbian **Σελά nna, Selánna**, the form used by Sappho. **[Early]**
- The best-supported internal derivation connects the word with **σέλας, sélas**, “brightness, gleam”: reconstructed Proto-Greek *selas-nā*. Chantraine and Beekes nevertheless regard the farther etymology as obscure. Connections with the Indo-European word for “sun” have been proposed but are not established. [Indo-European Cognate Relationships database](https://iecor.clld.org/cognatesets/4127)
- **Μήνη, Mḗnē** — “Moon” as measurer of the month, related to μήν, “month,” and ultimately PIE *mḗh₁n̥s. In later poetry and magic *Mene* may operate as a name or aspect of Selene. It must not automatically be equated with the male Anatolian moon-god Mēn. **[Later canonical/Syncretistic]**
- **Luna** — Latin “Moon,” from the Italic/Indo-European “light” family. Luna possessed an Italian cult of her own before complete literary assimilation to Greek Selene; “Luna = Selene” is therefore a Roman interpretive equation, not proof that the two cult histories were identical. **[Syncretistic]**
- **Phoebe/Phoibē**, “bright” — occasionally a poetic lunar designation, but Phoebe is also a distinct Titan and the name became strongly associated with Artemis. Its occurrence does not always mean Selene specifically.
- **Cynthia** — properly an epithet of Artemis from Mount Cynthus. Renaissance and Romantic adaptations sometimes substitute Cynthia or Diana for Selene in the Endymion story. **[Early-modern literary conflation]**

### Attested descriptions and titles

The literary tradition supplies many descriptive epithets but comparatively few firmly localized cult titles:

- **λαμπρά, lamprá**, “bright”; Hesiod, *Theogony* 19. **[Early]**
- **εὐπλόκαμος, euplókamos**, “fair-/rich-tressed”; Homeric Hymn 32. **[Archaic or classical hymnic]**
- **λευκώλενος, leukṓlenos**, “white-armed”; Homeric Hymn 32. **[Archaic or classical hymnic]**
- **φαεσίμβροτος, phaesímbrotos**, “shining for mortals”; Homeric Hymn 32. **[Archaic or classical hymnic]**
- **τανυσίπτερος, tanysí-pteros**, conventionally “long-winged” or “far-flying”; Homeric Hymn 32. The ancient-formulaic meaning is uncertain.
- **χρυσοστέφανος**, “golden-crowned/diademed”; Homeric Hymn 32. **[Archaic or classical hymnic]**
- **πανδῖα**, “all-bright” or “wholly divine,” appears as the name of her daughter Pandia rather than securely as Selene’s own cult title in Hymn 32.
- **ὀφθαλμὸς νυκτός**, “eye of night”; Aeschylus, *Seven against Thebes* 390. This is poetic description, not demonstrated cult nomenclature. **[Early classical]**
- **Pasiphaë, Πασιφάη**, “all-shining”; Pausanias reports an oracle of Pasiphaë at Thalamai and says some identified Pasiphaë there as Selene. That is a localized interpretive identification; it does not erase the separate Cretan Pasiphaë, wife of Minos. **[Regional/Learned]**
- The Orphic Hymn calls her, among other things, bull-horned, torch-bearing, night-roaming, all-seeing, nourisher, and “mother of time.” These belong to an imperial-period ritual-poetic theology, not the securely recoverable archaic core. **[Late antique or imperial ritual systematization]**

### Artemis, Hecate, and the “triple goddess”

The historical development must be separated into stages:

1. **Selene as the Moon itself** is securely archaic.
2. **Artemis receives lunar associations** only gradually. Aeschylus fr. 170 and later classical evidence are among the earliest signs; archaic Artemis was not simply another name for Selene.
3. **Hecate receives celestial and lunar associations** in classical and Hellenistic material, while retaining crossroads, liminal, ghostly, and chthonic functions.
4. **Imperial magical texts** freely invoke Selene, Artemis, Hecate, Persephone, and sometimes Isis as names or manifestations of a single cosmic power. PGM IV is particularly explicit. **[Syncretistic/Late antique]**
5. Roman poets and commentators systematized **Luna in heaven, Diana on earth, and Hecate/Proserpina below**. This is a genuine ancient syncretistic theology, but not the original structure of Greek religion.
6. The scheme **Artemis = Maiden, Selene = Mother, Hecate = Crone**, mapped onto waxing/full/waning moons, is not documented as an ancient Greek doctrine. Its familiar form is substantially a twentieth-century Neopagan development, strongly promoted by Robert Graves’s *The White Goddess* (1948) and later Wiccan literature. **[Modern invention built from ancient syncretisms]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

**Hesiod, *Theogony* 19 and 371–374, Greek, c. 700 BCE. [Early]** The proem lists bright Selene among the immortals sung by the Muses. The genealogy makes Theia, united with Hyperion, mother of Helios, Selene, and Eos. This proves a named divine personification and a developed celestial genealogy by the archaic period. It does not prove a temple, priesthood, or already-developed Endymion romance. [Greek text and translation](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0129)

No securely identified **Mycenaean *se-re-ne*** or equivalent divine name occurs in the Linear B religious tablets. The Linear B form *me-no*, associated with μήν, “month,” demonstrates lunar calendrical vocabulary, not the goddess Selene. Claims that Minoan crescent-like objects prove “Selene worship” are interpretive and cannot bridge the evidentiary gap.

### Principal literary corpus

#### Archaic and classical

- **Homer, *Iliad* 8.555–559**, traditional epic language fixed in writing around the late eighth or seventh century BCE: the moon shines among stars. The passage is vivid but not an independent narrative about Selene.
- **Hesiod, *Theogony* 19, 371–374**, c. 700 BCE: name and standard genealogy. **[Early]**
- **Hesiod, *Works and Days***, c. 700 BCE: lunar time is fundamental to its calendar, but it offers no developed Selene myth. Later references sometimes exaggerate its relevance.
- **Homeric Hymn to Hermes 99–100**, probably sixth century BCE or later: calls Selene daughter of **Pallas son of Megamedes**, a genealogy incompatible with Hesiod. **[Early variant]**
- **Sappho fr. 96**, late seventh/early sixth century BCE: Selanna outshines the stars when full. This is poetic personification, not yet the surviving Endymion narrative.
- **Sappho fr. 199**, known only through ancient quotation or testimony, apparently mentioned Endymion. The Oxford Classical Dictionary treats this as the earliest surviving evidence for Selene’s love for him, although the fragment is too small to reconstruct the story. **[Early but fragmentary]** [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/550495959)
- **Pindar**, early fifth century BCE: uses lunar imagery and “golden-eyed” moon language; scholia attached to Pindar preserve later genealogical material, including Nemea as a daughter of Zeus and Selene. The scholia must not be dated as though they were Pindar’s own words.
- **Aeschylus, *Seven against Thebes* 390**, 467 BCE: Selene as the “eye of night.”
- **Aeschylus fr. 170**, from a lost play: early evidence for treating Artemis in lunar terms. **[Early syncretistic movement]**
- **Sophocles fr. 535**: evidence for Hecate’s lunar identification; the fragmentary setting limits certainty.
- **Euripides, *Phoenician Women* 175**, produced c. 410–409 BCE: lunar poetic language; later commentators use it in discussing changing genealogy and reflected moonlight.
- **Aristophanes, *Peace* 406–413**, 421 BCE: contrasts Greek Olympian practice with supposedly “barbarian” sacrifice to Helios and Selene. Comedy is tendentious evidence, but it suggests that conspicuous independent astral cult could be represented as non-mainstream at Athens.
- **Plato, *Cratylus* 409a–b**, c. 390–360 BCE: Socrates gives playful etymological speculation about *selēnē*, including the Moon’s “new and old” light. It is philosophical wordplay, not reliable historical linguistics. **[Learned]**

#### Hellenistic

- **Theocritus, *Idyll* 2**, third century BCE: Simaetha repeatedly invokes Selene during an erotic binding rite and then narrates her affair. Selene is celestial witness and magical addressee. This is literary mime, although it reflects real forms of Greek erotic magic.
- **Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 4.55–65**, third century BCE: Selene sees Medea wandering at night and exults that she is not alone in love-madness, recalling her own visits to Endymion in the Latmian cave. This is the earliest substantially preserved literary placement of their affair at Latmos. **[Hellenistic canonical elaboration]**
- **Nicander**, second century BCE, lost poem or fragment: probably the Greek source behind Virgil’s Pan-and-Moon story, according to later scholarship; the dependence is plausible but not demonstrable in detail. **[Reconstruction]**
- **Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 1.7.5–6**, compiled probably first or second century CE from earlier sources: Endymion’s extraordinary beauty causes Selene to love him; Zeus permits him to choose his fate, and he chooses eternal sleep with ageless immortality. **[Later canonical]**

#### Roman and imperial Greek

- **Virgil, *Georgics* 3.391–393**, 29 BCE: Pan gives Luna snow-white wool, lures or deceives her into deep woods, and she does not reject him. Virgil introduces it with *si credere dignum est*, “if the tale deserves belief,” marking it as obscure or playful lore. This is the only complete surviving ancient notice. [Latin-context translation](https://www.theoi.com/Text/VirgilGeorgics2.html)
- **Ovid, *Fasti* 3.883–884; 4.373–374; *Ars Amatoria* 3.83; *Remedia Amoris* 255–258; *Heroides* 18.61–65**, c. 20 BCE–17 CE: Luna’s chariot, horned crescent, and sleeping Endymion become standard Roman poetic imagery.
- **Strabo, *Geography* 14.1.8**, Augustan/Tiberian period: reports the sanctuary and tomb of Endymion near Heracleia under Latmos, providing geographical cult-memory but not proof of an historical Endymion.
- **Pausanias, *Description of Greece* 3.26.1; 5.1.4–5; 5.11.8; 6.24.6**, c. 150–175 CE:
  - Thalamai had an oracle and statues of Pasiphaë and Helios; Pasiphaë was interpreted as Selene.
  - Eleans said Selene loved Endymion and bore him fifty daughters.
  - Selene and Helios appeared on the base of the chryselephantine Zeus at Olympia.
  - Stone images of Sun and Moon stood in the marketplace at Elis; the Moon bore crescent horns.
  These are among the best sources for material and regional evidence. [Pausanias translation](https://pausanias.symmachus.org/pausanias.pdf)
- **Lucian, *Dialogues of the Gods* 19**, second century CE: Selene confides to Aphrodite that she loves the sleeping Endymion and quietly descends to him; Aphrodite compares her own Adonis affair. Comic dialogue elaborates the romance but is not cult scripture.
- **Aelian, *On Animals* 12.7**, early third century CE, quoting the much earlier Epimenides: the Nemean lion declares itself sprung from fair-haired Selene, who cast or shook it down at Hera’s bidding. The quoted verse may preserve archaic material, but its transmission through Aelian makes exact dating uncertain.
- **Hyginus, *Fabulae* 30**, surviving through a defective manuscript tradition, probably imperial Latin: Luna nourishes or produces the Nemean lion in a two-mouthed cave. This differs from Hesiod’s monster genealogies and from Aelian’s version. **[Late variant]**
- **Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica* 10.334–336**, probably third century CE: the Horae are called daughters of Helios and Selene. This is a late and otherwise unusual genealogy.
- **Nonnus, *Dionysiaca***, fifth century CE: develops Selene’s chariot, horns, nocturnal course, and astral family in elaborate late-antique epic; it also participates in the highly syncretic astral theology of its era.
- **Orphic Hymn 9, “To Selene”**, probably composed in Asia Minor in the imperial period, often dated between the second and third centuries CE, though broader datings have been proposed. It prescribes incense/aromatics and invokes Selene as bull-horned, torch-bearing, night-running, all-seeing, female and male, waxing and waning, mother of time. The surviving medieval manuscript tradition is much later; the first printed Greek edition appeared in Florence in 1500. **[Imperial ritual theology]**
- **Greek Magical Papyri**, especially PGM IV, manuscripts chiefly third–fourth centuries CE from Roman Egypt:
  - PGM IV.2241–2358, “Document to the Waning Moon,” invokes a composite Mene/Selene-Hecate-Kore-Persephone-Isis in destructive magic.
  - PGM IV.2441–2621, the “Lunar Spell of Claudianus,” constructs an “Egyptian Selene” image and employs lunar hour-angels for attraction, binding, and dream-sending.
  - PGM IV.2708–2784 and 2785–2890 contain a coercive attraction rite and “Prayer to Selene for any spell,” identifying her with Hecate, Artemis, Persephone, and other powers.
  
  These texts are direct evidence for practitioners in Roman Egypt, not evidence that every Greek worshipper held the same theology. [PGM translation](https://faculty.umb.edu/gary_zabel/Courses/Phil%20281b/Philosophy%20of%20Magic/Arcana/Ancient/Papyri_Graecae_Magicae.pdf)

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain and powers

**[Early]** Selene is the Moon’s luminous presence and regular motion. She illuminates night, dims the stars at the full moon, and measures cyclical time. The Homeric Hymn describes light streaming from her immortal head and enclosing the earth when she has bathed in Ocean, dressed, yoked her horses, and driven into the sky.

**[Classical/Hellenistic]** Lunar visibility made her a witness to secret nighttime acts. Theocritus’s Simaetha repeatedly tells Selene to “shine brightly” while performing erotic magic. Selene’s association with desire is reinforced by Endymion and Pan, but she is not reduced to a love goddess.

**[Learned and magical]** Waxing and waning support doctrines of growth, diminution, generation, menstruation, childbirth, agriculture, sickness, and death. Such correspondences are prominent in philosophical, astrological, medical, and magical literature. They are not all identifiable as old cult attributes of the personified Selene.

**[Late antique]** Magical texts grant the syncretized lunar goddess authority over ghosts, compulsion, erotic attraction, dreams, harmful magic, crossroads, cosmic elements, and underworld beings.

### Chariot and animals

Homeric Hymn 32 describes Selene yoking shining, long-maned horses and driving them swiftly. Later art and literature vary the conveyance:

- a two-horse chariot or **biga**, most common;
- winged horses, especially in early classical vase painting;
- cattle or oxen, exploiting lunar “horns”;
- a single horse, mule, or ox in some descriptions and images;
- a veil billowing in an arc behind the head, suggesting the celestial vault.

The commonly repeated claim that her chariot was invariably silver is not an ancient constant. Gold, whiteness, brightness, and silver-like radiance vary by author and medium.

### Recognizable iconography

Common attributes include:

- crescent moon behind or above the head;
- crescent-like horns;
- torch or torches;
- long robe and mantle;
- chariot, usually with two horses;
- billowing veil;
- descent toward the sleeping Endymion;
- Helios as a balancing figure, one celestial body rising as the other sets.

The earliest securely identified chariot image is generally taken to be the interior of a red-figure cup attributed to the Brygos Painter, c. 490–480 BCE, Berlin Antikensammlung F 2293: a female lunar figure drives a two-horse chariot down toward Ocean. Attribution as Selene is strong because of the celestial setting, though the cup does not carry a name inscription. **[Early iconographic identification]**

At Olympia, Pausanias saw Selene riding a horse at one end of the sculptured base of Phidias’s Zeus, counterbalanced by Helios in his chariot. Because the statue was destroyed, Pausanias’s description is the principal witness.

At Elis, Pausanias saw a Moon image recognizable by horns projecting from its head. This is direct testimony to local iconography, though the sculpture is lost.

### Selene and Endymion sarcophagi

The myth became exceptionally popular in Roman funerary sculpture from the later second through third centuries CE. Selene descends from her chariot toward the eternally youthful sleeper, often accompanied by:

- Hypnos or a winged sleep figure pouring a potion;
- Erotes;
- Aurae controlling the horses;
- Tellus or a reclining earth figure;
- Helios rising on the opposite side;
- pastoral figures and animals.

A major example is the Metropolitan Museum’s Severan sarcophagus for Arria, early third century CE. Selene’s descent and departure frame Endymion’s sleep, while Helios and bucolic imagery convert the love myth into an image of cosmic recurrence, repose, and hoped-for blessed continuity after death. [Metropolitan Museum object record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/254590)

The imagery does not prove a mystery cult promising immortality through Selene. Funerary meaning is evident; a specific initiatory doctrine is not.

### Sacred animals, plants, colors, numbers, and days

- **Horses, oxen, cattle, and mules:** attested as vehicles or iconographic associates.
- **White wool/ram:** belongs to Virgil’s Pan episode; no evidence establishes the ram as Selene’s universal sacred animal.
- **Crescent horns/bull imagery:** prominent in Orphic and magical texts.
- **Poppies:** appear in some Roman Endymion compositions as emblems of sleep, usually carried by a sleep figure; they are not securely an independent Selene cult emblem.
- **White, silver, gold:** poetic colors of lunar brilliance, but fixed color-correspondence tables are largely later astrological or occult systematizations.
- **Fifty:** the daughters of Selene and Endymion. The usual explanation connects them with the roughly fifty lunar months in an Olympiad’s four-year cycle; this is plausible learned interpretation, not stated by Pausanias.
- **Monday:** Latin *dies Lunae* and its descendants commemorate Luna, not an independently documented archaic Greek holy day of Selene.
- **Noumenia:** the first visible crescent/new-month observance in Greek calendars. Modern worshippers often honor Selene then, but ancient Noumenia was broadly associated with the household gods, Apollo Noumenios, Hestia, Zeus, and the renewal of the month; an exclusive Selene festival is not established.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing genealogies

| Version | Parentage | Source and date | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Standard Hesiodic | Hyperion + Theia; siblings Helios and Eos | Hesiod, *Theogony* 371–374, c. 700 BCE | **[Early; ultimately dominant]** |
| Hymnic variant | Pallas son of Megamedes | Homeric Hymn to Hermes 99–100, probably sixth century BCE or later | **[Early variant]** |
| Euryphaessa version | Hyperion + Euryphaessa; siblings Helios and Eos | Homeric Hymn to Helios 4–7 | **[Archaic/classical variant]**; Euryphaessa may be an epithet or alternate identity of Theia |
| Daughter of Helios | Selene generated from or made daughter of the Sun | Later poetic, philosophical, and scholastic traditions | **[Learned systematization]**, sometimes explained through reflected moonlight |
| Euonyme/Basileia narrative | Hyperion’s children Helios and Selene; after Helios’s murder, Selene casts herself down and becomes the Moon | Diodorus Siculus 3.57, first century BCE, reporting an euhemerizing Atlantic story | **[Learned/euhemeristic variant]** |

### Consorts and offspring

- **Zeus:** father by Selene of **Pandia** in Homeric Hymn 32. **[Hymnic]**
- **Endymion:** Pausanias says Selene bore him **fifty daughters**. Their individual names are not transmitted. **[Regional Elis tradition]**
- **Zeus and Selene → Ersa/Herse (“Dew”):** attested in a fragment attributed to Alcman or later testimony. **[Fragmentary variant]**
- **Zeus and Selene → Nemea:** scholia to Pindar preserve this genealogy. It is not the same claim as Selene producing the Nemean lion. **[Late scholastic/local variant]**
- **Helios and Selene → the Horae:** Quintus Smyrnaeus 10.334–336. This is late and conflicts with Hesiod’s standard parentage of the Horae by Zeus and Themis. **[Late variant]**
- **Pan:** Virgil implies sexual union or successful seduction but names no offspring. **[Roman literary variant]**
- **Musaeus/Narcissus and other supposed children:** occasionally found in late compilations or modern lists; no sound early primary evidence establishes them as standard children of Selene.

### Every documented divine interaction

The following inventory includes specific encounters, unions, coordinated actions, or single-scene appearances. Mere membership in the same genealogy is separately marked.

- **Hyperion — father of Selene.** Theia bears Selene, Helios, and Eos after union with Hyperion; Hesiod, *Theogony* 371–374, c. 700 BCE. **[Early genealogy; no narrative encounter]**
- **Theia/Euryphaessa — mother of Selene.** Hesiod, *Theogony* 371–374; Homeric Hymn to Helios 4–7. **[Early competing names; no narrative encounter]**
- **Pallas son of Megamedes — alternate father.** Homeric Hymn to Hermes 99–100. **[Early variant; no narrative encounter]**
- **Helios — sibling and celestial counterpart.** Genealogy in Hesiod; joint balancing appearance on the base of Zeus at Olympia in Pausanias 5.11.8; frequent paired rising/setting in Roman art. **[Early genealogy; later iconographic interaction]**
- **Eos — sister.** Hesiodic genealogy and celestial triad; no surviving early myth gives Selene and Eos a substantial dialogue or conflict. **[Early genealogy; absence of narrative interaction]**
- **Zeus — sexual union and birth of Pandia.** Homeric Hymn 32.14–16. **[Hymnic/canonical]**
- **Zeus — grants Endymion a choice resulting in eternal sleep and agelessness after Selene falls in love with him.** Pseudo-Apollodorus 1.7.5. **[Later canonical]** The text does not say Selene herself requested or imposed the sleep.
- **Pan — seduces or deceives Luna/Selene with snow-white fleece and calls her into the woods; she accepts.** Virgil, *Georgics* 3.391–393, 29 BCE, probably drawing on Nicander. **[Later literary variant]**
- **Hera — orders Selene to bring forth or cast down the Nemean lion.** Epimenidean verse quoted by Aelian, *On Animals* 12.7. **[Possibly archaic fragment, late transmission]**
- **Aphrodite — converses with Selene about Endymion and their respective love troubles.** Lucian, *Dialogues of the Gods* 19, second century CE. **[Late comic elaboration]**
- **Artemis — progressively identified with Selene as terrestrial huntress/lunar goddess.** Aeschylus fr. 170 onward; explicit ritual fusion in the PGM. **[Syncretistic, not an encounter myth]**
- **Hecate — identified with Selene in lunar, magical, and chthonic invocations.** Sophocles fr. 535 and especially PGM IV, third–fourth centuries CE. **[Syncretistic]**
- **Persephone/Kore — invoked as the same or overlapping lunar power.** PGM IV.2241–2358 and 2785–2890. **[Late-antique magical syncretism]**
- **Isis — absorbed into composite lunar invocations in Roman Egypt.** PGM and wider Isiac theology. **[Late-antique Egyptian-Greek syncretism]**
- **Helios and Selene — parents of the Horae in Quintus Smyrnaeus.** *Posthomerica* 10.334–336. **[Late variant]**
- **Hypnos — appears beside Endymion, administering or personifying sleep, in Roman sarcophagus imagery.** Second–third-century CE visual corpus. **[Iconographic interaction; not securely preserved as an early literary episode]**
- **Eros/Erotes — accompany Selene’s approach to Endymion in Roman art.** **[Imperial iconographic elaboration]**
- **Aurae — restrain or guide her horses in Endymion sarcophagi.** **[Imperial iconographic elaboration]**
- **Tellus/Gaia — reclines beneath the celestial scene in Roman sarcophagi.** **[Imperial iconographic juxtaposition]**
- **Typhon — modern summaries sometimes claim Selene “battled Typhon.”** Nonnus places celestial gods within the Typhon conflict, but no early independent Selene-versus-Typhon myth is securely attested. **[Late epic context; popular overstatement]**
- **Apollo — no securely attested personal interaction.** Later solar identification of Apollo parallels Artemis’s lunarization but does not produce an ancient Selene–Apollo episode.
- **Dionysus — no major independent interaction before Nonnus’s late epic cosmology.**
- **Athena, Poseidon, Demeter, Ares, Hermes, and most Olympians — no specific surviving interaction merely follows from sharing the Greek pantheon.**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Selene and Endymion

#### Earliest core

Sappho fr. 199, if correctly interpreted, is the earliest surviving trace that connected the Moon with Endymion. It is too fragmentary to establish the cave, fifty daughters, cause of sleep, or nightly visits. **[Early, fragmentary]**

#### Latmian romance

Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 4.55–65, presents the developed Hellenistic form. Selene sees Medea wandering at night because of Jason and rejoices bitterly that another woman has been afflicted by Aphrodite. She recalls that she herself burns for beautiful Endymion and visits the Latmian cave. The story here functions as a divine parallel to Medea’s dangerous erotic compulsion.

Strabo locates Endymion’s sanctuary or tomb near Heracleia under Mount Latmos in Caria. This anchors the narrative to a real regional landscape but does not establish whether the Carian localization preceded the Elean one.

Lucian’s *Dialogues of the Gods* makes Selene describe how she stops her chariot, descends quietly, and gazes upon or kisses the sleeping youth. The text luxuriates in his unguarded beauty and is a comic-literary elaboration.

#### Elean version

Pausanias 5.1.4–5 reports that the Eleans made Endymion an early king. They showed his tomb at Olympia and said Selene loved him and bore fifty daughters. Other Elean genealogies gave him mortal wives—Asterodia, Chromia, or Hyperippe—and mortal dynastic children. Pausanias places these alternatives side by side instead of harmonizing them.

The Elean and Latmian traditions may reflect rival localization rather than a single narrative journey from Elis to Caria. Ancient sources do not explain their relationship.

#### Causes of the sleep

The sources disagree:

- **Choice:** Pseudo-Apollodorus says Zeus allowed Endymion to choose, and he chose perpetual sleep, remaining deathless and ageless.
- **Punishment:** A scholastic tradition makes sleep punishment for Endymion’s desire for Hera.
- **Astronomical/romantic inference:** Later retellings say Selene put him to sleep so she could visit or kiss him forever. Timothy Gantz stresses that no early source securely says the sleep was Selene’s idea. This familiar explanation should therefore be labeled a later rationalization.
- **Gift:** Some Roman and modern versions frame the sleep as divine preservation of beauty.

No single version is canonical across all periods.

#### Children and interpretation

Pausanias’s fifty daughters are often explained as the approximately fifty lunar months between Olympic festivals. This numerical reading is attractive and probably ancient or learned, but Pausanias does not explicitly state it.

#### Funerary transformation

Roman sarcophagi shift the story’s center from lunar calendrics to death, sleep, beauty, reunion, and recurrence. Endymion’s undying sleep provided an adaptable euphemism for death. Selene’s arrival suggested continuing love; her departure and return, paired with Helios, suggested cosmic cycles. **[Imperial visual reinterpretation]**

### Birth of Pandia

Homeric Hymn 32 praises Selene’s radiance and concludes that Zeus joined with her and she bore **Pandia**, “all-bright” or “all-divine,” exceptionally beautiful among the immortals. The Athenian festival **Pandia** is documented, but its divine recipient and etymological relation to Pandia remain debated. It is unsafe to claim that the festival was straightforwardly Selene’s birthday or an established Selene festival.

### Pan and the white fleece

Virgil’s *Georgics* says:

> With a gift of snowy wool—if the story is worthy of belief—Pan, god of Arcadia, beguiled you, Moon, calling you into the deep woods; nor did you spurn his call.

The passage is only three lines long. Later retellings sometimes say Pan disguised himself as a white ram; that detail derives from commentary and reconstruction rather than Virgil’s exact wording. Others say he offered a flock or wrapped himself in fleece. The responsible conclusion is that Virgil knew an obscure tale in which white wool enabled Pan’s successful seduction or deception of Luna, probably from Nicander. Consent is ambiguous because *fefellit* means “deceived,” while *nec aspernata* says she did not reject the invitation.

### The Nemean lion

This tradition must be kept distinct from the better-known monster genealogies:

- Hesiodic and Apollodoran traditions relate the lion to the offspring of Echidna, Orthrus, Chimaira, or Typhon, with textual uncertainty over the exact parents.
- Epimenides, quoted by Aelian 12.7, makes the lion announce that it sprang from fair-tressed Selene, who shuddered and cast it down at Hera’s bidding onto Nemea.
- Hyginus says Luna nourished or produced the lion in a cave.

Thus “the Nemean lion was Selene’s son” is one documented variant, not the universal Greek genealogy. Selene’s act may be biological birth, shaking the creature from the Moon, or poetic production; Aelian’s wording does not support a single literalized account.

### Selene and Medea

Apollonius’s scene is not a separate cult myth but an important divine–mortal comparison. Selene takes momentary satisfaction in Medea’s distress because Aphrodite has made both abandon propriety for love. It gives Selene an emotional voice rare in earlier poetry and makes the Endymion affair a paradigm of nocturnal eros.

### Euhemeristic death and celestial transformation

Diodorus Siculus reports a story of Basileia’s children Helios and Selene: hostile Titans murder Helios; Selene throws herself from a height; both later appear as Sun and Moon. This is a late euhemerizing construction that treats gods as deified primordial rulers. It conflicts with the mainstream conception of Selene as the Moon from the outset. **[Learned Hellenistic variant]**

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### General character of the evidence

Selene had less independent monumental cult in mainland Greece than her literary visibility might suggest. Robert Parker notes that Aristophanes could portray worship of Helios and Selene as characteristically “barbarian.” This does not mean Greeks never worshipped her: hymns, local images, invocations, oaths, magic, and syncretistic cult are all attested. It means evidence for a widespread network of autonomous Selene temples and priesthoods is lacking.

### Greek cult places

#### Thalamai, Laconia

Pausanias 3.26.1 describes a sanctuary/oracle of **Ino-Pasiphaë** at Thalamai and reports bronze statues of Helios and Pasiphaë. He says Pasiphaë was a title of Selene rather than the Cretan daughter of Helios. Consultation occurred through dreams: a person slept in the sanctuary and received an answer.

- Archaeological status: the precise sanctuary and its phases remain uncertain.
- Evidentiary status: Pausanias securely documents a second-century-CE interpretive tradition and an incubation oracle, not necessarily its foundation date.
- Classification: **[Regional/Syncretistic]**.

#### Elis and Olympia

Pausanias reports:

- images of Helios and Selene in the agora at Elis;
- Selene marked by crescent horns;
- Selene on or near the sculptured base of Phidias’s Zeus at Olympia;
- an Elean dynastic and funerary tradition surrounding Endymion.

These show strong regional mythic representation, but no securely documented large independent temple of Selene.

#### Gythium

An inscription from Roman-period Gythium reportedly associates Helios and Selene in cult. Its importance lies in paired astral worship rather than proof of an archaic Spartan institution. **[Imperial epigraphic cult]**

#### Asia Minor

The Latmos region preserved the landscape of Endymion. In Pisidia and neighboring regions, imperial coins and dedications show crescents, Luna/Selene shrines, or astral deities, sometimes interacting with the widespread Anatolian cult of Mēn. Individual monuments require care: a crescent by itself does not always identify Selene rather than Artemis, Mēn, Luna, or a general lunar symbol.

### Offerings and hymnic ritual

Homeric Hymn 32 was suitable as a rhapsodic or devotional proem but gives no sacrifice rubric.

The Orphic Hymn prescribes an aromatic offering. Its vocabulary—bull-horned, torch-bearing, nourishing, nocturnal—belongs to a small ritual collection probably used by an initiatory or household group in imperial Asia Minor.

The PGM prescribe highly specific, sometimes coercive rites:

- rooftop burning of goat fat and Ethiopian cumin on the thirteenth or fourteenth lunar day;
- creation of a composite “Egyptian Selene” image from clay, sulfur, and goat blood;
- animal substances, engraved gems, lamps, voces magicae, and long syncretistic invocations;
- erotic attraction, binding, dream sending, divination, hostile magic, and protection.

These are documented practices or prescriptions in magical handbooks. They should neither be sanitized nor generalized into “the religion of all Greeks.” [Faraone on the PGM hymn](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781315812564-36/hymn-selene%E2%80%93hecate%E2%80%93artemis-greek-magical-handbook-pgm-iv-2714%E2%80%9383-christopher-faraone)

### Sacrifice and contested practices

- Ordinary offerings—incense, liquids, cakes, animal products—are compatible with the hymnic and magical evidence.
- **No secure source documents a regular institution of human sacrifice to Selene.**
- Narratives of human sacrifice attached to Taurian Artemis cannot simply be transferred to Selene because Artemis later acquired lunar attributes.
- The PGM contain violent coercive language, animal blood, threats against gods, corpse-related ingredients, and rites intended to injure or compel humans. These are real textual evidence for late-antique ritual violence and coercion, but not human sacrifice to Selene.
- There is no credible evidence for “temple prostitution” in a Selene cult.
- There is no documented Selene-specific cult violence comparable to the better-attested political conflict surrounding some major civic sanctuaries.

### Priesthood, economics, and politics

No generally recognized pan-Hellenic priesthood of Selene is known. Local officials may have served paired astral or syncretistic cults, but surviving evidence does not support a centralized order.

Selene’s political function was primarily indirect:

- lunar regulation of calendars affected festivals, assemblies, debt, agriculture, and military timing;
- imperial imagery could use Sun and Moon to express cosmic dominion and eternity;
- paired Sol-Luna images validated emperors and the permanence of Rome;
- funerary workshops profited from the popularity of the Endymion motif.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before c. 700 BCE: unattested prehistory

Lunar observation and calendrical reckoning long predate Greek alphabetic literature. Minoan or Mycenaean artifacts may encode lunar cycles, but none securely names Selene. Claims of a prehistoric universal “Great Moon Goddess” behind Selene are speculative comparativism, not demonstrated continuity.

### c. 700–500 BCE: archaic divine personification

Hesiod supplies the stable celestial family: Theia and Hyperion produce Dawn, Sun, and Moon. Sappho supplies the Moon’s overwhelming beauty and perhaps the earliest Endymion association. The Homeric Hymns give ritual-poetic portraits and conflicting genealogy. Selene is already divine, female, luminous, and mobile, but independent institutional cult remains poorly visible.

### Fifth–fourth centuries BCE: classical differentiation and overlap

Selene remains distinct from Artemis and Hecate, while poets begin attaching lunar language to both. Philosophers discuss the Moon as a physical body; cult and natural philosophy coexist rather than one simply replacing the other. Athenian comedy can represent overt Sun-and-Moon sacrifice as foreign. No evidence shows a suppression of Selene by Artemis through institutional decree.

### Third–first centuries BCE: Hellenistic narrative and magical expansion

Apollonius fixes the influential Latmian Endymion scene. Theocritus makes Selene central to literary erotic magic. Astral religion, astrology, and intercultural contact encourage new equations. The distinction among Selene, Mene, Artemis, Hecate, Isis, and regional lunar divinities becomes permeable in some contexts while remaining intact in others.

### First century BCE–third century CE: Roman Luna and the visual peak

Roman poetry imports Selene’s myths into Luna. Endymion becomes a major artistic subject; Selene’s most abundant surviving visual corpus is Roman rather than archaic Greek. Her imagery becomes funerary, erotic, and cosmic.

The Roman **Luna** possessed an Aventine temple distinct in origin from Greek literary Selene. The temple is first securely mentioned in 182 BCE, though its foundation is placed between 292 and 219 BCE by modern topographical analysis; Tacitus attributes it traditionally to Servius Tullius. It was near the temple of Ceres: Livy says a storm tore away its door in 182 BCE and threw it against Ceres’s rear wall. Both temples were struck by lightning in 84 BCE. Mummius dedicated Corinthian spoils there after 146 BCE. The sanctuary perished in the fire of 64 CE and was apparently not rebuilt. Its anniversary was March 31. [Oxford Classical Dictionary on Luna](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/549652335) [Digital Augustan Rome](https://www.digitalaugustanrome.org/records/luna-aedes/)

Varro also mentions a nocturnally shining Palatine shrine of **Noctiluca**, sometimes identified with Luna. The identification is plausible but not certain.

### Third–fifth centuries CE: magical and theological fusion

The PGM and Orphic Hymns show the most elaborate ritual Selene: cosmic ruler, Hecate, Artemis, Persephone, Fate, nature, and underworld power. This is neither a survival unchanged from Hesiod nor mere literary fancy; it is evidence for real practitioners in imperial religious pluralism.

### Fourth–sixth centuries CE: Christianization and displacement

Imperial anti-sacrifice legislation, temple closures, confiscation, and Christianization affected pagan cult generally. No securely documented edict singles out Selene. Her independent sanctuaries were few, and her imagery was absorbed into:

- neutral astronomical personification;
- calendar and zodiac art;
- allegory of night;
- Christian cosmic imagery, where Sun and Moon witness the Crucifixion;
- literary education and mythography.

Claims of an underground, continuously organized Selene cult surviving from antiquity through the Middle Ages are unsupported.

### Medieval and Byzantine survival

The Greek word σελήνη remained standard learned vocabulary for the Moon. Mythographic manuscripts preserved Hesiod, hymns, Pausanias, Apollonius, and Lucian. Selene survived as a literary and astronomical personification rather than through demonstrable continuous public cult.

### Renaissance–nineteenth century revival

Classical texts and sarcophagi revived the Endymion subject in European art. Renaissance writers frequently substituted Diana or Cynthia for Selene. The myth became a vehicle for beauty, contemplative sleep, unattainable love, artistic inspiration, and the soul’s ascent. [BMCR review of *Endymion au carrefour*](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2008/2008.02.44/)

### Twentieth century–present

Modern Hellenic polytheists, eclectic Pagans, and occultists again address Selene in prayer and ritual. This is a genuine modern religious history but not proven institutional continuity. Organized reconstructionist Hellenism emerged especially in the late twentieth century; Greece recognized Hellenic Ethnic Religion as a “known religion” in 2017. The scale of Selene-specific worship is unknown.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature, music, and visual art

- **Michael Drayton, *Endimion and Phoebe* (1595):** uses Phoebe rather than Selene, demonstrating Renaissance lunar conflation.
- **John Lyly, *Endymion* (c. 1588):** transforms the myth into courtly allegory centered on Cynthia.
- **John Keats, *Endymion* (1818):** the most influential English Romantic reworking; the Moon appears principally as Cynthia. The poem expands the sparse ancient romance into a spiritual quest. Its opening, “A thing of beauty is a joy for ever,” profoundly reshaped the myth’s reception.
- **George Frederic Watts, *Endymion* (1872)** and numerous academic paintings render the lunar lover as an ethereal female presence.
- Opera, ballet, and masque traditions repeatedly use Diana, Cynthia, Phoebe, Luna, and Selene interchangeably. This interchange is reception history, not evidence of archaic identity.

### Occult and esoteric traditions

Medieval and Renaissance astral magic did not usually worship the Greek narrative personality Selene directly. It approached the **planetary Moon** through Arabic, Latin, Hermetic, astrological, and angelological systems:

- Arabic **Ghāyat al-Ḥakīm**, translated into Latin as the *Picatrix* in thirteenth-century Castile, prescribed lunar images, suffumigations, elections, and prayers.
- Marsilio Ficino’s *De vita libri tres* (1489) theorized celestial influence and sympathetic materials.
- Cornelius Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia* (first complete printed edition 1533) catalogued lunar correspondences—silver, water, fertility, imagination, bodily fluids, dreams, and particular spirits.

These systems descend partly from ancient astrology and magic but are not a continuous secret priesthood of Selene. [Penn State edition of the *Picatrix*](https://www.psupress.org/books/titles/978-0-271-08213-4.html)

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century occultism further combined the Moon with astral planes, psychic receptivity, femininity, and the subconscious. Golden Dawn and successor traditions placed the Moon in an elaborate Qabalistic planetary system. Selene’s Greek name may appear as poetic nomenclature, while the operative framework is largely Hermetic-Qabalistic.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist worship

Modern worshippers use:

- Homeric and Orphic hymns;
- libations, incense, cakes, milk, water, or wine;
- observances keyed to lunar phases;
- modern devotional poetry;
- reconstructed Greek household ritual.

Where these practices reproduce an ancient prescription, the source can be named; where practitioners select silver candles, crystals, “moon water,” or a Maiden-Mother-Crone structure, the practice belongs chiefly to modern occult or Pagan correspondence systems.

The claim that Selene was anciently “the Mother” in a fixed triad with maiden Artemis and crone Hecate is **[Modern invention]**. Ancient syncretism supports a three-realm goddess and many overlapping names, not that age-sequence.

### Psychology and gender symbolism

Jungian and post-Jungian writers interpret Selene as an archetype of cyclical consciousness, femininity, erotic contemplation, or the unconscious seeking the passive masculine Endymion. Such readings are twentieth-century psychological hermeneutics; they are not recoveries of ancient doctrine.

Modern feminist spirituality sometimes treats Selene as evidence of a suppressed prehistoric matriarchy. Broad claims of a single pre-Indo-European Moon Goddess overthrown by patriarchal Olympians exceed the evidence. Local female cult power and later marginalization are legitimate subjects; a universal prehistoric Selene religion is not demonstrated.

### Popular culture and commerce

- **Sailor Moon** uses the Greek lunar name indirectly through “Silver Millennium,” Queen Serenity, Princess Serenity, and Prince Endymion. It is a creative Japanese synthesis, not a direct adaptation of a single ancient source.
- The **Underworld** film franchise names its vampire protagonist Selene; the name signals night and moon imagery but the character is not the Greek goddess.
- The video game **Returnal** names its protagonist Selene and her spacecraft Helios; the work deliberately fragments Greek mythic names into psychological and science-fiction symbolism.
- Cosmetics, perfumes, jewelry, lunar calendars, astrology products, and wellness brands frequently use “Selene.” Their crystal, color, and personality correspondences are usually modern commercial or occult constructions.
- **SELENE**, the Selenological and Engineering Explorer, was JAXA’s lunar orbiter launched on September 14, 2007 and renamed **Kaguya** by public poll. The acronym deliberately evokes the Moon, while the Japanese nickname invokes the *Tale of the Bamboo Cutter*. [NASA mission history](https://science.nasa.gov/mission/kaguya/) [JAXA mission site](https://www.kaguya.jaxa.jp/)

No significant modern extremist appropriation centered specifically on Selene is documented in the sources examined. Greek religious symbols can occur in nationalist discourse, but transferring that general fact to Selene without direct evidence would be speculative.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Major reference traditions

- **L. R. Farnell, *The Cults of the Greek States*** remains valuable for collecting literary and cult evidence, but its evolutionary assumptions and its tendency to seek unitary divine origins require caution. Farnell correctly resisted the automatic projection of lunar identity onto early Artemis. [Digitized volume](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/pdf/bkf200b2414273B.pdf)
- **Walter Burkert, *Greek Religion*** treats astral gods within the larger structures of Greek ritual and emphasizes the limited civic cult of Helios and Selene in much of mainland Greece.
- **Timothy Gantz, *Early Greek Myth*** is crucial for separating early evidence from later mythographic synthesis. His warning that no source makes Selene the clear author of Endymion’s sleep corrects a common modern retelling.
- **LIMC, “Selene/Luna,”** remains the standard iconographic catalogue, especially for vase painting, sculpture, coins, and Endymion sarcophagi.
- **Pauly-Wissowa, “Selene,”** supplies the foundational philological collection, while **Brill’s New Pauly** and the **Oxford Classical Dictionary** provide updated summaries.
- **Karl Kerényi** offered influential phenomenological and symbolic readings. These are reception and interpretation, not substitutes for source stratification.
- **Robert Graves, *The Greek Myths* and *The White Goddess*,** remains culturally influential but is not a reliable guide to chronology or prehistoric religion. His unitary Triple Goddess and matriarchal ritual reconstructions routinely exceed the evidence.

### Principal controversies

#### Was Selene a major prehistoric goddess?

There is no accepted Linear B attestation, named Minoan image, or demonstrable institutional continuity. Lunar symbolism is ancient; identification of every crescent, horned woman, or astronomical alignment as “Selene” is methodologically circular. The strongest conclusion is that lunar observation is prehistoric, while the named Greek goddess is securely attested only in alphabetic poetry.

#### Is the name Indo-European?

The immediate derivation from *selas*, “brightness,” is plausible, but the farther origin is obscure. Assertions that Selene is securely a Proto-Indo-European goddess go beyond what the name alone proves.

#### Was Artemis always the Moon?

No. Archaic Artemis was a goddess of hunting, wild animals, transitions, girls, childbirth, and local civic cults. Lunar identification develops gradually and never wholly eliminates Selene. Farnell and modern scholarship reject projecting the full later equation backward.

#### Was Hecate originally the dark moon?

No early source defines Hecate simply as “the dark/new moon.” Her lunar aspect is real but develops alongside older liminal, protective, chthonic, and household functions. “Dark Moon Hecate” as the necessary third member of a phase triad is mainly modern systematization.

#### Did Selene put Endymion to sleep?

Not securely in the earliest recoverable sources. Pseudo-Apollodorus has Endymion choose sleep with Zeus’s permission. Other traditions treat it as punishment. Selene’s imposition of sleep is a widespread later explanatory retelling.

#### Was the Nemean lion her child?

Only in one branch: Epimenides as transmitted by Aelian, with a related Hyginian account. Other genealogies make the lion part of the Typhon–Echidna monster family. Both traditions must remain visible.

#### Was there human sacrifice?

No Selene-specific institution of human sacrifice is documented. Confusion usually comes from merging Selene with every ritual ever attributed to Artemis, Hecate, or a foreign lunar goddess.

### State of the evidence

The corpus is uneven:

- archaic poetry survives through medieval manuscripts;
- Sappho and early mythographic material are fragmentary;
- lost Greek works are sometimes known only through Roman poets or later scholia;
- cult buildings attributed to Selene are scarce or unidentified;
- Roman sarcophagi provide far more imagery than Greek sanctuaries;
- magical papyri survive by Egyptian preservation and disproportionately illuminate specialist practice;
- the great Zeus of Olympia and its Selene relief are lost;
- museum collections in Berlin, New York, Paris, Rome, and elsewhere preserve major visual evidence.

The result is not a smooth “biography” but a record with several different centers: archaic celestial poetry, Hellenistic romance and magic, Roman Luna and funerary art, late-antique syncretism, and modern revival.

## 10. Chronology

- **Second millennium BCE:** Lunar calendrical vocabulary and possible lunar symbolism occur in Aegean cultures; no secure named Selene attestation. **[Prehistory; inference limited]**
- **c. 700 BCE:** Hesiod names Selene and gives the Hyperion–Theia genealogy. **[Earliest secure attestation]**
- **Late seventh/early sixth century BCE:** Sappho describes Selanna outshining the stars; lost/fragmentary poetry probably connects her with Endymion.
- **Sixth century BCE or later:** Homeric Hymn to Hermes gives Pallas as her father; Homeric Hymn 32 praises her chariot and names Pandia as her daughter by Zeus.
- **Fifth century BCE:** Aeschylus calls her the eye of night; early lunar associations begin attaching to Artemis and Hecate.
- **421 BCE:** Aristophanes’ *Peace* represents sacrifice to Helios and Selene as foreign rather than normal Athenian civic practice.
- **c. 390–360 BCE:** Plato offers learned wordplay about Selene’s light in the *Cratylus*.
- **Third century BCE:** Theocritus makes Selene witness to an erotic spell; Apollonius gives the developed Latmian Endymion romance and Selene’s comparison with Medea.
- **Between 292 and 219 BCE:** probable foundation range for Luna’s Aventine temple, though later tradition attributed it to Servius Tullius.
- **182 BCE:** first secure literary notice of the Aventine Luna temple; storm tears off its door.
- **146 BCE or shortly after:** Mummius dedicates Corinthian spoils at the Luna temple.
- **84 BCE:** lightning strikes the temples of Luna and Ceres.
- **29 BCE:** Virgil publishes the Pan-and-Luna white-wool episode in *Georgics* 3.
- **Late first century BCE–early first century CE:** Ovid standardizes Luna’s horns, chariot, and Endymion in Roman poetic culture.
- **First century CE:** Strabo documents Endymion’s Latmian sanctuary/tomb; Diodorus preserves a euhemeristic Selene narrative.
- **64 CE:** Aventine Luna temple destroyed in Rome’s great fire; no secure rebuilding is known.
- **Second century CE:** Pausanias records Thalamai, Elis, Olympia, Pasiphaë-Selene, and the fifty daughters; Lucian dramatizes Selene and Aphrodite.
- **Second–third centuries CE:** Endymion sarcophagi become a major Roman funerary type.
- **Early third century CE:** Aelian transmits Epimenides’ Selene–Hera–Nemean lion variant.
- **Third–fourth centuries CE:** PGM manuscripts document extensive Selene-Hecate-Artemis-Persephone-Isis syncretism and coercive lunar magic.
- **Probably second–third centuries CE, with disputed broader range:** Orphic Hymn to Selene composed and used in a ritual collection.
- **Third century CE:** Quintus Smyrnaeus makes the Horae daughters of Helios and Selene.
- **Fifth century CE:** Nonnus expands Selene in late-antique astral epic.
- **Fourth–sixth centuries CE:** Christianization, anti-sacrifice laws, temple closures, and conversion displace public pagan cult; no Selene-specific persecution is documented.
- **Twelfth century CE:** Byzantine scholar John Diaconus Galenus demonstrably knows the Orphic Hymns.
- **Late fifteenth century:** Marsilio Ficino translates the Orphic Hymns into Latin, apparently without publication.
- **1500:** first printed Greek edition of the Orphic Hymns.
- **1580s–1590s:** Lyly and Drayton remake Endymion through Cynthia/Phoebe.
- **1818:** Keats publishes *Endymion*, giving the myth a central Romantic afterlife.
- **1948:** Robert Graves publishes *The White Goddess*, influential in the modern Maiden–Mother–Crone system.
- **Late twentieth century:** reconstructionist Hellenism and eclectic Pagan movements revive direct devotional use of Selene.
- **2007:** JAXA launches the SELENE/Kaguya lunar orbiter.
- **2017:** Hellenic polytheism gains formal recognition as a known religion in Greece; this does not establish the number of Selene-specific worshippers.
- **2026:** Selene remains an object of classical scholarship, modern devotion, occult practice, art, entertainment, astronomy, and commercial lunar symbolism.
