# The Keres: Research Dossier

**Class:** Female daimones or personified powers; collectively Keres, singular Kēr. In different passages they are death, an individual’s allotted death, spirits that bring or seize the dead, battlefield scavengers, or avenging powers. “Goddesses of violent death” is a useful modern shorthand, but it is narrower and more systematic than the ancient evidence.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The Homeric epics, conventionally composed in the late eighth or early seventh century BCE from older oral material. The word **κήρ** and plural **κῆρες** occur repeatedly in the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*, but range from the abstract “death/doom” to unmistakable personification. The earliest surviving manuscripts are many centuries later; no Kēr/Keres is securely identified in Linear B. Hesiod’s *Theogony*, probably about 700 BCE, is the earliest surviving genealogy to make Kēr and the Keres children of Night. The pseudo-Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles*, probably sixth century BCE in its transmitted form, supplies their fullest archaic physical description.

**Evidentiary labels used below:** **[Early]** securely attested archaic material; **[Classical]** fifth–fourth-century BCE evidence; **[Later]** Hellenistic, Roman, or late-antique elaboration; **[Regional/variant]** a geographically or textually limited version; **[Learned]** antiquarian, philosophical, mythographical, or scholarly systematization; **[Syncretistic]** equation with another being; **[Modern reconstruction]** an interpretation proposed by modern scholarship; **[Modern invention]** a claim or practice unsupported by ancient evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

The Keres are not characters with a continuous biography. They are a fluctuating poetic and religious category built around **kēr**, an ancient Greek word for destructive doom, death, or the mode of death allotted to an individual. In Homer, a warrior can avoid a kēr temporarily, encounter “two Keres” or possible destinies, be carried off by Keres, or have his Kēr weighed in Zeus’s scales. Only occasionally does a Kēr appear as a fully visible battlefield being. Hesiod turns the semantic field into genealogy: Night bears singular Kēr and later “the Keres and Moirai.” The *Shield of Heracles* transforms the abstraction into hideous female corpse-fighters with claws, white teeth, and bloodied clothing.

It is therefore unsafe to assume that every occurrence denotes the same class of winged female demon. Ancient Greek grammar can personify an abstraction without establishing a separately worshipped deity, while poetry can move between common noun and proper name within one episode.

### Names and forms

- **κήρ**, genitive **κηρός**, feminine: *kēr*, conventionally “death,” “doom,” “bane,” “destructive fate,” or a personified Death-spirit.
- **κῆρες**, plural: *Kēres*, Latinized **Keres**.
- Epic formulae include **κὴρ θανάτοιο / κῆρες θανάτοιο**, “doom(s) of death.” The genitive does not necessarily identify a named species; it may intensify or specify the sort of doom.
- **ὀλοὴ κήρ**, “destructive” or “baneful Kēr,” in the *Iliad*.
- **μέλαινα κήρ**, “black Kēr,” a poetic description of death rather than a securely independent cult title.
- **διχθάδιαι κῆρες**, Achilles’ “twofold Keres,” meaning his two alternative destinies in *Iliad* 9.
- **Kerostasia**, modern scholarly transliteration of Greek **κηροστασία**, “weighing of Keres/dooms.” The ancient title *Psychostasia*, “weighing of souls,” belonged to a lost Aeschylean play; the equivalence of kēr and psychē is contextual, not universal.
- Latin mythography sometimes renders the relevant Hesiodic being as **Fatum**, **Letum**, **Mors**, or **Tenebrae**. These are translations and reorganizations, not proof that Roman religion possessed a cult of “the Keres” under one fixed Latin name.

### Etymology

**[Early]** Greek usage establishes the semantic range “harm, destruction, death, doom,” but not a transparent derivation.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Older Indo-European etymologies connect κήρ with a root reconstructed as *\*ker-* or *\*ḱerh₂-* concerning destruction, cutting, wasting, or collapse. The phonology and the relation to Greek **κεραΐζω**, “ravage, slay,” remain disputed. Robert Beekes regarded the proposed inherited derivation as problematic, particularly the shape of the long-vowel stem. No secure connection exists with Greek **καρδία** (“heart”), the personal name “Keri,” or English “care.”

### Classifications through time

- **Homeric epic:** doom, allotment, death-agent, occasionally a visible battlefield personification.
- **Hesiodic genealogy:** daughters of Nyx and sisters or near-equivalents of the Moirai; punitive female powers.
- **Archaic and classical art:** a labeled taloned woman in the Eteocles–Polyneices scene; elsewhere miniature eidōla in weighing scenes or unidentified winged grotesques that scholars have interpreted as Keres.
- **Tragedy:** destructive and avenging powers, sometimes rhetorically identified with the Erinyes.
- **Later poetry:** a host of supernatural battlefield beings operating beside Thanatos, Moros, Eris, Enyo, and Kydoimos.
- **Modern handbooks:** usually “female spirits of violent death.” This formula largely derives from combining Homer’s battlefield passages with the *Shield*; the sources do not consistently assign all peaceful deaths to Thanatos and all violent deaths to Keres.

There are no securely attested individual names for members of the collective.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Homeric epics

The *Iliad* and *Odyssey* reached monumental written form around the late eighth or early seventh century BCE, although their diction preserves older oral layers. The textual witnesses are much later: papyri begin in the Hellenistic period, while complete medieval codices are later still.

Important occurrences include:

- **Iliad 2.302, 2.834; 3.32, 3.454; 9.411; 12.326, 12.402; 13.283; 15.287; 16.47, 16.687; 18.115; 21.548; 22.202–213; 23.78.** The meanings include death, possible destinies, personified agents, and a warrior’s allotted end.
- **Iliad 9.410–416:** Achilles says that two Keres carry him toward death. Remaining at Troy means a short life and imperishable fame; returning home means long life without that glory. **[Early]** These are alternative life-courses, not two monsters waiting to eat him.
- **Iliad 12.326–328:** Sarpedon observes that innumerable Keres of death surround mortals. The plurality naturally accommodates innumerable manners and occasions of dying.
- **Iliad 18.535–540:** On Achilles’ shield, Eris, Kydoimos, and a destructive Kēr mingle in battle. Kēr grips a wounded man, another unwounded man, and drags a corpse by the feet; her garment is red with human blood. **[Early]** This is Homer’s clearest full personification.
- **Iliad 22.208–213:** Zeus suspends his golden scales and places in them two **κῆρε τανηλεγέος θανάτοιο**, “Keres/dooms of death that lays men low,” for Achilles and Hector. Hector’s sinks toward Hades. The text weighs death-lots, not moral worth or postmortem souls. [Primary text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Aabo%3Atlg%2C0012%2C001%3A22)
- **Iliad 23.78:** Patroclus’ ghost says the Kēr allotted at birth has engulfed him. Here Kēr approaches personal destiny or lifespan.
- **Odyssey 8.70–73:** Zeus weighs the Keres of the Achaeans and Trojans during fighting, an analogue to *Iliad* 22.
- **Odyssey 11.171, 11.398:** “What Kēr of death subdued you?” means “what death killed you?” but preserves agent-like grammar.
- **Odyssey 14.207 and other formulae:** Keres carry people to Hades.

Homer therefore does **not** give one systematic doctrine. The lexical continuum itself is the earliest evidence.

### Hesiod

#### *Theogony*, approximately 700 BCE

At lines 211–225 Night produces a brood of destructive and liminal powers. The textual construction is complicated:

1. Nyx bears **Moros, black Kēr, Thanatos, Hypnos, and the Oneiroi**.
2. Later in the catalogue she bears **the Moirai and the pitiless Keres**, followed by Clotho, Lachesis, and Atropos.

The syntax leaves room for disagreement over whether the named Fates appositionally define both Moirai and Keres or only the Moirai. The following punitive clause—these powers pursue offenses of gods and humans until they exact punishment—has also been suspected of interpolation by some editors. **[Early, textually disputed]** Hesiod indisputably places Kēr/Keres among Night’s offspring; he does not clearly produce the neat modern hierarchy “Moirai determine death, Keres execute it.”

#### *Works and Days*, approximately 700 BCE

Lines 90–104 say that before Pandora’s jar was opened humanity lacked evils, hard toil, and diseases; afterward innumerable evils wander silently among humans. The Keres are not named. **[Modern reconstruction]** Identifying the jar’s released evils as Keres is Jane Harrison’s and later interpreters’ conceptual linkage, not an explicit Hesiodic statement.

#### *Shield of Heracles*, probably sixth century BCE

The poem circulated under Hesiod’s name but is generally treated as post-Hesiodic. Its shield scene, lines approximately 248–257, shows dark Keres gnashing white teeth, glaring, clawing at fallen men, drinking blood, and fighting among themselves over corpses. Once one seizes a newly wounded or fallen man, she throws her great claws around him; his soul descends to Hades while she drags or despoils his body. Their clothing is bloody. [Text and translation](https://www.theoi.com/Text/HesiodShield.html)

**[Later archaic elaboration]** This is the primary source for the modern image of the Keres as ravenous battlefield demons. Features such as fangs, claws, blood-drinking, and competition over corpses should not be retrojected automatically into every Homeric kēr.

### Archaic lyric

- **Mimnermus fr. 2**, seventh century BCE, preserved by Stobaeus: two dark Keres stand beside mortals, one bringing grievous old age and one death. **[Early]** Kēr here can personify alternative human ends, not violent battlefield death alone.
- Pindar’s poetry frequently personifies fate and death, but no surviving passage creates an independent Keres mythology comparable to the *Shield*. Claims that Pindar supplied their standard genealogy are unestablished.

### Classical tragedy

- **Aeschylus, *Seven Against Thebes* 1054–1060**, produced 467 BCE: the chorus addresses the Erinyes as Keres who have destroyed the house of Oedipus. **[Classical; syncretistic/rhetorical identification]**
- **Aeschylus, lost *Psychostasia***, fifth century BCE, known through Plutarch and Pollux: Zeus weighs the destinies or souls of Achilles and Memnon while Thetis and Eos plead for their sons. Whether the scale-pans contained Keres, psychai, or miniature images of the warriors depends on reconstruction.
- **Aeschylus, *Thracian Women* fr. 41**, preserved in a scholion to Sophocles’ *Ajax*: a daimon reveals the vulnerable point at which Ajax can be killed. Identifying this being as a Kēr is plausible but not certain.
- **Sophocles, *Oedipus Tyrannus* 463–482**, approximately 429 BCE: Apollo’s fiery lightning pursues the unknown murderer, accompanied by “dread, unerring Keres.” **[Classical]**
- **Euripides, *Electra* 1241–1250**, approximately 420–410 BCE: the Dioscuri refer to destructive fate/Keres in the catastrophe. The language does not establish a separate cult or episode centered on them.
- **Euripides, *Heracles*:** kēr-language describes lethal doom; translations that capitalize every occurrence can exaggerate personification.

### Hellenistic and Roman-period literature

- **Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 4.1665–1669**, third century BCE: a destructive Kēr or doom operates in the violent conclusion of the voyage. The usage remains poetic.
- **Cicero, *De natura deorum* 3.17**, 45 BCE: in a reductio against promiscuous divinization, Cicero lists **Tenebrae** among the children of Erebus and Night alongside Fate, Death, the Parcae, Dreams, and other abstractions. **[Learned Roman systematization]** “Tenebrae = Keres” is a translator’s alignment, not a cultic Roman equation.
- **Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca***, first or second century CE: preserves extensive Greek divine genealogy but gives no independent Keres cycle.
- **Pseudo-Hyginus, *Fabulae*, Preface**, probably first–second century CE in transmitted form: Night and Erebus produce Fate, Old Age, Death, Letum/Destruction, Sleep, Dreams, Nemesis, the Parcae, and others. **[Learned Latin genealogy]** The badly transmitted list reorganizes Hesiod; it does not consistently distinguish singular Kēr from Keres.
- **Pausanias 5.19.4**, second century CE: describes the archaic Chest of Cypselus at Olympia. Behind the kneeling Polyneices stood a woman with beastlike teeth and curved talons, explicitly labeled **Kēr**. Pausanias interprets her as showing that Polyneices has been taken by fate and that Eteocles deserved his end. The chest itself is lost. [Pausanias source index](https://www.theoi.com/Text/Pausanias5B.html)
- **Philostratus the Younger, *Imagines***, third century CE: continues literary descriptions of personified battle-death.
- **Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica***, probably third or fourth century CE: greatly expands their battlefield activity. Keres drive Penthesilea toward battle, direct missiles, accompany Thanatos and Kydoimos, and stand beside Achilles and Memnon as bright and dark destinies. **[Late epic elaboration]**
- **Orphic Hymn 12/13 to Heracles**, probably composed between the second century BCE and second century CE: asks Heracles to drive away evil Keres with his club and poisoned arrows and to bring relief from disease. **[Later ritual-poetic evidence]** This associates them with illness and apotropaic expulsion, but it is not evidence for a temple or priesthood of the Keres.
- Other Orphic and magical texts use kēr-language for banes and diseases. No securely identified spell in the major Greek Magical Papyri establishes a distinct, routinely invoked cult of the collective Keres.

### Important absences

The surviving Homeric Hymns, Diodorus, the main narrative of Apollodorus, and Pausanias’s catalogue of operating sanctuaries supply no independent Keres myth-cycle or institutional cult. Absence in these large corpora is significant, although it cannot prove that no local rite ever existed.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and actions

1. **Death as allotted outcome.** In Homer, each person’s kēr can be the death fixed or anticipated for that individual.
2. **Alternative destiny.** Achilles’ two Keres are mutually exclusive futures.
3. **Battlefield death.** Homer’s shield scene and the pseudo-Hesiodic *Shield* make battle their most vivid environment.
4. **Removal of the dead.** Keres “carry” mortals to Hades or seize fallen bodies.
5. **Corpse predation.** The *Shield* says they struggle over the fallen and drink blood.
6. **Familial and moral retribution.** Hesiod’s catalogue and Aeschylean tragedy bring them close to the Erinyes.
7. **Disease or contaminating bane.** Later Orphic texts and antiquarian interpretation extend kēr to sicknesses and harmful spirits.
8. **The moment or manner of death rather than afterlife judgment.** Nothing in the early corpus makes them judges of souls.

### Powers and limitations

Homeric Keres are inevitable in the aggregate but avoidable temporarily. A warrior can flee a Kēr; a god can avert or hasten a death; Achilles can choose between two courses. Zeus’s scales reveal or enact which death-lot prevails. They therefore do not possess unlimited independent sovereignty over life.

The claim that “Keres cannot kill; they only wait and feed” is a **modern harmonization**. Some passages make them agents that subdue, seize, direct, or destroy; others make them only the form or allotment of death. The sources never formulate the modern rule.

### Literary appearance

The fullest archaic description gives them:

- female grammatical and visual form;
- dark or black coloration;
- glaring eyes;
- white, gnashing teeth;
- great claws or talons;
- garments red with blood;
- a thirst for blood;
- competitive struggle over corpses.

Wings are not specified in the major epic descriptions. Winged Keres are partly an iconographic and modern scholarly reconstruction.

### Material images

#### Chest of Cypselus

**[Archaic image known from a Roman description]** The cedar Chest of Cypselus, traditionally dated to the early sixth century BCE and dedicated at Olympia, showed Eteocles and Polyneices fighting. A labeled Kēr stood behind Polyneices with beastlike teeth and taloned nails. Because the figure was inscribed, this is the safest iconographic identification. The object is lost; Pausanias’s second-century CE description is the surviving evidence.

#### Kerostasia and psychostasia vases

Late archaic black-figure vases show Hermes or Zeus holding scales with miniature human figures in the pans during the Achilles–Memnon contest. The miniature figures may be described as **eidōla**, **psychai**, or embodied death-lots. **[Contested iconography]** Calling each miniature a Kēr is defensible in light of Homeric weighing scenes, but the images are usually not labeled. The visual tradition shifts the weighed entities toward “lives” or “souls.”

#### Heracles and the winged grotesque

Jane Harrison illustrated a pelike found at Thisbe, then in Berlin, on which Heracles attacks a tiny, shriveled winged figure. She associated it with the Orphic prayer that Heracles drive away Keres and called the hero **Keramyntes**, “averter of Keres.” Other scholars have identified the figure as **Hepiales/Ephialtes**, a nightmare or fever demon. **[Contested]** Without an inscription, “Heracles fighting a Ker” is not secure.

#### Winged eidōla on funerary vessels

Small winged figures occur around graves and burial jars on Attic lekythoi. Harrison identified them with Keres and argued that Keres originally meant the souls of the dead. **[Modern reconstruction, not secure identification]** The images can be called eidōla or psychai without assuming that all are Keres.

### Symbols and unattested attributes

Secure attributes are teeth, claws, bloodied dress, battlefield corpses, and—through Kerostasia—the scale. No ancient evidence assigns the Keres a sacred animal, plant, color-calendar, number, weekday, planet, zodiacal sign, or permanent weapon. Vultures are an apt modern comparison, not their attested sacred animals. Black and blood-red are descriptive colors, not cult colors.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other beings

### Genealogical versions

| Source | Parentage or relation | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|
| Hesiod, *Theogony* 211–225, c. 700 BCE | Nyx bears singular black Kēr and later the Keres; no father stated | **[Early]** |

They have no named consorts, children, servants, mounts, or individually named members in securely surviving ancient evidence.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

The following list covers discrete interactions or direct relations in the extant corpus, excluding mere membership in an undifferentiated list.

- **Nyx:** Mother of singular Kēr and collective Keres. Hesiod, *Theogony* 211–225, c. 700 BCE. **[Early]**
- **Zeus:** Places two death-Keres in his golden scales for Achilles and Hector; Hector’s lot sinks. *Iliad* 22.208–213, late eighth/early seventh century BCE. **[Early]**
- **Zeus:** Weighs opposing Keres during the Achaean–Trojan battle. *Odyssey* 8.70–73, late eighth/early seventh century BCE. **[Early]**
- **Zeus:** In the lost *Psychostasia*, weighs the destinies/lives of Achilles and Memnon while their mothers plead. Aeschylus, fifth century BCE, reconstructed from Plutarch and Pollux. **[Classical, fragmentary]**
- **Eris:** Stands and acts with Kēr amid the battle on Achilles’ shield. *Iliad* 18.535–540. **[Early]**
- **Kydoimos:** Mingles with Eris and Kēr in the same shield battle. *Iliad* 18.535–540. **[Early]**
- **Moirai:** Listed with the Keres among Nyx’s daughters and possibly share their punitive function. Hesiod, *Theogony* 217–222. **[Early, syntactically disputed]**
- **Thanatos:** Sibling of Kēr/Keres in Hesiod; later marches beside them on the battlefield. Hesiod, *Theogony* 211–225; Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica* 1.306–310. **[Early genealogy; late joint action]**
- **Moros:** Sibling in Hesiod; rejoices with Keres and Eris in battle in Quintus Smyrnaeus 8.324–328. **[Early genealogy; late joint action]**
- **Hypnos:** Sibling in Hesiod’s Night genealogy; no surviving narrative interaction. *Theogony* 211–225. **[Early genealogy only]**
- **Oneiroi:** Siblings in Hesiod’s Night genealogy; no surviving narrative interaction. *Theogony* 211–225. **[Early genealogy only]**
- **Nemesis:** Sibling within Nyx’s brood; no surviving episode together. *Theogony* 223. **[Early genealogy only]**
- **Geras:** Sibling or cognate Night-born bane; Mimnermus places a Kēr of old age beside a Kēr of death. Mimnermus fr. 2, seventh century BCE. **[Early poetic association]**
- **Erinyes:** The chorus directly calls the Erinyes Keres who uproot Oedipus’s house. Aeschylus, *Seven Against Thebes* 1054–1060, 467 BCE. **[Classical syncretistic/rhetorical identification]**
- **Apollo:** Pursues Oedipus’s unidentified murderer with fiery force while dread Keres accompany him. Sophocles, *Oedipus Tyrannus* 463–482, c. 429 BCE. **[Classical]**
- **Hermes:** Holds the scales containing miniature lives, souls, or Keres in late archaic Kerostasia imagery. **[Archaic iconography, identification contested]**
- **Thetis:** Pleads for Achilles beside the divine scales in Aeschylus’s lost *Psychostasia*. The weighed entities may be psychai rather than independent Keres. **[Classical, fragmentary]**
- **Eos:** Pleads for Memnon in the same lost play. **[Classical, fragmentary]**
- **Heracles:** Is invoked to drive baleful Keres away with club and poisoned arrows. *Orphic Hymn to Heracles*, Hellenistic or Roman Imperial date. **[Later ritual-poetic interaction]**
- **Enyo:** Appears in the same battlefield tableau as the Keres in Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica* 5.25–35. **[Late epic elaboration]**
- **Hysminai:** Advance beside the Keres in that same shield description. Quintus Smyrnaeus 5.25–35. **[Late epic elaboration]**
- **Hades:** Keres carry or accompany mortals to Hades’ house, but no surviving source records a conversation, command, or personal meeting between Hades and the Keres. Homeric formulae, especially *Odyssey* 14.207. **[Early destination, not a personal interaction]**

No securely attested interaction survives with Athena, Ares, Persephone, Hecate, Dionysus, Artemis, Aphrodite, Poseidon, Hera, Demeter, or Charon. Modern charts that assign the Keres to one of these gods’ retinues are inferential.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The two Keres of Achilles

In *Iliad* 9 Achilles explains that Thetis has told him of two possible Keres. If he remains at Troy, he will die young but win undying fame; if he returns home, his glory will perish but his life will be long.

This is not a story in which two sisters physically compete. It dramatizes mutually exclusive destiny and heroic choice. The Keres “bear” Achilles toward different endings. **[Early]** Later mythographers commonly retell the choice as a prophecy of fate, suppressing the word’s personified ambiguity.

### Zeus weighs Achilles and Hector

As Achilles chases Hector around Troy, Zeus raises golden scales and places in them two death-dooms. Hector’s side sinks toward Hades; Apollo then leaves him, Athena aids Achilles, and Hector dies.

The scales do not judge virtue. Nor is Achilles’ own immediate death weighed: the lots concern which combatant’s death has arrived in that encounter. **[Early]** Later visual and dramatic traditions transferred the motif to Achilles and Memnon.

### The Kēr on Achilles’ shield

The divine smith Hephaestus makes Achilles’ shield, upon which battle is represented. Eris, Kydoimos, and destructive Kēr move among combatants. Kēr holds a living wounded man, an unharmed man, and a corpse dragged by its feet, while her clothing is bloody.

Because this is an image described inside a poem, it is simultaneously mythic iconography and metapoetic personification. It anticipates the more grotesque *Shield of Heracles* scene but does not mention blood-drinking or wings.

### The battlefield feast in the *Shield of Heracles*

The pseudo-Hesiodic shield carries multiple dark Keres fighting over fallen men. They expose teeth, extend claws, drink blood, and refuse to release a body until its flesh and blood are exhausted. **[Later archaic elaboration]** This passage is the source most responsible for the popular “corpse-eating female demons” image.

The poem imitates and intensifies Homer’s shield-description. Its monsters are more numerous and physically grotesque than Homer’s singular Kēr.

### The deaths of Eteocles and Polyneices

The brothers kill one another before Thebes. On the Chest of Cypselus, a labeled Kēr stands behind the fallen Polyneices. Pausanias interprets her as his fate and reads the scene morally in Eteocles’ favor. Aeschylus later calls the Erinyes/Keres destroyers of the whole Oedipal house.

These are related but not identical versions:

- **Chest:** one labeled death-power attached to Polyneices.
- **Aeschylus:** familial curses and Erinyes described as Keres.
- **Pausanias:** Roman-period antiquarian interpretation of an archaic image.

### Achilles and Memnon: Psychostasia

Aeschylus’s lost play apparently staged Zeus weighing the contestants’ lives, with Thetis and Eos on opposite sides. Vase paintings show a deity holding scales with miniature figures. Quintus Smyrnaeus later makes two Keres stand beside the warriors, one dark for doomed Memnon and one bright for victorious Achilles.

**Development:** Homeric weighing of Hector and Achilles → archaic visual weighing of Achilles and Memnon → Aeschylean drama → late epic’s bright and dark Keres. This is one of the clearest cases in which “death-lot,” “life,” “soul,” and “spirit” slide into one another without becoming permanently synonymous.

### Keres as avengers

Hesiod’s genealogy says the relevant daughters of Night pursue offenses by gods and humans. Aeschylus identifies Erinyes as Keres destroying a family. Sophocles lets Keres accompany Apollo in pursuit of Laius’s murderer.

**[Classical development]** The Keres thus overlap with punishment and blood-guilt, but they never replace the Erinyes as a stable, individually named avenging triad.

### The Anthesteria expulsion

The famous formula is transmitted in two forms:

- **θύραζε Κῆρες, οὐκέτ’ Ἀνθεστήρια** — “Out, Keres; Anthesteria is over.”
- **θύραζε Κᾶρες, οὐκέτ’ Ἀνθεστήρια** — “Out, Carians; Anthesteria is over.”

Zenobius’s stronger manuscript tradition gives **Kares**, “Carians,” with an aetiology that Carians or Carian slaves were admitted to the festival and dismissed afterward. Photius and the *Suda* transmit both forms. The tradition may go back through Didymus to an earlier Hellenistic collector.

Jane Harrison made **Keres** central to her reconstruction: during Anthesteria the dead emerged, wandered through Athens, and were expelled at the close; doors were smeared with pitch and participants chewed buckthorn against dangerous spirits. She linked small winged eidōla on funerary vases to these Keres.

Herbert Jennings Rose objected in 1948 that:

- “Keres = ghosts” would be unparalleled in surviving literary usage;
- **Kares** has stronger early manuscript support;
- the ghost interpretation may rest on correction of the text and circular comparison with Roman Lemuria.

The modern *Oxford Classical Dictionary* likewise treats the ghost-festival inference as problematic. Other scholars continue to defend **Keres**, noting the festival’s chthonic features and rites for Hermes Chthonios. The dispute cannot presently be closed.

Therefore:

- The proverb itself is **[Late-attested antiquarian evidence for an older saying]**.
- Its wording is **[Textually contested]**.
- “The Athenians expelled the Keres/ghosts annually” is **not securely established fact**.
- Even if Κῆρες is original, the referent could be dangerous spirits rather than ordinary ancestral souls.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Institutional cult

No temple, sanctuary, priesthood, festival, civic calendar entry, oracle, treasury, or securely identified altar dedicated specifically to “the Keres” is presently documented. Pausanias records their image but no sanctuary. Farnell’s surveys of Greek state cult do not reveal an institutional Keres cult.

This absence fits their literary character as dreaded powers more often averted than honored, but that is an interpretation, not proof that no household or local observance ever addressed them.

### Anthesteria

If the proverb read Κῆρες, the closing expulsion would be the most important ritual evidence. Because Κᾶρες is also transmitted, it cannot securely support a cult of Keres. The Chytroi included offerings associated with Hermes Chthonios and the dead, but no contemporary inscription dedicates them to Keres.

Harrison’s phrase that the Keres were “worshipped” at Anthesteria was already qualified by her own acknowledgement that “worship” was too precise. At most, her reconstruction proposes apotropaic management of dangerous beings.

### Orphic apotropaism

The *Orphic Hymn to Heracles* petitions Heracles to expel harmful Keres and diseases. This is genuine ritual poetry but belongs to a late hymn collection whose exact community and performance setting are uncertain. It supports **aversion**, not a sacrifice offered to the Keres themselves.

### Magical practice

Kēr could name a destructive force or disease in late magical and religious language. Yet the Greek Magical Papyri do not disclose a stable Keres cult, standard invocation, sigil, day, incense, or offering recipe. Modern online lists assigning such correspondences are **[Modern inventions]** unless tied to an independently cited ancient text.

### Sacrifice and controversial practices

No primary evidence connects the Keres with:

- human sacrifice;
- temple prostitution;
- dedicated animal sacrifice;
- military sacrificial killing;
- possession cults;
- an organized priesthood;
- ritual blood-drinking by worshippers.

Claims that dangerous chthonic powers in general received wholly burned offerings cannot be transferred automatically to the Keres. Harrison’s broad comparison of apotropaic holocausts is comparative reconstruction, not a Keres-specific dossier of sacrifice.

---

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the epics

No Mycenaean Linear B form securely naming Kēr or the Keres has been identified. A prehistoric Aegean “Keres cult,” Minoan death-goddess, or Indo-European battlefield-demon prototype is therefore hypothetical.

### Late eighth–early seventh century BCE: Homeric fluidity

Kēr is simultaneously vocabulary and being: the death that awaits a person, a possible destiny, an agent carrying people to Hades, and—on Achilles’ shield—a blood-covered female figure. This fluidity is probably older than any single systematic mythology.

### Around 700 BCE: Hesiodic genealogy

Hesiod incorporates Kēr/Keres into the Night genealogy and places them near Moros, Thanatos, Hypnos, the Moirai, Nemesis, and other personifications. Genealogy turns poetic concepts into a divine family without eliminating their abstract meaning.

### Sixth century BCE: visual and poetic embodiment

The *Shield of Heracles* gives corporeal, horrific detail. The Chest of Cypselus reportedly labels a taloned female figure Kēr. Kerostasia imagery translates death-lots into miniature bodies or souls in scales.

This is the nearest thing to a “peak” in visual individuation, not evidence of peak worship.

### Fifth century BCE: tragic moralization

Aeschylus and Sophocles connect Keres with inherited ruin, murder, Erinyes, and Apollo’s pursuit of pollution. Their domain broadens rhetorically from battlefield death toward retributive doom.

### Hellenistic period

Alexandrian scholarship collected proverbial and epic material; Apollonius continued poetic kēr language. The lost and fragmentary state of much literature prevents a complete account. No clear institutional cult emerges.

### Roman Imperial period

Mythographers reorganized the Night genealogy in Latin. Pausanias preserved the most important description of labeled Kēr iconography. Orphic hymns made the Keres apotropaically relevant as evils Heracles could repel. Quintus Smyrnaeus expanded them into an active battlefield host with colored individual death-lots.

### Late antiquity and Christianization

There is no documented imperial decree specifically suppressing a Keres cult, probably because no such institution is known. Christian polemic and changing ritual systems displaced the broader polytheistic environment in which their imagery was intelligible. Lexicographers and scholiasts preserved the word and the Anthesteria proverb.

### Byzantine and early-modern transmission

The Keres survived chiefly in manuscripts of Homer, Hesiod, tragedy, lexica, and mythographic handbooks. Renaissance and early-modern mythographers translated kēr inconsistently as Fate, Death, Doom, or Fury. No major Renaissance occult current comparable to those surrounding Hecate, Hermes, or the planetary gods centered on the Keres.

### Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries

Comparative religion and “survival” theories transformed the scholarly figure. Harrison’s *Prolegomena to the Study of Greek Religion* (1903; revised 1922) proposed a developmental sequence:

> vague dangerous sprite or ghost → disease-spirit → death-spirit → Fate, Erinys, Gorgon, Siren, and related specialized forms.

This was enormously influential but often exceeded the evidence. Harrison’s “primitive” psychology, evolutionary vocabulary, and confidence that vase eidōla were Keres reflect her era’s anthropology.

### Present

Keres circulate widely in mythology reference works, fantasy art, role-playing material, comics, and modern Hellenic-polytheist discussion. Contemporary individuals may address or “work with” them, but no continuous cult from antiquity has been demonstrated. Modern devotional offerings and correspondences are new religious constructions.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship as reception

Jane Harrison did more than summarize ancient passages: she created the dominant modern narrative that Keres began as the ghosts of the dead, swarmed at Anthesteria, caused disease like invisible “bacilli,” and differentiated into specialized demons. Her work remains historically important and often perceptive about semantic ambiguity, but its reconstructions must not be mistaken for ancient testimony. [Full text of Harrison](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/78248/78248-h/78248-h.htm)

Karl Kerényi inherited aspects of the ritualist and psychological tradition, particularly the importance of death and renewal at Anthesteria. Robert Graves’s mythographic syntheses similarly combine sources and speculative interpretation; neither should be cited as primary evidence.

### Occultism and esotericism

No substantial Renaissance Hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or canonical modern-grimoire tradition devoted specifically to the Keres has been located. Modern magical websites sometimes assign them offerings, planetary correspondences, sigils, or a fixed infernal hierarchy. These are **[Modern inventions]** unless their author explicitly presents them as new practice.

The Orphic Hymn to Heracles is occasionally repurposed as a Keres-banishing rite. That is a modern ritual adaptation of an ancient apotropaic text, not revival of a documented Keres liturgy.

### Neopaganism

Modern Hellenic polytheists discuss whether dangerous daimones should be propitiated, avoided, or treated only as literary personifications. Online testimony shows individual interest in praying or offering to them, but no reliable census establishes the scale of worship. Claims of uninterrupted transmission are unsupported.

### Literature, comics, and games

Modern fantasy tends to select the *Shield of Heracles* version: winged, blood-hungry female battlefield demons. DC-related reference databases, fantasy bestiaries, and role-playing settings commonly turn the indefinite collective into a biological “species” with standardized powers. That taxonomic precision is modern.

Rick Riordan’s mythology-derived fiction and its surrounding reference culture helped make numerous minor Greek daimones familiar to young readers, though details are adapted for narrative. Modern games and Greek-myth franchises often conflate Keres with harpies, Furies, or generic winged monsters. Such representations are reception, not evidence for ancient morphology.

### Political appropriation

No prominent, sustained nationalist or extremist movement centered specifically on the Keres was found. Classical martial imagery can be appropriated generally, but it would be irresponsible to attribute such use to the Keres without a documented group, text, or symbol.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Personification or lexical abstraction?

The fundamental debate is grammatical and historical. Does a passage refer to:

1. ordinary death;
2. an allotted manner of death;
3. an anthropomorphic goddess;
4. one of a class of daimones?

The correct answer changes passage by passage. Capitalization in English translations can prejudge the issue. Homer’s *Iliad* 18 Kēr is plainly personified; Achilles’ “two Keres” are more naturally alternative destinies; many formulaic occurrences remain ambiguous.

### Keres and Moirai

Hesiod puts them together, and translations sometimes render both “Fates.” Yet Homeric kēr emphasizes destructive outcome, while moira is a “portion” or allotment with a wider semantic range. Later systematization that makes the Moirai decision-makers and Keres subordinate executioners is neat but not stated as doctrine in an early source.

### Keres and Thanatos

Modern guides often divide “peaceful death” from “violent death.” Ancient usage is less orderly. Thanatos means death generally and can be violent; kēr can denote old age, disease, destiny, or battlefield slaughter. The opposition is typological, not an ancient theological law.

### Keres and Erinyes

Aeschylus can call Erinyes Keres because both express destructive, pursuing doom. This does not establish universal identity. The Erinyes possess named members, sustained myths, cult titles, and cult sites; the Keres do not.

### Keres and souls

Harrison equated the small winged dead on funerary vases with Keres. Critics note that the ordinary labels are eidōla or psychai and that the only literary passage making Keres mean “ghosts” would be the contested Anthesteria proverb. The art shows conceptual permeability, not a secure lexical identity.

### Κῆρες or Κᾶρες?

This is the dossier’s most consequential textual dispute. H. J. Rose argued that **Kares/Carians** had the better tradition and that the ghost interpretation was late or conjectural. Others defend **Keres**, citing the festival’s chthonic rites and the ease with which unfamiliar ritual language could be rationalized as an ethnic term. Both readings are anciently transmitted; neither may be silently removed.

### Iconographic attribution

Only the labeled Kēr on the Chest of Cypselus is secure. Winged grotesques, miniature scale-figures, and grave eidōla are interpretive identifications. LIMC-style iconographic cataloguing is essential precisely because literary resemblance alone does not name an image.

### Etymology and origin

Indo-European derivations remain uncertain. “Pre-Greek goddess,” “Minoan death demons,” and Near Eastern import theories lack direct naming evidence. Comparisons with Mesopotamian battlefield demons, Egyptian soul-weighing, Roman Lemures, Valkyries, or carrion birds may illuminate motifs but do not establish descent.

### State of the evidence

The record is unusually dependent on poetic diction:

- no Mycenaean attestation;
- no securely dedicated temple or inscription;
- one lost but reportedly labeled archaic image;
- a handful of surviving or described vases with disputed identification;
- extensive epic usage;
- late lexicographical and mythographic preservation.

The primary interpretive risk is converting a flexible Greek noun into a fixed modern monster species.
