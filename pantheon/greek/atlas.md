# Atlas: Research Dossier

**Class:** A male Titan (*Τιτάν*), hence a divine being of the pre-Olympian generation; in later rationalizing accounts also a primordial astronomer or North-African king, and in Ovid a giant transformed into a mountain.  
**Earliest secure attestation:** Homer, *Odyssey* 1.52–54, conventionally composed in the late eighth or early seventh century BCE: Atlas is the father of Calypso, knows “the depths of every sea,” and himself holds or guards the great pillars separating earth and heaven. Hesiod’s *Theogony* 507–520, approximately contemporary and perhaps slightly later, supplies the earliest genealogy and says that Atlas directly supports the broad sky at earth’s western limits.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Atlas is Greek **Ἄτλας**, genitive **Ἄτλαντος**, Latin **Atlas**. The usual English pronunciation and the adjective *Atlantean* conceal the Greek consonantal stem *Atlant-* found in **Ἀτλαντίς**, “daughter/island of Atlas,” **Ἀτλαντικός**, “of Atlas,” and **Ἀτλαντίδες**, “daughters of Atlas.” Latin and modern European forms include *Atlante* in Italian and Spanish and *Atlas/Atlante* in French and German usage.

### Etymology

The name has no universally accepted etymology.

- **Modern Indo-European reconstruction:** It has traditionally been connected with Greek **τλῆναι**, “to endure, bear,” ultimately Proto-Indo-European *telh₂-*, sometimes with an initial copulative or intensive *a-*. On this analysis Atlas is approximately “the bearer” or “the enduring one.”
- **Modern substrate reconstruction:** R. S. P. Beekes regarded the *-ant-* stem and the myth’s archaic, western setting as compatible with a Pre-Greek name. This is possible but not demonstrable from any surviving pre-Greek inscription.
- **Popular Amazigh derivation:** Comparisons with Amazigh *adrar*, “mountain,” circulate widely. Similarity is not proof of descent, and no documented chain shows Greek *Atlas* borrowed from *adrar*. The ancient name was already embedded in Greek poetry before the surviving detailed Greek descriptions of the North-African mountain.
- **Ancient interpretation:** Diodorus Siculus and later euhemerists made Atlas a learned king whose astronomical knowledge explained the claim that he “carried heaven.” This is an interpretation of an existing name and myth, not historical etymology.

No securely attested ancient cult epithet comparable to *Olympios*, *Soter*, or *Polieus* is known for Atlas. “Titan of endurance,” “god of astronomy,” and “god of strength” are modern classificatory summaries, not established Greek cult titles.

### Identifications and distinctions

Atlas was geographically associated with the far west, the Hesperides, Libya, and the mountain called Atlas. Ancient and early-modern authors consequently blurred several figures:

1. Hesiod’s divine sky-bearer.
2. Herodotus’s North-African mountain called Atlas.
3. Diodorus’s astronomer-king Atlas.
4. Plato’s Atlas, eldest son of Poseidon and Cleito and king of Atlantis.
5. A second Atlas whom some genealogists made the father of the Italian king Italus.
6. Ovid’s immense western king transformed into the mountain.

These are historically related names and reinterpretations, not securely one original personality. Plato’s Atlantean king is expressly a son of Poseidon, not Iapetus, and should not be silently inserted into the Titan’s genealogy.

Comparisons with Egyptian Shu, who separates sky-goddess Nut from earth-god Geb, or with Near-Eastern cosmic supporters are **modern comparative hypotheses**. Structural resemblance is real, but no ancient bilingual text equates Atlas with Shu. Philo of Byblos’s Phoenician History, preserved through Eusebius, includes an Atlas in a learned Phoenician cosmogony; its relationship to authentic Phoenician tradition is debated because Philo wrote in the Roman period and the text survives only indirectly.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest record

The earliest securely datable evidence is literary rather than epigraphic.

**Homer, *Odyssey* 1.52–54**: Athena tells Zeus that Odysseus is confined by Calypso, “daughter of baleful Atlas, who knows the depths of every sea and himself holds/guards the tall pillars that keep earth and heaven apart.” The Greek verb **ἔχει** can mean “holds,” “possesses,” or “guards.” Homer does not call Atlas a Titan, explain why he bears the pillars, place a globe on his shoulders, or mention a rebellion against Zeus. The text is the product of an oral-poetic tradition conventionally fixed around the late eighth or early seventh century BCE; complete medieval manuscripts are much later.

**Hesiod, *Theogony* 507–520**: Iapetus marries the Oceanid Clymene and fathers Atlas, Menoetius, Prometheus, and Epimetheus. Atlas, “through hard necessity,” holds the broad heaven with tireless head and hands at earth’s limits before the clear-voiced Hesperides; “wise Zeus assigned him this lot.” Hesiod does not say here that Atlas received it for leading the Titans. The explicit “leader of the Titans” explanation first appears much later.

The two early pictures need not be identical: Homer gives pillars that Atlas controls; Hesiod makes his body support heaven itself. Aeschylus later combines the images.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain and powers

Atlas is not originally the “god of the earth.” His defining early function is **cosmic separation and support**:

- Homer: he knows the sea’s depths and holds or guards the pillars dividing earth from sky.
- Hesiod: he directly upholds **οὐρανὸν εὐρύν**, “broad heaven.”
- Aeschylus/Pseudo-Aeschylus: he shoulders the combined “pillar of heaven and earth.”
- Later visual art: he supports a celestial sphere, identifiable by constellations.
- Modern popular art: he often carries a terrestrial globe. This is a late misunderstanding or deliberate reinterpretation, not what the archaic texts say.

His powers are largely inferred from this task: superhuman endurance, enormous scale, and knowledge of cosmic or marine boundaries. Diodorus’s astronomical expertise is a rationalized human accomplishment, not an early divine portfolio. Atlas has no securely attested thunderbolt, trident, helmet, sacred color, weekday, number, or sacrificial animal.

### Location

His early location is the **western limit of the inhabited world**, beside or before the Hesperides. Later geography attached the myth to Libya and the Atlas mountain. Homer’s remark that Calypso’s island lies at the “navel of the sea” has produced incompatible maps; the poem does not give coordinates linking Ogygia securely to Morocco, Malta, Gozo, or the Atlantic.

### Iconographic development

1. **Archaic Heracles–Atlas compositions:** Late-sixth/early-fifth-century Attic black-figure pottery shows Heracles temporarily bearing a star-marked architectural heaven while Atlas returns with apples. Athens National Archaeological Museum 1132, attributed to the Athena Painter, c. 525–475 BCE, is a clear example.
2. **Temple of Zeus at Olympia, east metope 4, c. 470–456 BCE:** Heracles supports the sky with a cushion; Athena lightly assists; Atlas approaches with the apples. This is major contemporary evidence that the exchange story existed before its fullest surviving literary narration in Pseudo-Apollodorus. The original is in the Archaeological Museum of Olympia.
3. **Chest of Cypselus and Amyclaean throne:** Pausanias describes earlier prestigious works—known to him in the second century CE—containing Atlas-related scenes. His testimony is literary documentation of now-lost art; dates assigned to the objects depend on archaeological and art-historical reconstruction.
4. **South-Italian vase painting:** Atlas can appear with Gaia or within broader cosmic/Titan imagery; identification depends on inscriptions or iconographic context.
5. **Farnese Atlas:** A marble statue, Naples National Archaeological Museum inv. 6374, generally dated to the second century CE, shows a kneeling, muscular Atlas supporting a celestial—not terrestrial—globe. Forty-one constellation figures and astronomical circles survive in relief. It may copy or adapt a Hellenistic original, but the proposed original cannot be securely reconstructed.
6. **Farnese-globe controversy:** Bradley Schaefer argued that constellation positions encode observations associated with Hipparchus, around 125 BCE. Critics question whether damaged reliefs, sculptural copying, and imprecise constellation boundaries can sustain such precision. A 2023 photogrammetric survey emphasizes that the object still requires careful physical and iconographic reassessment. “The lost star catalogue of Hipparchus is preserved on the Farnese Atlas” is therefore a modern contested hypothesis, not settled fact.
7. **Atlantes in architecture:** Male figures replacing columns became *atlantes* or *telamones*. The analogy is Atlas’s burden, but most such figures do not narrate a myth about Atlas personally.
8. **Renaissance to modern art:** Printed maps, emblem books, palace decoration, libraries, scientific institutions, and the Rockefeller Center bronze turned Atlas into an emblem of knowledge, universal scope, and burdensome responsibility.

### Attributes

Secure attributes are the celestial vault, pillar, sphere, bowed or kneeling pose, and occasionally the Hesperidean apples. The “world on his shoulders” is modern shorthand: where the ancient sphere carries constellations, it is heaven. Wings, chains, a hammer, or a terrestrial map are not standard ancient attributes.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Parents and siblings

- **Hesiod:** Iapetus + Clymene → Atlas, Menoetius, Prometheus, Epimetheus.
- **Pseudo-Apollodorus:** Iapetus + Asia → the same four sons.
- **Hyginus’s preface:** traditions vary among Clymene, Asia, and other Oceanid names because ancient genealogists repeatedly equated or substituted Oceanids.
- Calling Uranus and Gaia Atlas’s direct parents is a modern simplification: in Hesiod they are ancestral Titans, while Iapetus and Clymene are his immediate parents.

### Consorts and descendants

- **Calypso:** Homer calls her Atlas’s daughter but does not name her mother.
- **Maia and the Pleiades:** Hesiod knows the Pleiades as Atlas’s daughters; Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.10.1 makes Pleione their mother and names Maia, Electra, Taygete, Alcyone, Celaeno, Sterope, and Merope.
- **Aethra variant:** Later scholia and mythography sometimes substitute the Oceanid Aethra for Pleione.
- **Hyades and Hyas:** Hyginus gives Atlas and Pleione or an Oceanid twelve daughters and a son Hyas; five daughters become Hyades after mourning Hyas, while seven become Pleiades. This is late systematization of star groups.
- **Hesperides:** Hesiod makes them daughters of Night, not Atlas. Diodorus makes seven Hesperides daughters of Atlas and Hesperis. Other traditions call them daughters of Atlas without a named mother. “The Hesperides are Atlas’s daughters” is therefore a variant, not the archaic consensus.
- **Dione and Maera:** Sporadic genealogical sources count them among Atlantids; identities are complicated by homonymous figures.
- **Hermes:** Atlas is Hermes’s maternal grandfather through Maia.
- **Dardanus:** Atlas is ancestor of Dardanus through Electra in a major Trojan genealogy.
- **Niobe:** Ovid has Niobe claim Dione, an Atlantid, as her mother, creating another secondary descent from Atlas.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. The cosmic burden

**Early core:** Homer’s Atlas maintains pillars separating earth and heaven. Hesiod’s Atlas directly holds heaven at earth’s limits because Zeus assigned that fate. The image presupposes a layered cosmos in which sky and earth are separable physical regions.

**Classical development:** Pindar makes the burden proverbial. *Prometheus Bound* combines pillar and shoulder imagery, locates Atlas in the west, and parallels his suffering with Prometheus’s punishment.

**Later causation:** Hyginus explicitly makes Atlas leader of a Titan revolt and explains the burden as punishment. It is common in modern summaries to project this explanation backward into Homer and Hesiod, but the early poems do not state it.

**Modern distortion:** Atlas holds the sky, not planet Earth. Terrestrial globes became attached to him through post-classical cosmography and the visual similarity of celestial and terrestrial spheres.

### B. Atlas, Heracles, and the Hesperides

The labor has several irreconcilable versions.

1. **Atlas exchange:** Prometheus advises Heracles not to gather the apples himself. Heracles takes Atlas’s sphere or sky; Atlas obtains three apples from the Hesperides. Atlas then proposes delivering them to Eurystheus himself, intending not to resume his burden. Heracles agrees but asks Atlas to hold the sky momentarily while he places a pad on his head. Once Atlas takes it back, Heracles departs with the apples. This fullest form is Pseudo-Apollodorus 2.5.11.
2. **Direct acquisition:** Other art and texts show Heracles himself approaching the tree and dragon, or killing Ladon and taking the apples. In these versions Atlas need not participate.
3. **Hesperides’ gift:** Some representations emphasize peaceful reception from the maidens rather than theft or dragon combat.
4. **Early visual evidence:** The Athena Painter lekythos and Olympia metope prove that the Atlas exchange was established by the late Archaic/early Classical period, centuries before Pseudo-Apollodorus’s surviving handbook.
5. **Genealogical conflict:** In Hesiod the Hesperides are daughters of Night, though Atlas stands beside them. Diodorus and later writers make them Atlas’s daughters, tightening geographic proximity into kinship.
6. **Ritual/political setting:** At Olympia the scene formed part of a twelve-labor program on Zeus’s temple. It monumentalized Heracles’s divinely assisted accomplishment and contributed to the visual canon of twelve labors; this does not demonstrate worship of Atlas there.

### C. Perseus petrifies Atlas

In Ovid, Perseus reaches Atlas’s wealthy western kingdom after killing Medusa. Themis had warned Atlas that a son of Jupiter would despoil his golden tree. Atlas therefore walls the orchard, posts a dragon, and refuses Perseus hospitality. Unable to overcome Atlas by strength, Perseus displays Medusa’s head. Atlas’s beard and hair become forests, his shoulders ridges, and his bones stone; heaven and stars rest upon the resulting mountain.

This is a **late Roman poetic narrative**, not attested in Homer, Hesiod, surviving Greek tragedy, or Pseudo-Apollodorus. It creates a chronological contradiction if combined with the Heracles episode: Perseus, Heracles’s ancestor, petrifies Atlas before Heracles later exchanges the sky with him. Ancient myth did not require a single harmonized timeline; Ovid’s etiological metamorphosis and the Heracles cycle should remain distinct.

The story also transforms a divine cosmic supporter into an explanation for the Atlas mountain. Ovid’s Atlas is a king, orchard-owner, giant, mountain, and sky-supporter in succession.

### D. Atlas and the astral daughters

Hesiod’s *Works and Days* uses the Pleiades, daughters of Atlas, as practical agricultural markers: their heliacal rising signals harvest, their setting ploughing. Later mythographers add Pleione, pursuit by Orion, transformations into doves or stars, the “missing” Pleiad, the Hyades’ mourning for Hyas, and individual divine unions producing heroic dynasties.

The early evidence confirms Atlas’s connection with a star cluster but not the complete later family romance. The astral genealogy plausibly complements his cosmic location, yet the direction of development—myth producing star names or star lore producing genealogy—cannot be proved globally.

### E. Atlas as astronomer-king

Diodorus reports a Libyan tradition in which Atlas perfects astronomy, teaches the doctrine of the sphere, and is consequently said mythically to carry the cosmos. Heracles receives astronomical knowledge from him rather than literally taking up heaven. This is **euhemeristic systematization**: supernatural myth is rewritten as misunderstood cultural history.

Mercator later selected this wise royal astronomer—not simply the suffering Titan—as patron of cosmography. Modern accounts saying Mercator chose Atlas because he “held up the Earth” miss his stated learned rationale.

### F. Atlas, Atlantis, and the Atlantic

Plato’s Atlas is the firstborn twin of Poseidon and Cleito, ruler over Atlantis. Plato’s narrative says the island and Atlantic sea took their names from him. This figure is genealogically distinct from the son of Iapetus, although later antiquarians and popular writers merged them.

Herodotus had already used *Atlantis thalassa*, “Sea of Atlas/Atlantic sea,” and described Mount Atlas and the Atlantes before Plato’s dialogues. Thus Plato did not create every Atlas-derived western geographic term. Claims that Plato’s Atlantis proves a historical global Atlas civilization are modern speculation.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

No securely documented, autonomous ancient cult of Atlas is presently known.

- No temple securely identified as a temple of Atlas has been excavated.
- No priesthood of Atlas is established by inscription.
- No civic festival, sacrificial calendar, oracle, pilgrimage, or regular votive formula to Atlas is securely attested.
- No firm body of theophoric personal names proves broad Atlas worship.
- The *Orphic Hymns* contain no surviving hymn addressed to him.
- The Greek Magical Papyri do not establish a stable independent Atlas cult.

This absence must not be converted into “Greeks did not believe in Atlas.” Greek myth included many divine figures without a major civic cult. Atlas was highly visible in poetry and art but marginal in documented sacrifice and institutional worship.

The Olympia metope belongs to the temple of Zeus and represents Heracles’s labor; it is not a dedication to Atlas. Architectural atlantes are functional and symbolic sculptures, not ipso facto cult images.

Ancient local reverence for mountains called Atlas is possible, and geographical descriptions sometimes attribute sanctity or extraordinary properties to western mountains. The surviving evidence does not allow those practices to be identified confidently as worship of Hesiod’s Titan.

Consequently, there is no Atlas-specific evidence for human sacrifice, temple prostitution, institutional cult violence, or suppression of worship. Assertions otherwise are unsupported. The broad Christian suppression or transformation of polytheistic institutions in late antiquity affected Greek religion generally, but no documented closure of an Atlas temple can be named.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Archaic period, c. 750–480 BCE

Atlas enters surviving literature as a remote cosmic being tied to Calypso, sea-knowledge, western boundaries, and separation of heaven and earth. Hesiod embeds him in the Iapetid family and gives him a bodily burden imposed by Zeus. Astral genealogy connects him to the Pleiades. By the late sixth century BCE, artists depict his exchange with Heracles.

### Classical period, 480–323 BCE

Pindar and tragedy make his endurance proverbial. Monumental sculpture at Olympia canonizes the Heracles episode, with Athena physically assisting. Atlas functions as a cosmological and genealogical figure, not a prominent recipient of civic cult. Herodotus transfers the name into North-African ethnography and geography. Plato creates a separate royal Atlas for Atlantis.

### Hellenistic period, 323–31 BCE

Scholars, poets, and mythographers systematize divergent genealogies and constellation lore. Euhemerism increasingly presents gods as ancient kings, inventors, and astronomers. The Hesperides complex is localized variously in Libya, near Ocean, among Hyperboreans, and at western extremities. A Hellenistic sculptural prototype has often been proposed for the Farnese Atlas, but it is not extant.

### Roman imperial period

Virgil and Ovid monumentalize Atlas as mountain, ancestor, western boundary, and cosmic axis. Ovid creates the surviving full Perseus-petrifaction account. Hyginus explicitly makes him commander of a Titan revolt. Pseudo-Apollodorus preserves the definitive literary form of the Heracles trick. Pausanias documents older Atlas images. The Farnese Atlas gives the most famous surviving ancient visual form: a strained Titan under a mapped celestial sphere.

### Late antiquity and medieval transmission

Atlas survived through school texts, mythographic handbooks, astronomical manuscripts, Ovid, Virgil, and Christian chronography. Euhemerists and universal historians sometimes synchronized him with biblical patriarchs such as Enoch or Japheth. These are **Jewish, Christian, and antiquarian synchronisms**, not archaic Greek tradition.

The being ceased to be part of an operating public sacrificial system as Greco-Roman cult institutions disappeared, but his texts and images were transmitted rather than erased. Medieval manuscript art retained constellation lore and cosmic-support imagery, sometimes without a consistent distinction between the Titan, astronomer, and architectural supporter.

### Renaissance and early modern period

Humanists recovered ancient sculpture and texts. Atlas became a patron image of astronomy, geography, and encyclopedic knowledge. Gerard Mercator planned a cosmography and chose the learned Mauretanian king Atlas as its emblem. After Mercator’s death, Rumold Mercator published the complete surviving compilation in Duisburg in 1595 as *Atlas sive cosmographicae meditationes de fabrica mundi et fabricati figura*. The word then generalized from this particular title to a class of map books.

### Nineteenth century to present

Atlas became a metaphor for labor, responsibility, science, global coverage, and encyclopedic comprehensiveness. Classical revival art magnified the muscular supporter. New astronomical bodies, scientific projects, companies, rockets, robots, and geographic works took the name. Contemporary Hellenic polytheists may honor Titans within reconstructed personal practice, but no centralized, continuous Atlas cult or reliable count of Atlas devotees is documented.

The title **“Atlas of Journeys”** uses the post-Mercator sense “organized collection of maps or wide-ranging knowledge”; its namesake ultimately reaches the Atlas complex through Mercator, not through an independently attested ancient title.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Cartography and knowledge

Mercator’s 1595 title is the decisive semantic event. His preface identifies Atlas as a wise king of Mauretania skilled in astronomy and cosmography. The title page depicts a scholarly Atlas, not merely the punished rebel. Later publishers made “atlas” the generic term for a bound map collection. By extension it now denotes systematic visual collections in anatomy, botany, astronomy, linguistics, and computing.

### Literature and philosophy

- Percy Bysshe Shelley’s *The Witch of Atlas* (1820) uses “Atlas” geographically and mythically but invents its central witch; it is Romantic reception, not recovered Greek legend.
- Heinrich Heine, Nietzschean prose, and later poets repeatedly use Atlas as the figure who bears an unbearable world.
- Ayn Rand’s *Atlas Shrugged* (1957) turns Atlas into a political metaphor: productive individuals are imagined as supporting society and invited to withdraw that support. This is modern ideological reinvention, not interpretation of an ancient cult.
- The phrase “carrying the weight of the world” combines the ancient posture with the modern terrestrial-globe misconception.

### Visual and commercial culture

Lee Lawrie’s bronze *Atlas* at Rockefeller Center, installed in 1937, is among the most influential modern images: a nude muscular figure supports an armillary celestial sphere. It preserves the celestial rather than terrestrial object but radically modernizes the style and setting.

Atlases and atlantes became common in façades, furniture, clocks, libraries, bank buildings, and state architecture. Corporate names invoke strength, reach, or endurance: Atlas Air, Atlas Copco, Boston Dynamics’ Atlas robot, the Atlas rocket family, and numerous scientific surveys. These are commercial or technological appropriations.

### Film, comics, and games

Sword-and-sandal cinema used Atlas as a generic strongman, notably *Atlas in the Land of the Cyclops* (1961), rather than reproducing a specific ancient narrative. DC and Marvel publications have used characters named Atlas with varying relationships to the Titan. The *God of War* game series makes Atlas an active colossal Titan within a newly constructed war narrative. Such works materially reinforce the modern image of Atlas as a gigantic enemy or ally of Olympian gods; their plots are modern inventions.

### Occultism, New Age, and neopaganism

A dedicated Atlas doctrine is not a significant component of the canonical Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, Thelema, or surviving ancient magical papyri. Modern websites assign Atlas correspondences—planetary, elemental, chromatic, crystal, tarot, or psychological—but these usually lack ancient sources.

Jungian and self-help readings interpret him as the archetype of burden, over-responsibility, or endurance. The modern psychological label “Atlas personality,” applied to persons who habitually shoulder excessive responsibility, is metaphorical and non-ancient.

Modern Hellenic polytheists can and do formulate prayers or offerings to Atlas. Such devotion is a legitimate modern religious practice, but claims of an unchanged ancient ritual lineage are unsupported because no ancient liturgy or institutional Atlas cult survives.

### Politics and extremist appropriation

Atlas has been used generally in Western civilizational imagery, libertarian discourse through *Atlas Shrugged*, and muscular monumental art. Classical antiquity as a whole is appropriated by nationalist and far-right movements, but there is insufficient evidence to identify Atlas personally as a stable, distinctive extremist emblem comparable to the Spartan lambda or Roman fasces. Claims of an ancient “Aryan Atlas cult” or an Atlantean master race belong to modern racial occultism and pseudo-history, not Greek evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Central debates

1. **Pillars or sky?** Homer’s language permits “holds” or “guards” pillars; Hesiod clearly makes Atlas support heaven. Scholars debate whether these are separate cosmological conceptions or poetic expressions of the same function.
2. **Why is he punished?** Hesiod says Zeus allotted the task but does not connect it expressly to Atlas commanding the Titans. Hyginus supplies that causation much later. Modern retellings routinely conflate the strata.
3. **Name origin:** Indo-European “bear/endure” and Pre-Greek substrate analyses remain competing possibilities. Amazigh derivations are insufficiently demonstrated.
4. **Atlas and North Africa:** The divine western supporter, geographical mountain, ethnographic Atlantes, Diodoran king, and Ovidian mountain are related through ancient interpretive processes. Whether the myth arose from knowledge of the mountain, was projected onto it, or developed reciprocally cannot be established.
5. **Hesperides’ parentage:** Hesiod’s daughters of Night conflict with Diodorus and later sources making them Atlas’s daughters. The shift may transform adjacency into genealogy.
6. **Farnese Atlas:** Dating the Roman marble to the second century CE is broadly accepted; reconstructing its supposed Hellenistic model and astronomical source remains disputed. Schaefer’s Hipparchus thesis is ingenious but not consensus.
7. **Atlas and Atlantis:** Plato’s king is a distinct son of Poseidon. Attempts to use Titan genealogy to authenticate Atlantis confuse separate literary constructions.
8. **Ancient cult:** Atlas’s prominence in myth and art has encouraged assumption of worship. The lack of temples, priesthoods, calendars, and dedications argues instead for a principally poetic, cosmological, and decorative figure.
9. **Mercator’s intent:** Popular explanations say the map book was called an atlas because the Titan carried Earth. Mercator’s own preface points to the learned king and astronomer. The globe-on-shoulders image nevertheless encouraged later conflation.
10. **Comparative mythology:** Similar sky-separators and supporters occur in Egypt and Western Asia. Direct descent or equation requires historical transmission evidence; resemblance alone is not enough.

### State of the evidence

No Mycenaean Linear B occurrence of Atlas is securely recognized. No archaic inscription independently predates Homer and Hesiod. The textual tradition is therefore mediated by poems transmitted orally and copied in manuscripts many centuries later. Vase paintings and temple sculpture provide an essential independent control: they establish versions of the Heracles episode earlier than the surviving prose mythographers.

Important holdings include:

- Archaeological Museum of Olympia: the Atlas–Heracles–Athena metope.
- National Archaeological Museum, Athens: vase paintings including Athens 1132.
- National Archaeological Museum, Naples: Farnese Atlas, inv. 6374.
- Louvre and other major collections: fragments and copies of Heracles/Hesperides programs.
- Manuscript collections preserving Homer, Hesiod, Ovid, Hyginus, astronomical works, and Aratea.
- Digital corpora: Perseus/Scaife, Thesaurus Linguae Graecae, Digital LIMC, Arachne, and institutional museum databases.

### Kerényi and Graves

Karl Kerényi’s mythological syntheses are important twentieth-century reception and interpretation but should not override passage-level chronology. Robert Graves’s *The Greek Myths* combines ancient citations with speculative ritualism, poetic intuition, and proposed prehistoric systems; it is influential reception, not a critical edition or safe sole authority. Where Graves reconstructs lost matriarchal or calendrical meanings for Atlas, those claims require independent confirmation.
