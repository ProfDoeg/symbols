# Eris: Research Dossier

**Class:** In archaic and classical Greek sources, **Eris is a goddess and personified daimōn of strife, rivalry, quarrel, and battle**—Greek Ἔρις, *Éris*. The ordinary noun ἔρις means “strife,” “quarrel,” “contention,” or, in a potentially productive sense, “rivalry.” Roman poets render the figure as **Discordia**. Modern Discordianism recasts her as a supreme goddess of creative disorder or chaos; that characterization is a documented twentieth-century reinvention, not the normal ancient Greek classification.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Eris is securely attested in the Greek hexameter tradition represented by the *Iliad* and Hesiod, conventionally dated to approximately the late eighth or early seventh century BCE. The precise relative chronology of Homer and Hesiod remains disputed. No presently secure Linear B occurrence, Bronze Age inscription, independently datable cult inscription, or demonstrably earlier image names Eris. In the *Iliad* she is Ares’ “sister and companion,” a terrifying battle-presence who grows from small beginnings until her head reaches heaven; in Hesiod she is both the daughter of Night and mother of destructive abstractions, while *Works and Days* distinguishes two Strifes, one blameworthy and one economically productive.

## Evidentiary labels

The following labels are used throughout:

- **[Early attested]** Archaic evidence, principally Homer and Hesiod.
- **[Later canonical elaboration]** Classical or post-classical literary development that became standard.
- **[Regional variant]** A locally distinctive representation or genealogy.
- **[Learned systematization]** Mythographic, allegorical, philosophical, or antiquarian ordering of older material.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A historical equation with another divinity or personification.
- **[Late accretion]** Late-antique, medieval, or early-modern addition.
- **[Folklore]** Popular narrative not securely traceable to an ancient primary source.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A modern inference rather than an explicit ancient statement.
- **[Modern invention]** A demonstrably modern theology, symbol, attribute, ritual, or story.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Greek name

**Ἔρις, Eris**, genitive **Ἔριδος, Eridos**, is simultaneously a proper divine name and an ordinary Greek noun. Its semantic range includes quarrel, contention, conflict, rivalry, and the strife of war or litigation. Context—not capitalization in ancient manuscripts—determines how fully personified any occurrence is.

The traditional English “Discord” captures opposition and social division but can obscure the competitive, even productive, meaning found in Hesiod. “Strife” is generally broader. “Chaos goddess,” common in contemporary occult and popular writing, is misleading when retrojected into antiquity: Greek **Χάος, Chaos**, is a separate cosmogonic entity in Hesiod, whereas Eris is conflict or contention.

### Etymology

**[Secure linguistic evidence]** The divine name is transparently the personified noun ἔρις. The noun’s deeper etymology is uncertain. Pierre Chantraine and R. S. P. Beekes survey proposed connections, but Beekes concludes that there is no convincing derivation. Suggested relationships with ὀρίνω/ὀρίνω-type words (“stir, arouse”), ἐρείδω (“prop, press”), or Ἐρινύες (“Erinyes”) remain disputed; phonological or semantic obstacles prevent any from being established. A prehistoric Indo-European or “pre-Greek” origin is therefore not a fact. Related historical Greek forms include **ἐρίζω**, “quarrel, contend,” and **ἔρισμα**, “cause or object of contention.”

### Epithets and descriptive formulas

Eris did not acquire a well-developed corpus of local cult titles comparable to Athena Polias or Zeus Olympios. Her “epithets” are predominantly poetic descriptions:

- **ἄμοτον μεμαυῖα**, “raging relentlessly/insatiably,” and **ἄσχετον**, “unrestrainable,” express her battlefield force in Homer. **[Early attested]**
- **Ἄρεος ἀνδροφόνοιο κασιγνήτη ἑτάρη τε**, “sister and companion of man-slaying Ares,” *Iliad* 4.441. This is a Homeric relational formula, not proof of a settled cult genealogy. **[Early attested]**
- **στυγερή**, “hateful/abhorred,” and **καρτερόθυμος**, “hard-hearted,” characterize Hesiod’s destructive Eris. **[Early attested]**
- **πολύστονος**, “productive of many groans” or “much-lamented,” describes her in battle in *Iliad* 11. **[Early attested]**
- Latin poets call Discordia **demens** or otherwise represent her as maddened, bloody, or destructive. These are Roman literary developments, not Greek cult titles. **[Syncretistic identification; later literary elaboration]**

Modern devotional expressions such as “Eris Kallisti,” “Great Goddess Eris,” “goddess of chaos, laughter, confusion, and creativity,” or astrological rulerships are not known ancient titles. **[Modern invention]**

### Roman Discordia

Roman authors translated or assimilated Eris to **Discordia**, the personification of discord. Virgil’s *Aeneid* 8.702 places Discordia in the battle of Actium, clothed in a torn mantle; Statius and Seneca expand the infernal and civil-war imagery. This is best treated as a literary **interpretatio Romana**, not proof that Greek Eris and Roman Discordia always shared an identical cultic history. Concordia possessed a substantial Roman state cult; Discordia appears principally as her negative literary antithesis and lacks comparable institutional worship.

### Proposed equation with Enyo

Ancient poetry sometimes gives Eris and **Enyo** overlapping battlefield functions, and modern handbooks consequently call them “closely identified.” Yet no strong cult document establishes a formal fusion, and some texts place several war-personifications in the same scene as distinct beings. The equation should therefore be labeled **[modern scholarly comparison / partial literary assimilation]**, not a timeless identity.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest record

The earliest recoverable evidence is oral-derived archaic hexameter preserved in medieval manuscripts. The surviving physical manuscripts are many centuries later than the poems’ composition; “eighth-century evidence” means the probable formation of the poetic text, not an eighth-century papyrus in hand.

#### Homer, *Iliad*

**Language:** Archaic epic Greek.  
**Probable composition:** Late eighth or early seventh century BCE, with a long oral prehistory.  
**Surviving witnesses:** Principally medieval Byzantine manuscripts, supplemented by ancient papyri and quotations.

Important passages include:

- **4.440–445:** Eris is Ares’ sister and companion. Small at first, she grows until her head rests in heaven while her feet remain on earth, and she casts common hatred between the armies. **[Early attested]**
- **5.518–520:** Zeus sends Eris toward the Achaean ships bearing or holding a “portent/sign of war.” **[Early attested]**
- **11.3–14, 73:** Zeus sends Eris to the Achaeans with the sign of war; she cries terribly and strengthens each warrior’s determination. Later she rejoices at the slaughter and is uniquely present while the other gods remain in their halls. **[Early attested]**
- **18.535–540:** On Achilles’ divinely made shield, Eris, Kydoimos, and destructive Ker occupy a battle scene. **[Early attested, represented image within narrative]**
- **20.48–53:** When the gods join the battle, Eris bursts out; Athena and Ares answer with opposing cries. **[Early attested]**

These passages make Eris a battlefield power, sometimes a strongly visualized person and sometimes poetic personification. The *Iliad* says nothing about a golden apple or her exclusion from Peleus and Thetis’ wedding.

#### Hesiod, *Theogony*

**Language:** Archaic epic Greek.  
**Probable composition:** Approximately 700 BCE, though dating and layers remain debated.

At lines 211–232 Nyx—Night—produces a succession of powers without sexual union. Eris is Nyx’s daughter and in turn bears:

**Ponos** (Toil/Hardship), **Lethe** (Forgetfulness), **Limos** (Famine), the **Algea** (Pains), **Hysminai** (Fightings), **Machai** (Battles), **Phonoi** (Murders), **Androktasiai** (Manslaughters), **Neikea** (Quarrels), **Pseudea** (Falsehoods), **Logoi** (Words/Stories), **Amphillogiai** (Disputes), **Dysnomia** (Lawlessness), **Ate** (Ruin/Delusion), and **Horkos** (Oath).

The list is a genealogy of social and moral consequences as well as divine beings. No father is supplied. **[Early attested; genealogical systematization internal to Hesiod]**

#### Hesiod, *Works and Days* 11–26

Hesiod corrects or complicates the idea that there is only one Eris:

> “There was not, after all, only one kind of Erides, but upon the earth there are two.”

One deserves blame and promotes evil war and conflict; the other was set by Zeus at the roots of the earth and is beneficial. She makes a poor man emulate a wealthy one, potter compete with potter, craftsman with craftsman, beggar envy beggar, and singer compete with singer. Productive emulation energizes labor, though it remains rooted in envy.

This passage can be interpreted either as two distinct divine Strifes or as two manifestations/species of strife. The plural **Erides** and the language of separate origins support distinction; the poem does not give both elaborate personal biographies. **[Early attested variant]**

The “good Eris” is not evidence that Greeks generally worshipped a benevolent goddess of creativity. It is a particular Hesiodic ethical-economic analysis of competition.

#### Pseudo-Hesiod, *Shield of Heracles* 139–160

**Probable date:** Usually later than the core Hesiodic poems, perhaps sixth century BCE, though disputed.

Eris appears on Heracles’ shield amid Panic, Pursuit, Rout, and Slaughter, flying or raging over battle. The passage develops the Homeric shield tradition and confirms her visual association with warfare. **[Archaic or early classical elaboration; disputed authorship]**

### Other Greek sources

- **Epic *Cypria***: A lost poem of the Trojan Epic Cycle, probably seventh or sixth century BCE. Its plot survives chiefly through Proclus’ much later prose summary. At the wedding feast of Peleus, Eris initiates a **neikos peri kallous**, a dispute “about beauty,” among Athena, Hera, and Aphrodite; Zeus orders Hermes to take them to Paris/Alexandros for judgment. The summary does **not** mention an apple, an inscription, or Eris’ exclusion from the wedding. [The surviving Proclean summary](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/primary-source/epic-cycle-sb/) is therefore evidence for the quarrel but not, by itself, for the familiar golden-apple details. **[Archaic narrative known through late epitome]**
- **Alcman, fragment 146 PMGF:** The phrase “Eris, sister of Enyo” is reported in the lexicographical tradition; context is lost and the text cannot support a full genealogy. **[Archaic fragment, insecure context]**
- **Ibycus, fragmentary lyric:** Eris appears in martial or Trojan material, but the fragments do not create an independent cult dossier. **[Archaic fragment]**
- **Aesopic fable, later numbered 534/Chambry 129:** Heracles encounters a small object in the road; striking it makes it swell until Athena explains that it is Eris—leave contention alone and it stays small, but fight it and it grows. The transmitted prose collections are much later than the traditionally assigned sixth-century Aesop. **[Later fable attached to an archaic author]**
- **Aeschylus, *Seven Against Thebes* 1057:** “Eris is the last of the gods to finish an argument,” a proverbial characterization of self-perpetuating conflict. **[Classical attested]**
- **Euripides:** The *Trojan Women*, *Helen*, and *Iphigenia at Aulis* refer to the Judgment of Paris, but the extant relevant passages need not name Eris or an apple. This is important negative evidence: the judgment circulated independently of those details. **[Classical variant]**
- **Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 3.2:** Probably first or second century CE. Eris throws an apple “as a prize of beauty” for Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite; Zeus sends them with Hermes to Paris. The surviving passage does not itself say that Eris was barred from the wedding or preserve the inscription *kallistēi*. **[Later canonical elaboration]**
- **Pausanias, *Description of Greece* 5.19.2:** Second century CE. On the archaic Chest of Cypselus at Olympia, Eris stood between the fighting Ajax and Hector, described as extremely ugly; an inscription identified the scene. Pausanias saw or reports seeing the chest centuries after its putative manufacture. **[Antiquarian testimony to archaic art]**
- **Antoninus Liberalis, *Metamorphoses* 11:** Hera sends Eris between the married couple Polytechnos and Aedon after they boast that their love exceeds that of Zeus and Hera. Eris turns a craft contest into the beginning of rape, revenge, murder, and metamorphosis. **[Imperial-period regional tale]**
- **Lucian, *Dialogues of the Gods* 20:** A satirical dramatization of the Judgment of Paris. **[Imperial literary retelling]**
- **Philostratus the Younger, *Imagines* 10:** Ekphrastic treatment of Pyrrhus or battle imagery invokes personified conflict. **[Imperial rhetorical elaboration]**
- **Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica*:** Fourth century CE. Eris repeatedly delights in Trojan-war battle, shouts with Ares, and accompanies Keres, Phonos, and Kydoimos. The poem consciously extends Homeric diction. **[Late-antique Homerizing elaboration]**
- **Tryphiodorus, *Taking of Ilios*:** Probably third or fourth century CE; Eris participates in the atmosphere of Troy’s destruction. **[Late-antique elaboration]**
- **Nonnus, *Dionysiaca*:** Fifth century CE; Eris is used as an epic battle-personification in a highly learned Dionysiac poem. **[Late-antique elaboration]**
- **Colluthus, *Abduction of Helen* 1–64:** Late fifth or early sixth century CE. This is the fullest surviving Greek account centered on Eris at the wedding. Uninvited, she seeks a destructive gift, takes an apple, throws it into the divine feast, and sets Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite against one another; Hermes later bears it to Paris. **[Late-antique elaboration]**

### Latin sources

- **Virgil, *Aeneid* 8.700–703:** Discordia appears amid the battle of Actium, rejoicing in a torn mantle. **[Roman poetic identification]**
- **Ovid, *Heroides* 5 and 16:** The Judgment of Paris and apple are part of erotic Trojan retrospection, although Eris is not always foregrounded. **[Augustan elaboration]**
- **Hyginus, *Fabulae* 92:** All gods are invited to the wedding except Discordia. Denied entrance, she throws an apple from the doorway and declares that the most beautiful should take it. This is the clearest surviving ancient witness for exclusion, rejection at the door, and the apple’s spoken dedication. **[Imperial Latin canonical elaboration]**
- **Seneca, *Hercules Furens* 93–99:** Juno proposes dragging Discordia from the underworld with Crime, Impiety, Error, Rage, and related powers. **[Roman infernal elaboration]**
- **Statius, *Thebaid*:** Discordia is a civil- and fraternal-war power, particularly suited to the mutual destruction of Eteocles and Polynices. **[Flavian elaboration]**
- **Servius on Virgil:** Late-antique scholia systematize the apple narrative and literary genealogy. **[Learned systematization]**

### Corpora searched with negative or limited findings

- **Homeric Hymns:** No surviving hymn to Eris and no evidence of an independent Eris cult emerges from the collection. Ordinary “strife” vocabulary must not automatically be counted as a divine appearance.
- **Orphic Hymns:** No independent Hymn to Eris survives in the transmitted collection. Claims that an “Orphic Hymn to Eris” existed require a specific fragment or manuscript citation.
- **Greek Magical Papyri:** No securely identified, prominent invocation of Eris comparable to those of Hecate, Hermes, Helios, or Aphrodite is established in standard indexes. Modern “PGM rites to Eris” should not be treated as ancient without papyrus number, line, and Greek text.
- **Diodorus Siculus:** No major independent Eris narrative is supplied; Trojan and divine genealogical discussions do not constitute a separate cult record.
- **Pindar and the extant tragedians:** Eris frequently remains the common or personified idea of conflict rather than a narrative deity with an independent episode.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

1. **Battlefield strife:** Her most consistent early domain. Homer makes her a force that arouses armies, intensifies combat, and rejoices in slaughter. **[Early attested]**
2. **Quarrel and social conflict:** Hesiod makes disputes, lies, lawlessness, ruin, and oath-breaking her offspring. Aeschylus treats her as the force that keeps argument alive. **[Early and classical attested]**
3. **Competitive rivalry:** The good Eris of *Works and Days* motivates work through emulation. This is not peace or cooperation but socially useful competition. **[Early Hesiodic variant]**
4. **Marital and household discord:** Antoninus Liberalis’ Aedon and Polytechnos story applies Eris to destructive competition within marriage. **[Imperial regional variant]**
5. **Civil war:** Latin Discordia becomes especially effective as a symbol of Roman political and fraternal conflict. **[Roman literary development]**
6. **Cosmic chaos, creativity, humor, anti-dogmatism:** Central to Discordianism but not Eris’ ordinary ancient domain. **[Modern invention]**

### Form and scale

Homer’s Eris begins small but grows until her head touches heaven while her feet remain on earth. This describes the escalation of conflict rather than a fixed cult statue. Elsewhere she is a female battlefield figure, sometimes winged.

No ancient source supplies a stable hair color, eye color, sacred color, canonical garment, zodiacal sign, number, weekday, planet, flower, or personal animal. Lists assigning her black or red, the number five, Friday, ravens, snakes, or particular crystals are modern systems unless accompanied by a specific ancient citation.

### Ancient images

- **Shield of Achilles, *Iliad* 18:** The earliest literary image. Eris stands with Kydoimos and Ker in battle. It is an object inside poetry, not a surviving artifact. **[Early attested literary iconography]**
- **Shield of Heracles:** Eris appears amid personifications of battle. Again, literary rather than archaeological evidence. **[Archaic literary elaboration]**
- **Chest of Cypselus:** Pausanias reports an image of Eris between Ajax and Hector, “most ugly” in appearance, with an inscription. The chest itself is lost; its traditional early-sixth-century date depends on reconstruction of the Cypselid dedication. **[Antiquarian testimony]**
- **Berlin Antikensammlung F 1775:** An Attic black-figure cup of approximately 540 BCE depicts a winged, running female identified by the painted inscription **ΕΡΙΣ**. This is the earliest securely identified surviving image generally cited for Eris. She has no unique object besides wings and running pose. **[Secure archaic iconography]**
- **Black-figure plate attributed to the “Strife Painter”:** Two winged females and a serpent have been interpreted as Hesiod’s good and bad Erides. That identification is scholarly and contested; resemblance to Gorgons, Nike, or other winged daimones makes certainty impossible. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**
- **St Petersburg/Hermitage krater, conventionally ST 1807:** A fifth-century BCE red-figure scene identifies Eris and Themis as ordinary-looking female figures. The exact mythical reading has been debated. **[Classical iconography]**
- **Judgment-of-Paris pottery:** Eris is not consistently included. Hera, Athena, Aphrodite, Hermes, and Paris ordinarily suffice, demonstrating that she was not iconographically necessary to the judgment scene.
- **Roman and later sarcophagus imagery:** A figure hidden near a tree and throwing the apple appears in later art, including a sarcophagus lid formerly associated with Palazzo Riccardi in Florence. **[Roman or post-classical elaboration]**

The Sophilos dinos (c. 580–570 BCE) and François Vase (c. 570 BCE) depict the divine procession to Peleus and Thetis’ wedding but do not securely depict Eris disrupting it. Their absence warns against reading the later exclusion-and-apple story into every wedding image.

### Symbols

The **golden apple** became Eris’ defining symbol only through post-archaic narrative tradition. The *Cypria* summary names the beauty dispute but no apple; Pseudo-Apollodorus, Hyginus, and Colluthus explicitly supply one. The inscription **καλλίστῃ**, “to/for the fairest,” is not present in every ancient account and should not be attributed verbatim to the lost *Cypria*.

The **Sacred Chao**, pentagon, five-fingered “hand of Eris,” hot dog, and “five tons of flax” belong to Discordian scripture. **[Modern invention]**

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing genealogies

| Source | Genealogy | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|
| Homer, *Iliad* 4.441 | “Sister and companion” of Ares | **[Early attested]**, perhaps an ad hoc poetic kinship rather than formal parentage |
| Hesiod, *Theogony* 211–225 | Daughter of Nyx alone | **[Early attested]**, later the dominant handbook genealogy |
| Hesiod, *Works and Days* 11–26 | Two Erides; Zeus placed the beneficial one at earth’s roots | **[Early variant]**; Zeus is not called her biological father |
| Hyginus, *Fabulae*, preface | Latin Discordia among offspring of Nox and Erebus | **[Roman learned systematization]** |
| Later poetic association | Sister, companion, or near-equivalent of Enyo/Ares | **[Literary association]**, not a uniform genealogy |

Eris has no securely attested consort. Her Hesiodic children are born without a named father and are mostly personified consequences of conflict.

### Children in Hesiod

Every explicitly named offspring in *Theogony* 226–232 is a documented divine relation:

- **Ponos** — toil, hardship.
- **Lethe** — forgetfulness.
- **Limos** — famine.
- **Algea** — pains or sorrows.
- **Hysminai** — fightings.
- **Machai** — battles.
- **Phonoi** — murders.
- **Androktasiai** — manslaughters.
- **Neikea** — quarrels.
- **Pseudea** — falsehoods.
- **Logoi** — words, stories, or arguments.
- **Amphillogiai** — disputes.
- **Dysnomia** — lawlessness.
- **Ate** — ruin, delusion, reckless error.
- **Horkos** — oath, especially the oath that punishes perjury.

These are Hesiod’s genealogical poetics, not evidence that every figure possessed an independent cult.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Ares — sibling and battlefield companion:** Eris is called Ares’ sister and companion; both amplify combat. *Iliad* 4.440–445; 20.48–53. **[Early attested]**
- **Zeus — dispatched to battle:** Zeus sends Eris to the Achaean camp bearing a sign of war. *Iliad* 5.518–520 and 11.3–14. **[Early attested]**
- **Zeus — establishes beneficial Strife:** Zeus places the praiseworthy Eris at the roots of the earth for human benefit. Hesiod, *Works and Days* 17–19. **[Early variant]**
- **Nyx — mother:** Nyx bears hard-hearted Eris without a mate. Hesiod, *Theogony* 211–225. **[Early attested]**
- **Kydoimos — battlefield joint appearance:** Eris and the spirit of tumult stand together on Achilles’ shield. *Iliad* 18.535–540. **[Early attested]**
- **Ker — battlefield joint appearance:** Eris, Kydoimos, and destructive Ker occupy the same shield scene. *Iliad* 18.535–540. **[Early attested]**
- **Athena — beauty dispute:** Eris initiates the dispute in which Athena claims superiority. *Cypria* summary, probably preserving a seventh–sixth-century BCE plot. **[Archaic narrative through late epitome]**
- **Hera — beauty dispute:** Same episode and source. **[Archaic narrative through late epitome]**
- **Aphrodite — beauty dispute:** Same episode; Paris awards Aphrodite the victory after her promise of Helen. **[Archaic narrative through late epitome]**
- **Hermes — procedural link in the judgment:** Zeus orders Hermes to conduct the three disputants to Paris after Eris starts the quarrel. *Cypria* summary; Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 3.2. No direct conversation with Eris survives. **[Archaic plot / later elaboration]**
- **Zeus and Themis — Trojan-war plan:** In the *Cypria* summary Zeus and Themis plan the war, immediately followed by Eris’ initiation of the beauty dispute. The text suggests narrative coordination but does not record a meeting or command addressed to Eris. **[Archaic narrative; interaction not explicit]**
- **Hera — sent against Aedon and Polytechnos:** Hera dispatches Eris to create rivalry between the boasting couple. Antoninus Liberalis, *Metamorphoses* 11, second century CE. **[Imperial regional variant]**
- **Heracles — obstacle of swelling strife:** Heracles strikes the object Eris and causes it to grow; Athena explains its nature. Aesopic fable 534, in later transmission. **[Later fable]**
- **Athena — interpreter of Eris:** Athena tells Heracles that the swelling object is Eris and teaches that contention grows when fought. Same fable. **[Later fable]**
- **Enyo — possible sibling/associate:** Alcman’s fragmentary wording associates or calls Eris sister of Enyo; context is lost. **[Early fragment, uncertain]**
- **Ares — answering war cry:** Eris shouts in delight and Ares answers terribly in Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica* 8.324–330. **[Late-antique Homerizing elaboration]**
- **Keres and Moros — joint delight in slaughter:** Eris appears with death-powers in Quintus Smyrnaeus. **[Late-antique elaboration]**
- **Discordia with Roman infernal powers:** Seneca’s Juno proposes summoning Discordia with Scelus, Impietas, Error, Furor, and related abstractions. *Hercules Furens* 93–99. **[Roman literary systematization]**
- **Aneris/Harmonia — modern opposite:** Discordian texts construct Aneris, principle or goddess of order/non-being, as Eris’ counterpart. This relationship is absent from ancient Greek evidence. **[Modern invention]**

No securely attested direct mythic encounters with Apollo, Artemis, Demeter, Dionysus, Hades, Hecate, Poseidon, or most other Olympians have been established merely because they share scenes or a pantheon.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The two Erides

**Earliest source:** Hesiod, *Works and Days* 11–26, c. 700 BCE.

The poet begins by revising the proposition that only one Eris exists. One is cruel and fosters war; mortals honor her only under compulsion. The other is beneficial because she stimulates emulation. A poor farmer sees a prosperous neighbor and works harder; craftsmen, beggars, and singers compete with their peers.

The passage is neither a simple celebration of capitalism nor a claim that “Eris is secretly good.” Productive rivalry remains envy-driven, and Hesiod’s immediate purpose is to persuade Perses to seek prosperity through labor rather than litigation. Scholars dispute whether the two are ontologically separate goddesses, ethical aspects of a single abstraction, or a poetic classification of social conflict. The grammar presents two Erides; later mythographers normally retained one genealogical Eris and treated the beneficial one as a Hesiodic distinction.

### Eris in the Trojan War

#### Archaic battlefield Eris

The *Iliad* makes Eris a present force within the war but never its original cause. She moves among armies, expands to cosmic size, strengthens fighters, and rejoices in slaughter. Her role is intensification, not strategic allegiance: although Zeus sends her toward the Achaeans, strife is shared by both sides.

#### The *Cypria* beauty dispute

The lost *Cypria* placed a divine feast at Peleus’ wedding near the opening of the Trojan cycle. Zeus and Themis plan the war; Eris creates a quarrel about beauty among Hera, Athena, and Aphrodite; Hermes conveys them to Paris, who chooses Aphrodite in return for Helen.

The relationship between Zeus’ plan and Eris’ act is important. In the extant summary, Eris is not simply an independent vandal whose wounded vanity accidentally starts the war; her quarrel operates within a larger divine plan. Exactly how the original poem connected those acts cannot be recovered.

#### Development of the apple

- **The surviving *Cypria* summary:** quarrel about beauty; no apple stated. **[Archaic core as epitomized]**
- **Euripidean Judgment traditions:** competition among the goddesses; Eris and the apple can be absent. **[Classical variant]**
- **Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 3.2:** Eris throws an apple as the prize of beauty. **[Imperial canonical elaboration]**
- **Hyginus, *Fabulae* 92:** Eris alone is excluded, denied entry, and throws the apple from the doorway, saying the most beautiful should take it. **[Imperial elaboration]**
- **Colluthus:** Eris, uninvited and angry, deliberately chooses the apple as the seed of war and hurls it into the banquet. **[Late-antique fullest version]**
- **“Golden apple from the Garden of the Hesperides”:** The apple is golden in influential later tradition, but its acquisition from the Hesperides is not securely stated by the principal early witnesses. Treating that provenance as canonical is **[late or modern harmonization]**.
- **Written καλλίστῃ:** Familiar and ancient in broader tradition, but not preserved in the Proclean *Cypria* summary or Pseudo-Apollodorus’ wording. Hyginus gives the message as speech rather than necessarily an engraved Greek word. The precisely inscribed apple should not be assigned confidently to the *Cypria*. **[Later canonical detail of uncertain first date]**

Thus the “apple of Discord” is genuinely ancient but not securely part of the earliest recoverable form.

### Eris, Heracles, and the swelling object

In the Aesopic fable Heracles sees something apple-like in a narrow way and strikes it. It doubles in size; repeated blows make it larger. Athena tells him it is Eris: ignored, it remains small, but combat makes it grow. The moral converts Homer’s image of expanding Strife into advice against feeding quarrels.

Its attribution to “Aesop” does not date the surviving prose text to the sixth century BCE. The fable’s manuscript history is later, and its original date is uncertain.

### Aedon and Polytechnos

Antoninus Liberalis preserves a story attributed to the lost *Metamorphoses* of Boios or Nicander’s tradition. Aedon and her husband Polytechnos boast that they love one another more than Hera and Zeus. Hera sends Eris, who inspires a contest: Aedon will weave, Polytechnos will complete a chariot board, and the loser must provide a female servant. Hera helps Aedon win. Polytechnos retaliates by raping Aedon’s sister Chelidon and presenting her disguised as a slave. Discovery leads Aedon and Chelidon to kill and serve Polytechnos’ son Itys to him; the family is ultimately transformed into birds.

This is not part of Eris’ early Homeric dossier. It is a localized imperial-period witness to her as the catalyzing power of competitive domestic catastrophe.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

The central finding is negative: **Eris is richly literary but poorly, if at all, documented as the recipient of an independent ancient public cult.**

No securely identified temple, permanent priesthood, festival, civic calendar entry, oracle, pilgrimage center, treasury, sacred law, sacrificial tariff, or substantial body of votive inscriptions dedicated specifically to Eris is presently known. Standard surveys of Greek religion accordingly treat her primarily among personified powers and poetic daimones rather than institutional cult gods.

This does not prove that no person ever prayed or made an apotropaic offering to Strife. It means that such practice cannot currently be documented. Nor should the absence of a monumental cult be generalized to all abstractions: Nike, Nemesis, Eirene, Peitho, Homonoia, and Roman Concordia acquired well-attested cults.

### Archaeological and epigraphic record

- Berlin F 1775 proves that artists and viewers could recognize a named, winged Eris by the sixth century BCE.
- Pausanias’ Chest of Cypselus testimony shows that an identified Eris could occupy prestigious sanctuary art at Olympia.
- These are representations, not dedications to her.
- No established theophoric personal-name series demonstrates broad Eris worship. Greek names formed from conflict vocabulary require individual analysis and cannot automatically be treated as Eris-theophoric.
- No evidence associates Eris with temple prostitution.
- No evidence associates an Eris cult with human sacrifice.
- No ancient evidence supports a priesthood practicing ritual violence in her name.
- No documented ancient suppression, temple destruction, or forced conversion can be assigned specifically to her because no institutional sanctuary has been established.

Claims of an ancient “Temple of Eris,” hereditary “Erisian priestesses,” apple sacrifices, or a secret mystery cult are **unestablished** unless accompanied by an inscription, archaeological context, or ancient textual citation.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Archaic period, c. 750–600 BCE

Eris emerges in extant literature as an intensely personified feature of battle and social conflict. Homer emphasizes martial escalation. Hesiod gives her a place in a genealogy of Night and destructive abstractions, then distinguishes harmful and productive rivalry. The lost *Cypria* connects her to the quarrel behind the Judgment of Paris.

The earliest securely identified material representation, Berlin F 1775, belongs around the mid-sixth century BCE. The lost Chest of Cypselus was also traditionally archaic.

### Classical period, c. 500–323 BCE

Eris remains useful to tragedy and lyric as a personification of feud, argument, and reciprocal violence. The Judgment of Paris is well known, but surviving classical treatments do not require the golden apple or Eris’ exclusion. Vase painters sometimes represent her, yet no independent cult florescence can be demonstrated.

### Hellenistic and Roman periods, 323 BCE–third century CE

Mythographers regularize genealogies and narrative chains. Pseudo-Apollodorus explicitly gives Eris the apple; Hyginus supplies the most familiar exclusion-at-the-door scenario. Roman Discordia becomes a political and poetic figure for civil war, torn social order, and imperial enemies. This is an expansion in literary utility, not evidence of growing worship.

Antoninus Liberalis supplies the household-discord myth. Pausanias documents an archaizing image at Olympia. Learned allegory increasingly makes Eris a moral or cosmological category.

### Late antiquity, fourth–sixth centuries CE

Quintus Smyrnaeus, Nonnus, Tryphiodorus, and Colluthus reactivate Eris through archaizing epic. Colluthus produces the fullest surviving account of her apple and motives. Christianization displaced the public cults of many Greek gods, but there is no known anti-Eris decree or destroyed Eris temple. Her survival was textual and artistic.

### Byzantine and medieval transmission

Eris survives in manuscripts, lexica, scholia, mythographic summaries, and allegorical readings. In western Europe she is normally encountered through Latin **Discordia** and the Trojan story. Medieval images of the wedding and apple can blend classical narrative with contemporary dress and moral allegory. These are transmissions and reinterpretations, not evidence of continuous worship.

### Renaissance to nineteenth century

Humanist recovery of Homer, Hesiod, Ovid, Hyginus, and mythography renewed the subject in painting, emblem books, opera, pageantry, and allegory. “The apple of discord” became a common European idiom for an object or issue that provokes dispute. Artists generally emphasize the three rival goddesses and Paris; Eris may appear as an ominous intruder or remain offstage.

Mythographers such as Karl Kerényi sensitively explored Eris as an archaic personification, while Robert Graves combined ancient evidence with speculative ritual reconstructions. Graves is influential reception, not a reliable substitute for source-by-source historical demonstration.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Eris entered new religious, occult, popular, and scientific systems. None represents uninterrupted survival from an ancient cult.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Discordianism

Discordianism arose among **Greg Hill** and **Kerry Thornley** in the United States in the late 1950s and early 1960s. Its foundational work, the *Principia Discordia*, circulated in extremely small early editions and expanded through successive versions.

The publication chronology is itself discordant:

- Thornley’s later introduction recalled a bowling-alley revelation in **1958 or 1959**, in which a chimpanzee-like manifestation communicated a vision.
- A copy associated with the later House Select Committee on Assassinations bears evidence of an early **1964** form.
- The fourth edition’s colophon says that *Principia Discordia or How the West Was Lost* was published in New Orleans in **1965** in five copies, followed by a second edition of 100 copies in 1969, a third of 500 copies in 1969, and the fourth in March 1970.
- The often-repeated date **1963** probably reflects early copying or composition traditions, but the surviving publication evidence is inconsistent. The safest formulation is that Discordian scripture developed and first circulated **c. 1963–1965**, with the first well-documented, explicitly numbered publication history beginning in 1965.

Discordian Eris is not simply the hostile Greek battlefield daimōn. She becomes the divine reality of disorder, creativity, play, contradiction, and resistance to imposed categories. The movement constructs:

- **Aneris**, principle of sterile order/non-being;
- the **Sacred Chao**, usually a yin-yang-like emblem containing a pentagon and golden apple;
- the **Law of Fives**;
- the **five seasons** and Discordian calendar;
- **Pope cards** and the claim that every person is a pope;
- the prohibition or ritual eating of hot-dog buns;
- the myth of the **Original Snub**;
- “Greyface,” an apostle of destructive order.

All are **[modern inventions]**, although they creatively reuse the ancient apple narrative. Academic interpretations differ on whether Discordianism is principally parody, religion, “invented religion,” “liquid religion,” social commentary, occult system, or several at once. Practitioners likewise vary between literal devotion, agnosticism, performance, humor, and magic. Calling it merely a joke misses its religious use; calling it an ancient restoration falsifies its history.

### *Illuminatus!* and conspiracy culture

Robert Shea and Robert Anton Wilson’s *Illuminatus!* trilogy (published 1975) spread Discordian motifs far beyond the small original network. It mixed Eris, the Illuminati, anarchism, psychedelic counterculture, conspiracy parody, and competing realities. “Operation Mindfuck” and Discordian disinformation culture influenced later internet humor and conspiracy play. These are modern reception, not ancient attributes.

### Chaos magic

British chaos magic emerged during the 1970s and 1980s through figures including Peter J. Carroll and Ray Sherwin. Discordian flexibility and the deliberate adoption of temporary belief systems strongly influenced it. Phil Hine and later practitioners sometimes treat Eris as a personification or “hidden hand” of Chaos.

This use changes her domain from Greek *eris*, conflict, to the modern metaphysical and magical concept “Chaos.” Later occult equations with Kali, Ishtar, Lilith, Babalon, or other powerful female figures are eclectic ritual correspondences, not historical ancient syncretisms. **[Modern invention]**

### Neopaganism and reconstructionism

Some modern polytheists worship Eris using Homeric or Hesiodic language; others adopt Discordian theology or combine the two. Internet devotional communities attest current practice, but no reliable census establishes its scale. Historically responsible reconstruction must acknowledge that ancient evidence provides virtually no cult calendar, priesthood, sanctuary ritual, or offering regime to reconstruct. Modern altars, prayers, feast days, colors, crystals, and apple offerings are new religious creativity.

### Psychology and archetypal interpretation

Modern Jungian and post-Jungian writing can read Eris as shadow conflict, excluded femininity, creative tension, envy, or the disruptive force that exposes false order. Such readings are interpretive traditions beginning in modern depth psychology; Jung did not transmit an ancient Erisian psychology.

### Literature, art, film, comics, and games

Eris or Discordia appears repeatedly as villain, trickster, or chaos deity. Particularly influential popular adaptations include animated and fantasy works in which she becomes a sovereign of chaos, shadow, or cosmic destruction. These depictions often give her seductive beauty, dark magical powers, monsters, or a desire to destroy the world—features not furnished by Homer or Hesiod. The DreamWorks film *Sinbad: Legend of the Seven Seas* (2003), for example, materially popularized an elegant cosmic “goddess of discord,” but its characterization is a modern fantasy construction.

### Astronomy

The trans-Neptunian body provisionally designated **2003 UB313** was discovered in images taken in 2003 and publicly announced in 2005. Its apparent similarity to Pluto intensified the dispute over the definition of “planet.” On 13 September 2006 the International Astronomical Union approved **(136199) Eris**; its moon became **Dysnomia**, after Hesiod’s daughter of Eris. The choice deliberately alluded to the astronomical discord the discovery had produced. This is one of the most consequential modern receptions of her name and genealogical tradition.

### Political and extremist appropriation

Discordian imagery has intersected with anarchist, countercultural, prankster, and conspiracy milieus. The evidence does not support describing Eris as the historical patron deity of a major ancient or modern extremist movement. Individual use of Erisian or chaos language in radical subcultures must not be converted into a generalized claim about worshippers. No documented ancient political “Eris cult” organized violence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Personification or fully individualized goddess?

The principal controversy is not whether Eris “existed” but how Greek texts personify an abstraction. Homer allows rapid movement between noun and agent: strife can be an event, a force sent by Zeus, and Ares’ gigantic sister. Hesiod makes genealogy do conceptual work. Scholars consequently differ over whether “personification” understates divine reality or “goddess” overstates institutional cult.

The evidence favors a spectrum. Eris is grammatically and narratively divine in several passages, but she lacks the stable civic cult and individualized iconographic repertoire of a major Olympian.

### One Eris or two?

Hesiod explicitly says there are two Erides in *Works and Days*, while the *Theogony* presents one destructive daughter of Nyx. Proposed solutions include:

1. *Works and Days* deliberately revises the *Theogony*.
2. The two poems reflect different traditions.
3. “Two” means ethical aspects rather than separate divine individuals.
4. The productive Eris is a poetic innovation serving Hesiod’s argument with Perses.

None can be conclusively proved. Presenting one morally ambivalent Eris as the universal Greek doctrine silently harmonizes the sources.

### Homeric sister of Ares

Some commentators treat “sister and companion” as a true genealogy; others regard it as an ad hoc expression of functional kinship. Homer supplies no parents for the pair in that passage. Hesiod’s Nyx genealogy became dominant in handbooks because it is systematic, but it does not erase Homer’s formulation.

### Did the *Cypria* contain the apple?

The lost epic’s surviving summary states that Eris creates a beauty dispute. It does not name an apple. Possibilities are:

- The original poem included the apple and Proclus omitted it.
- The original used another mechanism, and the apple entered later.
- Several *Cypria* traditions circulated.

Pseudo-Apollodorus, Hyginus, and Colluthus prove that the apple was ancient, but not that it stood in the archaic poem. Assigning the golden inscribed apple confidently to Stasinus is therefore unjustified.

### Iconographic disputes

Winged females in archaic art are difficult to distinguish without inscriptions. Berlin F 1775 is secure because ΕΡΙΣ appears beside the figure. Other proposed Erides depend on scene, pose, and comparison. The interpretation of two winged figures as Hesiod’s good and bad Strifes is ingenious rather than certain.

### Cultic minimalism

Older mythological dictionaries sometimes use “goddess” as if literary personification implied a cult. Conversely, rigidly calling Eris “only an abstraction” ignores her divine kinship, agency, and images. The defensible distinction is between **divine characterization**, which is secure, and **institutional worship**, for which evidence is minimal.

### Comparative-origin theories

No credible evidence identifies Eris as a renamed Sumerian, Akkadian, Persian, Norse, or Vedic goddess. General comparisons with Near Eastern combat figures or Indo-European abstractions may illuminate personification as a cultural technique but do not establish descent or identity. Equations with Ishtar, Kali, Lilith, or Babalon belong to modern occultism.

### Primary evidence and editions

The core evidence survives in:

- Homeric and Hesiodic manuscript traditions and ancient papyri;
- scholia and lexica preserving fragments;
- Proclus’ *Chrestomathy* summary, transmitted through Byzantine channels and the *Iliad* manuscript tradition;
- mythographic manuscripts of Pseudo-Apollodorus, Hyginus, Antoninus Liberalis, and others;
- museum pottery, especially Berlin F 1775 and Hermitage material;
- Pausanias’ description of the now-lost Chest of Cypselus.

Modern study should begin with critical editions—West for Hesiod and the Epic Cycle, modern Homeric editions, Jacoby/BNJ-type fragment collections, and LIMC for images—rather than treating compilations as primary witnesses. Timothy Gantz’s *Early Greek Myth* is particularly useful for separating early sources from later synthesis; Walter Burkert situates personification within Greek religion; Farnell is valuable but dated and must be checked against later epigraphy and archaeology. Kerényi belongs to interpretive scholarship; Graves frequently presents speculative reconstruction too confidently.

### Contested-material findings

The dedicated searches for etymology, origin, syncretism, human sacrifice, suppression, occultism, neopaganism, and political appropriation produced the following defensible conclusions:

- The deeper etymology of ἔρις is unresolved.
- No pre-Greek, Indo-European, or Near Eastern divine origin is established.
- Enyo is a functional associate, not a securely documented complete fusion.
- Roman Discordia is a literary-cultural translation with a distinct Roman reception.
- No evidence establishes human sacrifice, temple prostitution, or cult violence for Eris.
- No specific suppression or destroyed Eris sanctuary is documented.
- Ancient continuous worship into modernity is not established.
- Discordianism is a twentieth-century invented religion, though participants may practice it sincerely.
- Chaos-goddess theology, the Sacred Chao, Aneris, Law of Fives, modern correspondences, and eclectic equations are modern creations.
- No broad organized extremist appropriation specific to Eris is demonstrated.
