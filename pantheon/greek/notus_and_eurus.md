# Notus and Eurus: Research Dossier

**Class:** Male wind gods/personified directional winds (*Anemoi*) of Greek religion and poetry; in many passages they remain meteorological forces with divine agency rather than independently characterized anthropomorphic gods.

**Earliest secure individual attestations:** Both **Notus/Notos** (Νότος) and **Eurus/Euros** (Εὖρος) occur in the Homeric epics, conventionally dated to the late eighth or early seventh century BCE, although the surviving medieval manuscripts are much later. Notus also receives an explicit genealogy in Hesiod’s *Theogony*, approximately 700 BCE. Eurus does not: Hesiod conspicuously omits him from the children of Eos and Astraeus. Earlier Mycenaean Linear B tablets from Knossos attest an *a-ne-mo i-je-re-ja*, “priestess of the winds,” but neither Notus nor Eurus is named individually; those tablets establish a Bronze Age cultic category, not the identity or genealogy of either later god.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Notus

**[Securely attested early]** Greek **Νότος**, *Nótos*, Latinized **Notus**, is simultaneously the common noun “south wind,” the southern quarter, and the personified divine wind. LSJ documents the ordinary meteorological meaning from Homer onward and the opposition to Boreas. Ancient usage does not consistently mark off “the god Notus” from “the wind νότος”; personification must be inferred from grammar, agency, genealogy, direct address, or iconography.

The Roman functional equivalent is **Auster**, although Latin poets also use the borrowed **Notus**. This is a translation or literary equation, not evidence that an originally independent Roman Auster and Greek Notus possessed identical cult histories. In Ovid, Virgil, Seneca, and Statius, *Auster* and *Notus* can be poetic alternatives for the rain-bearing southern wind.

The etymology of νότος is unresolved. Connections with Greek words for moisture—*noterós*, “wet”—have been proposed, as has an Indo-European root concerning flowing or wetness; Robert Beekes regarded a pre-Greek origin as plausible. The safe conclusion is lexical, not etymological: already in early Greek the word means the south wind, frequently imagined as humid, mist-bearing, or stormy.

Documented descriptions functioning like epithets include:

- **[Securely attested early]** “Strong-hearted” or forceful among the winds, through Hesiod’s collective phrase *ánemoi krateróphrones* at *Theogony* 378–380.
- **[Securely attested early]** Rain- and autumn-storm-bearing in *Works and Days* 663–677.
- **[Later canonical elaboration]** *Hygrós* and *barýs*, “moist” and “heavy,” in Aristotelian meteorology.
- **[Late ritual poetry]** The power assigned “humid clouds” and “fruitful rains” in *Orphic Hymn* 82.
- **[Roman literary elaboration]** *Auster* as cloud-darkening, soaked, destructive to crops, or storm-bearing in Virgil and Ovid.
- **[Learned systematization]** **Leuconotus**, “White Notus,” and compound winds such as **Euronotus/Euroauster** and **Libonotus**, in increasingly fine Hellenistic and Roman wind roses. These are meteorological subdivisions, not offspring or epithets of an independently narrated god.

### Eurus

**[Securely attested early]** Greek **Εὖρος**, *Eûros* or conventionally *Euros*, Latin **Eurus**, designates an eastern or east-southeastern wind. Its exact bearing varies with period and wind system. Homer’s Eurus cannot automatically be plotted at precisely 90 degrees east. On the eight-wind scheme monumentalized at Athens and described by Vitruvius, Eurus occupies the **southeast**, while Apeliotes occupies due east. Latin authors similarly distinguish eastern *Subsolanus* from southeastern *Eurus/Vulturnus*.

The etymology is uncertain. Ancient and later suggestions linked Εὖρος with **ἠώς/ἕως**, dawn, or **αὔρα**, breeze; Vitruvius himself offers the latter association. No derivation commands consensus. It is unrelated to **εὐρύς**, “broad,” notwithstanding occasional popular confusion.

Documented descriptions include:

- **[Securely attested early]** A force opposing or colliding with Notus in Homer.
- **[Securely attested early]** A wind that melts snow deposited by Zephyrus, *Odyssey* 19.205–239.
- **[Regional cultic attestation]** “Saviour of Sparta,” **σωτὴρ Σπάρτας**, in an archaic or classical popular-song fragment preserved on the Strasbourg papyrus. The fragment invokes Eurus to come with victory and the paean cry; it is the strongest evidence for an individual, favorable Eurus outside epic meteorology.
- **[Hellenistic learned systematization]** Southeast wind in the eightfold compass.
- **[Roman literary elaboration]** Dweller toward dawn, Persia, and Nabataea in Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 1.61–66.
- **[Modern invention or oversimplification]** “God of autumn” is a modern schematic assignment made by matching four winds to four seasons. Archaic Greece commonly reckoned three seasons, and no early source gives Eurus a stable autumnal portfolio.

### Classification and equivalences

Both are **theoi/daimones in the broad Greek sense of divine natural powers**, but neither has the sustained personal mythology of Boreas. “Minor god” is a serviceable modern classification, not an ancient rank. Ancient literature alternates freely between physical wind, animate power, winged man, horse, and divine servant.

The principal historical equations are:

- Notus = Latin **Auster**, established in Roman translation and poetry by the first century BCE.
- Eurus = Latin **Eurus** or **Vulturnus/Volturnus**, depending on the wind scheme.
- Eurus ≠ Apeliotes in the eight-wind Tower scheme, though earlier or later authors sometimes use both for broadly easterly winds.
- Notus and Eurus combine lexically in **Euronotus**, the intermediate south-southeast wind. Euronotus is not evidence that the two gods merged in myth.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The Mycenaean background

**[Securely attested collective evidence; not individual attestation]** Knossos tablets KN Fp 1 and KN Fp 13, written in Linear B during the Late Bronze Age, include **a-ne-mo i-je-re-ja**, interpretable as *anemōn hiereia*, “priestess of the winds.” The tablets are administrative clay records. They demonstrate that winds could receive organized ritual attention and possess a priestess in Mycenaean Crete. They do **not** name Notus or Eurus, give a four-wind system, establish the Hesiodic genealogy, or prove continuity into any particular historical Greek cult.

### Homer

**[Securely attested early]** The *Iliad* and *Odyssey* are the earliest sources naming both winds:

- *Iliad* 2.144–146 compares an agitated assembly to the Icarian Sea stirred by **Eurus and Notus**, sent down from Zeus’s clouds.
- *Iliad* 3.10 has Notus pour mist around mountain peaks.
- *Iliad* 11.305–308 compares opposing battle lines to Zephyrus and Notus contending over a forest.
- *Iliad* 16.765–769 explicitly uses the dual for **Eurus and Notus contending with one another** over a mountain woodland. This proves that the Homeric poet understood them as distinct.
- *Iliad* 23.192–230 has the Winds feasting in Zephyrus’s house when Iris summons them to ignite Patroclus’s pyre. The specifically summoned winds are Boreas and Zephyrus, not Notus or Eurus; the latter may belong to the assembled collective but perform no named action.
- *Odyssey* 5.291–296: Poseidon raises every wind against Odysseus; Eurus and Notus fall or clash together, alongside stormy Zephyrus and wave-driving Boreas.
- *Odyssey* 5.331–332 contrasts the winds capable of wrecking Odysseus, including Notus.
- *Odyssey* 10.1–27: Aeolus keeps the courses of the winds and binds the troublesome ones in a bag. Neither Notus nor Eurus is individually named in the crucial bag passage; assigning a particular act to either is therefore inference.
- *Odyssey* 19.205–239 uses Eurus in a simile: snow piled by Zephyrus melts when Eurus blows.

Homer gives neither wind parents, consort, offspring, individualized dwelling, cult title, nor autonomous adventure. Their primary “myth” is their agency in sea, weather, woodland, and simile.

### Hesiod

**[Securely attested early]** *Theogony* 378–382 says that Eos bore to Astraeus the strong-hearted winds—Zephyrus, Boreas, and Notus—and then the Morning Star and other stars. Eurus is absent.

At *Theogony* 869–880, after Zeus defeats Typhoeus, Hesiod distinguishes the beneficial, god-sent winds—Notus, Boreas, and clear Zephyrus—from destructive, erratic winds descended from Typhoeus. Again Eurus is absent.

This omission has three possible interpretations:

1. Hesiod knew a three-wind or three-season divine scheme in which Eurus was not one of Eos’s beneficial children.
2. “Argestes” or another wind-name occupied a place later associated with Eurus.
3. The omission is poetic rather than doctrinal.

No surviving Hesiodic line authorizes silently inserting Eurus into the genealogy. The now-familiar four-brother family is a later harmonization.

**[Securely attested early]** *Works and Days* 663–677 warns sailors not to remain at sea when new wine, autumn rain, and fierce Notus arrive. Zeus’s rain and Notus’s violent sea belong to practical seasonal instruction, not a separate narrative cycle.

### Homeric Hymn to Apollo

**[Archaic/classical hymnic attestation]** At *Hymn to Pythian Apollo* 408–439, Notus blows the Cretan ship rapidly toward Crisa as Apollo directs the sailors who will become his Delphic priests. Notus functions as a favorable instrument of the god’s plan. Eurus does not appear.

### Lyric, tragedy, and comedy

- **Pindar, fr. 104b:** Boreas stirs Notus’s onset; the winds mark the singer’s control of musical force.
- **Bacchylides, fr. 13:** Dawn calms Boreas, and sailors fill their sail with Notus.
- **Strasbourg popular-song fragment 858:** Eurus is invoked as “saviour of Sparta,” with a victory prayer. **[Regional cultic or civic-poetic evidence]**
- **Sophocles, *Antigone* 335–337; *Philoctetes* 1456–1459:** Notus appears as a violent or wet weather force, not a dramatic character.
- **Aristophanes, *Birds* 1397 ff.:** the path of Notus helps map aerial movement.

The surviving tragedies do not give either wind a major myth. Claims that Aeschylus, Sophocles, or Euripides supplied a lost autonomous Notus or Eurus cycle cannot be established.

### Scientific and geographical prose

Herodotus, Aristotle, Theophrastus, Timosthenes, and later Roman technical writers discuss winds as physical and geographical phenomena. Their schemes differ. Aristotle’s *Meteorology* treats Notus as the principal southerly and subdivides adjacent winds; Eurus occupies a sector rather than an immutable modern compass point. This material is essential evidence for changing classification but not necessarily for cult.

Vitruvius, *De architectura* 1.6.4–10, reports both a four-wind and an eight-wind theory. He describes Andronikos of Kyrrhos’s octagonal marble tower, its eight sculpted winds, and the bronze Triton weather vane. In his Latin scheme Eurus lies between Solanus/east and Auster/south.

### Mythography

- **Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 1.2.3:** Eos and Astraeus produce the stars and winds collectively. Because the winds are unnamed, this cannot by itself prove that the author included Eurus in Hesiod’s genealogy.
- **Pseudo-Hyginus, preface:** lists children of Astraeus and Aurora, but the transmitted list is corrupt or duplicative, giving Zephyrus/Favonius twice in some editions. It names Notus but is unsafe evidence for a clean four-wind genealogy.
- Diodorus and Pausanias preserve wind cult and geographical traditions but no separate biography of either god.

### Orphic Hymns

**[Late ritual text]** *Orphic Hymn* 82, probably composed in Roman imperial Asia Minor, is addressed to Notus with frankincense. It asks the wing-footed power of humid clouds to send fruitful rain. Its manuscript witnesses are medieval, and the collection itself is commonly dated between the second and third centuries CE, although older elements may be embedded within it.

There is **no surviving Orphic Hymn to Eurus**. Modern lists that give all four cardinal winds matching Orphic hymns have regularized the corpus: it contains hymns to Boreas, Zephyrus, and Notus.

### Greek Magical Papyri

The extant *Papyri Graecae Magicae*, chiefly from Roman Egypt, invoke winds, directions, cosmic breath, Helios, and divine assistants. A review of the standard English collection does not disclose a securely identifiable independent cult or sustained spell-cycle of Notus or Eurus comparable to the hymn to Notus. Directional wind names can occur as cosmological vocabulary, but claims of a distinct “PGM cult of Eurus” or “Notus initiation” are unestablished.

### Later Greek and Roman poetry

- Virgil, *Georgics* 1.441–460: Notus is a rain-bearing enemy of crops, trees, and herds.
- Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.50–156: Aeolus releases storm winds at Juno’s request; Eurus and Notus participate in the assault on Aeneas’s fleet before Neptune restores order.
- Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 1.56–68: the creator stations Eurus in the dawn lands and Auster/Notus in the cloudy south.
- Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 1.262–312: Jupiter releases rain-soaked Notus for the flood of Deucalion while restraining clearing winds. This is the fullest ancient anthropomorphic portrait of Notus but is Roman Augustan elaboration, not archaic Greek tradition.
- Ovid, *Heroides* 11: Aeolus rules Notus, Zephyrus, Boreas, and “wanton” Eurus.
- Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica* 12.189 ff.: the four winds, imagined as a team, draw Zeus’s chariot while Iris guides them.
- Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 6.18–28 and 37.70: explicitly includes Eurus among the children of Eos and Astraeus. This fifth-century CE genealogy is the first surviving unambiguous extension of that parentage to Eurus.
- Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 13.381 ff.: the Libyan king Psyllus wages war on Notus after the hot wind destroys his crops; the united winds sink Psyllus’s fleet. This is **late-antique narrative elaboration**, not an archaic myth.
- Musaeus, *Hero and Leander*, sixth century CE: winds function in the fatal sea-storm, continuing the literary personification.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**Notus:** south, humidity, mist, rain, dangerous late-season sailing, turbulent sea, and—in some texts—parching heat. The apparent contradiction reflects actual Mediterranean variability and changing literary settings. Hesiod emphasizes autumnal storm; Aristotle records wetness and heaviness; Nonnus can make Notus burn Libyan crops.

**Eurus:** east or southeast, changeable maritime weather, snow-melting warmth in the *Odyssey*, and occasionally destructive or “barren” weather in later literature. No ancient source establishes a universal Eurus portfolio of “autumn, intellect, dawn magic, and the element air.” Those are modern correspondence systems.

Neither wind has securely attested sacred animals, plants, numbers, weekdays, planets, or colors. Lists giving Notus red/fire and Eurus yellow/air belong to Renaissance or modern ceremonial magic.

### Tower of the Winds

**[Secure material evidence]** The Horologion of Andronikos of Kyrrhos in Athens bears the most important surviving individual images. The exterior inscriptions and reliefs identify eight winds:

Boreas—Kaikias—Apeliotes—**Euros**—**Notos**—Lips—Zephyros—Skiron.

The building is variously dated. Older accounts commonly place it around 100–50 BCE or the mid-first century BCE. Hermann Kienast and sculpture specialist Vasiliki Karanastasi argue for the last quarter of the second century BCE; Varro and Vitruvius provide a terminus ante quem in the first century BCE. The safest formulation is **late Hellenistic, probably second to first century BCE**.

- **Eurus/Euros**, on the southeast face, is a mature winged, bearded man wrapped tightly in a heavy mantle. The bundled clothing signifies an ominous or inclement wind; he carries no unique weapon.
- **Notus**, on the south face, is winged and pours water from an upturned vessel, unmistakably signaling rain.
- Their names are cut above the reliefs. These inscriptions, catalogued as SEG 33.199, remove the uncertainty that often attends anonymous wind figures.
- The lost bronze Triton on the roof rotated in the wind and pointed toward the relevant relief with a rod.
- Sundials occupied the exterior; a water-clock operated within or beside the building.

This monument does not prove that it was a temple to the individual winds. Varro calls it a **horologium**; Vitruvius presents it as an astronomical and architectural demonstration. Its imagery is religiously personified, but its principal documented functions were meteorological and horological.

### Other imagery

**[Probable or collective identification]** Winged male winds occur on vases, reliefs, mosaics, and sarcophagi, but without inscriptions many cannot be assigned confidently to Notus or Eurus. Equine winds also appear in literature and art:

- Winds may be imagined as horses controlled by Aeolus.
- On the Pergamon Altar’s eastern frieze, horse-like winds draw Hera’s chariot; assigning each horse individually to Notus or Eurus is uncertain.
- Quintus Smyrnaeus makes all four draw Zeus’s chariot.
- Roman mosaics sometimes place busts of winds in the corners of a seasonal or cosmic composition, but individual names and bearings must be checked object by object.

The common online image of Eurus as a youthful autumn god holding grain is frequently a confusion with **Apeliotes**, who carries fruit and grain on the east face of the Tower.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical variants

**Notus—early version:** Eos + Astraeus → Notus, Boreas, Zephyrus, Eosphorus, and the stars; Hesiod, *Theogony* 378–382.

**Eurus—Hesiodic record:** absent. Hesiod does not call him their son.

**Collective mythographic version:** Eos + Astraeus → “the winds”; Pseudo-Apollodorus 1.2.3. Whether Eurus is included is likely but unstated.

**Four-wind late version:** Eos + Astraeus → Boreas, Zephyrus, Notus, Eurus; explicit in Nonnus, fifth century CE, and dominant in modern handbooks.

**Typhon distinction:** In Hesiod, irregular destructive winds descend from Typhoeus, whereas Notus, Boreas, and Zephyrus are beneficial divine winds. Since Eurus is omitted from both named groups, assigning him to Typhon is also not explicit.

Neither god has an anciently attested spouse, lover, child, servant, mount, or personal enemy. Modern genealogies supplying such relations are inventions or unsupported extrapolations.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Eurus — Notus:** contend with each other over a mountain forest; *Iliad* 16.765–769, late eighth/early seventh century BCE. **[Securely attested early]**
- **Eurus — Notus:** jointly churn the Icarian Sea; *Iliad* 2.144–146. **[Securely attested early]**
- **Eurus — Notus, Boreas, and Zephyrus:** collide in Poseidon’s storm against Odysseus; *Odyssey* 5.291–296. **[Securely attested early]**
- **Eurus — Zephyrus:** Eurus melts snow previously piled by Zephyrus; *Odyssey* 19.205–239. **[Securely attested early; simile rather than narrative encounter]**
- **Notus — Zephyrus:** oppose one another over woodland; *Iliad* 11.305–308. **[Securely attested early]**
- **Notus — Boreas:** Boreas stirs the onset of Notus; Pindar fr. 104b. **[Classical lyric]**
- **Notus — Boreas and Eos:** Boreas’s dangerous sea ceases when Eos arrives and sailors receive Notus; Bacchylides fr. 13. **[Classical lyric]**
- **Notus — Zeus:** Zeus’s heavy autumn rain accompanies Notus; *Works and Days* 663–677. **[Securely attested early]**
- **Notus — Apollo:** Notus drives Apollo’s Cretan ship toward Crisa; *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* 408 ff. **[Archaic/classical hymnic]**
- **Notus and Eurus — Poseidon:** Poseidon rouses them with all winds to wreck Odysseus; *Odyssey* 5.291–296. **[Securely attested early]**
- **Notus and Eurus — Aeolus:** Aeolus controls or confines the winds collectively; *Odyssey* 10, developed explicitly in Virgil, Ovid, and *Heroides* 11. **[Early collective relation; later canonical personalization]**
- **Notus and Eurus — Juno/Hera:** Juno orders Aeolus to release the winds against Aeneas; Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.65–86. **[Roman elaboration]**
- **Notus and Eurus — Neptune/Poseidon:** Neptune rebukes and disperses the winds after their attack on Aeneas’s fleet; *Aeneid* 1.124–156. **[Roman elaboration]**
- **Notus — Jupiter/Zeus:** Jupiter releases rain-soaked Notus to cause the Deucalion flood; Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 1.262–312. **[Roman elaboration]**
- **Notus — Deucalion and Pyrrha:** Notus helps inundate their world but never engages them personally; Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 1. **[Roman elaboration; indirect interaction]**
- **Eurus — Aurora/Eos and Astraeus:** explicitly their child only in Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 6 and 37. **[Late-antique systematization]**
- **Notus — Eos and Astraeus:** their child in Hesiod. **[Securely attested early]**
- **Notus and Eurus — Zeus, Iris, and Aion:** they form part of Zeus’s four-wind chariot-team; Iris drives or leads the team, whose chariot Aion constructed; Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica* 12.189 ff. **[Late canonical elaboration]**
- **The Winds — Zephyrus and Iris:** the winds feast in Zephyrus’s house when Iris arrives; *Iliad* 23.192–230. Notus and Eurus are not individually named in the action. **[Secure collective scene; individual participation uncertain]**
- **Notus — the allied Winds and Psyllus:** Notus destroys Psyllus’s crops; the winds jointly sink his retaliatory fleet; Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 13.381 ff. **[Late-antique accretion]**

No documented interaction with Aphrodite, Hades, Athena, Artemis, Dionysus, Hecate, or the zodiac has been found merely because those beings later appear in elemental correspondence tables.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The conflict of winds

The oldest “story” is not an autonomous legend but a recurrent epic image: winds oppose one another, tear forests, and confuse the sea. In *Iliad* 16, Eurus and Notus contend while trees strain and branches crash. In *Odyssey* 5, Poseidon gathers the four principal winds into the storm that threatens Odysseus.

**Development:** Homer treats conflict among winds as an intelligible physical process rendered divine. Ovid later turns the same idea into cosmology: the world’s maker assigns each wind a separate quarter because their fraternal conflict could tear the cosmos apart.

### Aeolus and the confined winds

In *Odyssey* 10, Aeolus, dear to the gods, knows or controls the winds’ courses. He confines unfavorable winds in an oxhide bag and sends the favorable one to carry Odysseus home. The sailors open the bag, and the released winds drive the ship back.

Notus and Eurus are not individually named in this episode. Roman poetry increasingly treats all named winds as Aeolus’s subjects imprisoned in caverns. Virgil’s *Aeneid* gives the system its most influential form: Aeolus holds the winds in a mountain prison until Juno purchases their release.

**Historical layer:** Homeric collective myth → Augustan monumentalization → Renaissance and modern standard image of “Aeolus, god/king of the winds.” Aeolus should not be retroactively treated as the biological father of Notus or Eurus.

### Notus and the flood

Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* 1 gives Notus his most substantial narrative role. Jupiter decides to destroy humanity by flood. He confines the dry, cloud-dispersing north wind and releases Notus, whose wings, beard, hair, and body stream with water. Notus crushes the clouds; rain falls; Neptune summons the rivers; Deucalion and Pyrrha survive.

**[Roman elaboration]** Neither Hesiod nor Apollodorus assigns the Greek Deucalion flood to Notus in this graphic way. The claim that “Notus destroyed the Bronze Age” is therefore misleading unless specifically attributed to Ovid’s Roman retelling.

### Psyllus’s war against Notus

Nonnus relates that hot Notus scorched the crops of Psyllus in Libya. Psyllus launches a fleet to attack the southern winds. The winds combine and bury the expedition at sea.

**[Late-antique accretion]** This myth reflects both ethnogeography and the literary motif of a mortal warring against nature. It is not present in archaic or classical sources.

### Zeus’s wind-chariot

Quintus Smyrnaeus depicts Zeus riding a chariot drawn by the four winds, guided by Iris and manufactured by Aion. The winds are probably imagined as horses.

**[Late canonical elaboration]** The episode expands earlier equine wind imagery; it is not a classical cult doctrine.

### Negative finding

Eurus has no surviving autonomous seduction, combat, foundation, or rescue myth. Notus has none before Roman and late-antique elaboration. Their relative absence from narrative is genuine evidence of their position within Greek tradition, not a lacuna to be repaired with invented biographies.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Collective wind cult

**[Mycenaean epigraphic evidence]** The Knossos “priestess of the winds” is the earliest evidence for institutional wind cult, but continuity into later worship is possible rather than proven.

**[Classical historical evidence]** Herodotus 7.178–189 reports that during Xerxes’ invasion the Delphians advised the Greeks to pray to the winds as allies. The Athenians sacrificed to Boreas specifically because of his marriage to Oreithyia and later established an altar by the Ilissus. This documents the efficacy and cult of winds but privileges Boreas, not Notus or Eurus.

**[Epigraphic/regional evidence]** The Strasbourg fragment invoking “Eurus, saviour of Sparta” suggests a local protective or victory-bearing role for Eurus. Whether it accompanied sacrifice or a recurring festival is unknown.

**[Roman-imperial testimony to Greek cult]** Pausanias 2.12.1 records an altar of the Winds below Athena’s sanctuary at Titane. A priest sacrificed there one night annually, performed secret rites at four pits, chanted reputed charms of Medea, and sought to tame the winds. Pausanias 9.34.3 records an altar of the Winds in Coronea’s marketplace.

These are collective altars. Neither passage distinguishes offerings to Notus from those to Eurus.

An Athenian cult calendar of the first century CE reportedly prescribes the Winds a twelve-knobbed cake without wine. This again concerns the collective.

### Temples and priesthood

No securely documented independent temple of Notus or Eurus is known. “Sanctuary of the Winds” at Titane may mean an altar complex rather than a roofed temple. The Tower of the Winds was a horologion, not demonstrably their temple.

The only firmly named office is the Mycenaean priestess of the winds and Pausanias’s later priest at Titane. Recruitment, tenure, privileges, and revenues are not preserved.

### Sacrifice, magic, and coercive rites

Ancient wind ritual could involve ordinary offerings, cakes, animal sacrifice, pits, prayer, charms, or attempts to bind and divert hostile weather. Literary and comparative evidence also records severe rites undertaken to obtain favorable winds.

Roland Hampe’s *Kult der Winde in Athen und Kreta* argued for chthonic features in wind sacrifice and discussed traditions of human sacrifice to appease winds. The evidence, however, does **not** establish a human sacrifice specifically to Notus or Eurus. Stories such as Iphigenia at Aulis concern Artemis’s control of the winds, while other propitiatory killings belong to legendary military contexts. They cannot be reassigned to these two gods.

No evidence supports temple prostitution associated with either wind. No cult violence, compulsory conversion in their name, or autonomous political priesthood is documented.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Late Bronze Age, approximately 1400–1200 BCE

Collective wind cult is documented at Knossos through the Linear B “priestess of the winds.” Individual Notus and Eurus are unattested.

### Archaic period, approximately 750–500 BCE

Homer names both as active natural/divine forces. Hesiod makes Notus a son of Eos and Astraeus but omits Eurus. Agricultural and maritime poetry fixes Notus’s association with mist, rain, and dangerous autumn seas. A Spartan song invokes Eurus as savior.

### Classical period, approximately 500–323 BCE

Lyric and drama continue chiefly meteorological uses. Philosophers systematize wind direction and physical qualities. Collective propitiation of winds is historically visible, especially in wartime and seafaring. No independent narrative cult of either god emerges.

### Hellenistic period, 323–31 BCE

Geographers and meteorologists multiply named compass winds. The eightfold system distinguishes Eurus as southeast from Apeliotes as east. The Tower of the Winds monumentalizes this learned cosmic order, probably in the later second or first century BCE.

### Roman imperial period

Greek wind figures are translated into Latin literary vocabulary: Notus/Auster and Eurus/Vulturnus. Virgil and Ovid enlarge their narrative roles. Pausanias records collective altars and ritual. The *Orphic Hymns* preserve actual ritual invocation of Notus but none to Eurus.

### Late antiquity

Quintus Smyrnaeus and Nonnus produce the fullest divine narratives: the equine chariot-team, Eurus’s explicit parentage, and Psyllus’s war against Notus. These works reflect learned epic systematization rather than newly attested mass cult.

### Christian and Byzantine eras

There is no documented empire-wide campaign directed specifically at Notus or Eurus. Their cult disappeared with the wider transformation and suppression of traditional civic sacrifice. The words survived in literature, lexica, navigation, meteorology, and the manuscript tradition.

The Tower of the Winds appears to have acquired Christian use, possibly as a baptistery, bell tower, or martyrium; exact functions remain disputed. Reuse is evidence of institutional transformation, not proof that Christians targeted the wind reliefs for iconoclasm.

### Ottoman era

The Tower was used by Mevlevi or other Sufi dervishes as a place of worship or gathering. Its partial burial helped preserve the lower structure. Reports that this use deterred Lord Elgin from removing it are plausible modern historical tradition, not ancient evidence.

### Modern period

Excavation and clearance in the nineteenth century restored the Tower to archaeological visibility. Classical editions, museums, lexica, and comparative mythology transformed Notus and Eurus from locally variable winds into standardized members of a four-god set.

Contemporary Hellenic polytheists sometimes honor the Anemoi, including Notus and Eurus. This is **modern revived worship**, not demonstrable institutional continuity from antiquity. Its scale is not reliably quantified.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Renaissance and early modern occultism

Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa’s *Three Books of Occult Philosophy* (1533) gives the four principal winds as Eurus/east, Notus/south, Zephyrus/west, and Boreas/north. Agrippa incorporates classical natural philosophy into a Christian occult cosmos. His table is an early-modern synthesis, not evidence for an ancient elemental ritual.

Subsequent magical correspondence systems aligned:

- Eurus—east—air—dawn;
- Notus—south—fire—heat;
- Zephyrus—west—water;
- Boreas—north—earth.

This arrangement conflicts with ancient Notus’s especially strong connection with water and rain, showing that ceremonial symmetry, not documentary Greek cult, produced it.

### Golden Dawn and successor traditions

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn’s directional and elemental rituals influenced modern occultism, Thelema, neopagan circle-casting, and popular “four winds” invocations. Notus and Eurus are sometimes substituted for archangels or used alongside them. Specific scripts circulating online are modern compositions.

Ophiel’s *The Art and Practice of Astral Projection* directs practitioners to vibrate “EURUS” in the east and “NOTUS” in the south while visualizing air and tropical fire. This is a twentieth-century occult exercise and reverses or simplifies several ancient meteorological traits.

Claims that the Golden Dawn preserved a secret, continuous Notus-Eurus mystery cult are unsupported.

### Modern paganism and popular mythology

Modern Hellenic reconstructionists may offer incense, water, wine, or prayer to the winds. The Orphic hymn provides an ancient model for Notus; Eurus worship must rely more heavily on Homer, the Spartan fragment, collective Anemoi ritual, and modern reconstruction.

Frequently repeated modern inventions include:

- Eurus as unambiguously “the god of autumn.”
- Notus as exclusively hot and dry.
- Fixed sacred colors, crystals, tarot cards, zodiac signs, weekdays, and planets.
- Romantic partners or children for either god.
- Notus as the canonical destroyer of an entire Greek “Bronze Age race,” without acknowledging that the specific flood role is Ovidian.
- Eurus shown with grain: usually a confusion with Apeliotes.
- All four winds having Orphic hymns.
- Aeolus as their father rather than their keeper or ruler.

### Literature, games, and commerce

Notus and Eurus appear as names for ships, aircraft, businesses, weather systems, fictional characters, game attacks, and fantasy wind spirits. Such uses usually draw on the lexical meanings “south wind” and “east wind,” not ancient narrative. “Eurus” in modern fiction may be a personal name wholly detached from the Greek god. No single modern film or novel has reshaped their reception as decisively as Ovid’s rain-soaked Notus or the Tower reliefs.

No substantial nationalist or extremist appropriation specific to either deity was found. Generic classical imagery may be used politically, but attributing such use to a Notus or Eurus cult would exceed the evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Were they gods or poetic personifications?

Older positivist scholarship sometimes separated “real cult gods” from literary personification. The evidence resists that binary. The winds receive priests, altars, sacrifice, hymns, and direct prayer, yet the same names remain ordinary weather nouns. Greek religion did not require a natural force to cease being physical before it could be divine.

### Did Mycenaean wind cult continue into historical Greece?

The Linear B priestess makes Bronze Age cult certain; direct institutional continuity is not. Roland Hampe and later scholars identify suggestive parallels in Cretan and Athenian practice, chthonic sacrifice, pits, and wind-taming. The chronological break and absence of individually named Mycenaean winds prevent proof.

### Why does Hesiod omit Eurus?

This is the central genealogical dispute. Proposed answers include a three-season system, varying archaic wind roses, Eurus’s unfavorable status, identification with Argestes or Apeliotes, or simple poetic selection. The omission cannot be emended away. Nonnus’s four-son genealogy is more than a millennium later.

### Where exactly did Eurus blow from?

Homeric geography does not map neatly onto the later eight-point compass. Nineteenth-century writers attempted to derive precise bearings from the Icarian Sea and snow-melting passages, sometimes placing Eurus north of east or broadly southeast. Aristotle, Timosthenes, the Tower, Vitruvius, and Roman authors use different systems. “East wind” is the broad translation; “southeast wind” is correct for the Tower and many Roman systems.

### How should the Tower be dated?

Older scholarship favored the mid-first century BCE. Kienast’s architectural study and Karanastasi’s sculpture analysis favor the last quarter of the second century BCE. Letter forms are not decisive; the building’s unusual architecture lacks close chronological parallels. Varro and Vitruvius establish that it existed by the mid-first century BCE.

### Primary evidence and editions

The Homeric and Hesiodic texts survive mainly through Byzantine manuscripts but are reconstructed through papyri, quotations, scholia, and medieval codices. Important standard resources include:

- Martin L. West’s critical editions of Hesiod and Homeric poetry.
- Allen and Munro, and later Oxford editions of Homer.
- Evelyn-White’s public-domain Loeb translations for Hesiod and the Homeric Hymns.
- Francis Vian’s Budé edition of Nonnus.
- Hans Dieter Betz’s *Greek Magical Papyri in Translation*.
- *LIMC* entries and WebLIMC for wind iconography.
- *SEG* and Attic Inscriptions Online for the Tower inscriptions.
- Timothy Gantz, *Early Greek Myth*, valuable precisely because it separates early evidence from later handbooks.
- Walter Burkert, *Greek Religion*, for the ritual and institutional setting.
- Lewis Richard Farnell, *The Cults of the Greek States*, now dated in method but still useful as an evidence collection.
- Roland Hampe, *Kult der Winde in Athen und Kreta*.
- Hermann Kienast, *Der Turm der Winde in Athen*.
- Pamela A. Webb, *The Tower of the Winds in Athens—Greeks, Romans, Christians, and Muslims*.

Kerényi is useful as twentieth-century synthetic interpretation. Robert Graves is reception history, not a safe guide to chronology or cult: his harmonizations and ritualist conjectures must be checked against the cited ancient passage.

Theoi.com is a useful passage index, but its headings sometimes turn contested inferences—such as seasonal assignments or dwellings—into declarative summaries. Perseus, the TLG, Packard Humanities Institute, papyrological databases, and critical editions remain preferable for verification.

## 10. Chronology

- **Late Bronze Age, approximately 1400–1200 BCE:** Knossos Linear B tablets record a “priestess of the winds.” Neither Notus nor Eurus is individually named.
- **Late eighth–early seventh century BCE:** Homer names Notus and Eurus as distinct, contending winds in the *Iliad* and *Odyssey*.
- **Approximately 700 BCE:** Hesiod makes Notus a son of Eos and Astraeus; Eurus is omitted.
- **Seventh–sixth century BCE, with later textual development:** *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* makes Notus propel Apollo’s Cretan ship.
- **Archaic/classical period:** A Spartan song invokes Eurus as “saviour of Sparta.”
- **Fifth century BCE:** Pindar, Bacchylides, Sophocles, Herodotus, and Aristophanes attest winds in poetry, weather, navigation, and cult.
- **Fourth century BCE:** Aristotle systematizes wind directions and physical qualities; his scheme does not simply equal the later Tower’s eight points.
- **Late second–first century BCE:** Tower of the Winds built at Athens; inscribed reliefs securely identify Euros on the southeast and Notos on the south.
- **37 BCE:** Varro’s *De re rustica* mentions the Athenian horologion.
- **Late first century BCE:** Vitruvius describes Andronikos’s tower and eight-wind system.
- **29–19 BCE:** Virgil’s *Aeneid* gives Eurus and Notus roles in Aeolus’s storm.
- **Approximately 8 CE:** Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* assigns the winds cosmic quarters and makes Notus the rain-agent of Jupiter’s flood.
- **First–second centuries CE:** Greek and Roman technical literature continues to refine compass systems; collective wind offerings appear in an Athenian calendar.
- **Second century CE:** Pausanias records altars and rites to the Winds at Titane and Coronea.
- **Second–third centuries CE, probable:** *Orphic Hymn* 82 invokes Notus for fruitful rain; no corresponding Eurus hymn exists.
- **Fourth century CE:** Quintus Smyrnaeus presents the four winds as Zeus’s chariot-team.
- **Fifth century CE:** Nonnus explicitly makes Eurus a child of Eos and Astraeus and tells Psyllus’s war against Notus.
- **Sixth century CE:** Musaeus continues the winds’ late-antique literary role in *Hero and Leander*.
- **Byzantine period:** Ancient cult lapses; names survive in manuscripts, lexica, meteorology, and the reused Tower.
- **Ottoman period:** Dervishes use the Tower of the Winds; its reliefs remain visible while the structure becomes partly buried.
- **1751–1762:** James Stuart and Nicholas Revett survey and publish the Tower, strongly influencing European neoclassicism.
- **1830s–1840s:** Archaeological clearance exposes the Tower more fully.
- **1860s–1967:** Modern scholarship increasingly connects literary, Mycenaean, and archaeological evidence; Hampe publishes *Kult der Winde in Athen und Kreta*.
- **1981–1999:** *LIMC* systematizes ancient iconographic evidence.
- **2014:** Kienast’s major architectural reassessment argues for a late-second-century BCE date for the Tower.
- **2017:** Pamela Webb publishes the Tower’s long history of Greek, Roman, Christian, and Muslim reuse.
- **Present:** Notus and Eurus remain subjects of classical study, weather nomenclature, modern occult correspondences, popular media, and small-scale revived polytheist worship.

### Evidentiary conclusion

The reconstructable record is asymmetrical. Notus is an archaically genealogized god, a practical weather danger, an occasional favorable sailing wind, a recipient of a late ritual hymn, and eventually a Roman and late-antique narrative actor. Eurus is equally old as a named Homeric force and may have possessed a favorable local Spartan invocation, but his genealogy, seasonal identity, and standardized southeast iconography are later constructions. Their paired identity rests principally on Homeric opposition, learned wind systems, and the Tower of the Winds—not on a shared cycle of personal myths.

The surviving evidence supports collective worship of winds much more strongly than independent cults of Notus and Eurus. No individual temples, spouses, descendants, sacred calendars, mysteries, human sacrifices, temple prostitution, or continuous secret cult can be established for either. Their thin individual mythology is not a defect in the record: it is the central historical fact about the “least storied” Anemoi.
