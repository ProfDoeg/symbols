# The Anemoi: Research Dossier

**Class:** A collective of personified wind gods or daimones (*ἄνεμοι*, “winds”), sometimes treated simply as natural winds; in later scientific, artistic, and mythographic systems, a directional group of four, eight, twelve, or more named winds.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The Knossian Linear B tablets KN Fp 1 and KN Fp 13, Late Bronze Age, conventionally assigned to the fourteenth–thirteenth centuries BCE, record *a-ne-mo i-je-re-ja*, conventionally interpreted as *anemōn hiereiai*, “for/to the priestess of the Winds.” KN Fp 1 records an allocation of oil. This securely establishes an institutional “Winds” cult or office in Mycenaean Crete but does **not** name Boreas, Notus, Eurus, or Zephyrus, supply a genealogy, or prove that the later directional system already existed. The individual name Zephyrus may also occur in Linear B as *ze-pu₂-ro*, but its interpretation and whether it denotes the deity rather than the wind require more caution than the priestess title.

## Evidentiary key

- **[Early attested]** Mycenaean through Archaic evidence, approximately fourteenth–sixth centuries BCE.
- **[Classical elaboration]** Fifth–fourth centuries BCE.
- **[Hellenistic/Roman systematization]** Third century BCE–fourth century CE.
- **[Regional variant]** Local cult, nomenclature, or narrative not demonstrably pan-Hellenic.
- **[Late antique/medieval accretion]** Late antiquity through the Middle Ages.
- **[Early-modern reception]** Renaissance through eighteenth century.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** Scholarly inference rather than an explicit ancient statement.
- **[Modern reinvention]** Modern religious, occult, literary, commercial, or popular-cultural construction.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The collective

Greek **ἄνεμος**, *ánemos*, means “wind”; the plural **ἄνεμοι**, *Ánemoi*, means “winds.” In literary Greek the same words can denote meteorological winds, personified powers, or gods. Context—not capitalization in modern editions—determines the degree of personification. Homer can make winds clash like weather in one passage and feast in a house, hear prayer, and accept promised offerings in another.

The Roman collective is **Venti**, singular **Ventus**, likewise simultaneously “winds” and divine personifications. Roman literature usually translates or adapts Greek directional names rather than preserving a single one-to-one pantheon:

| Direction/system | Greek | Common Latin correspondence | Caution |
|---|---|---|---|
| North | Βορέας, *Boreas*; sometimes Ἀπαρκτίας, *Aparktias* | Aquilo; Septentrio in meteorological systems | Aquilo can be northeast in an eight-wind scheme; equivalence varies |
| South | Νότος, *Notos* | Auster | Comparatively stable |
| West | Ζέφυρος, *Zephyros* | Favonius | Favonius becomes especially associated with mild spring weather |
| East/southeast | Εὖρος, *Euros* | Eurus or Vulturnus | Eurus is not consistently due east |
| Northeast | Καικίας, *Kaikias* | Aquilo/Vulturnus, depending system | Systems conflict |
| Equinoctial east | Ἀπηλιώτης, *Apeliotes* | Subsolanus/Solanus | Tower iconography treats him as fruitful |
| Southwest | Λίψ, *Lips* | Africus | Moist or storm-bearing |
| Northwest | Σκίρων, *Skiron*, or Ἀργέστης, *Argestes* | Caurus/Corus | Local and authorial variation |

### The principal four

#### Boreas — Βορέας

Boreas is the cold, violent north or north-northeast wind, characteristically imagined as coming from Thrace. Ancient folk etymologies and modern proposals connect the name with roaring, devouring, or mountains, but no derivation commands consensus. Claims that it simply means “devourer” or “mountain wind” should be labeled conjectural.

His recurrent descriptions include “swift,” “stormy,” “cold,” and “Thracian.” Roman poets commonly call him **Aquilo**, though Aquilo’s precise compass position changes with the author. Boreas is the most individualized Anemos: husband or abductor of Oreithyia, father of the Boreads, cultic ally of Athens, and a recognizable winged figure in art.

#### Notus — Νότος

Notus is the south wind, especially a bearer of moisture, cloud, and dangerous rain. Comparison with Greek *nótios*, “southern; moist,” is plausible, but deeper etymology is uncertain. Latin **Auster** is its usual equivalent. The mild modern expression “southerly breeze” badly represents much ancient poetry: Homeric and Roman Notus/Auster regularly belongs to shipwreck, obscured skies, and torrential rain.

#### Zephyrus — Ζέφυρος

Zephyrus is the west wind. The word occurs early and may be represented by Linear B *ze-pu₂-ro*. Its ultimate etymology is disputed; an often-repeated connection with **ζόφος**, *zóphos*, “western darkness,” is suggestive rather than demonstrated.

Zephyrus can be violent in Homer but becomes increasingly the gentle, fertile, spring-bearing west wind in lyric, Hellenistic, Roman, and later art. Latin **Favonius**, from a word associated with favoring or fostering, accentuates the beneficent aspect. The English common noun “zephyr,” a gentle breeze, is therefore a reception-history narrowing of a more ambivalent ancient figure.

#### Eurus — Εὖρος

Eurus is the most unstable member of the modern “cardinal four.” Homer knows him, but Hesiod omits him from the children of Eos and Astraeus. Depending on the scheme, Eurus is east, east-southeast, or southeast. Aristotle and Vitruvius place him southeast; equinoctial east belongs to Apeliotes/Subsolanus. Claims that Eurus was canonically “the god of autumn” are modern tidying: ancient sources do not establish a universal four-winds/four-seasons grid.

### The Tower’s eight

The reliefs and inscriptions of the Athenian Horologion of Andronikos of Kyrrhos, the “Tower of the Winds,” formalized an influential eightfold arrangement:

- **Boreas, north:** bearded, heavily clothed, blowing through a conch.
- **Kaikias, northeast:** empties hailstones from a shield.
- **Apeliotes, east:** a youthful bearer of fruit and grain.
- **Eurus, southeast:** bearded and wrapped in a heavy cloak.
- **Notus, south:** empties a water vessel, signifying rain.
- **Lips, southwest:** holds or pushes part of a ship, conventionally its stern.
- **Zephyrus, west:** youthful, lightly clad, scattering flowers.
- **Skiron, northwest:** bearded, emptying a vessel associated with harsh weather.

This is **[Hellenistic/Roman systematization]**, not evidence that Homer possessed the same eightfold taxonomy.

### Classification

Homeric winds cross the boundary between phenomenon and person: they are *anemoi* that strike the sea, but Boreas and Zephyrus also inhabit a house and respond to Iris. Hesiod’s beneficial winds are divine children of divine parents, while destructive squalls derive from Typhoeus. Later lexicographers call winds gods or daimones. “Minor gods” is a useful modern shorthand, not a formal ancient rank.

Aeolus is not interchangeable with the Anemoi. In the *Odyssey* he is a mortal or quasi-divine ruler favored by the gods who confines and dispenses winds. Virgil’s *Aeneid* makes him a more explicitly divine prison-keeper under Jupiter’s authority. The popular formulation “Aeolus is the wind god and the Anemoi are his sons” is not the early Greek genealogy.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Mycenaean evidence

**KN Fp 1 and KN Fp 13 — Linear B, Knossos, Late Bronze Age. [Early attested]**  
The tablets are palace-accounting documents, not myths. KN Fp 1 distributes oil in a religious context to recipients including deities, “all the gods,” and the “priestess of the Winds.” The title implies recognized cult personnel and an economically visible institution. It does not disclose the rite, the Winds’ number, or their names. The Heraklion Archaeological Museum identifies the priestess as a major member of the Knossian priesthood.

### Homeric corpus, conventionally eighth–seventh centuries BCE

**The *Iliad*. [Early attested]**

- 2.145–149: Zephyrus and Notus agitate a field; the winds function as simile and meteorological force.
- 5.524–527; 9.4–7: Boreas and Zephyrus rush from Thrace and raise waves.
- 16.148–151: Zephyrus fathers Achilles’ immortal horses Xanthus and Balius by the Harpy Podarge.
- 20.221–229: Boreas, in the shape of a dark-maned stallion, impregnates the mares of Dardanian Erichthonius, producing twelve extraordinary fillies.
- 23.192–230: Achilles prays and pours libations to Boreas and Zephyrus, promising sacrifice so that Patroclus’ pyre will burn. Iris finds all the winds feasting in Zephyrus’ house; Boreas and Zephyrus cross the Thracian sea and kindle the fire. This is the clearest early narrative treating them as social and cult-responsive divine persons.

**The *Odyssey*. [Early attested]**

- 5.291–332: Poseidon raises Boreas, Eurus, Notus, and Zephyrus together against Odysseus.
- 10.1–79: Aeolus confines the courses of the other winds in a hide bag and releases the favorable west wind to carry Odysseus home; the crew opens the bag and the released winds return the ship to Aeolia.
- 12.325–426 and elsewhere: Eurus, Notus, and Zephyrus appear as weather powers affecting navigation.
- 19.200–206: wind imagery juxtaposes Boreas and Zephyrus.

Homer supplies no genealogy for the four and does not present a stable compass diagram.

### Hesiod, approximately 700 BCE

**The *Theogony* 371–382, 869–880. [Early attested]**  
Eos (Dawn) and Astraeus (the Starry) produce “strong-hearted” Zephyrus, Boreas, and Notus, followed by the morning star and stars. Eurus is conspicuously absent. After Zeus defeats Typhoeus, destructive, damp, erratic winds issue from Typhoeus, explicitly excepting Notus, Boreas, and “clear” Zephyrus. These three are beneficial because they perform a divinely appointed seasonal function; Typhoeus’ winds bring ruin at sea and on land.

Some translations insert Argestes among the lawful winds at 870, reflecting textual or interpretive variation. The governing distinction remains ordered directional winds versus ruinous squalls.

**Works and Days 504–535. [Early attested]**  
Boreas becomes the harsh winter wind that bends forests, attacks livestock, and chills people. This is agricultural calendar poetry, not a separate myth.

### Lyric, tragedy, and early mythography

- **Alcaeus fr. 327, seventh–sixth century BCE:** a fragmentary genealogy reportedly makes Eros the child of Zephyrus and Iris. **[Regional/poetic variant]**
- **Acusilaus, late sixth–early fifth century BCE, fragments:** preserves a variant in which Heracles kills the Boreads. **[Early mythographic variant]**
- **Simonides fr. 535, sixth–fifth century BCE:** a ritual song summons a favorable wind for the Athenian sacred ship. The surviving testimony is later, so the precise wording and setting remain mediated.
- **Aeschylus, *Agamemnon* 692–699, 458 BCE:** metaphorically calls Helen a child of destructive forces including Zephyrus; it is poetic characterization, not a stable genealogy.
- **Sophocles, *Antigone* 944–987, 442/441 BCE:** recalls Cleopatra, daughter of Boreas, and the remote Thracian landscape.
- **Pindar, fragments and victory poetry, early fifth century BCE:** employs Boreas, Zephyrus, and Notus as powerful directional and climatic agents.

### Historical prose and cult testimony

**Herodotus, *Histories* 7.178–189, c. 430s–420s BCE. [Classical attested cult]**  
Before Xerxes’ invasion, Delphi receives an oracle to pray to the Winds. The Delphians establish an altar at Thyia and continue propitiatory sacrifice. Athenians identify Boreas as their “brother-in-law” through Oreithyia, sacrifice to Boreas and Oreithyia during the naval crisis of 480 BCE, and afterward establish a sanctuary by the Ilissos. Herodotus explicitly declines to assert that prayer caused the storm; he reports what Athenians said.

### Hellenistic and Roman mythography

- **Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica*, third century BCE:** Zetes and Calais, winged sons of Boreas and Oreithyia, pursue the Harpies and rescue Phineus.
- **Pseudo-Aristotle, *On the Universe* 394b, Hellenistic or Roman date:** gives a learned twelve-wind arrangement. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Diodorus Siculus 4.43–44, first century BCE:** incorporates the Boreads into the Argonautic narrative.
- **Virgil, *Georgics* and *Aeneid*, first century BCE:** Romanizes the winds as Venti under Aeolus; Juno purchases Aeolus’ release of a storm against Aeneas.
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 1 and 6, c. 8 CE:** assigns cosmic quarters to the four winds, depicts Auster/Notus as the deluge’s rain-bearer, and narrates Boreas’ violent abduction of Oreithyia.
- **Ovid, *Fasti* 5, c. 8 CE:** Flora/Chloris makes Zephyrus her husband and attributes floral fertility to their union. **[Roman elaboration]**
- **Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 1.9 and 3.15, first–second century CE:** systematizes the parentage and children of Boreas and the Argonautic role of the Boreads.
- **Hyginus, *Fabulae*, first–second century CE in transmitted form:** gives Latinized genealogies of Aquilo/Boreas and the Boreads.
- **Pausanias, *Description of Greece*, c. 150–180 CE:** records altars, sacrifices, local rites, and images, including Boreas with serpent tails on the Chest of Cypselus.
- **Aelian, *On Animals* 4.6, second–third century CE:** reports the belief that mares could conceive from Boreas or Notus.
- **Philostratus, *Imagines* 1.24, early third century CE:** makes Zephyrus jealous of Apollo over Hyacinthus. This detail is late, not part of the earliest Hyacinthus tradition.
- **Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica*, probably fourth century CE:** uses equine winds and elaborates late epic genealogies.
- **Nonnus, *Dionysiaca*, fifth century CE:** expands divine genealogies, including Pothos as child of Zephyrus and Iris. **[Late-antique elaboration]**

### Orphic Hymns

The collection is generally dated to the second or third century CE, probably from western Asia Minor and intended for ritual performance. Hymns 79–81 address Boreas, Zephyrus, and Notus with frankincense offerings:

- Boreas is asked to restrain cold and clear the sky.
- Zephyrus is “sea-born,” vernal, grassy, gentle, and favorable to ships.
- Notus governs moisture-bearing clouds and fertile rain.

Eurus has no corresponding surviving hymn. These hymns are real ancient ritual texts but are neither Bronze Age nor the work of a historical Orpheus.

### Greek magical papyri

The PGM, principally manuscripts from Roman Egypt dated second century BCE–fifth century CE, invoke directional winds within cosmological and syncretistic operations. A magical prayer to a supreme being “of the four winds” fuses wind cosmology with solar, Hermetic, Egyptian, and daimonic language. Such material demonstrates ritual reuse of wind directions; it does not prove a continuous secret “Anemoi mystery tradition” from Mycenaean Greece.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and powers

The Anemoi govern directional air movement, seasonal weather, navigation, agricultural moisture, cold, and storms. Their power is real but limited:

- They can raise or flatten seas and accelerate or destroy ships.
- They kindle fire, as at Patroclus’ pyre.
- They carry rain, cloud, hail, flowers, grain, and seasonal change.
- They can be confined by Aeolus and commanded or released by Zeus, Poseidon, Hera/Juno, or another superior deity.
- They can fertilize mares in poetic and natural-historical tradition.
- Their arrival may be summoned or averted by sacrifice, prayer, song, charm, or apotropaic rite.

There is no ancient canonical allocation of sacred colors, weekdays, numbers, or planets to the four principal Anemoi. Modern tables that assign such correspondences are occult or neopagan constructions.

### Form

Early epic does not fix a single body type. Wind may be invisible force, anthropomorphic diner, flying divine being, stallion, or horse-breeding breath.

By Classical vase painting, Boreas and Zephyrus are commonly winged males. Boreas tends to be mature, bearded, vigorous, and Thracian in costume; Zephyrus tends to be youthful. Pausanias’ account of the archaic Chest of Cypselus says Boreas had serpent tails instead of feet, demonstrating that wings were not his only ancient form.

### Major surviving images

- **Chest of Cypselus at Olympia**, traditionally early sixth century BCE, known only from Pausanias 5.19: Boreas carries Oreithyia and has serpent tails. The chest is lost. **[Literarily attested archaic image]**
- **Attic red-figure vases, especially c. 480–430 BCE:** Boreas pursues or seizes Oreithyia. A British Museum column krater of about 460 BCE presents him as a bearded, winged Thracian; an oinochoe shows the seizure while Erechtheus mourns.
- **Zephyrus and Hyacinthus vases, fifth century BCE:** a winged adult embraces or carries a youth. Some identifications are disputed because uninscribed pairs might instead be Eros and a generic youth.
- **Tower of the Winds**, Athens, approximately 100–50 BCE, though proposed dates vary: eight inscribed winged male winds differentiated by age, clothing, and meteorological attribute. This is the single most influential surviving Anemoi monument.
- **Greco-Roman seasonal mosaics**, especially from the eastern Mediterranean: wind heads or winged busts accompany seasons, months, or agricultural abundance.
- **Roman sarcophagi and cosmological reliefs:** winds frequently appear as winged heads blowing from corners.
- **Pergamon Altar:** claims that all four principal Anemoi appear unequivocally as Hera’s chariot horses are interpretive rather than epigraphically certain; the surviving frieze does contain divine chariots and cosmic personifications, but specific equine identifications should not be treated as secure without the relevant reconstruction.

### The Tower’s function

Vitruvius, *De architectura* 1.6.4–7, describes Andronikos’ octagonal marble tower and its eight winds. A bronze Triton on the roof acted as a vane, pointing toward the currently blowing wind. Sundials occupied the exterior; an internal hydraulic installation is generally understood as a water clock.

The building is consequently both scientific and religious-iconographic. Calling it a “temple of the winds” overstates the evidence: its documented ancient identity is a **horologion**, a timekeeping and meteorological monument, not a conventional temple with an established priesthood and sacrificial cult.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Principal genealogy

**Hesiodic genealogy [Early attested]:**

- Parents: **Eos**, Dawn, and **Astraeus**, the Starry.
- Children explicitly named: **Zephyrus, Boreas, Notus**.
- Siblings: the morning star and other stars.
- Destructive storm winds: children or products of **Typhoeus**, specifically distinguished from the orderly three.

**Later four-wind genealogy [Hellenistic/Roman systematization]:**

- Boreas, Zephyrus, Notus, and Eurus become a standard sibling tetrad, all conventionally assigned to Eos and Astraeus.
- This repairs Hesiod’s omission of Eurus but is not what the extant *Theogony* says.

**Boreas’ family:**

- Consort/abductee: **Oreithyia**, daughter of Erechtheus.
- Sons: **Zetes and Calais**, the winged Boreads.
- Daughters: **Cleopatra** and **Chione**.
- Through Chione and Poseidon: grandson **Eumolpus**.
- Through Cleopatra and Phineus: grandsons variously named Plexippus and Pandion.

**Zephyrus’ variants:**

- With the Harpy **Podarge**: horses **Xanthus and Balius** (*Iliad* 16).
- With **Iris**: **Eros** in Alcaeus; **Pothos** in Nonnus. These are separate poetic genealogies, not a continuous canonical marriage.
- With **Chloris/Flora**: **Carpus** in later accounts; Ovid primarily presents Zephyrus as Flora’s husband.
- With an unnamed Harpy: **Arion** in late epic variants.

Notus and Eurus possess little securely attested individual genealogy.

### Documented divine interactions

The following inventory separates explicit episodes from mere membership in the same pantheon.

- **Astraeus — Eos and Astraeus generate Zephyrus, Boreas, and Notus.** Hesiod, *Theogony* 378–382, c. 700 BCE. **[Early attested]**
- **Eos — Mother of the lawful winds by Astraeus.** Hesiod, *Theogony* 378–382. **[Early attested]**
- **Typhoeus — His destructive storm winds are contrasted with Boreas, Notus, and Zephyrus.** Hesiod, *Theogony* 869–880. **[Early attested contrast, not siblinghood]**
- **Iris — Carries Achilles’ prayer to the winds at Zephyrus’ house; Boreas and Zephyrus obey.** Homer, *Iliad* 23.192–230. **[Early attested]**
- **Poseidon — Rouses all four named winds against Odysseus.** Homer, *Odyssey* 5.291–332. **[Early attested]**
- **Aeolus — Confines and dispenses winds for Odysseus.** Homer, *Odyssey* 10.1–79. **[Early attested; Aeolus is custodian, not father]**
- **Zeus — Exercises ultimate meteorological authority; later appoints Aeolus as custodian and deploys winds.** Homeric weather passages; later mythography and Virgil. **[Early authority, later systematization]**
- **Podarge — Zephyrus mates with the Harpy and sires Xanthus and Balius.** Homer, *Iliad* 16.148–151. **[Early attested]**
- **Iris — Zephyrus fathers Eros by Iris.** Alcaeus fr. 327, seventh–sixth century BCE. **[Early poetic variant]**
- **Apollo — Zephyrus becomes Apollo’s rival for Hyacinthus and diverts Apollo’s discus in jealousy.** Explicit principally in Philostratus, *Imagines* 1.24, and later commentators. **[Roman-era/late elaboration]**
- **Chloris/Flora — Zephyrus abducts or marries the flower goddess and grants her dominion over flowers.** Ovid, *Fasti* 5.183–378. **[Roman elaboration]**
- **Poseidon — Fathers Eumolpus with Chione, Boreas’ daughter, and rescues their exposed infant.** Pseudo-Apollodorus 3.15.4. **[Later canonical mythography]**
- **Juno/Hera — Commands Aeolus to release the winds against Aeneas and promises Deiopea.** Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.50–80. **[Roman literary elaboration]**
- **Neptune/Poseidon — Rebukes and disperses Aeolus’ released winds.** Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.124–156. **[Roman literary elaboration]**
- **Apollo — Gives Delphi the oracle instructing Greeks to pray to the Winds.** Herodotus 7.178. **[Classical cultic narrative]**
- **Medea — Her charms are reportedly sung at the Titane wind ritual to quiet the blasts.** Pausanias 2.12.1. **[Regional ritual tradition]**
- **Oceanus — Iris leaves the winds’ feast to attend divine sacrifice beside Oceanus.** Homer, *Iliad* 23.205–211. **[Early joint narrative context]**
- **The Harpies — The sons of Boreas pursue and expel them from Phineus.** Apollonius, *Argonautica* 2; Pseudo-Apollodorus 1.9.21. Boreas himself does not directly fight them. **[Hellenistic/canonical elaboration]**
- **Pothos — Zephyrus and Iris become Pothos’ parents.** Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 47.340. **[Late-antique variant]**

No securely surviving narrative shows Notus or Eurus individually marrying, fighting, or conversing with another named god in early Greek poetry. Their usual role is collective meteorological action.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Ordered winds and Typhoeus’ storm winds

**Earliest full source:** Hesiod, *Theogony* 378–382 and 869–880.

Hesiod creates two classes. Eos and Astraeus generate named winds integrated into cosmic and seasonal order. Typhoeus produces lawless squalls that wreck sailors and damage crops. The contrast is functional rather than ethical in a modern sense: named winds can still be violent, while rain-bearing Notus can be agriculturally necessary.

Later retellings often collapse both groups into “the four winds under Aeolus.” That synthesis is convenient but loses Hesiod’s distinction.

### The winds and Patroclus’ pyre

**Earliest source:** Homer, *Iliad* 23.192–230.

Patroclus’ pyre will not ignite adequately. Achilles stands apart, prays to Boreas and Zephyrus, promises sacrifices, and pours wine. Iris hears and visits the winds, who are feasting in Zephyrus’ house. Boreas and Zephyrus cross the sea with a roar, fan the flames through the night, and then return home.

The episode joins myth and ritual evidence: divine winds are addressed through prayer, libation, and promised animal offering. Homer does not say the promised sacrifice is actually performed, so it should not be silently supplied.

### Aeolus’ bag

**Earliest source:** Homer, *Odyssey* 10.1–79.

Aeolus entertains Odysseus, binds the courses of hostile winds inside a hide sack, and leaves one wind—conventionally understood as Zephyrus—to speed the ship toward Ithaca. Odysseus’ crew, suspecting hidden treasure, opens the sack; the winds erupt and drive them back.

Aeolus here is “dear to the immortal gods,” not unambiguously one of the Olympians. The winds are controllable forces with individual courses, not necessarily anthropomorphic prisoners. Virgil later monumentalizes Aeolus’ cavern as a political prison of rebellious Venti.

### Boreas and Oreithyia

**Earliest evidence:** probably sixth-century art and fifth-century references; Herodotus presupposes the marriage by c. 430 BCE.

Boreas desires Oreithyia, daughter of the Athenian king Erechtheus, and carries her from Attica to Thrace. Sources disagree on the location: the Ilissos river, the Areopagus, or another Athenian setting. Plato’s *Phaedrus* 229b–d has Socrates mention a rationalizing interpretation: a northern gust blew the girl from the rocks while she played with Pharmaceia. Socrates declines to devote himself to systematic demythologizing.

Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* 6 supplies the fullest psychological version: Boreas first attempts gentle courtship but decides that violence better suits his nature, spreads his wings, and abducts her. This Roman account is a literary expansion, not the earliest form.

Their children become embedded in the Argonautic cycle. Herodotus turns the marriage into political theology: Boreas is Athens’ in-law and therefore an ally against Persia.

### The Persian fleet and the Athenian cult

**Primary source:** Herodotus 7.178–189.

In 480 BCE storms damaged Xerxes’ fleet. Delphi, following an oracle, established an altar and propitiated the Winds at Thyia. Athenians invoked Boreas and Oreithyia because Boreas was their “brother-in-law.” After the storm they established his sanctuary beside the Ilissos.

Herodotus reports causation as a claim rather than fact: he does not know whether the Athenians’ prayer produced the storm. The historically secure core is that Greeks interpreted the weather through cult and subsequently commemorated the winds.

A later story reported for Thurii says that after Boreas destroyed an enemy fleet the city granted him citizenship, a house, land, and an annual festival. This is **[Regional, later testimony]** and should not be retrojected into early Athenian practice.

### The Boreads and the Harpies

**Developed source:** Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 2, third century BCE; later Apollodorus and Hyginus.

Zetes and Calais, winged sons of Boreas, accompany the Argonauts. When the Harpies repeatedly foul or steal the blind Phineus’ food, the Boreads pursue them through the air. Variants differ:

- The Boreads kill or nearly kill the Harpies.
- Iris intervenes and obtains an oath that the Harpies will stop tormenting Phineus.
- The Harpies escape to islands subsequently called the Strophades, “Turning Islands.”
- In some accounts the Boreads later die at Heracles’ hands; in others they perish during the pursuit.

The narrative extends Boreas’ aerial nature into hereditary wings and speed.

### Zephyrus and the immortal horses

**Earliest source:** Homer, *Iliad* 16.148–151.

The Harpy Podarge conceives Xanthus and Balius by Zephyrus while grazing beside Oceanus. The horses belong first to Peleus and then Achilles. Their speed materially embodies wind parentage. Later authors expand the motif of winds impregnating mares into a natural-history marvel.

### Boreas and the mares of Erichthonius

**Earliest source:** Homer, *Iliad* 20.221–229.

Boreas takes stallion form and mates with Erichthonius’ mares. Twelve offspring can run over fields without bending grain and across wave crests without sinking. The episode is distinct from Zephyrus and Podarge, though later summaries often merge the “wind-horses.”

### Zephyrus, Apollo, and Hyacinthus

The earliest surviving complete Hyacinthus stories make Apollo kill the youth accidentally. The jealous-wind version is later: Zephyrus, also enamored of Hyacinthus, diverts Apollo’s discus so it strikes him. Philostratus provides an explicit Roman-era version. Modern handbooks often present Zephyrus’ jealousy as if it were part of a single ancient canonical account; it is a **late variant**.

### Zephyrus and Chloris/Flora

In Ovid’s *Fasti* 5, Chloris says that Zephyrus seized her but repaired the violence through marriage and granted her rule over flowers. Roman floral mythology, calendar poetry, and Greek wind personification are deliberately fused. Botticelli’s *Primavera* later made this version central to European art.

### The deluge

In Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 1, Jupiter locks away cloud-dispersing Boreas and releases rain-soaked Auster/Notus to help destroy humankind by flood. Iris replenishes the clouds. This is a Roman literary deployment of the winds within a Greek deluge framework, not an early autonomous Anemoi myth.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Knossos

The Linear B “priestess of the Winds” is the earliest secure institutional evidence. The oil allocation shows palace support or accounting involvement. No associated temple has been securely identified from the tablet, and reconstructing the priestess’ rites would be speculation.

### Delphi and Thyia

Herodotus 7.178–179 records an altar to the Winds in the precinct of Thyia, daughter of Cephisus, founded in response to the Persian emergency. Delphians continued to “propitiate” the winds in Herodotus’ day. This is explicitly cult, not merely poetic personification.

### Athens and the Ilissos

Herodotus 7.189 reports a sanctuary of Boreas beside the Ilissos after 480 BCE. The cult’s political meaning was kinship: Boreas, as husband of an Athenian princess, became an allied in-law. Plato’s *Phaedrus* places the Oreithyia story in the same river landscape.

Pausanias 1.37.2 also records an altar of Zephyrus along the Sacred Way, near the sanctuary of Demeter and Kore associated with the Phytalidae. This is individual cult evidence for Zephyrus.

Hesychius preserves the term **Heudanemoi**, “Wind-lullers,” for specialized Athenian religious personnel, while **Anemokoitai**, “Wind-bedders,” are associated with Corinth. Because Hesychius is a late lexicographic witness, the titles probably preserve earlier institutions but cannot by themselves establish their exact date or duties.

### Titane in Sicyonia

Pausanias 2.12.1 records an altar at the foot of the hill. A priest sacrificed to the winds one night annually, performed secret rites at four pits to “tame” their fierceness, and reportedly chanted charms of Medea. The four pits may encode four directions, but Pausanias does not name the winds or explain the ritual mechanics.

### Coronea

Pausanias 9.34.3 saw an altar of the Winds in the marketplace beside an altar of Hermes Epimelius. No foundation story or ritual calendar survives.

### Methana

Pausanias 2.34.2 describes an agricultural rite against **Lips**, the damaging southwest wind. While the vines were sprouting, two men split an entirely white cock and ran in opposite directions around the vineyards carrying the halves. They then buried them at the starting point. This is documented animal sacrifice and apotropaic boundary ritual, not human sacrifice.

### Sparta and Taygetus

Later literary testimony reports Spartan horse sacrifice to the Winds on Mount Taygetus. The practice fits the equine character of winds but lacks the strong contemporary documentation available for the Delphic and Athenian cults.

### Roman evidence

Anzio produced inscriptions conventionally identified as altars to the Winds, Neptune, and Tranquillitas (CIL X 6642–6644). Suetonius reports that Octavian sacrificed to Neptune, the Winds, and Calm before naval operations against Sextus Pompey. Roman worship therefore retained the winds as strategic maritime powers.

### Negative findings

No evidence found supports:

- human sacrifice specifically to the Anemoi;
- temple prostitution in their cult;
- an autonomous pan-Hellenic Anemoi priesthood with a central hierarchy;
- a single universal festival of “the Four Winds”;
- a continuous initiatory “Anemoi mystery school”;
- ancient fixed colors, crystals, tarot cards, planets, or weekdays for each wind.

The Methana cock and Homeric promise of sacrificial victims are animal offerings. Claims of human sacrifice arise from conflating wind cult with unrelated Greek sacrificial controversies.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Late Bronze Age: institutional plurality

The Knossian priestess title shows that “the Winds” were already recipients of organized religious attention. Nothing establishes a fourfold compass pantheon. This stage is better described as a collective cult than as the fully formed Anemoi of modern charts.

### Archaic period: personification and genealogical ordering

Homer preserves four familiar names but no systematic fourfold family. The winds remain meteorological forces while also possessing houses, social life, sexuality, and responsiveness to offerings. Hesiod distinguishes three ordered children of Eos and Astraeus from Typhoeus’ destructive squalls. Boreas and Zephyrus acquire especially rich personhood through horse myths and, by the sixth century, visual narratives.

### Fifth century BCE: civic and political cult

The Persian Wars transformed Boreas into a publicly useful Athenian relative and military ally. Delphi cultivated the Winds collectively. Athenian vase painters made the Boreas–Oreithyia abduction exceptionally prominent around and after the Persian Wars; scholarship reasonably connects the visual fashion with the civic cult, although an individual vase’s intention cannot be proven.

Natural philosophy simultaneously began classifying winds by direction, temperature, moisture, and season. Mythic agents and meteorological categories coexisted.

### Fourth–first centuries BCE: scientific multiplication

Aristotle’s *Meteorology* maps named winds asymmetrically according to observed sunrise, sunset, and local conditions. Theophrastus studies their behavior. Timosthenes reportedly used twelve winds; Eratosthenes and Andronikos favored eight.

The Tower of the Winds made eight directions monumental and anthropomorphic. This was not a “promotion” of eight gods into a new cult so much as an architectural fusion of meteorology, timekeeping, urban design, and inherited personification.

### Roman period: Venti, literature, and cosmic ornament

Roman authors translate and rearrange the Greek names. Virgil places the Venti in Aeolus’ mountain prison as potentially anarchic forces controlled by imperial-like command. Ovid individualizes Boreas, Zephyrus, and Auster in narratives of abduction, fertility, and flood.

Wind gods become common framing devices in mosaics, sarcophagi, zodiacal art, and maps. They occupy corners and margins because their iconographic role is to animate the world’s four quarters.

### Late antiquity

The Orphic Hymns preserve direct ritual address to Boreas, Zephyrus, and Notus. Magical papyri incorporate four-wind cosmology into syncretistic rites. Nonnus expands genealogies through late-antique mythopoesis.

Christianization displaced public sacrificial cults, but there is no recorded empire-wide campaign specifically against Anemoi worship. Winds survived in literature, seasonal imagery, geographical nomenclature, and manuscripts. The Tower itself was reused in Christian and later Islamic contexts; this architectural continuity is not continuity of ancient Anemoi cult.

### Medieval transmission

Isidore of Seville and other encyclopedists transmitted Latin wind nomenclature. Carolingian manuscripts diagrammed twelve winds, sometimes adding vernacular names. Maps and cosmological diagrams retained blowing wind heads even when pagan gods were no longer cult recipients. This is learned transmission, not covert pagan survival.

### Renaissance and Enlightenment

Printed Ptolemies and cosmographies surrounded the earth with named wind heads. Agrippa’s *Three Books of Occult Philosophy* integrated Boreas, Notus, Eurus/Apeliotes, and Zephyrus into elemental natural magic.

James Stuart and Nicholas Revett’s *Antiquities of Athens* publicized the Tower’s architecture and reliefs in 1762. James Wyatt’s Radcliffe Observatory at Oxford, completed in 1794, deliberately reproduced the octagonal model and wind figures.

### Nineteenth–twenty-first centuries

Classical philology separated source strata more sharply: Homeric personification, Hesiodic genealogy, local cult, philosophical wind roses, and Roman elaboration. Archaeology and museum cataloguing established the scale of Boreas–Oreithyia iconography.

Contemporary Hellenic polytheists sometimes worship individual winds or the collective using ancient hymns and modern prayers. Online testimony establishes actual practice but not its scale, institutional unity, or direct continuity from antiquity. The revival is best classified as **modern reconstructionist religion drawing on ancient sources**.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Cartography

Ancient wind names became directional labels before north-oriented, magnetically based compass roses became standard. Medieval and Renaissance cartography often represented the world as surrounded by blowing heads, sometimes labeled in paired Greek and Latin nomenclature.

A 1513 Strasbourg Ptolemy’s *Sphera in plano* surrounds the world with twelve wind heads, including Boreas/Aquilo, Notus/Auster, Zephyrus/Favonius, and Eurus/Vulturnus. Michael Angelus Blondus’ *De ventis et navigatione* (1546) preserves a comparable learned wind rose. Seventeenth-century “anemographic” tables expanded the scheme across several languages.

This reception gradually changed the Anemoi from worshiped or narrated beings into diagrammatic signs. Modern compass points did not simply “descend from the four gods”: Greek wind directions, Roman nomenclature, Mediterranean sailing terms, magnetic-compass practice, and portolan-chart conventions converged.

### Visual art

Botticelli’s *Primavera*, c. 1480s, shows blue Zephyrus seizing Chloris, who transforms into flower-bearing Flora. Its source is principally Ovid’s *Fasti*, making the painting a Renaissance reactivation of Roman literary mythology.

Wind-head masks became conventional in maps, fountains, clocks, and architectural ornament. “Zephyr” increasingly signified gentle spring air; Boreas remained the harsh bearded north.

### Occultism

Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia*, published in complete form in 1533, assigns the principal winds to the world’s quarters and describes their elemental qualities. Later ceremonial magic incorporated directional winds into broader elemental correspondences. These systems are **early-modern and modern occult syntheses**, not recoverable Mycenaean doctrine.

Golden Dawn-derived elemental quarters—east/air, south/fire, west/water, north/earth—sometimes acquire Anemoi names in successor practice. The exact correspondences vary and should not be attributed to classical Greek cult.

### Neopaganism

Modern Hellenic polytheists use the Orphic Hymns, incense, libations, seasonal observance, and personal devotional practice. Modern prayers assigning a fixed candle color, crystal, tarot suit, or archangel to each Anemos are inventions or eclectic adaptations unless their modern source is named.

There is no reliable census of exclusive Anemoi worshipers, no demonstrated unbroken hereditary cult, and no authoritative modern Anemoi liturgy.

### Literature, music, games, and commerce

“Boreas,” “Zephyr,” “Notus,” “Eurus,” and “Anemoi” are common names for ships, winds, software, vehicles, fictional factions, albums, businesses, and weather projects. Most borrow directional or atmospheric associations rather than the ancient myths. Commercial naming is reception evidence, not cultic survival.

Modern fantasy frequently presents four “Wind Kings,” assigns each a season and color, or makes them Aeolus’ sons. That neat tetrad is largely a modern synthesis of Homeric names, Hesiodic kinship, Tower iconography, Roman season symbolism, and occult directional tables.

No substantial evidence surfaced for nationalist or extremist appropriation centered specifically on the Anemoi. Boreas can appear in northern or “Hyperborean” political imagery, but a use of “Boreal” or “Hyperborean” language is not by itself evidence of devotion to Boreas.

## 9. Historiography and contested material

### How many winds?

There was never one ancient answer.

- Homer names four but does not diagram them.
- Hesiod genealogizes three lawful winds and excludes Typhoean squalls.
- Aristotle uses a larger and asymmetric observational system.
- Timosthenes is associated with twelve.
- Andronikos monumentalizes eight.
- Vitruvius explains four, eight, and numerous local subdivisions.
- Pliny says the earliest scheme had four, a later one eight, and a compromise twelve.

The “canonical four” is therefore a useful modern teaching device, not a timeless Greek dogma.

### Why does Hesiod omit Eurus?

Proposals include:

1. The three named winds correspond to the three-season Greek year.
2. Eurus was regarded as irregular or destructive and therefore excluded from the lawful triad.
3. The poetic passage reflects an older or local taxonomy.
4. The transmitted text or traditional grouping has obscured an earlier place for Argestes or Eurus.

No surviving ancient explanation decides the matter. “Eurus was the autumn god” is not a solution supplied by Hesiod.

### Are the winds gods or metaphors?

The opposition is modern. Greek discourse permitted a wind to be physical air, a named direction, a god, a poetic agent, and a cult recipient without requiring mutually exclusive categories. The *Iliad*’s feast, prayer, and sacrifice establish genuine personification; its weather similes establish equally genuine natural description.

### Etymology

The name *anemos* is Indo-European, but the individual names are difficult. Popular derivations from “roar,” “darkness,” “moisture,” or geography range from plausible to speculative. Robert Beekes and other modern etymologists emphasize uncertainty and possible pre-Greek elements. Assertions of certain Persian, Sanskrit, Akkadian, or Egyptian derivation are unsupported by the evidence reviewed.

### Boreas and Thrace

Boreas’ Thracian home may reflect Greek observation that northern weather arrived through Thrace, coupled with Greek construction of Thrace as cold and marginal. It does not prove borrowing from a separately documented Thracian god of identical name. “Thracian Boreas” is secure poetic geography; a pre-Greek or Thracian origin remains reconstruction.

### Rationalization of Oreithyia

Plato preserves the explanation that a gust blew Oreithyia from a cliff. This is not proof that the myth originated as a weather accident. It proves that fifth–fourth-century intellectuals could naturalize inherited myth. Likewise, modern solar, seasonal, or “morning mist” allegories are interpretations, not recovered original meanings.

### Human sacrifice and cult violence

No source securely attests human sacrifice to the Anemoi. The Methana rite kills a cock. Achilles promises victims to Boreas and Zephyrus in a funeral context, but Homer does not identify them as human. The nearby sacrifice of Trojan captives on Patroclus’ pyre is performed for Patroclus, not the winds.

Boreas’ taking of Oreithyia is represented as abduction and, explicitly in Apollodorus and Ovid, sexual coercion. That narrative violence should not be softened into an uncomplicated wedding. It is mythic narrative, not evidence of a corresponding cult practice.

### Graves and Kerényi

Karl Kerényi’s synthetic presentations remain influential reception history but often seek archetypal coherence across divergent sources. Robert Graves’ *The Greek Myths* is especially prone to speculative ritual, political, and psycho-sexual reconstructions. Neither should be used as primary evidence. Claims derived from Graves require independent ancient attestation.

### State of the evidence

The evidence is uneven:

- Linear B gives institutional terminology without narrative.
- Homer and Hesiod give narrative and cosmology without detailed cult administration.
- Classical art heavily favors Boreas and Zephyrus.
- Herodotus and Pausanias provide local cult evidence separated by centuries.
- The Tower provides a nearly intact monumental taxonomy.
- Lexicographers preserve titles whose original settings are lost.
- The Orphic Hymns survive through medieval manuscripts descended largely from a narrow textual tradition.
- Many early wind images are uninscribed, making identification probabilistic.

Major scholarly tools include *LIMC* for iconography, *Realencyclopädie* and *Brill’s New Pauly* for historical reference, TLG and Perseus for texts, Beazley Archive Pottery Database for vases, and epigraphic corpora including CIL and Greek inscription databases.

### Modern scholarship

Walter Burkert’s *Greek Religion* remains foundational for the relationship between nature powers, ritual, and civic cult. Timothy Gantz’s *Early Greek Myth* is especially useful for keeping early sources separate from Roman mythography. Farnell’s *Cults of the Greek States* is old but documentary and still valuable when checked against later archaeology and epigraphy. Roland Hampe’s *Kult der Winde in Athen und Kreta* directly addresses the Cretan and Athenian evidence. Kora Neuser’s work on ancient representations of winds, *LIMC*, Beazley Archive records, and museum catalogues are central for iconography.

Theoi.com is useful as a searchable quotation index, but its category labels and synthesized genealogies are modern editorial structures. Perseus and similar repositories supply older public-domain editions and translations; dates and textual readings should be checked against current critical editions. Kerényi is best treated as interpretive synthesis and Graves as modern reception unless independently corroborated.
