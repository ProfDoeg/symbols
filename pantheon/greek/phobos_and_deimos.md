# Phobos and Deimos: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Greek divine personifications (*daimones*), specifically the embodied forces of battle-fear, panic, dread, and rout; in genealogical poetry they are male gods and sons of Ares and Aphrodite. Phobos alone also possessed a documented civic cult.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the *Iliad*, conventionally composed in the later eighth or early seventh century BCE from older oral material. Phobos and Deimos appear together at *Iliad* 4.439–440, 11.36–37, and 15.119–120; Phobos is explicitly called Ares’ “dear son” at 13.298–300. No secure Mycenaean Linear B occurrence is known.

## Evidentiary key

- **[Early attested]** Archaic Greek evidence, approximately eighth–sixth centuries BCE.
- **[Classical elaboration]** Fifth–fourth centuries BCE.
- **[Regional cult]** Evidence peculiar to a city or locality.
- **[Later canonical]** Hellenistic or Imperial Greek and Roman elaboration.
- **[Learned systematization]** Genealogy or equation produced by mythographers, antiquarians, or philosophers.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A historically situated translation or merger with another culture’s figure.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** Scholarly inference not stated by the ancient evidence.
- **[Modern invention/reception]** Post-antique attribution, symbolism, worship practice, or fictional development.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The pair

Phobos and Deimos are not originally two richly individualized characters with separate biographies. In the earliest poetry they are simultaneously:

1. ordinary Greek words for states or effects of fear;
2. personified powers active on battlefields;
3. members of Ares’ divine entourage;
4. eventually, in Hesiod, genealogical sons of Ares and Aphrodite.

Their individuality is unequal. Phobos has the clearer genealogy, iconography, epithets, independent invocations, and cult. Deimos normally appears beside him. No ancient source securely calls them twins; “the twins” is a convenient modern description inferred from their paired birth in Hesiod.

### Phobos

- **Greek:** Φόβος, *Phóbos*.
- **Basic senses:** fear, panic, terror; the act or condition of flight or rout; that which causes fear.
- **Divine classification:** male personification or *daimōn*, also called a *theos* in poetic contexts.
- **Etymology:** related to Greek φοβέω, *phobéō*, “put to flight, frighten,” and the intransitive φοβέομαι, “flee in fear, be afraid.” Its oldest semantic field joins fear to flight rather than to a modern clinical “phobia.” Exact Proto-Indo-European reconstruction is less secure than the Greek derivational relationship; proposals connecting it with a root meaning “flee” are linguistic reconstruction, not ancient theology. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**
- **Ancient contextual translations:** Fear, Panic, Rout, Flight. No one English word covers all occurrences.
- **Epithets:**
  - **φίλος υἱός, “dear son” of Ares:** *Iliad* 13.299. **[Early attested]**
  - **φίλαιμος / philaimatos, “blood-loving” or “delighting in blood”:** Aeschylus, *Seven against Thebes* 45, where the Seven swear by Ares, Enyo, and Phobos. **[Classical elaboration]**
  - Modern phrases such as “lord of panic,” fixed planetary colors, sacred days, crystals, herbs, or sigils are not established ancient titles. **[Modern invention]**

### Deimos

- **Greek:** Δεῖμος, *Deîmos*.
- **Basic senses:** dread, terror, a fearful object or apparition.
- **Divine classification:** male personification or *daimōn*; genealogical god in Hesiod.
- **Etymology:** belongs to the Greek fear vocabulary represented by δείδω, *deídō*, “fear,” and δέος, *déos*, “dread/awe.” Ancient writers sometimes indulged in explanatory wordplay, but no surviving cult theology assigns Deimos a technical psychological stage distinct from Phobos.
- **Ancient contextual translations:** Dread, Terror, Horror.
- **Epithets:** no stable independent cult epithet is securely attested.

The widespread formula that “Deimos is anticipatory dread, whereas Phobos is panic during battle” is a useful modern semantic distinction, not a doctrine stated by Homer or Hesiod. Their meanings overlap, and translators regularly reverse “Fear,” “Terror,” “Dread,” and “Panic.”

### Roman names and equations

Roman authors did not preserve a single fixed one-to-one equivalence:

- *Pavor* — panic, trembling fear.
- *Metus* — fear or apprehension.
- *Terror* — terror.
- *Formido* — dread or alarm.
- *Timor* — fear.

Ovid, Statius, Valerius Flaccus, Hyginus, and Apuleius redistribute these names according to literary context. Thus “Phobos = Pavor and Deimos = Metus” may fit one passage but is not a universally valid Roman theology. **[Syncretistic identification / learned translation]**

Pseudo-Hyginus’ mythographic preface names *Formido* among the children of Mars and Venus, apparently compressing or translating the Greek pair. Apuleius’ *Metamorphoses* 10.30 calls two armed boys accompanying the stage-Athena *Terror* and *Metus*, illustrating the portability of fear-personifications rather than a stable cult equation.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Homeric evidence

The *Iliad* is the earliest surviving secure evidence. Its text was composed through an oral-poetic tradition and attained broadly recognizable form around the late eighth or early seventh century BCE; the medieval manuscripts are far later, while papyrus fragments and ancient quotations witness earlier textual transmission.

- **4.439–445:** Ares drives one host, Athena the other; Deimos, Phobos, and Eris move among the combatants. They are external divine powers and simultaneous descriptions of battlefield experience. **[Early attested]**
- **5.738–742:** Athena puts on Zeus’ aegis, upon which are Eris, Alkē, Iōkē, and the head of the Gorgon. Some translations or discussions associate the aegis generally with Phobos, but the named list here does not simply reproduce the paired gods.
- **11.32–37:** Agamemnon’s shield bears the glaring Gorgon, “and around her were Deimos and Phobos.” The Greek describes a figured arrangement. The popular rendering that their names were “inscribed” on the shield overstates the text; no writing is required by the passage. **[Early attested]**
- **13.298–300:** Meriones and Idomeneus are likened to Ares going to battle with Phobos, “his dear son,” powerful and fearless enough to rout even a steadfast warrior. This is the earliest explicit father–son relationship, but it names only Phobos. **[Early attested]**
- **15.119–120:** Ares orders Deimos and Phobos to harness his horses when he intends to avenge his mortal son Ascalaphus. They function as attendants or charioteers. **[Early attested]**

Homer never names Aphrodite as their mother, never calls them twins, and gives no independent birth story.

### Hesiodic corpus

- **Hesiod, *Theogony* 933–937**, around 700 BCE: Aphrodite bears Harmonia and “Phobos and Deimos, terrible gods who disorder the close ranks of men in chilling war, together with Ares the sacker of cities.” This is the earliest surviving explicit statement that both are sons of Ares and Aphrodite. **[Early attested genealogical systematization]**
- **Pseudo-Hesiod, *Shield of Heracles***, probably sixth century BCE though transmitted with the Hesiodic corpus:
  - lines 139–160 describe a terrifying central shield-image called Phobos or “a fear,” depending on textual interpretation, made of adamant and staring backward with fiery eyes;
  - around 191–196 Phobos and Deimos stand beside Ares’ chariot;
  - around 450–466 they retrieve the wounded Ares after Heracles strikes him and drive him to Olympus.
  This poem provides their fullest Archaic narrative action. **[Early/later Archaic elaboration]**
- ***Works and Days*:** uses ordinary fear vocabulary but contains no securely individualized episode of the divine brothers. Absence matters: they are not actors in its moral and agricultural myth.
- **Hesiodic manuscript issue:** the works survive principally in medieval copies, supplemented by ancient papyri and quotations; “Hesiodic” attribution does not establish single authorship or one date.

### Other Archaic and Classical evidence

- **Stesichorus fr. 207**, seventh–sixth century BCE, preserved only through a later scholion on Pindar: Kyknos, son of Ares, killed travelers and planned to build Phobos a temple from their skulls. The fragmentary and indirect transmission makes its exact wording and original narrative context uncertain. **[Early attested fragment / mythic human violence]**
- **Aeschylus, *Seven against Thebes* 42–48**, produced 467 BCE: the Seven sacrifice a bull over a black shield, touch its blood, and swear by Ares, Enyo, and blood-loving Phobos either to destroy Thebes or die. This is a dramatic representation of a martial oath, not evidence that historical Greek armies routinely performed this exact rite. **[Classical elaboration]**
- **Aeschylus, *Seven* around 497:** Phobos is poetically said to boast of victory at a gate. The ambiguity between person and emotion is deliberate.
- **Pindar:** the surviving odes provide no major independent narrative about the pair. Later scholia attached to Pindar preserve Stesichorus’ Kyknos material, which must not be cited as though Pindar himself narrated it.
- **Sophocles and Euripides:** fear vocabulary is extensive, but no securely individualized Phobos-and-Deimos cycle comparable to Homer or pseudo-Hesiod survives.
- **Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 2.5.11**, first or second century CE in its surviving form: relates Heracles’ killing of Kyknos but does not securely preserve the specific skull-temple-to-Phobos detail in every version. **[Later mythographic compilation]**
- **Diodorus Siculus:** no substantial independent Phobos-and-Deimos myth.
- **Homeric Hymns:** no hymn to either being and no important paired episode.
- **Orphic Hymns:** no surviving individual hymn to Phobos or Deimos. The hymn to Ares asks the god to restrain furious battle and turn toward peace, but does not establish a separate mystery cult of his sons.
- **Orphic fragments:** no secure independent cosmogony or genealogy of the pair rivaling Hesiod’s.
- **Greek Magical Papyri:** no well-attested, standard invocation or spell centered on Phobos and Deimos as a pair. Words for fear naturally appear in coercive and protective magic, but that is not proof of a cult of the personified gods. Claims of a “PGM ritual of Phobos and Deimos” require a precise papyrus and line; none is established here.

### Imperial and late-antique literature

- **Plutarch, *Theseus* 27.2**, late first/early second century CE: before fighting the Amazons, Theseus sacrifices to Phobos in obedience to an oracle. This is late literary testimony about a heroic-age rite, not contemporary evidence from Bronze Age Athens. **[Later canonical antiquarian tradition]**
- **Plutarch, *Alexander* 31.9:** Alexander conducts secret rites and sacrifices to Phobos before Gaugamela in 331 BCE. The event is reported some four centuries later, although Plutarch drew on lost Hellenistic histories. **[Later testimony to a claimed historical sacrifice]**
- **Plutarch, *Cleomenes* 8–9:** describes a sanctuary of Phobos beside the Spartan ephors’ common mess and interprets Spartan veneration of fear as support for civic obedience. **[Regional cult, preserved in later testimony]**
- **Pausanias 5.19.4**, mid-second century CE: on the Chest of Kypselos at Olympia, Agamemnon’s shield carried a lion-headed Phobos. The chest is lost; Pausanias’ description is the evidence.
- **Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica* 5.25–35 and 10.51–59**, probably third century CE: Phobos and Deimos appear in shield imagery and accompany Eris as martial daimones. **[Late epic elaboration]**
- **Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 2.414–419**, fifth century CE: Zeus arms Phobos with lightning and Deimos with thunder against Typhon. Later books make them Ares’ charioteers in the Indian war and his helpers at sea. **[Late-antique elaboration]**
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 4.481–485:** Pavor and Terror attend Tisiphone when she spreads madness in Athamas’ house. **[Roman literary translation]**
- **Statius, *Thebaid* 3.424, 7.64–70, 10.556–564:** Fear-personifications inhabit Mars’ palace and ravage Thebes. **[Roman epic elaboration]**
- **Valerius Flaccus, *Argonautica*:** Pavor/Terror participate in scenes of divinely inspired slaughter; elsewhere Latin commentators can treat fear-powers as Mars’ attendants or horses. **[Roman poetic transformation]**
- **Suda**, tenth century CE: preserves late summaries in which fear-powers share Ares’ humiliating failure to force Hephaestus back to Olympus. Such entries are Byzantine learned preservation, not evidence for an early independent myth.

## 3. Domain, powers, attributes, and iconography

### Domain and action

Their securely attested domain is martial fear:

- disorganizing compact ranks;
- driving warriors into flight;
- intensifying the onset and noise of battle;
- inspiring terror through divine armor;
- harnessing or driving Ares’ chariot;
- protecting or evacuating Ares when wounded;
- functioning as fearsome images on shields.

They are not securely ancient gods of anxiety disorders, nightmares, trauma, horror entertainment, courage therapy, or generalized personal “shadow work.” Those are modern extensions.

Fear could operate in two directions. A warrior wanted Phobos to fall upon the enemy, while Spartan political interpretation made fear of authority productive within the citizen body. Their power is therefore neither simply “evil” nor protective: it is relational.

### Physical form

No early text supplies a stable anatomy.

- Homer treats them as mobile divine presences and as shield images.
- The *Shield of Heracles* gives Phobos—or a personified image of fear—fiery eyes, a terrible mouth, and adamantine material.
- Pausanias’ lost Chest of Kypselos had a Phobos with a lion’s head on Agamemnon’s shield.
- Other works render Phobos anthropomorphically as a young charioteer.

Attempts to identify every winged, leonine, snake-haired, or gorgoneion-like Archaic demon as Phobos are disputed. The Italian *Enciclopedia dell’Arte Antica* concludes that efforts to establish an exclusive Phobos iconography have not produced satisfactory results. **[Modern scholarly caution]**

### Important depictions

1. **Agamemnon’s shield in the *Iliad*** — textual ekphrasis, late eighth/early seventh century BCE. Gorgon at center, Deimos and Phobos around her. **[Early literary image]**
2. **Heracles’ shield in pseudo-Hesiod** — sixth century BCE poetic ekphrasis. Fiery-eyed Phobos and the pair beside Ares’ chariot. **[Archaic literary image]**
3. **Chest of Kypselos at Olympia** — traditionally dated to the early sixth century BCE but known only from Pausanias: lion-headed Phobos on Agamemnon’s shield. **[Lost Archaic object, Imperial description]**
4. **Berlin black-figure oinochoe, approximately 530 BCE** — Phobos, identified by inscription, drives Ares’ chariot during the Kyknos episode. It is unusually valuable because the label prevents purely subjective identification. **[Archaic material attestation]**
5. **François Vase, approximately 570 BCE** — winged monstrous figures on the handles have sometimes been identified as Phobos and Deimos, but they are not securely named; the identification remains conjectural. **[Disputed modern identification]**
6. **Halicarnassus mosaic**, fourth century CE, British Museum 1857,1220.416 — a stone floor medallion catalogued as the mask of Phobos within a twelve-petaled rosette. This is the principal securely provenanced surviving monumental image called Phobos, although its interpretation depends partly on iconographic comparison rather than a surviving label. **[Late Roman material evidence]**

### Symbols and correspondences

Secure ancient associations are:

- Ares’ chariot and horses;
- shields and shield devices;
- the Gorgon/gorgoneion as an adjacent terror-image;
- leonine features for Phobos in one antiquarianly recorded work;
- blood and the black shield in Aeschylus’ oath scene;
- battlefield noise, rout, armor, lightning, and thunder in later poetry.

No secure ancient evidence assigns them:

- fixed sacred animals;
- sacred plants;
- ritual colors;
- numbers;
- weekdays;
- zodiacal signs;
- crystals;
- tarot cards;
- standardized personal sigils.

Modern lists that do so are creative devotional or occult systems. **[Modern invention]**

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Genealogical versions

| Source | Parentage or relationship | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|
| *Iliad* 13.299 | Phobos is Ares’ dear son | **[Early attested]** |
| *Iliad* 15.119 | Phobos and Deimos are Ares’ attendants; sonship is not stated there | **[Early attested]** |
| Hesiod, *Theogony* 933–937 | Both sons of Ares and Aphrodite; Harmonia is born in the same union | **[Early genealogical systematization]** |
| Pseudo-Hesiod, *Shield* | Ares is their father/commander; they serve him | **[Archaic elaboration]** |
| Hyginus, preface | Mars and Venus produce Harmonia and Formido | **[Roman learned compression]** |
| Stobaeus, *Anthology* 1.49.44 | A transmitted learned genealogy reportedly calls Phobos a son of Selene | **[Late learned variant]** |

Hesiod’s genealogy became dominant because it integrated the Homeric battle daimones into a family structure: Ares produces warfare’s dread with Aphrodite, while their other child Harmonia embodies concord. The text itself does not explain the union allegorically, and the popular claim that the brothers represent “fear of losing love” is modern speculation.

They have no securely attested consorts, children, mounts, priestly servants, or independent divine courts.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Ares:** accompany him in battle; Phobos is his son; harness and drive his chariot; stand beside it; rescue him after Heracles wounds him. *Iliad* 4.439–445, 13.298–300, 15.119–120; Hesiod, *Theogony* 933–937; pseudo-Hesiod, *Shield* 191–196, 450–466. **[Early attested]**
- **Aphrodite:** bears both brothers to Ares. Hesiod, *Theogony* 933–937. They do not otherwise interact directly in early surviving narrative. **[Early genealogical systematization]**
- **Harmonia:** co-child of the Ares–Aphrodite union; no surviving direct episode between her and the brothers. Hesiod, *Theogony* 933–937. **[Early genealogical relation; no interaction]**
- **Eris:** moves through battle beside them; in Quintus they follow her steps. *Iliad* 4.440–445; Quintus Smyrnaeus, *Posthomerica* 10.51–59. **[Early association / late elaboration]**
- **Enyo:** appears with them in the martial divine cluster; the Seven swear by Ares, Enyo, and Phobos. *Iliad* 5.592 and Aeschylus, *Seven* 42–48; fuller paired tableaux occur in later epic. **[Early association / Classical invocation]**
- **Athena:** Phobos and Deimos oppose or animate combatants on the battlefield where Athena directs the opposing side; fear-images also occur on armor associated with her. *Iliad* 4.439–445 and 5.738–742. Apuleius later makes Terror and Metus armed stage-attendants of Minerva. **[Early juxtaposition / Roman theatrical elaboration]**
- **Gorgon/Gorgo:** the brothers surround her face on Agamemnon’s shield. *Iliad* 11.36–37. This is a joint image, not genealogy. **[Early attested]**
- **Heracles:** fights Ares and wounds him; Phobos and Deimos retrieve their father and drive him away. Pseudo-Hesiod, *Shield* 450–466. **[Archaic elaboration]**
- **Kyknos:** the mortal or semi-divine son of Ares plans a skull-built temple to Phobos; Phobos does not personally answer him in the surviving fragment. Stesichorus fr. 207. **[Archaic fragmentary variant]**
- **Zeus:** gives Phobos lightning and Deimos thunder against Typhon. Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 2.414–419. **[Late-antique accretion]**
- **Typhon:** is frightened or attacked through the weapons Zeus assigns the brothers. Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 2.414–419. **[Late-antique accretion]**
- **Dionysus:** Phobos and Deimos drive Ares’ chariot when Ares joins the Indian war against Dionysus. Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 29.364 onward. **[Late-antique accretion]**
- **Hera:** in Nonnus’ Indian-war narrative she helps mobilize opponents of Dionysus, including Ares’ contingent; no early direct exchange with the brothers exists. **[Late-antique indirect interaction]**
- **Tisiphone:** Latin Pavor and Terror accompany the Erinys when she spreads madness. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 4.481–485. **[Roman literary transformation]**
- **Hephaestus:** Byzantine summaries associate them with Ares’ failed effort to compel Hephaestus to release Hera; this is not securely early. *Suda*, relevant mythographic entry. **[Medieval learned preservation/late variant]**
- **Selene:** Stobaean material makes Phobos her son in an isolated genealogy; no narrative interaction survives. **[Late learned variant]**

No documented interaction is securely attested with Apollo, Artemis, Demeter, Persephone, Poseidon, Hermes, Hestia, Pan, Hades, Eros, or the Dioscuri merely because they share the Greek pantheon.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The battle entourage of Ares

**[Early attested core]** In the *Iliad*, they move wherever battle becomes a divinely driven rout. They are not neutral observers: they heat or disorder the fighters, accompany Ares, and harness his horses. Phobos’ filial designation anchors the personification within Ares’ household.

**Development:** Hesiod supplies Aphrodite as mother. Pseudo-Hesiod converts the compressed Homeric relationship into visual and narrative action: the brothers stand beside the war-god’s chariot and rescue him. Imperial epics multiply the attendants, weapons, and allegorical companions.

### The wounding and rescue of Ares

In pseudo-Hesiod’s *Shield*, Heracles confronts Kyknos, kills him, and fights Ares. Athena warns and assists Heracles. When Heracles wounds Ares in the thigh, Phobos and Deimos quickly bring the chariot, lift their father aboard, and drive him to Olympus.

This closely resembles the *Iliad*’s treatment of wounded gods but is not the same episode. The brothers’ characteristic role is logistical and protective: they do not defeat Heracles themselves. **[Archaic elaboration]**

### Kyknos’ skull temple

Stesichorus’ fragment says Kyknos murdered travelers and intended to construct a temple to Phobos from their skulls. Heracles prevents the project by killing him.

- **Human sacrifice issue:** the victims are murdered travelers in a heroic myth, not documented sacrificial victims at a historical sanctuary.
- **Temple issue:** it is a planned mythic building, not an excavated temple.
- **Variant:** other Kyknos traditions make the intended skull-temple belong to Ares or Apollo, or omit it. The variation warns against treating “Phobos’ temple of skulls” as a settled canonical story.
- **Function:** it intensifies Kyknos’ savagery and justifies Heracles’ killing; any claim that it preserves an actual headhunting cult is speculative. **[Early fragment / contested reconstruction]**

### The Seven’s oath

In Aeschylus, the Seven kill a bull over a black shield and swear by Ares, Enyo, and blood-loving Phobos to destroy Thebes or die. It is one of the clearest invocations of Phobos as an oath-power.

The rite is embedded in drama and reported by a scout; it is not direct ritual documentation. It does, however, depend on an audience understanding Phobos as a force whose presence could bind and brutalize warriors. **[Classical dramatic elaboration]**

### Theseus and the Amazons

Plutarch reports that an oracle instructed Theseus to sacrifice to Phobos before fighting the Amazons at Athens. The Amazons’ sacrifice before the Theseia and local tombs are separately reported, but no surviving Classical inscription confirms an Athenian temple of Phobos. **[Late antiquarian account of a heroic-age regional tradition]**

### Zeus’ weapons against Typhon

Nonnus gives Phobos lightning and Deimos thunder in the cosmic struggle against Typhon. This creates a neat differentiation absent from early poetry. Modern illustrations sometimes treat lightning and thunder as their permanent individual symbols; they are instead local features of one fifth-century CE epic. **[Late-antique accretion]**

## 6. Cult, sanctuaries, and ritual practice

### Sparta

Phobos’ clearest cult is Spartan, but the evidence needs careful grading.

Plutarch’s *Cleomenes* reports:

- a *neōs* or sanctuary/temple of Phobos;
- proximity to the ephors’ common dining place or headquarters;
- asylum sought there by the surviving ephor Agylaeus during Cleomenes III’s coup in 227 BCE;
- an explanation that Spartans honored fear because controlled fear, shame, and reverence maintained the constitution.

This establishes that a sanctuary was believed to stand by the ephorate in the late Hellenistic setting of the story. Plutarch wrote approximately three centuries later and moralizes the arrangement. There is no securely identified excavated Phobos temple at Sparta and no surviving dedicatory inscription from it.

Some modern accounts say its doors were closed in peacetime or that Spartan armies regularly sacrificed to Phobos before every battle. These claims should not be generalized without exact ancient citations. The well-attested Spartan pre-battle sacrifices to Zeus, Athena, Artemis Agrotera, or Enyalios are not automatically sacrifices to Phobos.

Thus the requested “Spartan sacrifice to Phobos” divides into three claims:

1. **A Spartan sanctuary existed:** supported by Plutarch. **[Regional cult, later literary testimony]**
2. **Spartan political ideology valued fear:** explicitly Plutarch’s interpretation. **[Imperial moral interpretation of regional practice]**
3. **A regular pre-battle sacrifice to Phobos:** not securely demonstrated by the principal evidence. **[Unestablished generalization]**

### Alexander before Gaugamela

Plutarch says Alexander spent the night before Gaugamela, 331 BCE, performing secret rites with the seer Aristander and sacrificing to Phobos. The object may have been to send terror against Darius’ army, but Plutarch does not spell out that intention. Calling it “psychological warfare” is a modern interpretation.

No contemporary inscription or campaign diary confirms the sacrifice. Nevertheless, it is a specific ancient testimony and cannot simply be discarded. **[Later historical tradition]**

### Athens and Theseus

The sacrifice before the Amazon battle is known from Plutarch’s *Theseus*. It may preserve an older local aetiology, but its antiquity cannot be independently demonstrated. No enduring Athenian priesthood or festival of Phobos is known.

### Offerings and priesthood

Secure evidence does not establish:

- a priesthood of either brother;
- a festival calendar;
- an oracle;
- pilgrimage;
- temple estates;
- extensive votive series;
- theophoric personal names showing broad popularity;
- cult of Deimos independent of Phobos.

The absence is especially notable for Deimos. He is richly poetic but almost cultically invisible.

### Sacrifice and violence

- The Seven sacrifice a bull while invoking Phobos: dramatic, not epigraphic ritual evidence.
- Theseus and Alexander sacrifice to Phobos in Plutarch: late testimony about earlier events.
- Kyknos’ human victims and skull-temple are mythic murders, not proof of historical human sacrifice.
- No evidence establishes temple prostitution, institutionalized human sacrifice, or a violent Phobos/Deimos priesthood.
- No archaeological evidence connects the pair with mass killing or trophy skull sanctuaries.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before approximately 750 BCE

No certain Bronze Age attestation exists. Ares is probably present in Linear B as *a-re*, but neither Phobos nor Deimos has a secure Linear B divine entry. Their absence may reflect the fragmentary administrative corpus; it cannot prove that the concepts or oral figures did not exist.

### Approximately 750–650 BCE: Homeric formation

The *Iliad* presents them as animate battle powers, shield devices, attendants, and—in Phobos’ case—a son of Ares. The semantic movement from emotion to god remains fluid.

### Approximately 700–550 BCE: genealogy and visualization

Hesiod makes Aphrodite their mother. The *Shield of Heracles*, Stesichorus, early painted pottery, and the Chest of Kypselos make Phobos more visibly anthropomorphic or monstrous. The pair acquire action but still no broad narrative cycle of their own.

### Fifth–fourth centuries BCE

Aeschylus invokes Phobos in a blood-oath. Literature continues to exploit the oscillation between an emotion and a divine agent. Direct cult evidence remains thin.

### Hellenistic period

Phobos becomes entangled with royal warfare and Spartan constitutional ideology in traditions later recorded by Plutarch. Cleomenes’ coup gives the Spartan sanctuary a concrete narrative setting. Alexander’s alleged sacrifice shows Phobos available to high-command ritual.

### Roman Imperial period

Greek antiquarians and Roman poets catalogue, translate, and allegorize the pair. Latin *Metus*, *Pavor*, *Terror*, and *Formido* do not form a consistent mapping. Pausanias preserves otherwise lost iconographic information; Plutarch preserves the main cult evidence.

### Late antiquity

Nonnus expands their mythology with lightning, thunder, Typhon, Dionysus, and naval help for Ares. The Halicarnassus mosaic monumentalizes Phobos in a domestic or villa-floor decorative setting.

There is no evidence that Christianity singled out their cult for a special campaign of suppression. Their institutions, already limited, disappeared within the broader contraction and prohibition of public polytheistic sacrifice between the fourth and sixth centuries CE. Claims that Christian writers transformed Phobos and Deimos into named demons require specific texts; no continuous medieval demonological cult is documented.

### Byzantine to early modern periods

The names survive in manuscripts, lexica, school texts, mythographies, and allegorical art. They do not maintain a demonstrable continuous popular cult. Renaissance and early-modern artists could personify Fear and Terror, but usually through Latin allegory rather than reconstructed worship of the brothers.

### Nineteenth century to present

Astronomical naming in 1877 made the names far more globally familiar than their ancient cult ever was. Twentieth- and twenty-first-century popular culture, psychology, military language, science fiction, and revived polytheism then produced new identities around them.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### The moons of Mars

American astronomer Asaph Hall discovered Mars’ two satellites at the United States Naval Observatory in Washington in August 1877: Deimos first, then Phobos several days later. Hall adopted the Homeric names, traditionally said to have been suggested by Henry Madan of Eton, from *Iliad* 15.119 where Ares calls Phobos and Deimos to harness his horses.

The inner moon became **Mars I, Phobos**; the outer, **Mars II, Deimos**. The USGS/IAU nomenclature retains Hall’s names. Modern official summaries sometimes call the Homeric beings Ares’ “horses,” “attendants,” or “sons.” In Homer’s passage they harness the horses; they are not themselves unambiguously horses. The “horses” description reflects later compression or Latin poetic variants.

The astronomical bodies now dominate the names’ reception:

- Phobos is approximately 22 kilometers across and orbits Mars in about 7 hours 39 minutes.
- Deimos is approximately 12–13 kilometers across and orbits in about 30.3 hours.
- Their composition and origin remain debated: captured-asteroid and giant-impact/debris-disk models have both been proposed.
- These scientific questions concern the moons, not ancient beliefs about the gods.

### Psychology and language

“Phobia” derives through Greek *phobos*, but modern psychiatric diagnoses are not cultic survivals. Terms such as arachnophobia or social phobia use a Greek lexical element; they do not invoke Phobos as an acting god. A Jungian reading of the brothers as shadow-archetypes or successive levels of fear is modern interpretive psychology. **[Modern reinvention]**

### Occultism and Neopaganism

No major Renaissance grimoire, Golden Dawn grade system, *Liber 777* table, or classical planetary-magic scheme gives Phobos and Deimos a prominent anciently continuous office. The seven-planet occult system had no place for the moons of Mars before 1877.

Contemporary Hellenic polytheists and eclectic practitioners sometimes worship them, often alongside Ares, with modern prayers, candles, planetary associations, martial symbolism, exposure therapy, or “shadow work.” Such practice is real as contemporary religion but reconstructs from sparse evidence. Claims about mandatory black/red candles, obsidian, particular incense, days, runes, or sigils are modern choices, not recovered Greek ritual.

No reliable census isolates their modern worshippers, so its scale cannot be established. Online testimony suggests a small, decentralized practice rather than a major organized cult.

### Literature, film, comics, and games

The astronomical names encouraged their use for threatening extraterrestrial places and characters. Notable modern receptions include:

- science fiction set on or around Mars;
- *Sailor Moon*, where Phobos and Deimos are names attached to Rei Hino’s crows and later supernatural attendants;
- *Assassin’s Creed Odyssey*, whose protagonist’s horse is named Phobos;
- *Warframe*, where Deimos is a transformed Martian moon and major game location;
- comic-book and fantasy characters that turn the ancient personifications into villains, demons, warriors, or literal twins.

These works materially shape contemporary imagery but do not transmit ancient iconography faithfully. Horns, black wings, matching armor, exclusive red/black color schemes, or a moral opposition between “panic” and “terror” generally belong to modern design.

### Political and extremist appropriation

Fear and terror are common political metaphors, but no well-documented major modern extremist movement is organized around Phobos and Deimos as cult figures. Occasional use in military, gaming, weapon-system, or online aliases is commercial or rhetorical reception, not evidence of an ideological religious cult. A stronger claim would require organizational documentation.

## 9. Historiography and contested material

### Personification or “real god”?

The opposition is misleading. Greek language can make an emotion, its supernatural cause, and a divine person continuous with one another. Homer’s Phobos can be a battlefield condition, a figured object, and Ares’ son without contradiction. Modern taxonomies that insist he must be either “only an abstraction” or “a full god” impose sharper boundaries than the texts do.

### Was Deimos less individualized?

Yes, on present evidence. Phobos receives:

- explicit individual sonship in Homer;
- an independent oath invocation;
- a cult at Sparta;
- sacrifices by Theseus and Alexander in Plutarch;
- labeled or described iconography.

Deimos is chiefly paired with Phobos or embedded in martial tableaux. This is an evidentiary asymmetry, not proof that Greeks considered him unreal or unimportant.

### Shield devices

Three traditions must be separated:

1. Homer’s Agamemnon shield: Gorgon with Deimos and Phobos around her.
2. Pseudo-Hesiod’s Heracles shield: Phobos/fear as a terrifying central figure and the pair beside Ares’ chariot.
3. Pausanias’ Chest of Kypselos: a lion-headed Phobos on Agamemnon’s represented shield.

These are not descriptions of one surviving artifact. They are different poetic and artistic constructions. The notion that Phobos and Deimos had universal standardized shield emblems is unsupported.

### Lion-headed Phobos

The lion head rests on Pausanias’ description of one lost Archaic work. Scholars have consequently proposed Phobos as the identity of various leonine or winged demons, but unlabeled figures cannot be securely named from resemblance alone. The Halicarnassus mosaic is catalogued as Phobos but is about a millennium later.

### Human sacrifice

The contested evidence is the Kyknos fragment. It describes murdered strangers and an unrealized skull-temple. It neither records a sanctuary excavation nor says a community sacrificed humans to Phobos. Calling it evidence of “the human-sacrifice cult of Phobos” is methodologically unsound.

### Spartan cult

The cult is literary-attested, not archaeologically well documented. Plutarch is indispensable but late and philosophically interpretive. Arguments that the sanctuary embodied fear of ephoric authority are plausible because of its stated location and Plutarch’s explicit explanation, but its date of foundation, architecture, ritual calendar, and duration remain unknown.

### Alleged syncretism

Roman translations are literary equations, not necessarily cult mergers. No evidence shows Greek Phobos and Deimos absorbing an older Near Eastern pair, nor a formal state cult joining them to Egyptian, Persian, or Mesopotamian fear gods. Cross-cultural comparison may identify analogous war-demons, but analogy is not historical identity.

### Kerényi and Graves

Karl Kerényi’s synthetic retellings are influential modern interpretations, not primary evidence. Robert Graves’ *The Greek Myths* often joins scattered material into psychologically or ritually suggestive narratives and advances speculative ritual origins; it should be used as twentieth-century reception, with every ancient claim checked independently. Neither establishes a lost Phobos-and-Deimos mystery cult.

### Primary evidence and editions

The central evidence survives in:

- the Homeric and Hesiodic manuscript and papyrus traditions;
- fragments collected in modern editions of Greek lyric;
- Aeschylean manuscripts;
- Imperial manuscripts of Plutarch, Pausanias, Quintus, and Nonnus;
- labeled or attributed vase painting;
- the British Museum’s Halicarnassus mosaic;
- Pausanias’ description of the now-lost Chest of Kypselos.

Important modern frameworks include Farnell on Greek cult, Burkert on Greek religion, Gantz on early myth, Stafford on deified abstractions, LIMC on iconography, Pauly-Wissowa and *Brill’s New Pauly* on the textual dossier. The principal methodological advance has been to study personifications simultaneously as language, image, cult, and social practice rather than dismissing them as “mere allegory.”

| Approximately 700 BCE | Hesiod’s *Theogony*: both become sons of Ares and Aphrodite, born beside Harmonia. |
| Seventh–sixth century BCE | Stesichorus’ lost Kyknos poem reportedly describes the planned skull-temple to Phobos. |
| Early sixth century BCE | Chest of Kypselos traditionally dated to this period; later seen by Pausanias, with lion-headed Phobos on Agamemnon’s shield. |
| Sixth century BCE | *Shield of Heracles*: Phobos’ terrifying shield image; both brothers beside Ares’ chariot and rescuing him. |

| 227 BCE | During Cleomenes III’s coup at Sparta, Agylaeus seeks refuge in the small sanctuary of Phobos, according to Plutarch. |
| First century BCE–first century CE | Ovid and other Latin poets translate and redistribute the pair as *Pavor*, *Metus*, *Terror*, and related abstractions. |
| Late first–early second century CE | Plutarch records the Spartan sanctuary, Theseus’ sacrifice, and Alexander’s rite. |
| Mid-second century CE | Pausanias describes the lion-headed Phobos on the Chest of Kypselos. |
| Second century CE | Apuleius stages Terror and Metus as armed attendants of Minerva. |
| Third century CE, approximately | Quintus Smyrnaeus revives the pair in post-Homeric epic. |
| Fourth century CE | Halicarnassus mosaic with mask of Phobos produced; now British Museum 1857,1220.416. |
| Fifth century CE | Nonnus gives them lightning and thunder against Typhon and makes them Ares’ charioteers against Dionysus. |
| Fourth–sixth centuries CE | Public polytheistic sacrifice and cult institutions decline or are prohibited; no specific suppression record for their cult survives. |
| Tenth century CE | The *Suda* preserves late mythographic notices. |
| Renaissance–eighteenth century | Survival through classical manuscripts, lexica, allegorical art, and mythography; no demonstrated continuous cult. |
| August 1877 | Asaph Hall discovers Mars’ two moons and names them Phobos and Deimos from *Iliad* 15.119. |
| Twentieth century | Spacecraft observation and psychiatric “phobia” terminology make Phobos internationally familiar; science fiction expands both names. |
| Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries | Games, comics, animation, occult correspondence lists, and revived Hellenic worship create new representations. |
| Present | Astronomical names are official; limited modern devotional practice exists, but no reliable estimate of its scale is available. |

## 11. Findings in brief

The most securely reconstructable early identity is not that of two elaborate “twin gods,” but of a paired vocabulary of battle terror made animate in epic. Homer gives them movement, divine agency, armor imagery, and service to Ares; Hesiod gives them Aphrodite as mother. Phobos subsequently develops farther than Deimos: he can receive an oath, sacrifice, sanctuary, and recognizable images.

The Spartan cult is genuine as an ancient literary report but materially elusive. The celebrated skull-temple belongs to Kyknos’ violent myth and supplies no evidence of historical human sacrifice. The lion head is a particular iconographic tradition, not Phobos’ universal form. The fixed modern distinction between anticipatory Deimos and immediate Phobos, as well as their supposed colors, symbols, crystals, and occult offices, is modern system-building.

Their most consequential modern transformation occurred in 1877. Naming Mars’ moons after Ares’ attendants converted two comparatively minor Greek daimones into permanent objects of scientific language and furnished the foundation for most subsequent global reception.
