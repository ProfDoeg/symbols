# Scylla and Charybdis: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** two distinct female sea monsters or personified maritime dangers, later treated as a proverbial pair. Scylla (Greek **Σκύλλα**, epic **Σκύλλη**, *Skylla/Skyllē*) is an anthropo-zoomorphic cave-dwelling predator; Charybdis (**Χάρυβδις**, *Kharybdis*) is a swallowing and disgorging marine power, usually visualized as a whirlpool rather than given a stable body.

**Earliest secure attestation:** both occur together in *Odyssey* 12, composed in an oral-poetic tradition and conventionally dated to the late eighth or early seventh century BCE, although Martin L. West argued for the last third of the seventh. The oldest physical witnesses to Homer are later: fragmentary papyri begin in the third century BCE, and the earliest reported papyrus of *Odyssey* 12 is pre-Aristarchean rather than contemporary with composition. Thus “Homer, eighth/seventh century BCE” dates the poem’s formative textual stage, not a surviving artifact. [Cambridge history of the text](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/homer-odyssey-books-xiii-and-xiv/history-of-the-text/084C45F1DE0E89B357E966F607D261E2), [West’s dating argument](https://academic.oup.com/book/3033/chapter-abstract/143756897), [University of Michigan on Homeric transmission](https://apps.lib.umich.edu/online-exhibits/exhibits/show/translating-homer--from-papyri/translating), [earliest reported Book 12 papyrus](https://www.schoyencollection.com/literature-collection/classical-greek-literature-collection/homer-odyssey-ms-5069).

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early]** securely attested Archaic or early Classical material.
- **[Classical]** fifth–fourth-century BCE evidence.
- **[Canonical elaboration]** influential Hellenistic or Roman literary development.
- **[Regional variant]** geographically localized or locally inflected evidence.
- **[Learned systematization]** mythographic, scholastic, antiquarian, or rationalizing synthesis.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** equation with another figure rather than simple kinship.
- **[Medieval/early-modern accretion]** post-antique transmission or reinterpretation.
- **[Folklore]** vernacular or localized tradition without demonstrated ancient continuity.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** scholarly inference from incomplete evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** a recent assertion not established in ancient evidence.

These labels classify evidence and transmission, not whether a narrated event “really happened.”

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Scylla

**[Early]** In *Odyssey* 12.73–126, Scylla is an immortal or deathless evil—Circe says there is no fighting her—living in a cave high in a smooth, cloud-covered cliff. She possesses twelve dangling feet, six long necks, and six terrible heads, each mouth furnished with three rows of closely packed teeth. Her voice resembles that of a newborn puppy, an intentionally grotesque mismatch between sound and lethal body. She fishes from her cave for dolphins, dogfish, and larger sea creatures and takes one sailor with each head when a ship passes. Homer does not say that her torso is female, that dogs grow from her waist, that she has a fish tail, or that she was once a maiden. Those are later literary or artistic forms.

Her Homeric mother is **Κραταιίς**, *Krataiïs/Crataeis* (*Odyssey* 12.124). Circe advises Odysseus to call upon this mother if Scylla attacks again. The name evokes Greek **κραταιός**, “strong/mighty,” but whether Crataeis began as an independent being, an epithet, or a secondary genealogical formation cannot be established.

The principal forms are:

- Greek **Σκύλλα**, with epic **Σκύλλη**.
- Latin **Scylla**.
- Adjectival Latin *Scyllaeus*, “Scyllaean/pertaining to Scylla.”
- Modern Italian **Scilla**, also the Calabrian place-name.
- English **Scylla**, conventionally pronounced “SILL-a.”

The name’s derivation is unsettled. Homer’s puppy comparison encouraged association with **σκύλαξ** (*skylax*, “puppy/whelp”), while later explanation also invoked **σκύλλειν**, “tear, rend.” LSJ calls derivation from *skylax* probably erroneous; modern summaries therefore properly treat the name as of unknown origin, with the canine explanation as ancient or traditional wordplay rather than demonstrated historical etymology. [LSJ entry](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn%3Acite2%3Ascaife-viewer%3Adictionaries.v1%3Alsj-n95308/).

No securely attested cult epithet belongs to Scylla. Phrases such as “Ausonian Scylla” in Apollonius identify geography; “Tyrrhenian Scylla” in tragedy is literary geography, not a temple title.

### Charybdis

**[Early]** Homer calls her **δῖα Χάρυβδις**, commonly translated “divine Charybdis,” at *Odyssey* 12.104 and 12.235. The adjective marks superhuman status or poetic distinction; it does not establish an organized cult. Circe also calls her “destructive” or “baneful.” Charybdis has no Homeric bodily description, parents, former mortal identity, or explicit origin story. Her identity is her action: she swallows the sea and vomits it out three times a day.

Forms include:

- Greek **Χάρυβδις**, *Khárybdis*.
- Latin **Charybdis**.
- Italian **Cariddi**.
- English **Charybdis**.

The etymology is unknown. No secure Indo-European derivation or source-language borrowing has won agreement. Attempts to extract a literal meaning such as “sucker-down” from her behavior are explanatory guesses, not established philology.

The title **Τρίηνος/Trienos**, “three-times,” is sometimes connected with Charybdis because of her three daily cycles, or with a sea-being named as Scylla’s parent in a corrupt mythographic genealogy. The equation “Charybdis = Keto Trienos” is **[Modern reconstruction]**, not a securely attested ancient identification.

### Their pairing

**[Early]** Homer juxtaposes them but does not call them sisters, allies, or a collective. They constitute asymmetrical dangers:

- Scylla inflicts a limited but unavoidable loss: six men.
- Charybdis threatens total destruction: ship and crew.

The later idea that they cooperate, consciously guard a passage together, or are sisters is **[Modern invention]** unless a particular modern fictional continuity is expressly being described.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The Homeric core

The decisive passages are:

1. ***Odyssey* 12.73–126**: Circe describes the two cliffs and hazards. Scylla cannot be fought; Odysseus should sail swiftly past and invoke Crataeis against a second attack. Circe recommends Scylla’s limited predation over Charybdis’s destruction of the entire ship.

2. ***Odyssey* 12.201–259**: Odysseus conceals Scylla from his crew, arms himself despite Circe’s warning, and watches Charybdis. While the crew look toward the whirlpool, Scylla snatches six of the strongest sailors. Odysseus calls this the most pitiable sight of his wanderings. The narrative does not name the victims.

3. ***Odyssey* 12.426–446**: after Zeus destroys the ship for the cattle of Helios, Odysseus is driven back to Charybdis. He clings to the fig tree above her until she disgorges the mast and keel, then paddles away on them. Scylla does not attack him again. [Greek and English text, first passage](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D12%3Acard%3D234), [return to Charybdis](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D12%3Acard%3D426).

This is the earliest recoverable form of the pair. The six-headed morphology, three rows of teeth, twelve feet, cave, puppy voice, triple swallowing, fig tree, six victims, and Circe’s tactical advice are all **[Early]**. Scylla’s maidenhood and magical transformation are not.

### Archaic genealogical fragments

**[Early or early post-Homeric, fragmentary]** A lost Hesiodic poem, the *Megalai Ehoiai* (“Great Ehoiai”), fragment 262 Merkelbach–West/fragment 150 in another numbering, reportedly made Scylla the daughter of **Hecate and Phorbas**. The attribution survives only by quotation; neither date nor wording has Homeric security. Timothy Gantz discusses it as an early competing genealogy. [Gantz, *Early Greek Myth* excerpt](https://open.online.uga.edu/gantzmythsources/chapter/p-26/).

Acusilaus, a late-sixth- or early-fifth-century genealogist, reportedly made Scylla the child of **Hecate and Phorkys**. The fragment is indirectly preserved. [Digital Fragmenta Historicorum Graecorum](https://www.dfhg-project.org/DFHG/ret.php?urn=urn%3Alofts%3Afhg.1.acusilaus.acusilai_fragmenta.genealogiae%3A5).

Neither the extant *Theogony* nor *Works and Days* supplies the familiar Scylla narrative. The Homeric Hymns, extant Pindaric corpus, and Orphic Hymns likewise contain no foundational treatment of the pair. Claims that “Hesiod’s *Theogony* says Scylla is the daughter of Phorkys and Ceto” commonly collapse a general sea-monster genealogy into a statement the extant poem does not make.

### Classical references

**[Classical]** Aeschylus, *Agamemnon* 1233–36 (performed 458 BCE), has Cassandra compare Clytemnestra to Scylla, “a raging mother of Hades” dwelling among rocks and barking destruction. This establishes Scylla as a recognized metaphor for a predatory woman by the mid-fifth century, but contributes no voyage or genealogy.

**[Classical]** Euripides, *Medea* 1342–43 (431 BCE), makes Jason call Medea a lioness, “not woman,” with a nature more savage than Tyrrhenian Scylla. “Tyrrhenian” shows western-Italian localization in Classical poetic imagination.

**[Classical]** Plato, *Republic* 588c (c. 380 BCE), invokes Scylla, Chimaera, and Cerberus while constructing a many-headed composite creature as an image of the soul. This is philosophical reuse, not zoological testimony.

**[Classical]** Aristotle, *Poetics* 1461b, mentions a composition called *Scylla* in discussing faulty or unintelligible musical performance—possibly Timotheus’s dithyramb. The work is lost; its content cannot safely be reconstructed from the title.

There is no surviving Classical tragedy devoted to Charybdis, no securely identified oracle, hymn, sacred law, or dedication to either monster, and no securely attested dramatic account of Scylla’s transformation before the Hellenistic and Roman evidence.

### Hellenistic Argonautic tradition

**[Canonical elaboration]** Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 4.825–963 (third century BCE), retrojects the pair into the voyage of the Argo. Hera directs Thetis to save the Argonauts; Thetis summons the Nereids, who carry or guide the ship through the dangers like girls passing a ball. Charybdis may swallow the heroes “at one gulp”; Scylla is the deadly “Ausonian” daughter of Phorkys and night-wandering Hecate, here identified with or called Crataeis. [Text and translation](https://www.theoi.com/Text/ApolloniusRhodius4.html), [critical teaching commentary](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/apollonius-argonautica/argonautica-iv-737-830).

This episode both adopts the Homeric pair and systematizes Scylla’s genealogy. It does not narrate a maiden’s transformation.

Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Library* 1.9.25 and *Epitome* 7.20–23, probably first or second century CE, summarizes both the Argonautic and Odyssean passages. His Scylla is daughter of Crataeis and **Trienus or Phorkos**, with female face and breast, six canine heads issuing from her flanks, and twelve canine feet. The textual “Trienus” may conceal *Triton* or another corrupted name. [*Epitome* 7](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0022%3Atext%3DEpitome%3Abook%3DE%3Achapter%3D7&highlight=siren), [genealogical notes](https://www.theoi.com/Text/ApEc.html).

### Roman literary elaboration

**[Canonical elaboration]** Virgil, *Aeneid* 3.420–432 (29–19 BCE), places the pair unambiguously in the Sicilian strait. Helenus advises Aeneas to avoid it by a long southern circuit. Scylla is beautiful and human above, but below is a vast sea-dragon with dolphins’ tails and a womb or belly of wolves; Charybdis sucks down and casts up the sea three times. Virgil additionally explains Sicily and Italy as once joined and torn apart. [*Aeneid* 3](https://www.theoi.com/Text/VirgilAeneid3.html).

**[Canonical elaboration]** Ovid develops the maiden-transformation narrative across *Metamorphoses* 13.730–14.74 (completed c. 8 CE). Glaucus loves the beautiful Scylla; she rejects him. He asks Circe for a love spell. Circe desires Glaucus, is rejected, poisons Scylla’s bathing inlet with herbs and incantations learned from or associated with Hecate, and causes barking dogs to erupt around Scylla’s lower body. Scylla thereafter attacks Odysseus in hatred of Circe and is eventually changed into a rock. Ovid does not make Amphitrite the transformer. [Latin text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0029%3Abook%3D14), [English translation](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0028%3Abook%3D14).

**[Canonical elaboration]** Hyginus, *Fabula* 125, probably transmitted from a Roman mythographic handbook of uncertain date, calls Scylla a daughter of Typhon, makes her woman above and fish below, and gives her six dogs. She consumes six of Ulysses’ men. Charybdis swallows and disgorges three times daily. Elsewhere in Hyginus the parentage becomes Typhon and Echidna. [Latin teaching text](https://cla.virginia.edu/system/files/Continuous%20Text.pdf).

Later Latin poetry—Propertius, Seneca, Statius, Silius Italicus, and others—principally uses the pair as geographic hazards, epic shorthand, or figures for danger. These allusions attest canonical familiarity but do not independently establish cult or early genealogy.

### Scholia and late learned accounts

**[Learned systematization]** Scholia on *Odyssey* 12 preserve mutually incompatible accounts:

- Scylla as daughter of Hecate and Phorkys.
- Scylla as daughter of Crataeis and Triton, Poseidon, Tyrrhenus, or Phorkys.
- Stesichorus reportedly naming **Lamia** as her mother.
- Charybdis as daughter of Poseidon and Gaia.
- Charybdis as a voracious female who stole or ate cattle belonging to Heracles and was hurled into the sea by Zeus’s thunderbolt.
- Another Charybdis tradition makes her inundate land for Poseidon.

These are valuable witnesses to ancient exegesis, but their dates are not automatically those of Homer; scholia preserved in medieval manuscripts can contain Hellenistic cores, later additions, or both. A modern critical edition of the D-scholia documents this layered transmission. [Critical D-scholia project](https://kups.ub.uni-koeln.de/1831/), [source collection for Charybdis](https://www.theoi.com/Pontios/Kharybdis.html).

### Sources searched but yielding no subject-specific dossier evidence

The extant *Works and Days*, Homeric Hymns, Orphic Hymns, Greek Magical Papyri, and Pausanias contain no established cult, invocation, spell, temple, or extended myth of this pair. Diodorus is relevant to Sicilian geography and mythography generally but supplies no independent foundational Scylla–Charybdis cycle comparable to Homer, Apollonius, or Ovid. Silence here matters: neither monster belongs to the regularly invoked ritual pantheon.

## 3. Domain, attributes, powers, and iconography

### Domains and actions

**Scylla’s early domain** is the inaccessible cliff and the predation zone immediately around it. Her characteristic action is selective seizure: one victim per head. Circe says weapons are useless because Scylla is not mortal and attacks too quickly.

**Charybdis’s early domain** is cyclic engulfment. The low opposite rock bears a large fig tree; beneath it Charybdis consumes the sea. Homer’s three daily ingestions and expulsions do not accurately describe ordinary Mediterranean tides and should not be converted into a scientific observation without qualification.

Neither has an anciently documented sacred animal, plant, day, color, number, festival, or celestial body. Scylla’s dogs, fish tails, dolphins, and marine creatures are anatomical or contextual motifs, not proven cult animals. Charybdis’s fig tree is a narrative survival point for Odysseus, not a documented sacred tree.

### Scylla’s changing body

1. **[Early textual form]** Six heads on long necks, twelve feet, three rows of teeth in each mouth, puppy-like voice. No explicitly female torso.

2. **[Classical artistic form]** By the fifth century BCE artists use a single attractive female head and torso, canine foreparts at the waist, and one or two fish or serpentine tails. This visually legible form differs sharply from Homer’s six complete heads. The Metropolitan Museum’s survey dates the earliest artistic representations to the fifth century BCE. [Metropolitan Museum study](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Dangerous_Beauty_Medusa_in_Classical_Art_The_Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art_Bulletin_v75_no_3.pdf).

3. **[Late Classical/Hellenistic form]** Scylla brandishes an oar, rudder, rock, sword, or trident and sometimes seizes sailors. A Getty fragment, c. 375–350 BCE, shows the female torso, fish tail, two dogs, and trident. [Object description](https://www.theoi.com/Gallery/P27.2.html).

4. **[Hellenistic funerary/decorative form]** A third-century BCE Canosan askos gives Scylla modeled above the vase body; such works show that she was a popular visual monster in South Italian funerary production, not necessarily the recipient of worship. [Metropolitan Museum askos](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/248471).

5. **[Roman monumental form]** The Scylla group from Tiberius’s grotto at Sperlonga depicts Odysseus’s ship and Scylla attacking from below. Its precise date and relationship to Hellenistic prototypes remain debated, but it belongs to an elite Roman program of Odyssean sculpture, not a sanctuary. [Sperlonga museum](https://direzioneregionalemuseilazio.cultura.gov.it/en/luoghi/museo-archeologico-nazionale-e-area-archeologica-di-sperlonga/?accessible=true).

6. **[Roman political image]** Sextus Pompey’s Sicilian silver denarii of 42–40 BCE show Scylla wielding a rudder as a club; the obverse’s Pharos of Messana and Neptune situate the image within claims to naval command. This is political iconography, not evidence that Sextus instituted her cult. [British Museum coin](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_1853-0104-3).

### Charybdis in art

No securely identified ancient iconographic type comparable to Scylla’s is established. Charybdis is normally represented indirectly—as turbulent water, a mouth-like vortex, or a geographic feature—or omitted while Scylla supplies the humanoid visual drama. Modern artists frequently give Charybdis teeth, tentacles, a gigantic mouth, or an abyssal body; these are **[Modern invention]**, though compatible with her swallowing action.

### Competing genealogies

| Scylla | Hecate and Phorbas | Hesiodic *Megalai Ehoiai*, fragment, probably Archaic | **[Early fragmentary variant]** |
| Scylla | Hecate and Phorkys | Acusilaus fragment, late 6th/early 5th c. BCE | **[Early regional/genealogical variant]** |
| Scylla | Lamia as mother | Stesichorus as reported by scholia | **[Fragmentary learned report]** |
| Scylla | Hecate/Crataeis and Phorkys | Apollonius, *Argonautica* 4, 3rd c. BCE | **[Canonical elaboration]** |
| Scylla | Crataeis and Trienus or Phorkos | Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Epitome* 7 | **[Learned systematization; text uncertain]** |
| Scylla | Typhon; elsewhere Typhon and Echidna | Hyginus, *Fabulae* | **[Roman systematization]** |

| Charybdis | Poseidon and Gaia | Homeric scholia/Servian tradition | **[Late learned systematization]** |
| Charybdis | Voracious woman punished by Zeus for taking Heracles’ cattle | Servius and scholastic tradition | **[Late-antique learned narrative]** |
| Charybdis | Agent of Poseidon who floods land | Scholiastic tradition | **[Learned variant]** |

There is no secure ancient statement that Scylla and Charybdis are sisters. Nor is there one canonical family tree from which all forms descend.

### Every documented interaction with named divine beings

“Interaction” is used strictly. Parentage without a narrated meeting is listed as a relation; active encounters are distinguished.

- **Scylla — Crataeis:** mother–daughter relation; Circe tells Odysseus to invoke Crataeis to restrain another attack. *Odyssey* 12.124–26, late eighth/early seventh century BCE. **[Early]**

- **Scylla — Hecate:** Hecate is her mother in the Hesiodic fragment, Acusilaus tradition, and Apollonius; Ovid makes Circe’s drugs/incantations Hecatean. No surviving early scene shows mother and daughter acting together. **[Early fragmentary genealogy / later canonical elaboration]**

- **Scylla — Phorbas:** father in the *Megalai Ehoiai* fragment; no narrated interaction survives. **[Early fragmentary genealogy]**

- **Scylla — Phorkys/Phorkos:** father in Acusilaus, Apollonius, and mythography; a later tradition says he restores Scylla after Heracles kills her, but this is preserved indirectly through Dionysius of Samos and scholia. **[Genealogical variant / learned accretion]**

- **Scylla — Lamia:** mother in the Stesichorean report; no interaction preserved. **[Fragmentary variant]**

- **Scylla — Triton/Trienus/Tyrrhenus:** alternative father in mythographic or scholastic texts; no scene survives, and part of the variation may be textual corruption. **[Learned systematization]**

- **Scylla — Typhon and Echidna:** parents in Hyginus’s monster catalogue; no interaction narrated. **[Roman learned systematization]**

- **Scylla — Glaucus:** Glaucus desires the maiden Scylla and asks Circe to make her reciprocate; Scylla rejects him and he mourns her transformation. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 13–14. **[Canonical Roman elaboration]**

- **Scylla — Circe:** Circe envies Scylla, poisons her bathing place, and transforms her lower body into barking dogs after Glaucus refuses Circe. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14. **[Canonical Roman elaboration]** Homer’s Circe knows how to navigate Scylla but is not her transformer.

- **Scylla — Heracles:** Scylla takes one of Geryon’s cattle; Heracles kills her. Phorkys reportedly restores her. Dionysius of Samos fragment 12 and later scholia. **[Hellenistic or earlier fragmentary variant]**

- **Scylla — Poseidon:** Poseidon appears as a possible father in scholia; there is no secure narrative encounter. **[Learned genealogical variant]**

- **Scylla — Charybdis:** co-presence on opposite sides of the passage, *Odyssey* 12 onward. No text says they confer, ally, fight, or share kinship. **[Early juxtaposition; absence of interaction is a finding]**

- **Charybdis — Poseidon:** father–daughter relation and, in one scholastic account, Charybdis floods land on his behalf. **[Late learned systematization]**

- **Charybdis — Gaia:** mother in scholia; no narrated interaction. **[Late learned genealogy]**

- **Charybdis — Zeus:** Zeus strikes or hurls Charybdis into the sea as punishment for her voracity, theft of Heracles’ cattle, or flooding activity, depending on the late account. **[Late learned accretion]**

- **Charybdis — Heracles:** Charybdis steals or eats cattle Heracles is transporting from Geryon; Zeus punishes her. **[Late learned accretion]**

- **Scylla and Charybdis — Hera:** Hera orders Thetis to ensure that the Argonauts do not fall victim to them. The monsters do not answer Hera directly. Apollonius, *Argonautica* 4. **[Canonical Hellenistic elaboration]**

- **Scylla and Charybdis — Thetis and the Nereids:** Thetis, assisted by the Nereids, guides the Argo safely through or around their zone. Apollonius, *Argonautica* 4. **[Canonical Hellenistic elaboration]**

- **Scylla and Charybdis — Odysseus:** Odysseus is a mortal hero during the narrative, not yet a god. Scylla takes six men; Charybdis later nearly consumes Odysseus. *Odyssey* 12. **[Early heroic interaction, included for cross-reference]**

- **Scylla and Charybdis — Aeneas:** likewise a hero rather than an acting god in Virgil; Helenus warns him away and he avoids the strait. *Aeneid* 3. **[Roman canonical elaboration]**

No documented interaction was found with Athena, Apollo, Artemis, Aphrodite, Ares, Demeter, Dionysus, Hermes, Persephone, or Hades. Mere membership in a shared mythological universe is not an interaction.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. Odysseus’s first passage

**[Early]** Circe maps a sequence of dangers: Sirens, Wandering Rocks, then the paired cliffs. The Scylla cliff is high, smooth, cloud-capped, and inaccessible even to an arrow; her cave faces west toward Erebus. The other rock is lower and bears the fig tree over Charybdis.

Circe makes the ethical and tactical calculation explicit: losing six men is preferable to losing everyone. Odysseus nevertheless puts on armor and takes spears, apparently hoping to fight. The crew, terrified by Charybdis, do not see Scylla’s attack coming. Each head lifts one man; they call Odysseus’s name while being carried away. The episode therefore joins inescapable loss with the commander’s concealment of danger.

The often-repeated names “Stesius, Ormenius, Anchimus, Ornytus, Sinopus, and Amphinomus” do not occur in Homer. They belong to later scholastic or modern compilatory tradition and should not be presented as Homeric.

### B. Odysseus’s return to Charybdis

**[Early]** After the cattle of Helios are killed, Zeus destroys the ship. Odysseus lashes together the keel and mast and is driven back. Charybdis swallows the wreckage; he jumps and hangs from the fig tree, with nowhere to brace his feet. When she eventually disgorges the timbers, he drops, reaches them, and paddles away. Homer remarks that Poseidon himself could not rescue a person caught in Charybdis, emphasizing her effective irresistibility rather than necessarily ranking her above Poseidon cosmologically.

### C. The Argonauts

**[Canonical Hellenistic elaboration]** Apollonius inserts the hazards into a voyage myth chronologically earlier than Odysseus. Hera, protecting Jason, recruits Thetis; Thetis summons her Nereid sisters. Their divine aid prevents the forced sacrifice that defines Odysseus’s passage. Pseudo-Apollodorus later summarizes the same sequence.

The myth’s literary function is comparative: the Argo survives through collective divine favor, whereas Odysseus must accept loss. It also anchors the hazards in the “Ausonian” west.

### D. Scylla, Glaucus, and Circe

**[Canonical Roman elaboration]** Ovid supplies an origin tailored to *Metamorphoses*: Scylla is a desired maiden rather than primordial monster. Glaucus’s desire leads him to Circe; Circe’s desire for Glaucus produces jealousy; Scylla’s refusal is answered by bodily transformation. This account explains the later artistic female torso and barking lower body but postdates both Homer and fifth-century images. It should not be projected backward as the “original” myth.

Alternative post-antique summaries sometimes substitute Amphitrite as the jealous transformer because Poseidon desired Scylla. That configuration belongs to variant mythographic transmission, not Ovid’s account.

### E. Scylla and Heracles

**[Fragmentary variant]** In the tradition attributed to Dionysius of Samos, Scylla seizes one of the cattle Heracles took from Geryon. Heracles kills or dismembers her; Phorkys burns or restores her, enabling her continued existence. The reconstruction depends on fragments and scholia and is less secure than the Homeric and Ovidian cycles. [Discussion of the fragmentary tradition](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/apollonius-argonautica/intro/scylla-tradition).

### F. Charybdis’s punishment

**[Late learned accretion]** Servian and scholastic accounts humanize Charybdis retroactively as an insatiably hungry female, a thief of Heracles’ cattle, or Poseidon’s flooding agent. Zeus’s thunderbolt casts her into the sea, where her devouring nature becomes the whirlpool. None of this appears in Homer. The familiar internet narrative that she “helped Poseidon steal land from Zeus until Zeus transformed her” is a harmonized expansion of late scholia, not a continuous early canonical story.

### G. The localization myth

**[Classical onward]** The pair became fixed to the Strait of Messina: Scylla at the Calabrian promontory and Charybdis near the Sicilian shore. Strabo, *Geography* 1.2 and 6.2, treats the Homeric geography as substantially western and describes Charybdis near Messana as a gulf or eddy into which countercurrents drive ships. [Strabo, Book VI](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Aabo%3Atlg%2C0099%2C001%3A6).

Homer does not name Messina, Rhegium, Italy, or Sicily in the episode. Consequently:

- identification with Messina is ancient and dominant;
- it is not explicit in the earliest text;
- whether Homer already intended Messina remains disputed;
- alternative ancient or modern placements, including the Bosporus, are minority reconstructions.

The Strait has real reversing currents and vortices, especially near the Sicilian shore, but later claims that navigation was virtually impossible exaggerate the physical phenomenon. Ancient small craft could still find the passage formidable. The myth is not reducible to a nautical report: Homeric Charybdis’s triple daily cycle and Scylla’s predation are poetic constructions.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

The dossier’s central cultic finding is negative.

No securely identified temple, altar, priesthood, festival, sacred law, sacrificial calendar, oracle, mystery, votive inscription, theophoric personal-name series, or civic cult is known for either Scylla or Charybdis. Neither appears as a regular recipient in the Homeric Hymns, Orphic Hymns, or Greek Magical Papyri.

Circe’s instruction to call on **Crataeis**, rather than sacrifice to Scylla, is narrative apotropaic advice. It does not establish a sanctuary or ritual office.

Objects called “votive plaques” in museum catalogues require caution: “votive” describes an archaeological object class or probable function, not necessarily dedication to the depicted monster. Scylla was useful as an apotropaic, decorative, funerary, marine, and epic image. None alone proves worship.

There is accordingly no documented:

- human sacrifice to Scylla or Charybdis;
- temple prostitution;
- cult violence;
- suppression of their priesthoods;
- conversion of a Scylla or Charybdis temple;
- continuous secret cult.

Modern pages assigning Scylla sacred colors, stars, owls, octopuses, ritual days, or human sacrifice at a stellar rising are **[Modern invention]** unless they identify a checkable ancient source. The contested-material search located such claims but no supporting inscription, ritual text, or archaeological report.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Late eighth/early seventh century BCE: epic formation

The pair enter the documentary record in *Odyssey* 12. Scylla is individualized anatomically; Charybdis is primarily a process. Their opposition encodes limited versus total loss.

### Sixth–fifth centuries BCE: genealogy and metaphor

Genealogists connect Scylla to Hecate, Phorbas, or Phorkys. Tragedians can assume audiences recognize “Scylla” as shorthand for barking female destructiveness. Fifth-century artists replace Homer’s six long-necked heads with a single female torso, canine protomes, and marine tail.

### Fourth–third centuries BCE: iconographic and Alexandrian consolidation

Scylla becomes a common South Italian and Hellenistic marine hybrid. Apollonius integrates both monsters into the Argonautic cycle and gives divine rescuers a role. Alexandrian scholarship begins the textual and explanatory traditions later preserved in scholia.

### First century BCE–first century CE: Roman canonical form

Virgil fixes the pair within an Italian/Sicilian national geography. Ovid creates the most influential transformation story. Sextus Pompey appropriates Scylla for naval-political imagery at Messana. Roman mythographers classify her among monster families and attempt to give Charybdis biography and genealogy.

### Imperial Roman period

Scylla flourishes in sculpture, mosaics, gems, lamps, vessels, and elite Odyssean display. The Sperlonga group monumentalizes the ship attack. Charybdis remains iconographically elusive. This was likely the peak of visual circulation, but not of cult.

### Late antiquity and Byzantium

Commentators, Servius, Eustathius, lexica, and scholia preserve and harmonize genealogies. With the Christianization of the Roman Empire, pagan mythology is increasingly transmitted as literature, grammar, allegory, geography, and moral example. Because no institutional cult of the pair is demonstrable, there is no discrete “suppression of their cult” to chronicle.

### Medieval western Europe

Knowledge persists through Latin Virgil, Ovid, Servius, mythographic handbooks, and proverbial literature. A ninth-century mural at Corvey has been interpreted as an Odyssean Scylla scene. Medieval geographers sometimes relocated the pair: Theodoricus Monachus, for example, compared the Pentland Firth to their strait. Such comparisons are learned geographical reception, not surviving local worship.

### Renaissance to eighteenth century

Printed Homer, Virgil, Ovid, and Erasmus renew the pair. Visual artists increasingly prefer Ovid’s eroticized triangle—maiden Scylla, Glaucus, jealous Circe—over Homer’s purely predatory cave monster.

### Nineteenth–twenty-first centuries

Philology separates Homeric, genealogical, Ovidian, and iconographic strata. Scylla and Charybdis become standard terms in politics, economics, medicine, law, psychoanalysis, and popular narrative for opposed dangers. Hellenic polytheist individuals may address or venerate them today, but no reliable population estimate or demonstrable institutional continuity from antiquity exists.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### The proverb

The modern expression “between Scylla and Charybdis” means being forced to navigate between opposed dangers. A related form means escaping one danger only to fall into another.

Erasmus included **Τὴν Χάρυβδιν ἐκφυγὼν τῇ Σκύλλῃ περιέπεσον**—“Having escaped Charybdis, I fell upon Scylla”—in the *Adagia*, associating it with Latin forms such as *evitata Charybdi in Scyllam incidi*. [English scholarly selection from Erasmus](https://nnty.fun/downloads/books/TAEL/QUOTATIONS/CLASSIC%20WORKS/The%20Adages%20of%20Erasmus%20-%20University%20of%20Toronto%20Press.%20William%20Barker%20%282001%29.pdf).

The English “between a rock and a hard place” is analogous, but direct descent from Homer is not established merely by similarity. “Out of the frying pan into the fire” is closer to Erasmus’s “escape one, fall into the other” form than to the simultaneous-choice image.

### Art

Important modern treatments include:

- Peter Paul Rubens, *Glaucus and Scylla* (seventeenth century): Ovidian transformation and erotic pursuit.
- Eglon van der Neer, *Circe Punishing Glaucus by Turning Scylla into a Monster* (1695): Circe poisons the bathing place.
- Henry Fuseli, *Odysseus between Scylla and Charybdis* (1794–96): sublime terror and impossible choice.
- Nineteenth-century academic and Symbolist images frequently turn Scylla into a femme fatale, a development grounded more in Ovid and post-Classical gender symbolism than in Homer.

### Literature

Dante uses Scylla and Charybdis as inherited geographic and moral imagery. Milton and later English poets employ the names proverbially. James Joyce titled the ninth episode of *Ulysses* “Scylla and Charybdis”; its opposing interpretive positions transpose the maritime dilemma into argument. The title does not retell the monster episode literally.

### Psychology and esotericism

Freudian, Jungian, and archetypal readings treat the pair as competing psychic dangers, engulfment versus fragmentation, or destructive maternal images. These are twentieth-century interpretive systems, not recoveries of ancient doctrine.

No substantial role for either monster is established in Renaissance ceremonial magic, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, the canonical grimoires, or the Greek Magical Papyri. Modern occult correspondences assigning planets, tarot cards, colors, crystals, or ritual offerings are **[Modern invention]** unless explicitly presented as the creator’s new practice.

### Popular media

Scylla and Charybdis appear in films and television derived from the *Odyssey*, Rick Riordan’s novels and adaptations, comics, role-playing games, *God of War*, *SMITE*, and numerous fantasy bestiaries. Modern productions commonly:

- treat them as sisters or a coordinated pair;
- give Charybdis a visible body and teeth;
- make Scylla’s six “heads” six independent dogs;
- allow heroes to kill either monster;
- combine the Circe transformation with incompatible genealogies.

These are legitimate modern retellings but should be labeled as such. For example, a game’s claim that the two call each other sisters is canon internal to that game, not Greek evidence.

### Political and commercial use

“Scylla and Charybdis” remains a politically neutral reusable metaphor applied to war, fiscal policy, diplomacy, constitutional conflicts, and maritime chokepoints. Scylla also names ships, military operations, software, zoological taxa, astronomical projects, and commercial products. Such naming demonstrates cultural recognition, not religious continuity.

No significant documented nationalist or extremist cult appropriation comparable to the use of some Olympian or Indo-European symbols was found. Absence of evidence should not be inflated into proof that no isolated usage exists.

## 9. Historiography and contested questions

### Was the Homeric location Messina?

The dominant ancient identification is Messina, explicit in later Greek and Roman geography. Strabo rationalized Charybdis as a real eddy near Messana. Yet Homer supplies no western place-name in Book 12, and the relative positions and distances do not map neatly onto the modern strait. Three positions therefore need separation:

1. **Homer intended Messina:** plausible because early Greek sailors knew western routes, but not demonstrable from the episode alone.
2. **The episode drew on generalized seafaring motifs and was later localized:** also plausible.
3. **A different strait, such as the Bosporus, lies behind it:** proposed but not dominant.

The exact physical “identification” is therefore **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**, while ancient localization at Messina is securely attested historical reception.

### Is Scylla’s name connected with dogs?

Homer’s puppy voice and later canine anatomy make the link attractive. LSJ nevertheless marks derivation from *skylax* as probably erroneous. The canine reading may be poetic wordplay or secondary iconographic interpretation rather than etymology.

### Was Scylla originally a goddess?

There is no cultic dossier supporting demotion from a once-worshipped “great goddess.” Her female torso and descent from Hecate do not prove prior goddess status. Claims that she was an aboriginal sea goddess displaced by Olympian religion remain **[Speculative modern reconstruction]**.

### Is Charybdis simply a whirlpool?

In Homer she is grammatically and poetically animate—“divine,” swallowing and vomiting—but physically undescribed. Calling her only a natural whirlpool erases personification; giving her a fixed monster body goes beyond the text. The best description is “personified swallowing marine hazard.”

### Did Circe transform Scylla in Greek myth from the beginning?

No. Homer’s Circe knows Scylla but does not claim responsibility. Ovid provides the full surviving Circe–Glaucus transformation narrative around 8 CE. Earlier lost versions may have existed, but that cannot be established from the surviving record.

### Was Charybdis punished for helping Poseidon against Zeus?

Only late scholastic material gives Poseidon–Gaia parentage, flooding, voracity, cattle theft, and punishment. Popular accounts often combine these separate variants into one seamless biography. That composite is **[Modern harmonization]**.

### Were they worshipped or offered human victims?

No evidence establishes this. Literary victims are narrative casualties, not ritual sacrifices. Modern pagan pages that assign Scylla human sacrifice, sacred stars, or elaborate correspondences do not substitute for inscriptions or ritual texts.

### Major scholarship

- Otto Waser’s *Skylla und Charybdis in der Literatur und Kunst der Griechen und Römer* (1894) remains a foundational collection of literary and visual material, although its chronology and interpretive assumptions require updating. [Digitized monograph](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/waser1894/0106/text_ocr).
- Timothy Gantz, *Early Greek Myth* (1993), is especially valuable for separating early genealogical fragments from later synthesis.
- LIMC, “Skylla I,” catalogues the ancient iconography and its types.
- Walter Burkert’s *Greek Religion* and Lewis Richard Farnell’s *Cults of the Greek States* are important controls precisely because the pair do not emerge as institutional cult deities.
- Modern Homeric criticism debates oral composition, textual fixation, interpolation, geography, and narratological function.
- Kerényi is useful as twentieth-century interpretive reception.
- Robert Graves frequently harmonizes, ritualizes, and euhemerizes variants; he should be cited as modern mythographic reception, not as primary evidence.
