# Ares: Research Dossier

**Class:** Olympian god (*theos*), personified power of battle, and divine warrior in Greek tradition.  
**Earliest secure attestation:** Mycenaean Greek **a-re** in Linear B administrative records, probably referring to Ares, c. 1400–1200 BCE; a Knossian tablet records an oil offering to *a-re*, while compounds containing *a-re* occur at Knossos, Pylos, Mycenae, and Thebes. The reading is secure, but not every compound is necessarily theophoric, and the tablets preserve no myths about him. Jennifer Gulizio’s study remains the fundamental treatment of this material.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early]** Securely attested Mycenaean, Archaic, or early-Classical evidence.
- **[Canonical]** A widespread literary formulation that became standard, without implying theological uniformity.
- **[Regional]** Local cult, iconography, genealogy, or narrative.
- **[Late]** Hellenistic, Roman-imperial, late-antique, Byzantine, or mythographic elaboration.
- **[Syncretistic]** An historically situated identification with another culture’s deity.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A modern inference rather than an ancient statement.
- **[Modern invention/reception]** Post-antique reinterpretation, fiction, occult correspondence, or revived practice.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

**[Early]** Ares is both an anthropomorphic Olympian and the active presence of violent battle. Homer can move almost imperceptibly between Ares the individual god and *arēs* as war, battle, or martial fury. This is not evidence that the deity began as a literary abstraction: the Mycenaean evidence predates Homeric poetry, and later inscriptions document actual worship. Ares embodies aggressive force, battle noise, bloodshed, sudden reversal, and the warrior’s terrifying power; Athena more often represents disciplined strategy, defense, and effective civic warfare, but that contrast is neither absolute nor a universal Greek dogma.

In the *Iliad* Ares supports first one combatant and then another, is called *alloprosallos*—approximately “leaning now to one side, now the other”—and personifies the instability of battle. Zeus tells him that he is the most hateful Olympian because quarrel, war, and fighting are dear to him, yet immediately has Paeon heal him because he remains Zeus’s son. The scene is characterization inside an epic narrative, not proof that Greeks generally rejected his worship. [Homer, *Iliad* 5](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0134%3Abook%3D5)

### Names and forms

- **Ἄρης — Árēs/Ares.** The standard Ionic-Attic form.
- **Ἄρευς — Áreus.** Aeolic and poetic variant important to etymological reconstruction.
- **a-re.** Linear B syllabic spelling; case and interpretation depend on the individual tablet.
- **Ἐνυάλιος — Enyálios/Enyalios.** Frequently an epithet or functional equivalent of Ares in later interpretation, but in Homer, inscriptions, and some cults Enyalios can be an independent god. Treating the two as timelessly identical erases real historical variation.
- **Mars/Mavors/Mamers.** Roman and Italic names belonging originally to a distinct Roman god. Greek and Roman literary translation convention equated Ares and Mars, but Mars possessed his own priesthoods, calendar, civic ancestry, agricultural-protective rituals, and exceptionally high Roman status. “Mars is Ares” is therefore a Graeco-Roman translation and syncretism, not an original identity.

### Etymology

**[Disputed]** Ancient etymology began at least with Plato’s *Cratylus* 407d, which connected Ares with masculinity and courage through *arrēn* (“male”). That is philosophical wordplay, not historical linguistics.

A longstanding modern derivation connects Ἄρης with Homeric **ἀρή**, “bane, ruin, destruction.” Chantraine, Kretschmer, and others accepted some relationship; Beekes remained cautious because the direction of derivation and the morphology are uncertain. Michael Weiss instead reconstructs a Proto-Indo-European root noun connected with Latin *ruere* and Vedic *rav-*, explaining Aeolic Ἄρευς through Stang’s Law. No etymology commands universal assent. Alexander Millington also explores contact with the Anatolian war-and-plague god Iyarri, but resemblance and possible influence do not establish that Ares was simply borrowed from Iyarri. [Millington, “Iyarri at the Interface”](https://brill.com/previewpdf/book/edcoll/9789004253414/B9789004253414_020.xml); [Weiss, “Ares the Ripper”](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3Ae01a91bc-8605-4b21-9a53-17075901bdc9)

### Principal poetic epithets

**[Early unless otherwise marked]**

- **βροτολοιγός (*brotoloigos*)** — “bane/destroyer of mortals.”
- **ἀνδροφόνος / ἀνδρειφόντης** — “man-slaying.”
- **μιαιφόνος (*miaiphonos*)** — “blood-stained,” “blood-defiling.”
- **τειχεσιπλήτης (*teichesiplētēs*)** — “stormer/smiter of walls.”
- **χάλκεος, χαλκοκορυστής** — “bronze,” “bronze-armored.”
- **ὀβριμόθυμος** — “strong-spirited,” “violent-hearted.”
- **θοῦρος** — “furious,” “impetuous.”
- **πολεμόκλονος** — “raiser of battle tumult.”
- **λαοσσόος** — “rouser/rallier of the host.”
- **ἄατος πολέμοιο** — “insatiate of war.”
- **ἐγχέσπαλος** — “spear-brandishing.”
- **χρυσοπήληξ** — “golden-helmeted.”
- **Ἐνυάλιος** — “Enyalios”; either epithet or neighboring war god depending on source.
- **Ἐνυώ** and **Ἔρις** are not epithets but associated divine figures, Enyo as battle goddess and Eris as strife.

### Cult titles

- **Ἄρης Ἀφνειός — Ares Aphneios, “Abundant/Wealthy.”** **[Regional]** Near Tegea. Pausanias connects it with Aerope, who allegedly continued nursing Ares’s son after dying in childbirth.
- **Ἄρης Γυναικοθοίνας — Gynaikothoinas, “Feasted by Women.”** **[Regional]** Tegea. Tegean women reportedly sacrificed to Ares after defeating the Spartans and excluded men from the sacrificial meat.
- **Ἄρης Θηρίτας — Theritas, “Beastly/Fierce.”** **[Regional]** Laconia. Pausanias knew a story that Thero nursed him but preferred derivation from *thēr*, “beast.”
- **Ἄρης Ἵππιος — Hippios, “Of Horses.”** **[Regional/Late]** Attested locally and appropriate to the chariot-driving warrior, but never a universal title.
- **Ἄρης Ἐπήκοος — Epēkoos, “Who listens/responds.”** **[Regional, Roman imperial]** Probably attested at Amblada in Pisidia.
- **Ἄρειος — Areios, “of Ares/warlike.”** Applied to other gods, notably Athena Areia and Aphrodite Areia; it does not always mean that those gods were subordinate to Ares.

A convenient collection of literary testimonia, useful when checked against its cited editions, is the [Theoi cult index](https://www.theoi.com/Cult/AresCult.html).

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Mycenaean evidence

**[Early: c. 1400–1200 BCE]** Linear B *a-re* appears in palace records. The strongest cultic item is an allocation of oil at Knossos; personal-name compounds containing *a-re* occur at several palace sites. The documents are clay administrative tablets, unintentionally fired in palace destructions. They establish that the name or divine element existed in Bronze Age Greek and probably received offerings. They do **not** establish Homeric genealogy, Thracian residence, the Aphrodite affair, or any other later myth. The tablets can be searched through the [DAMOS corpus](https://damos.hf.uio.no/about/content/); the focused study is [Gulizio, “A-re in the Linear B Tablets”](https://sites.utexas.edu/scripts/files/2016/07/gulizio_2001.pdf).

### Archaic epic and hymnic corpus

- **Homer, *Iliad*** — Greek, monumental composition conventionally dated c. 750–700 BCE, transmitted in much later papyri and medieval manuscripts. Ares fights for Troy, abandons a promise to Hera and Athena, is wounded by Diomedes with Athena’s assistance, rebuked by Zeus, healed by Paeon, restrained from avenging Ascalaphus, and defeated by Athena in the battle of the gods. This is the richest early characterization.
- **Homer, *Odyssey* 8.266–366** — c. late eighth/early seventh century BCE. Demodocus sings of Ares and Aphrodite caught in Hephaestus’s invisible bonds before the male gods. [Primary text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D8)
- **Hesiod, *Theogony* 921–937** — c. 700 BCE. Zeus and Hera beget Ares; Ares and Aphrodite beget Phobos, Deimos, and Harmonia. [Primary text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0130%3Acard%3D901)
- **Hesiodic *Shield of Heracles*** — probably sixth century BCE, although anciently transmitted under Hesiod’s name. Ares intervenes for his son Cycnus and is opposed by Athena and Zeus.
- **Homeric Hymn 8, “To Ares.”** Its planetary language—Ares as bearer of the fiery sphere and ruler of the sevenfold courses—differs from early epic. M. L. West argued that the poem is late and related to Proclus, probably fifth century CE, rather than an Archaic hymn. It must not be used uncritically as evidence for early Greek astral theology. [West’s study](https://electronicsandbooks.com/edt/manual/Magazine/C/Classical%20Quarterly%20UK/1970/West%20-%201970%20-%20The%20Eighth%20Homeric%20Hymn%20and%20Proclus.pdf)

### Classical poetry, drama, and history

- **Aeschylus, *Seven Against Thebes* 42–48** — 467 BCE. The Seven sacrifice a bull over a black shield and swear by Ares, Enyo, and blood-loving Phobos.
- **Aeschylus fragments** — fifth century BCE. A papyrus fragment characterizes Ares as Hera’s violent, ungovernable son.
- **Sophocles, *Oedipus Tyrannus* 190–215** — c. 429–425 BCE. The chorus metaphorically identifies plague with “the brazen-shielded Ares,” showing extension from battlefield killing to civic devastation; this does not prove a general cult of “Ares the plague god.”
- **Euripides, *Phoenician Women*, *Heracles*, and lost/fragmentary plays** — late fifth century BCE. Ares is woven into Theban genealogy, the dragon, the Spartoi, and Harmonia.
- **Herodotus, *Histories* 2.59–64, 4.59–62, 5.7** — c. 430s–420s BCE. Through *interpretatio graeca*, Herodotus calls Egyptian, Scythian, and Thracian gods “Ares.” These passages document Greek translation of foreign religions, not proof that those peoples worshipped the Greek Olympian under that name.

### Hellenistic and Roman-imperial corpus

- **Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica*** — third century BCE. Ares owns the Colchian field and dragon; the Amazons are closely associated with him; Aeetes bears armor given by Ares after the god killed Mimas.
- **Callimachus and Hellenistic poetry** — third century BCE. Ares functions as divine warrior and poetic metonym for battle.
- **Diodorus Siculus, *Library of History*** — first century BCE. Rationalizing and synthetic accounts of Amazons, Dionysian wars, and genealogies.
- **Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca*** — first or second century CE in its surviving form, not the second-century-BCE scholar Apollodorus of Athens. It systematizes Ares’s genealogy, Aloadae imprisonment, Cadmus, Amazons, and heroic conflicts.
- **Pausanias, *Description of Greece*** — c. 150–180 CE. Essential eyewitness/literary record of sanctuaries, images, and local myths in Athens, Laconia, Arcadia, Olympia, and elsewhere. [Perseus catalogue and text](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/library/urn%3Acts%3AgreekLit%3Atlg0525.tlg001.perseus-eng2/)
- **Hyginus, *Fabulae*** — Latin, probably second century CE with a corrupt later transmission. Mythographic summaries and genealogies; useful but not automatically evidence of early Greek belief.
- **Antoninus Liberalis, *Metamorphoses*** — second/third century CE. Includes the late story of Ares becoming a fish during the gods’ flight from Typhon.
- **Nonnus, *Dionysiaca*** — Greek, fifth century CE. Vast late-antique elaboration: Ares participates in Dionysus’s Indian war and assumes highly rhetorical and astrological forms.

### Orphic and magical texts

**[Late ritual corpus]** *Orphic Hymn* 65, probably Roman imperial, calls Ares indestructible, man-slaying, blood-smeared, and wall-battering but asks him to stop raging strife, yield to Aphrodite and Dionysus, exchange arms for Demeter’s work, and grant youth-nurturing peace. Frankincense is prescribed. [Greek text and translation](https://www.hellenicgods.org/orphic-hymn-to-ares)

**[Syncretistic, c. second century BCE–fifth/sixth century CE]** The Greek Magical Papyri come from Graeco-Roman Egypt and combine Greek, Egyptian, Jewish, and other divine names. Ares occurs mainly as a source or analogue of martial strength and within planetary correspondences, not as the center of a single stable “Ares cult.” PGM references must be cited spell by spell; the collection’s multilingual, composite nature forbids treating it as a Greek canonical scripture. [Betz edition](https://mahabodhi.saranagamana.org/studies/texts/other/The_Greek_Magical_Papyri_in_Translation.pdf); [Berlin PGM database](https://berlpap.smb.museum/pgm/)

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain

**[Early]** Ares is the divine reality of close combat, killing, blood, rout, battle din, warrior aggression, and warfare’s capacity to change sides. Homer also uses his name metonymically for fighting and weapons. He can impart courage and force, rally troops, and accompany lawful civic armies; he is therefore not merely a demon of “evil war.”

**[Classical development]** Tragedy extends Ares metaphorically to plague, civil bloodshed, sexual violence, and social ruin. Oaths invoke him as an enforcer in military contexts. This is functional extension, not evidence for a separate universal jurisdiction over disease or justice.

**[Regional imperial development]** In southern Asia Minor, Ares could be a civic protector, oracle-associated god, mounted deity, guardian against brigandage, and perhaps protector of crops and livestock. Coins of Amblada show him with a trident; Clarian oracles prescribed images of Ares with Hermes and Dike/Thesmos at Syedra and Iconium. These are local hybrid developments, not properties of every Greek Ares. [Arroyo-Quirce, 2026](https://www.jaha.org.ro/index.php/JAHA/article/download/1502/1623)

### Appearance and attributes

Ares has no animal-headed or otherwise nonhuman standard form. In Archaic and Classical art he appears as:

- a bearded, fully armed hoplite with helmet, spear, cuirass, greaves, and round shield;
- a youthful, often beardless and sometimes nude warrior, especially from the Classical period onward;
- a chariot warrior accompanied by Phobos and Deimos;
- a participant in Olympian assemblies, Gigantomachy scenes, the birth of Athena, and the Cycnus-Heracles combat.

Because these are ordinary warrior attributes, identification may depend on an inscription, narrative context, companion figures, or comparison with established types. A lone armed youth is not automatically Ares.

The *LIMC* article “Ares” catalogues the surviving iconography; Irmgard Beck’s work on vase painting and P. Bruneau’s *LIMC* treatment remain central. The comprehensive modern review is Alexander Millington’s [*War and the Warrior*](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/1427880/).

### Important images

- **[Early, sixth century BCE]** Attic black-figure vases show Ares in scenes such as Heracles’s battle with Cycnus and divine assemblies.
- **[Classical]** Alcamenes, a fifth-century-BCE sculptor, made the Ares statue later reported in Athens. The original is lost; Roman statue types sometimes associated with it remain disputed.
- **[Hellenistic/Roman]** The “Ares Borghese” type portrays a youthful helmeted nude with an ankle ring. Its date, original identity, relation to Alcamenes, and whether the ring alludes to restraint remain debated. [Hesperia study](https://www.ascsa.edu.gr/publications/hesperia/article/85/3/577-625)
- **[Roman]** Pompeian wall paintings of Ares/Mars and Aphrodite/Venus translate the Greek affair into Roman domestic decorative language; identification often reflects Roman Mars as much as Greek Ares.
- **[Regional imperial]** Pisidian coins depict hoplite, mounted, and trident-bearing forms.

### Animals, plants, colors, planets

- **Animals:** Dogs are sacrificed to Enyalios at Sparta; vultures and certain warlike birds appear in later literary association. The boar is attached to the late Ares-kills-Adonis variant, not a universal cult animal.
- **Dragons/serpents:** The Ismenian dragon at Thebes and Colchian dragon are sacred guardians belonging to Ares in myth.
- **Horses:** Chariot and *Hippios* associations occur, but no pan-Hellenic “sacred horse doctrine” is attested.
- **Planet:** The Greek planet Πυρόεις (“Fiery”) became increasingly identified with Ares in Hellenistic and late-antique astrology. Latin *Mars* supplied the modern planetary name. This astral identity is later than the Mycenaean and Homeric evidence.
- **Metal/color/day:** Iron, red, and Tuesday belong principally to late-antique, Roman, astrological, and medieval planetary systems. They should not be projected wholesale into Archaic cult.
- **Number:** Five as “the number of Mars” derives from later astrological and Hermetic systems, not ancient Greek worship of Ares.

## 4. Genealogy and divine relations

### Parentage and siblings

**[Canonical Archaic]** Hesiod and Homer make Ares the son of **Zeus and Hera**. Hebe and Eileithyia are full siblings in Hesiodic genealogy; Hephaestus’s paternity and birth vary among sources.

**[Late Roman variant]** Ovid’s *Fasti* 5 says Hera/Juno conceived Mars without Jupiter after Flora supplied a magical flower. This is a Roman story about Juno and Mars, modeled against the fatherless birth of Minerva and/or Vulcan traditions. It is not early Greek evidence about Ares.

### Partners and offspring

**Aphrodite:** Hesiod makes her the mother by Ares of **Phobos**, **Deimos**, and **Harmonia**. The *Odyssey* makes her Hephaestus’s unfaithful wife or partner, but does not name children in the net episode. Later art and poetry regularize the pair.

Other traditions assign Ares numerous mortal or semi-divine partners and children, often to mark a warrior, violent king, Amazon, or regional founder as martial:

- **Aglaurus/Aglauros:** mother of Alcippe.
- **Otrera:** commonly mother of Amazon queens in later mythography.
- **Astynome or Pelopia:** named as mothers of Cycnus in competing traditions.
- **Cyrene:** mother of Diomedes of Thrace in one genealogy.
- **Demonice:** mother of Evenus, Molus, Pylus, and Thestius in Apollodoran genealogy.
- **Aerope:** mother of Aeropus in Arcadian local myth.
- **Pyrene:** sometimes mother of Cycnus.
- **Harpinna:** mother of Oenomaus in one tradition; other texts make Oenomaus a son of Alxion or Hyperochus.
- **Sterope:** another mother assigned to Oenomaus.
- **Protogeneia:** mother of Oxylus in some traditions.
- **Dotis/Chryse:** attached to regional sons in late genealogies.

Named children include Phobos, Deimos, Harmonia, Alcippe, Cycnus, Diomedes of Thrace, Ascalaphus, Ialmenus, Evenus, Oenomaus, Meleager in a minority tradition, Dryas, Lycaon, Tereus in some genealogies, and Amazon queens such as Hippolyta, Antiope, Melanippe, and Penthesilea. Lists differ because genealogical attribution was a flexible method of defining character and local prestige.

### Every documented divine interaction

The following list separates actual narrative or cultic conjunctions from mere co-membership in the pantheon.

- **Zeus — father, commander, critic, and healer-by-proxy.** Ares is his son; Zeus calls him the most hateful Olympian but orders Paeon to heal him. *Iliad* 5, c. 750–700 BCE. **[Early]**
- **Hera — mother and political superior.** Ares initially promises Hera and Athena to aid the Achaeans; Hera later complains of his Trojan slaughter and restrains him from avenging Ascalaphus. *Iliad* 5 and 15. **[Early]**
- **Athena — principal battlefield opponent.** She helps Diomedes wound Ares, later fells him with a boundary stone, and repeatedly represents controlled force against his unstable violence. *Iliad* 5 and 21. **[Early]**
- **Aphrodite — lover and mother of his children.** Their affair is exposed by Hephaestus; Hesiod makes them parents of Phobos, Deimos, and Harmonia. *Odyssey* 8; *Theogony* 933–937. **[Early/canonical]**
- **Hephaestus — deceived partner and captor.** He traps Ares and Aphrodite in invisible bonds and summons the gods to witness their adultery. *Odyssey* 8. **[Early]**
- **Poseidon — prosecutor or hostile relative.** Poseidon prosecutes Ares for killing his son Halirrhothius in the Areopagus etiological myth. Pausanias 1.28.5 and later sources. **[Regional/late-attested]**
- **Apollo — ally at Troy and athletic victor over Ares.** Apollo urges Ares against Diomedes; Pausanias reports a tradition that Apollo defeated Ares in boxing at the primordial Olympic Games. *Iliad* 5; Pausanias 5.7.10. **[Early; late-reported variant]**
- **Artemis — associated in Thracian interpretatio and alternate killer of Adonis.** Herodotus lists “Ares, Dionysus, and Artemis” as Thracian gods under Greek names; in one Adonis variant Artemis rather than Ares sends the boar. **[Syncretistic/variant]**
- **Hermes — rescuer.** Hermes frees Ares after Otus and Ephialtes imprison him in a bronze jar for thirteen months. *Iliad* 5.385–391. **[Early]**
- **Paeon — healer.** At Zeus’s command Paeon heals Ares’s wound. *Iliad* 5.899–904. **[Early]**
- **Hebe — attendant and sister.** She bathes and clothes Ares after his healing. *Iliad* 5.905. **[Early]**
- **Eris — battle companion.** Strife appears beside Ares in Homeric battle description. *Iliad* 4.440–445. **[Early]**
- **Enyo — battle companion and cult neighbor.** Aeschylus places Ares, Enyo, and Phobos together in the oath of the Seven; later genealogy sometimes makes Enyalios the child of Ares and Enyo. **[Early/variant]**
- **Phobos — son and attendant.** Child of Ares and Aphrodite in Hesiod; accompanies battle and appears in martial oaths. **[Early]**
- **Deimos — son and attendant.** Child of Ares and Aphrodite; personifies terror in battle. *Theogony* 934. **[Early]**
- **Harmonia — daughter.** Child of Ares and Aphrodite and wife of Cadmus. *Theogony* 937; later Theban traditions elaborate Ares’s role in the marriage. **[Early/canonical]**
- **Dionysus — fellow Olympian and peacemaking alternative.** *Orphic Hymn* 65 asks Ares to yield to Dionysus’s revels; late stories say Dionysus, where Ares failed, persuaded Hephaestus to release Hera. **[Late]**
- **Demeter — peaceful counter-domain.** *Orphic Hymn* 65 asks Ares to exchange weapons for Demeter’s agricultural works. **[Late ritual]**
- **The Muses, Graces, Hours, Apollo, Artemis, Aphrodite, Hebe, Harmonia, and Hermes — joint divine celebration.** Ares sports among them at Apollo’s Olympian feast. *Homeric Hymn to Apollo* 186–206. **[Archaic/early-Classical hymn]**
- **Hades — indirectly connected through the Aloadae crisis.** No surviving early source gives Ares a substantive direct encounter with Hades; shared underworld or “chthonic” classification is a modern construction. **[Absence of evidence]**
- **Thanatos — recovered or avenged in later Sisyphus traditions.** Some late summaries make Ares release Death after Sisyphus binds him; this is not in the earliest surviving Sisyphus evidence. **[Late]**
- **Dike/Thesmos and Hermes Argeiphontes — joint protective images.** Clarian oracles ordered cities in Roman Asia Minor to erect them with Ares against brigandage. **[Regional/syncretistic imperial]**
- **Onuris/Anhur — Egyptian identification.** Greek interpreters equated an Egyptian martial deity with Ares; Antoninus Liberalis’s fish transformation is a late mythographic expression of that translation. **[Syncretistic]**
- **Scythian “Ares” — interpretative equation.** Herodotus calls the Scythian sword-god Ares and describes sacrifices at brushwood altars. The indigenous name is not preserved by him. **[Syncretistic, fifth century BCE]**
- **Thracian “Ares” — interpretative equation.** Herodotus presents Ares among the principal Thracian gods. This does not demonstrate an imported Thracian origin for Greek Ares. **[Syncretistic]**
- **Mars — Roman equation.** Translation and artistic assimilation joined the two gods, especially under Roman rule, while Roman cult institutions retained a distinct Mars. **[Syncretistic]**
- **Maḥrem — Aksumite equation.** Greek royal inscriptions translate the Aksumite dynastic god Maḥrem as Ares. **[Syncretistic, early centuries CE]**
- **Typhon — enemy from whom Ares flees.** Antoninus Liberalis says Ares became a fish in Egypt while escaping Typhon. **[Late]**
- **Mimas — defeated Giant.** Ares kills Mimas in the Gigantomachy; attested in Hellenistic epic and vase iconography. **[Canonical/Hellenistic literary attestation]**

No secure narrative interaction is attested merely because Ares and another Olympian appear in the same divine catalogue. Such catalogic co-presence has not been inflated into an “interaction” here.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Ares in the Trojan War

**[Early]** The *Iliad* supplies the fullest early Ares narrative. Although he had agreed with Hera and Athena to support the Achaeans, Aphrodite draws him toward Troy. Apollo urges him to stop Diomedes. Athena gains Zeus’s permission to intervene, guides Diomedes’ spear, and wounds Ares. His cry is likened to nine or ten thousand warriors; he flees to Olympus, complains to Zeus, is rebuked, healed, and restored.

The scene does not make him powerless: his body, voice, and casualties are enormous, and a mortal can wound him only through Athena’s agency. His defeat dramatizes the subordination of undirected battle fury to Zeus’s order and Athena’s intelligence.

In Book 15 Ares learns that his son Ascalaphus has died and prepares to enter battle against Zeus’s prohibition; Athena physically disarms and restrains him. In Book 21, once Zeus permits divine combat, Athena again defeats him with a stone. Ares is therefore emotionally bound to his children and formidable, but strategically unreliable.

### Ares and Aphrodite in Hephaestus’s net

**[Early]** In *Odyssey* 8 the bard Demodocus sings that Helios reveals Ares and Aphrodite’s affair to Hephaestus. Hephaestus constructs invisible, unbreakable bonds around his bed, traps the couple, and calls the gods. Poseidon persuades him to release Ares after guaranteeing compensation; Ares escapes to Thrace and Aphrodite to Cyprus.

Important distinctions:

- The *Odyssey* presents a comic divine performance nested inside another narrative.
- It does not establish that every Greek community regarded Aphrodite as Hephaestus’s wife.
- Hesiod independently makes Ares and Aphrodite parents of Phobos, Deimos, and Harmonia without mentioning adultery.
- The familiar image of Ares as Aphrodite’s permanent “husband” is a later harmonization.
- Late stories that Ares tried unsuccessfully to force Hephaestus back to Olympus depend on post-Homeric and especially Byzantine testimony.

### The Aloadae imprison Ares

**[Early]** *Iliad* 5.385–391 says Otus and Ephialtes, the sons of Aloeus, bound Ares and confined him in a bronze jar for thirteen months. He nearly perished; their stepmother Eriboea informed Hermes, who rescued him. Later mythographers integrate this episode into the Aloadae assault on Olympus. The Homeric passage does not narrate a full war or explain the capture.

### Cadmus, the dragon, and Harmonia

**[Archaic core, later systematization]** Cadmus kills the dragon guarding Ares’s spring at Thebes and sows its teeth, producing armed Spartoi. In Apollodorus’s systematic version Cadmus serves Ares for eight years as expiation, after which Zeus gives him Harmonia, Ares and Aphrodite’s daughter. Their wedding unites the Theban founder with Ares’s family.

The story explains Theban autochthonous warrior ancestry and recurrent civic violence. Whether it preserves a historically dominant Theban cult of Ares is disputed: older scholarship inferred one from myth, but epigraphic and numismatic confirmation is unexpectedly slight.

### Ares, Cycnus, and Heracles

**[Archaic]** In the Hesiodic *Shield*, Cycnus, Ares’s violent son, attacks travelers and challenges Heracles. Athena advises Heracles; Zeus protects the cosmic order. After Heracles kills Cycnus, Ares attacks despite Athena’s warning. Heracles wounds him in the thigh and Ares is carried to Olympus.

Variants relocate Cycnus and alter his mother, conduct, and motivation. Some later versions say he built a temple from travelers’ skulls, an embellishment absent from the earliest complete account.

### Halirrhothius and the Areopagus

**[Athenian regional aition, securely attested only late]** Pausanias reports that Halirrhothius, son of Poseidon, violated or attempted to violate Alcippe, daughter of Ares and Aglauros. Ares killed him and became the first defendant tried for bloodshed on the Hill of Ares, the Areopagus; the gods acquitted him.

The myth explains the court’s name and homicide jurisdiction. Aeschylus’s *Eumenides* gives a different foundational legal narrative centered on Orestes and does not foreground Ares’s trial. The Halirrhothius story should therefore not be projected automatically into early Athenian legal history. [Pausanias translation](https://pausanias.symmachus.org/pausanias.pdf)

### Amazons as daughters of Ares

**[Archaic/Classical collective genealogy, elaborated later]** Greek authors repeatedly locate the Amazons at the margins of the Greek world and call them daughters or descendants of Ares. Individual queens—Hippolyta, Antiope, Melanippe, and Penthesilea—are assigned to Ares, often with Otrera as mother. This paternity expresses martial character and geographic otherness; it is not one stable family tree.

Penthesilea’s Trojan expedition belonged to the lost *Aethiopis* and survives through summaries and later authors. Ares’s direct participation in Amazon campaigns is much more fully elaborated in Hellenistic and Roman sources than in surviving Archaic poetry.

### Adonis

**[Late variant]** Ares, jealous of Aphrodite’s love for Adonis, becomes or sends the boar that kills him. Other versions blame Artemis, Apollo, or an ordinary boar. The Ares version is not the earliest securely recoverable core and should not be presented as canonical.

### Gigantomachy and Typhon

**[Archaic visual/Hellenistic literary]** Ares participates in the gods’ war against the Giants and kills Mimas. In the much later Antoninus Liberalis, he flees Typhon to Egypt and assumes fish form. These belong to different narrative strata: the Gigantomachy integrates him into Olympian defense; the Typhon story participates in Graeco-Egyptian interpretive mythology.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### General distribution

Older scholarship portrayed Ares as nearly cultless. That conclusion depended heavily on literary hostility and evidence available before many inscriptions were published. Modern surveys document cult or dedications in well over a hundred communities, though Ares rarely rivaled Athena, Apollo, Artemis, or Zeus as the organizing patron of a major mainland polis. “Few monumental pan-Hellenic centers” is defensible; “the Greeks did not worship Ares” is not. [Millington’s reassessment](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/1427880/1/Alexander_Thomas_Millington_Ares_-_Full_PhD_Thesis_%28corrected%29.pdf)

### Athens and Attica

**[Classical–Roman]**

- A precinct and cult existed at **Acharnae**, probably the chief Attic center before the Roman period.
- Ares and Athena Areia occur in the fourth-century-BCE **Ephebic Oath**, linking them with the military obligations of citizens.
- A Classical temple was dismantled and transferred stone by stone to the Athenian Agora in the Augustan age, probably from an Attic deme. Its new setting and dedications to Ares and Augustus tied the cult to Roman imperial ideology and Mars Ultor.
- Pausanias saw statues of Ares by Alcamenes, Athena, and two Aphrodites in the Agora sanctuary.
- The Areopagus connected Ares etiologically with homicide jurisdiction, but it was not simply “Ares’s temple court.”

The excavation and reconstruction history is documented by the American School’s [study of the Temple of Ares](https://www.ascsa.edu.gr/uploads/media/hesperia/146631.pdf).

### Sparta and Laconia

Popular summaries exaggerate Ares as “patron god of Sparta.” Athena, Artemis Orthia, Apollo, the Dioscuri, and other gods had conspicuous Spartan institutions. Nevertheless, martial cults of Ares and especially Enyalios are documented:

- Pausanias saw an ancient **Enyalios in fetters**, interpreted as ensuring that martial power would remain in Sparta. The source says Enyalios, not unambiguously Ares.
- At the Phoebaeum, Spartan youth contingents sacrificed puppies to Enyalios before a brutal nocturnal contest. The dog sacrifice is attested; calling it human sacrifice is incorrect.
- A sanctuary of **Ares Theritas** stood on the road from Sparta.
- At Geronthrae, an annual festival of Ares excluded women from the sacred grove—strikingly opposite the Tegean women’s cult.

### Tegea and Arcadia

- **Ares Gynaikothoinas:** Women allegedly defeated a Spartan army under Marpessa, sacrificed victory offerings to Ares, and excluded men from the meat.
- **Ares Aphneios:** Connected with Aerope’s miraculous post-mortem nursing of her child Aeropus.
- Additional images and altars reported by Pausanias show that Arcadian Ares was not confined to a single destructive identity.

### Olympia

Pausanias records an altar of Ares within the Altis. He was present in the ritual geography of the pan-Hellenic sanctuary but had no festival there comparable to Zeus’s.

### Crete and the Mycenaean continuum

Linear B evidence at Knossos gives Bronze Age depth, but continuity across the post-palatial centuries cannot simply be assumed. Later Cretan inscriptions and cults can demonstrate renewed or continuing worship only at their respective dates.

### Thrace

Homer frequently calls Thrace Ares’s favored country, and Herodotus says Thracians worshipped “Ares.” Yet Greek authors routinely located martial and violent figures in Thrace and translated indigenous gods through Greek categories. No evidence proves that Greek Ares originated there. “Thracian Ares” is partly Greek ethnography and *interpretatio graeca*, not a transparent indigenous name.

### Asia Minor

**[Hellenistic–late antique regional florescence]** Ares was especially prominent in parts of Lycia, Pisidia, Kabalia, Cilicia, and Ionia.

- **Metropolis in Ionia:** inscriptions and coins from the second century BCE to third century CE document one of the densest local Ares cults known. [Christina Sponsel’s study](https://www.university-press.fau.de/en/product/978-3-96147-074-7/)
- **Syedra:** inscriptions, coinage, and a Clarian oracle connect Ares with civic defense against brigands.
- **Iconium:** another Clarian response prescribed a cultic image grouping.
- **Amblada:** a tribe bore Ares’s name; dedications and imperial coinage show a trident-bearing form.
- **Ekizce/Phaselis territory:** inscriptions identify a sanctuary of Ares.

These cults may incorporate indigenous Anatolian martial and protective gods. A simple model of either “pure Greek colonization” or unchanged native survival is inadequate.

### Sacrifice and contested human sacrifice

**Animal sacrifice is secure.** Bulls, sheep, goats, dogs, incense, libations, and battlefield vows appear in literary or cultic evidence. The black-shield bull sacrifice in *Seven Against Thebes* is narrative ritual, not documentation of a recurring historical festival.

**Historical human sacrifice to Greek Ares at Sparta is not established.** Porphyry, writing in late antiquity, says that “Apollodorus” reported Spartan human sacrifice to Ares. The report is late, brief, polemically situated, and archaeologically unconfirmed. It may concern mythic prehistory or confusion with another rite. The supposed *hekatomphonia*—sometimes described as sacrificing a human after killing one hundred enemies—was actually an animal offering associated with Zeus in the relevant sources. Claims of routine Spartan human sacrifice to Ares should be labeled unverified.

**Foreign rites translated as Ares are separate evidence.** Herodotus describes Scythian prisoners sacrificed to a sword representing “Ares.” This is testimony about a Scythian cult interpreted through Greek categories, not evidence that Greeks sacrificed prisoners to the Olympian Ares.

### Priesthood, economics, and politics

No single pan-Hellenic priesthood of Ares existed. Local priests, civic magistrates, military units, youth organizations, and festival officers managed particular cults. At Amblada a *panegyriarch* is documented; at Athens dedications name priests; at Metropolis inscriptions reveal civic organization. The cult could ratify military service, victory, imperial loyalty, community defense, and local identity.

## 7. Historical evolution

### c. 1400–1200 BCE: Mycenaean horizon

*A-re* receives an offering and appears in personal-name material. No narrative survives. This defeats claims that Homer invented Ares or that the god must have entered Greece only in the historical period.

### c. 1100–800 BCE: evidentiary gap

The collapse of palace literacy leaves no continuous written record. Continuity is probable but cannot be documented year by year.

### c. 750–600 BCE: epic consolidation

The *Iliad*, *Odyssey*, Hesiodic poetry, and visual art establish Ares as Zeus and Hera’s son, destructive battle power, lover of Aphrodite, father of personified terrors and Harmonia, opponent of Athena and Heracles, and vulnerable member of the Olympian hierarchy.

### Sixth–fifth centuries BCE: civic and tragic diversification

Military oaths, local worship, drama, and vase painting place Ares inside civic religion while exploiting him as an image of plague, civil bloodshed, and uncontrolled force. Herodotus translates foreign gods as Ares, producing the enduring “Thracian” and “Scythian Ares” traditions.

### Fourth–first centuries BCE: geographical expansion and systematization

Local inscriptions become more visible; mythographers organize genealogies; Hellenistic poetry expands Amazon, Argonautic, and Gigantomachic material. Anatolian cults develop distinctive protective and oracular forms. Astral identification with the planet grows.

### First century BCE–third century CE: Graeco-Roman transformation

Ares is increasingly interpreted through Mars and vice versa, but the Roman god remains institutionally distinct. The transferred Athenian temple and Augustus dedication exemplify political repurposing. Pausanias records numerous local cults whose origins are usually impossible to date from his testimony alone. PGM texts integrate martial names and planetary powers into syncretistic magic.

### Third–sixth centuries CE: late-antique elaboration and displacement

Orphic hymns, astrology, Nonnus, and late mythography preserve and reshape Ares. Christianization progressively ended public sacrificial institutions through imperial legislation, temple closure, economic change, and local conversion. There is no evidence for an institutionally continuous Ares priesthood surviving into medieval Greece.

### Byzantine and medieval periods

Ares survives primarily as classical vocabulary, allegory, manuscript mythology, and the Greek name of the planet Mars. Byzantine lexica preserve late stories. Planetary magic usually operates through Mars rather than a historically reconstructed Greek cult.

### Renaissance to nineteenth century

Humanism restored Homeric and mythographic texts to Western art and education. Ares/Mars became an allegory of War, often paired with Venus as Love overcoming or distracting War. The fusion is predominantly Roman-classical reception, not revival of ancient Ares ritual.

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries

Archaeology, Linear B decipherment, epigraphy, and iconographic catalogues dismantled the older image of a purely literary, cultless, foreign god. Contemporary Hellenic polytheists may worship Ares, often using Homeric or Orphic hymns and reconstructed offerings. This is a modern religious revival after a long institutional discontinuity, not demonstrable unbroken transmission. Academic study of contemporary Hellenism emphasizes its negotiation of archaeology, Greek identity, Orthodoxy, and modern religious freedom. [University of Erfurt project](https://www.uni-erfurt.de/en/research/research-profile/research-projects/hellenic-polytheism-in-contemporary-greece-beliefs-practices-and-the-symbolic-struggle-over-hellenicity-greekness)

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Occult and esoteric reception

Renaissance and early-modern magic inherited the planetary **Mars**, with iron, red, Tuesday, the fifth planetary sphere, courage, conflict, surgery, and destructive operations. These systems are chiefly astrological, Hermetic, Arabic-Latin, and Kabbalistic developments. Using the name Ares within them is usually a classicizing substitution, not preservation of an ancient Ares rite.

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn associated Mars with **Geburah**, the fifth Sephirah, and built ritual invocations from Renaissance planetary magic and modern ceremonial correspondences. Israel Regardie’s published compilation includes an “Invocation to Mars and Geburah.” [Regardie text](https://scriptaetveritas.com.br/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/ISRAEL_REGARDIE_COMPLETE_GOLDEN_DAWN_SYSTEM_OF_MAGIC.pdf)

Modern occult manuals that combine Ares, Mars, Thor, Hachiman, Kabbalah, and PGM formulae are explicitly syncretic modern constructions. Their practices may be religiously meaningful to users, but they are not ancient Greek survivals.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist worship

Organized Hellenic revival accelerated in the late twentieth century; Greece’s YSEE was founded in 1997. Ares today may be approached as patron of soldiers, athletes, martial artists, trauma survivors, courage, disciplined anger, or protection. Some of these emphases extrapolate ancient martial functions; “Ares as patron of trauma recovery” is a modern pastoral interpretation.

There is no reliable census of Ares devotees specifically. Online visibility cannot establish scale or continuity. Contemporary prayers combining Orphic Hymn 65, red candles, Tuesday, planetary hours, and gym or military symbolism blend historically distinct Greek and astrological systems.

### Literature, art, comics, film, and games

- Renaissance and Baroque “Mars and Venus” paintings derive largely from the *Odyssey*, Ovid, and Roman allegory.
- Percy Bysshe Shelley’s *Prometheus Unbound* and other Romantic works use classical war gods allegorically rather than cultically.
- DC Comics’ **Ares**, especially as a Wonder Woman antagonist, helped fix the modern image of an armored supervillain who feeds on war.
- The *God of War* game series depicts Ares as the manipulative former patron and enemy of Kratos. This is a modern fictional mythology.
- The 2017 film *Wonder Woman* makes Ares the hidden instigator of human war. Ancient sources instead portray him as a participant and personification of war, not the sole metaphysical cause of all warfare.
- Rick Riordan’s novels recast Ares within modern young-adult fantasy.
- Commercial names—missiles, military systems, software, vehicles, sports teams, and corporations—use “Ares” as shorthand for aggression, strength, or war. They provide evidence of reception, not religious tradition.

### Political and extremist appropriation

Warrior gods and classical martial aesthetics have been used by nationalist and extremist movements, but the best-documented far-right neopagan appropriations concentrate on Germanic/Norse religion rather than Ares specifically. Ares imagery can appear in militarist or masculinist settings, yet no large, historically coherent “Ares extremist cult” is established. General claims about paganism and the far right must not be transferred automatically to every Ares devotee. [Oxford overview](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/62249/chapter-abstract/551452607)

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Ares a foreign or Thracian god?

Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century scholars, including Wilamowitz and Jane Harrison, often treated Ares as a Thracian or barbarian import. Their evidence was chiefly Homeric localization, Ares’s violent character, and Greek ethnography. Linear B *a-re* demonstrated his presence in the Mycenaean world centuries before the surviving Thracian testimonia. It does not prove a uniquely “native Indo-European” origin, but it makes a late Thracian import untenable.

Possible Anatolian comparison with Iyarri remains a contact hypothesis, not consensus proof of derivation.

### Was he merely an abstraction?

The overlap between god and common noun encouraged theories that Ares personified an earlier abstract “battle.” Mycenaean offerings and historical cult undermine any strong claim that poets simply personified a word. Homeric language nevertheless deliberately exploits the inseparability of the god from his sphere.

### Was Ares hated or insignificant?

Farnell, Nilsson, Otto, Vian, and Burkert emphasized his narrow domain, defeats, and limited monumental cult. Farnell described him as disconnected from “higher civilization,” language now recognizable as value-laden. Nilsson thought Homer largely preserved his Olympian standing.

Later epigraphic discoveries and Gonzales’s and Millington’s surveys reveal much wider worship. Millington argues that Ares embodied both war itself and the warrior archetype and that Greek respect for his causal power cannot be reduced to affection or moral approval. The current corrective should not be exaggerated in the opposite direction: widespread dedications do not make him the chief god of Sparta or every army.

### Ares versus Athena

Older evolutionary readings made Athena “civilized war” defeating a primitive Ares. The texts certainly contrast their behavior, but the claim that this records a historical replacement of an old berserker religion by rational warfare is modern reconstruction. Greek armies invoked multiple gods; Athena can be violent, and Ares can sanction civic defense and oaths.

### Ares and Mars

Classical translation and Roman art fused their mythologies, but modern scholarship distinguishes their cultic histories. Mars had the *flamen Martialis*, Salii, March and October festivals, the Campus Martius, the Roman foundation genealogy through Romulus, and agricultural-protective prayers such as Cato’s lustration. Those institutions cannot be back-projected onto Ares. [Art Institute discussion](https://publications.artic.edu/roman/reader/romanart/section/497/497_anchor)

### Human sacrifice

The Spartan claim rests on late literary testimony and lacks corroboration. Scythian sacrifice is better documented but concerns a foreign god whom Herodotus translates as Ares. Confusing these bodies of evidence has produced the popular falsehood that historical Greek worshippers routinely sacrificed humans to Ares.

### State of the evidence

- Linear B tablets are dispersed among excavation collections and published through specialist corpora.
- Literary texts survive through papyri, quotations, and medieval manuscripts, often separated from composition by centuries.
- Inscriptions appear in *Inscriptiones Graecae*, *Supplementum Epigraphicum Graecum*, regional corpora, and excavation reports.
- Sculptures and painted pottery are dispersed internationally; *LIMC* is the standard iconographic catalogue.
- Numerous cult images and temples are lost; Pausanias is sometimes the only witness.
- Theoi.com is a useful source index but is neither a critical edition nor a substitute for checking passages.
- Graves’s synthetic *Greek Myths* is modern literary mythography and frequently presents conjectural ritual explanations as if established.
- Kerényi is influential reception and phenomenological interpretation, not a primary source.
- Gantz’s *Early Greek Myth* is especially valuable because it organizes variants by early source rather than harmonizing them.
