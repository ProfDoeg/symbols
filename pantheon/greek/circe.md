# Circe: Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess in the earliest Greek sources—Homer calls her a *δεινὴ θεὸς αὐδήεσσα*, “formidable goddess endowed with speech” (*Odyssey* 10.136); later Greek and Roman authors increasingly classify her as an enchantress, poisoner, or witch. She is not originally a mortal woman subsequently deified.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The orally composed Greek *Odyssey*, especially Books 10–12, conventionally dated to the late eighth or early seventh century BCE. It supplies Circe’s name, divine status, parentage, island, household, drugs, wand, animal transformations, encounter with Odysseus, and prophetic guidance. Hesiod’s *Theogony*, approximately 700 BCE, independently places her in the family of Helios. These are literary attestations whose earliest surviving physical copies are much later papyrus fragments and medieval manuscripts, not eighth-century autographs.

**Evidence labels used below:** **[Early]** securely attested archaic evidence; **[Classical]** fifth–fourth-century BCE evidence; **[Hellenistic]** third–first-century BCE elaboration; **[Roman]** Roman-period text, object, or cult; **[Regional variant]** geographically localized tradition; **[Learned systematization]** mythographic, allegorical, or rationalizing arrangement; **[Late antique/medieval]** later transmission or reinterpretation; **[Early modern]** Renaissance–eighteenth-century reception; **[Modern reconstruction]** scholarly inference rather than an explicit ancient statement; **[Modern invention/reception]** demonstrably modern adaptation or religious claim.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identity

**[Early]** Circe is Κίρκη (*Kírkē*), an immortal daughter of the sun-god Helios and the Oceanid Perse/Perseis, sister of Aeëtes, inhabiting the wooded island Aeaea. Homer’s Circe combines dangerous and beneficent functions: she drugs and transforms strangers but subsequently restores them, hospitably maintains Odysseus’s company for a year, directs the consultation of Tiresias, explains the dangers ahead, and equips the travelers with accurate navigational and ritual knowledge. “Witch” captures her *pharmaka* and incantatory power but not her explicit Homeric classification as a goddess.

### Names and forms

- **Κίρκη — Kírkē. [Early]** Standard Ionic and Attic Greek form, metrically secure in Homer and Hesiod.
- **Circe. [Roman]** Latin transliteration, with the conventional English pronunciation inherited through Latin and French.
- **Kirke. [Modern scholarly convention]** Direct transliteration of Greek, useful when distinguishing the Greek figure from her Latin reception.
- **Aeaea/Aiaia, Αἰαίη. [Early]** Circe’s island. Ancient authors later relocated it to the Tyrrhenian west, especially Monte Circeo; Homer supplies no cartographically secure location.
- **Aeaea/Aeaean goddess, Aeaea puella, Aeaea regina. [Roman literary]** Geographic poetic titles rather than known cult epithets.
- **Circe Titanis/Titania. [Roman literary]** Ovid emphasizes descent from the solar Titan line.
- **Sol’s/Helios’s daughter. [Early–Roman]** Her most persistent identifying patronymic.
- **πολυφάρμακος (*polyphármakos*), “knowing/possessing many drugs.” [Early formulaic attribute]** A Homeric descriptor closely associated with Circe. It means mastery of many *pharmaka*, substances that may heal, poison, stupefy, or transform; translating it simply as “witch” loses the pharmacological ambiguity.
- **δεινὴ θεὸς αὐδήεσσα, “formidable goddess with human/articulate speech.” [Early]** *Odyssey* 10.136. The last adjective distinguishes articulate anthropomorphic divinities from beasts; it does not mean that Circe alone among gods can speak.
- **εὐπλόκαμος, “fair-haired/fair-tressed.” [Early]** Homeric physical epithet.
- **dea saeva, “savage goddess.” [Roman]** Virgil, *Aeneid* 7.19, a decisive negative reframing: the Trojans hear her working and the transformed animals raging but Neptune carries them safely past.
- **Circes sanctissima. [Roman cultic, inscription reported as CIL X 6422]** “Most holy Circe,” associated with an imperial-period restoration at Circeii. The reading and its precise architectural referent require consultation of the CIL record; it should not be converted into evidence for a large, pan-Mediterranean priesthood.

### Etymology

**[Uncertain]** No etymology commands agreement.

1. A traditional comparison connects Κίρκη with κίρκος, a hawk or falcon. The association is linguistically possible and encouraged older interpretations of Circe as a bird or Near Eastern “Mistress of Animals,” but Homer never depicts her as a hawk.
2. Connections with Greek κίρκος, “ring/circle,” weaving, or a shuttle are largely associative and do not establish derivation.
3. Comparative theories deriving Circe from an Anatolian, Mesopotamian, or prehistoric bird-goddess remain **modern reconstructions**. The recurring western/eastern geography, solar genealogy, animals, and hawk comparison are suggestive, not proof of direct descent. A review of Nanno Marinatos’s “Mistress of Animals” argument notes precisely this proposed eastern origin, while not turning it into a settled identification.
4. English *Circean* came to mean alluring but morally degrading; *Circe* itself could function as a common noun for a dangerously seductive woman. These are post-antique semantic developments, not ancient etymology.

### Classification through time

- **[Early]** Immortal goddess with a domestic palace, nymph attendants, divine knowledge, and transformative *pharmaka*.
- **[Classical–Hellenistic]** Goddess and expert purifier or ritual specialist; increasingly a paradigmatic enchantress.
- **[Roman]** Both *dea* and *maga/venefica*: divine descent coexists with literary construction as a dangerous erotic witch.
- **[Christian allegorical reception]** Emblem of lust, sensuality, heresy, bodily appetite, or the world’s deceptive pleasures.
- **[Modern reception]** Witch-goddess, feminist heroine, survivor, herbalist, or patron of transformation. These classifications often draw upon antiquity but are not themselves ancient cult titles.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence and its limits

**[Early]** *Odyssey* 10.135–12.143 is the foundational dossier. At 10.135–139 Circe is Helios’s and Perse’s daughter and Aeëtes’s sister. Her house stands in a forest clearing among docile wolves and lions. She sings while weaving. She mixes Pramnian wine, cheese, meal, honey, and harmful drugs; twenty-two of Odysseus’s men lose human shape and become swine while retaining human consciousness. Hermes gives Odysseus *moly*, tells him to threaten Circe with a sword, and requires her great oath before sexual union. Circe restores the crew “younger, taller, and more handsome,” entertains them for a year, sends Odysseus to Tiresias, and afterward explains the Sirens, Scylla, Charybdis, and the cattle of Helios. The Greek and an older English translation are available through [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0136%3Abook%3D10).

What Homer does **not** say:

- He does not call Circe a mortal witch, priestess of Hecate, or ruler of a historical island.
- He does not identify Aeaea with Monte Circeo.
- He does not say that every visitor becomes a pig: lions and wolves surround the house, and Eurylochus fears transformation into several possible animals, but the narrated crew become swine.
- He does not identify *moly* botanically.
- He names no child of Circe and Odysseus and describes no pregnancy.
- He does not narrate Circe creating Scylla.
- Odysseus’s intercourse with Circe follows an armed confrontation, divine instructions, and an oath against further harm. Calling the episode either uncomplicated romance or straightforward rape imposes categories the poem does not formulate, though coercion, fear, leverage, and divine–mortal inequality are integral to the scene.

**[Early]** Hesiod, *Theogony* 956–957, gives Helios and Perseis as parents of Circe and Aeëtes. Lines 1011–1016 assign Circe and Odysseus the sons Agrius, Latinus, and—in the transmitted text—Telegonus, ruling distant Tyrrhenians. The authenticity and composition history of the concluding heroic genealogies are disputed; their Italian orientation may represent a secondary layer. The text and edition history can be consulted through the [Perseus/Scaife record](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/library/urn%3Acts%3AgreekLit%3Atlg0020.tlg001/).

### Corpus

- **Homer, *Odyssey* 10–12; Greek; late eighth/early seventh century BCE. [Early]** Complete encounter, transformation, *moly*, year-long residence, necromantic instructions, and route prophecy.
- **Hesiod, *Theogony* 956–957, 1011–1016; Greek; ca. 700 BCE, with possible later material at the poem’s end. [Early/disputed layer]** Genealogy and Italian sons.
- **Hesiodic *Catalogue of Women*, fr. 46; archaic poem surviving through a scholium on Apollonius. [Early fragment/indirect transmission]** Circe is conveyed west in Helios’s chariot; the attribution depends on later quotation.
- **“Homeric Epigram” 14; date and attribution uncertain. [Archaizing/learned transmission]** Invokes “daughter of Helios, polypharmakos Circe” to harm craftsmen. It is evidence for Circe as a name of magical aggression, not securely the work of Homer.
- **The *Nostoi*, fragment 4; probably seventh or sixth century BCE, surviving through Eustathius. [Archaic fragment/indirect]** Telemachus marries Circe while Telegonus marries Penelope.
- **The *Telegony*, attributed to Eugammon of Cyrene; probably sixth century BCE; lost except for testimonia and tiny fragments. [Archaic cyclic elaboration]** Telegonus accidentally kills Odysseus, takes the corpse, Penelope, and Telemachus to Aeaea; Circe makes the survivors immortal; paired marriages follow. Proclus’s much later summary is translated at [Livius](https://www.livius.org/sources/content/epic-cycle/telegony/). Modern reconstruction remains difficult because Proclus and Pseudo-Apollodorus are not interchangeable witnesses, as emphasized by the [Cambridge study of the poem](https://www.cambridge.org/core/books/abs/greek-epic-cycle-and-its-ancient-reception/telegony/0E8926C0EC3A7D21F1425E985B7A05CE).
- **Sophocles, *Odysseus Acanthoplex* or *Odysseus Wounded by the Spine*; fifth century BCE; fragmentary tragedy. [Classical variant]** Dramatized Odysseus’s fatal encounter with Telegonus. Circe’s exact surviving role is slight because the play is lost; reconstructed plots must not be treated as a complete script.
- **Apollonius Rhodius, *Argonautica* 3.309–313; 4.559–752; Greek; third century BCE. [Hellenistic canonical elaboration]** Helios transported Circe west; she ritually purifies Jason and Medea of Apsyrtus’s murder, recognizes Medea as kin, but refuses hospitality after discerning the crime.
- **Lycophron, *Alexandra* 795–798 and scholia; Greek; probably third century BCE, scholia later. [Hellenistic/learned variant]** Allusive Telegonus material and later elaborations involving resurrection, Cassiphone, and Telemachus. Claims derived only from Byzantine scholia are not securely Lycophron’s own.
- **Parthenius, *Erotica Pathemata* 12; Greek; first century BCE. [Hellenistic regional romance]** Daunian Calchus pursues Circe; she maddens and confines him like a pig, then releases him under oath.
- **Diodorus Siculus, *Library* 4.45; Greek; first century BCE. [Learned rationalization]** Reconfigures the family: Hecate, daughter of Perses, is Aeëtes’s wife and mother of Circe and Medea; Circe becomes a historical-seeming poisoner and queen. This is not Homer’s genealogy.
- **Virgil, *Eclogue* 8.70; *Aeneid* 3.386, 7.10–24; Latin; first century BCE. [Roman]** Circean herbs become proverbial; Aeaea is localized near Italy; Circe is a savage goddess whose transformed victims fill the night with animal cries.
- **Propertius 3.12; Horace, *Epodes* 17 and *Odes* 1.17; Latin; first century BCE. [Roman literary reception]** Circe becomes a comparison for erotic enchantment and dangerous female magic.
- **Strabo, *Geography* 5.3.6 and 9.1.13; Greek; late first century BCE–early first century CE. [Roman geographic/cult testimony]** Reports a temple at Circeii, a displayed cup of Odysseus, medicinal roots, and a “tomb of Circe” on the Pharmacussae.
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 13.898–14.440; Latin; ca. 8 CE. [Roman canonical elaboration]** Creates the most influential full narratives of Circe’s jealous transformation of Scylla and her transformation of Picus and his companions. The [University of Virginia text](https://ovid.lib.virginia.edu/trans/Metamorph14.htm) preserves the sequence.
- **Ovid, *Fasti* 4.69–70; Latin; early first century CE. [Roman]** Treats the Circean shore as Italian evidence of Odysseus’s travels.
- **Pseudo-Apollodorus, *Bibliotheca* 1.9.24 and *Epitome* 7.14–37; Greek; first or second century CE. [Learned systematization]** Summarizes Argonautic purification, Homeric wanderings, Circe’s sons, and Telegonus. The relevant *Bibliotheca* passage is available at [Perseus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0022%3Atext%3DLibrary%3Abook%3D1%3Achapter%3D9).
- **Pseudo-Hyginus, *Fabulae* 125, 127, 199; Latin; surviving compilation probably second century CE or later. [Roman mythographic systematization]** Gives Nausithous and Telegonus as sons, adds Athena’s direction of the postmortem marriages, and attributes Scylla’s transformation to Circe.
- **Pausanias 5.19.7; Greek; second century CE. [Roman antiquarian testimony]** Identifies Odysseus and Circe among scenes on the archaic Chest of Cypselus at Olympia, an identification based on four female attendants rather than an inscription.
- **Oppian, *Halieutica* 2.497–506; Greek; second/third century CE. [Later poetic elaboration]** Circe gives Telegonus the stingray-spine spear that kills Odysseus.
- **Orphic *Argonautica* 1217–1275; Greek; probably fourth century CE. [Late antique retelling]** Reworks the Argonautic visit. The extant *Orphic Hymns* contain no hymn to Circe; claiming an ancient Orphic Circe cult from them is unsupported.
- **Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 13.327–328, 37.10–70; Greek; fifth century CE. [Late antique]** Makes Faunus/Phaunos a son of Circe and Poseidon.
- **Fulgentius, *Mythologies* 2.9; Latin; fifth/sixth century CE. [Christian allegory]** Moralizes Scylla and Circe in terms of lust and prostitution. The text is available in the [Theoi classical-text library](https://www.theoi.com/Text/FulgentiusMythologies2.html).

### Negative corpus findings

**[Absence of evidence]** Circe has no surviving individual Homeric Hymn or Orphic Hymn. She is not a principal figure in extant Pindar, Aeschylus, Sophocles, or Euripides; Sophoclean evidence is fragmentary. No secure Circe invocation appears in the standard indexed divine names of the Greek Magical Papyri. The PGM demonstrate a later magical environment rich in Helios, Hecate, and syncretistic names, but they cannot automatically be counted as Circe’s cult corpus; compare the searchable [PGM concordance](https://cornelius-abrasax.github.io/pgm-concordance/).

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and powers

- **Pharmaka. [Early]** Circe mixes drugs into food and drink, applies a different substance to reverse transformation, and possesses knowledge exceeding mortal reach.
- **Transformation. [Early]** Her wand and drugs alter bodies while leaving human consciousness intact. Later authors greatly enlarge her repertoire.
- **Binding and release. [Early]** She controls entry to and departure from animal form; this reversible power distinguishes her from simple destruction.
- **Song and weaving. [Early]** Odysseus’s men hear her before seeing her. These are marks of elite divine femininity shared with Calypso, not proof of a separate weaving cult.
- **Sexual power. [Early, reception greatly amplified]** Sexual union with Odysseus belongs to the Homeric episode. Her later construction as the universal embodiment of lust is allegorical reception.
- **Prophecy and geographic knowledge. [Early]** She knows Odysseus’s identity from Hermes’s prior prophecy, prescribes necromantic procedure, and accurately describes future hazards.
- **Purification from blood guilt. [Hellenistic]** Apollonius gives her the ritual competence to purify Jason and Medea.
- **Necromancy. [Early, limited]** Circe instructs Odysseus how to reach and consult the dead; she does not herself summon Tiresias in Homer.
- **Immortalization. [Archaic cyclic tradition known through later epitome]** In the *Telegony*, she makes Penelope and Telemachus immortal.
- **Weather and celestial magic. [Roman]** Ovid says her incantations darken the sky and veil Helios. These are not Homeric attributes.
- **Inventress of magic or botany. [Hellenistic/Roman learned systematization]** Diodorus and Pliny convert mythic pharmaceutical skill into a history of discoveries. This is ancient rationalization, not biographical fact.

### Form, symbols, and limitations

**[Early]** Circe is anthropomorphic, beautiful-haired, and human-speaking. Homer gives no fixed eye color, complexion, crown, staff type, sacred color, number, day, or zodiacal body.

Recognizing her in ancient art usually depends upon a narrative cluster:

- cup, mixing vessel, or bowl;
- wand or staff;
- Odysseus drawing or carrying a sword;
- men in partial animal transformation;
- loom or weaving apparatus;
- later, lions, wolves, pigs, herbs, or a cauldron.

The cup, wand, loom, and animals are identifying attributes in narrative art; none is securely documented as a universal cult emblem.

Her powers have limits:

- Hermes’s *moly* defeats her drug.
- Odysseus’s sword and insistence on an oath constrain her.
- In Ovid she cannot or will not alter Glaucus, so she attacks Scylla.
- Her knowledge is extensive but not omnipotence: Homer sends Odysseus onward to Tiresias for information unavailable from her alone.

### Moly

**[Early]** Hermes pulls from the ground a plant with black root and milk-white flower that gods call μῶλυ. It is difficult for mortals to uproot. Ancient and modern identifications—garlic, snowdrop, mandrake, rue, pharmacological antidote—remain speculative. The narrative stresses divine accessibility, not botanical reproducibility. Later Ptolemaic mythography invents an etiology from the blood of a giant killed with Helios’s aid; that is a **late learned elaboration**, not Homeric background.

### Iconographic record

- **Ca. 550–500 BCE, perhaps ca. 530: Clazomenian black-figure amphora fragment from Tell Defenneh, Egypt, British Museum 1888,0208.106.e. [Archaic material evidence]** Circe holds a vessel beside Odysseus and a companion partially transformed into a boar. This is among the earliest secure surviving images; the [museum record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/G_1888-0208-106-e) supplies fabric, findspot, and dating.
- **Late sixth/fifth century BCE: black-figure and red-figure vessels. [Archaic/Classical]** Painters favor the moment of transformation or Odysseus’s armed confrontation. Hybrid men—not merely complete pigs—make transformation visually legible.
- **Ca. 450–420 BCE: Boeotian black-figure skyphos, British Museum 1893,0303.1. [Classical]** Circe’s loom appears with Odysseus and an animal-transformed sailor; see the [British Museum catalogue](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/G_1893-0303-1).
- **Fifth century BCE: Attic calyx-krater attributed to the Persephone Painter. [Classical]** Odysseus pursues Circe above another scene; [Metropolitan Museum object 253627](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/253627).
- **Chest of Cypselus, traditionally early sixth century BCE, lost; described by Pausanias in the second century CE. [Archaic object/later identification]** A sleeping couple in a grotto with four attendants was identified by Pausanias as Circe and Odysseus. Because it lacked a reported label, the identification is plausible rather than certain.
- **Roman wall painting, sarcophagi, gems, and manuscript illustration. [Roman]** Emphasis shifts toward glamorous enchantress, wand, cup, and beasts.
- **Renaissance cassoni and emblem books. [Early modern]** Circe becomes an erotic and moral antagonist: rational male constancy confronts animal appetite. A study of cassone imagery traces the role of Latin rationalizations and Boccaccio in this change: [“Transforming Circe”](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-0752/12/3/105).
- **Nineteenth-century art. [Modern reception]** John Collier’s *Circe* (1885), Wright Barker’s *Circe* (1889), and John William Waterhouse’s *Circe Offering the Cup to Ulysses* (1891) and *Circe Invidiosa* (1892) helped establish the jeweled, sexually commanding femme fatale.
- **John Flaxman, engraved 1805. [Modern reception]** Neoclassical outline art returned to an austere vase-like treatment; the [Metropolitan Museum record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/705224) documents the print.

**Negative findings:** No securely ancient “official portrait type” comparable to Athena Parthenos is established; no canonical colors, moon phase, crystal, tarot card, or modern “sigil of Circe” is documented in ancient evidence.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical variants

| Tier | Source and date | Parentage or offspring |
|---|---|---|
| **[Early]** | *Odyssey* 10.135–139; late eighth/early seventh century BCE | Helios + Perse, daughter of Oceanus; Aeëtes is her brother. |
| **[Early]** | Hesiod, *Theogony* 956–957 | Helios + Perseis; siblings Aeëtes and, in the larger genealogy, Pasiphae. |
| **[Hellenistic]** | Apollonius, *Argonautica* | Helios + Perse; Aeëtes is brother, Medea niece. |
| **[Learned rationalization]** | Diodorus 4.45; first century BCE | Hecate + Aeëtes produce Circe, Medea, and Aegialeus. This makes the standard brother her father and the standard niece her sister. |
| **[Archaic/disputed textual layer]** | *Theogony* 1011–1016 | By Odysseus: Agrius, Latinus, and Telegonus in the transmitted form. |
| **[Archaic cyclic]** | *Telegony* and *Nostoi* fragments | By Odysseus: Telegonus; Circe eventually marries Telemachus. |
| **[Roman mythography]** | Hyginus 125 | By Odysseus: Nausithous and Telegonus. |
| **[Roman regional genealogy]** | Xenagoras quoted by Dionysius of Halicarnassus 1.72.5 | By Odysseus: Rhomos, Anteias, and Ardeias, eponymous founders of Rome, Antium, and Ardea; see [Dionysius’s text](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Dionysius_of_Halicarnassus/1D%2A.html). |
| **[Roman mythography]** | Hyginus 127 | Circe and Telemachus produce Latinus. |
| **[Late antique]** | Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* | Circe and Poseidon produce Phaunos/Faunus. |
| **[Late scholastic variant]** | Lycophron scholia/Tzetzes | Cassiphone is Circe’s daughter; her father varies or is implicit. |

There is no single canonical family tree. The Italian sons function in Greek and Roman literature as mechanisms for grafting heroic Greece onto Italian peoples and cities.

### Register of every securely documentable named divine interaction

“Interaction” here means direct action, encounter, kinship act, or shared episode—not mere membership in a pantheon.

- **Helios — father and transporter. [Early/Hellenistic]** Homer and Hesiod identify him as Circe’s father; Apollonius 3.309–313 says he carried Circe west in his chariot.
- **Perse/Perseis — mother. [Early]** *Odyssey* 10.139 and *Theogony* 956–957; no narrative encounter survives.
- **Oceanus — maternal grandfather. [Early genealogy]** *Odyssey* 10.139; no direct interaction survives.
- **Aeëtes — brother and fellow traveler in one tradition. [Early/Hellenistic]** Homer names him her brother; Apollonius has him recall accompanying Helios when Circe was taken west.
- **Pasiphae — sister. [Hesiodic/learned genealogy]** Inferred from shared parents in the Hesiodic solar genealogy and made explicit by later mythographers; no direct scene between them survives.
- **Hermes — defeats and mediates her magic. [Early]** *Odyssey* 10.275–332: Hermes gives Odysseus *moly*, predicts Circe’s actions, and dictates the sword threat and binding oath. Circe and Hermes do not converse onstage.
- **Aphrodite — causes or sanctions conception. [Early/disputed layer]** *Theogony* 1014 says Circe bore Telegonus “through golden Aphrodite”; later Ovid speculates that Venus may have made Circe unusually susceptible to love because Helios exposed the goddess’s adultery.
- **Scylla — transformed victim. [Roman canonical elaboration]** Hyginus 199 and Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14: Circe poisons Scylla’s bathing place, turning her lower body monstrous. Homer knows Scylla but never attributes her form to Circe.
- **Glaucus — rejected divine suitor. [Roman canonical elaboration]** Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 13.898–14.74: Glaucus asks Circe to win Scylla for him; Circe proposes herself; he refuses; she retaliates against Scylla.
- **Hecate — mother and teacher in a rationalized genealogy; name of Circe’s spells in Ovid. [Hellenistic/Roman learned systematization]** Diodorus makes Hecate Circe’s mother and pharmacological instructor; Ovid calls Circe’s incantations *carmina Hecateia*. No early source shows Circe worshipping Hecate.
- **Athena/Minerva — directs the Telegonus settlement. [Roman mythographic variant]** Hyginus 127: Athena tells Telegonus, Telemachus, and Penelope to return to Aeaea and advises the paired marriages. This detail is absent from Proclus’s summary.
- **Poseidon — consort and father of Phaunos. [Late antique]** Nonnus, *Dionysiaca* 13 and 37.
- **Phaunos/Faunus — son trained in her woodland knowledge. [Late antique]** Nonnus says he learned the wild places from Circe before joining Dionysus’s Indian war.
- **Zeus — recognizes blood guilt requiring purification. [Hellenistic]** In Apollonius and Pseudo-Apollodorus, Zeus’s anger after Apsyrtus’s murder will not cease until Circe purifies Medea and Jason. Zeus and Circe do not meet.
- **Argo — divine speaking ship directs the Argonauts to her. [Hellenistic]** Apollonius 4 and Pseudo-Apollodorus 1.9.24.
- **Helios and an unnamed Giant/Picolous — alleged joint combat associated with *moly*. [Roman-era learned myth]** Ptolemy Hephaestion’s *New History*, summarized by Photius, says Helios aided Circe against a giant whose blood produced *moly*. This is a very late etiology and its details are unstable.

**Absences:** There is no securely attested ancient meeting between Circe and Calypso, Artemis, Persephone, Dionysus, or Medea’s patron Hecate in Homer. Similar functions or shared genealogy do not constitute an interaction.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The men transformed into swine

**[Early]** After the Laestrygonian disaster, Odysseus reaches Aeaea. Half the surviving company, led by Eurylochus, finds Circe’s stone house. Enchanted wolves and lions behave like tame dogs. Circe invites the men inside, mixes food, Pramnian wine, and drugs, then strikes them with her wand. Their bodies become those of swine, but their minds remain human. She confines and feeds them acorns.

Eurylochus warns Odysseus. Hermes meets him, provides *moly*, and scripts the confrontation. When Circe’s drug fails, Odysseus draws his sword. Circe recognizes the prophesied hero and invites him to bed; following Hermes’s warning, he first compels her to swear the gods’ great oath that she will not emasculate or otherwise harm him. Odysseus then refuses food until she restores his companions. She anoints them with a counter-drug; they emerge rejuvenated.

**Development:** Vase painters made hybrid bodies—human with boar head, tail, or limbs—more common than Homer’s complete bodily change because hybrids show the process. Roman and later art makes the cup and wand permanent emblems. Allegorists convert the pigs into visible animal appetites latent within men. The common claim that Circe exposed the men’s “true forms” is an interpretive allegory, not Homer’s explicit statement.

### The year on Aeaea and journey to the dead

**[Early]** Circe’s role reverses after the confrontation. She bathes and clothes Odysseus’s men, supplies food, and urges them to recover. They remain for a year until the crew asks Odysseus to remember home.

Circe says he must first consult the dead Tiresias. She specifies the route to Ocean, the libation trench, offerings, sacrificial victims, and the order in which shades must be admitted. Elpenor dies accidentally on Aeaea’s roof immediately before departure. After the *Nekyia*, the ship returns; Circe helps bury Elpenor and privately briefs Odysseus on the Sirens, Scylla and Charybdis, and Helios’s cattle. She counsels choosing Scylla’s limited destruction over Charybdis’s total loss.

**Development:** Later hostile receptions regularly omit this beneficent half of the episode. The result is a selective “evil seductress” assembled from the transformation scene but stripped of her indispensable aid.

### Circe, Medea, and the murder of Apsyrtus

**[Hellenistic]** In Apollonius, Jason and Medea murder or dismember Apsyrtus during their escape. Zeus sends storms, and the speaking Argo announces that only Circe can purify them. Circe dreams of blood and fire, washes in the sea, and receives the supplicants without initially knowing them. She performs purification with a newborn pig, water, and offerings to Zeus the Purifier and the Erinyes. Once she recognizes Medea’s language and lineage and learns the crime, she rebukes her and expels the couple.

The episode establishes Circe as a grave ritual authority rather than merely an enchantress. Her grotesque mixed creatures in Apollonius also expand the animal entourage beyond Homer’s tame lions and wolves.

### Telegonus and the death of Odysseus

**[Archaic cyclic elaboration, surviving indirectly]** Circe bears Telegonus and tells him his father is Odysseus. He sails in search of him, lands unknowingly on Ithaca, and begins taking cattle or supplies. Odysseus resists and is accidentally killed by Telegonus’s spear tipped with a stingray spine. On recognizing his father, Telegonus carries the corpse, Penelope, and Telemachus to Aeaea. Circe makes Telemachus and Penelope immortal; Telegonus marries Penelope and Telemachus marries Circe.

Variants include:

- Sophocles’s lost tragedy emphasized recognition after kin-killing.
- Pseudo-Apollodorus sends Penelope and Telegonus to the Isles of the Blessed.
- Hyginus introduces Athena’s instructions and makes Latinus the child of Circe and Telemachus.
- Oppian says Circe herself provided the fatal stingray weapon.
- Later scholia add Cassiphone, resurrection, further marriage, and reciprocal killings.

The complete original *Telegony* does not survive. Modern summaries that confidently narrate motives, dialogue, or Circe resurrecting Odysseus usually conflate Proclus, Apollodorus, scholia, and later invention.

### Scylla’s transformation

**[Roman elaboration]** In Ovid, the newly divine Glaucus asks Circe for a charm to make Scylla reciprocate his desire. Circe instead offers herself. Glaucus refuses; Circe will not harm him and poisons Scylla’s bathing pool. Scylla’s lower body becomes a ring of barking canine monsters. Hyginus gives the same basic jealousy plot more compactly.

**Source distinction:** Homer’s Scylla is already monstrous, born of Crataeis, and Circe merely explains how to pass her. Circe as Scylla’s maker is therefore not the “original myth” but a highly influential Roman-period causation story. Ovid’s episode is available across [Books 13–14](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidMetamorphoses13.html).

### Picus and Canens

**[Roman elaboration]** Circe sees Picus, king of Ausonia and husband of Canens, while gathering herbs. She creates a phantom boar to separate him from his companions and asks him to accept her. He refuses out of fidelity. Circe transforms him into a woodpecker and his protesting followers into assorted beasts. Canens wastes away in grief.

The story explains Picus’s bird form and embeds Circe in Latin royal mythology. It is Ovidian, not Homeric, and becomes important to later portrayals of Circe as sexually vengeful.

### Calchus

**[Hellenistic regional variant]** Parthenius’s Daunian king Calchus persistently pursues Circe although she loves Odysseus. She drugs him into madness and puts him in the pigsty. A Daunian army obtains his release after he swears never to return. The episode mirrors the Homeric swine transformation but turns Circe’s resistance to an unwanted suitor into the central motive.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

Circe’s ancient cult was real but sparse and heavily concentrated in Italy. It should not be reconstructed as a standard Greek civic cult from narrative prominence alone.

### Circeii/Monte Circeo

**[Roman literary and archaeological evidence]** Strabo 5.3.6 describes Circaeum as an island-like mountain surrounded by sea and marsh, rich in medicinal roots, with a small city, a temple of Circe, an altar of Athena, and a bowl presented as Odysseus’s. Cicero, *De natura deorum* 3.48, says Circe was devoutly worshipped at the Roman colony of Circeii; he deploys the fact philosophically to expose inconsistent criteria for divinity.

The promontory’s summit contains a monumental platform traditionally called the Ara di Circe. The official archaeological description places it at 540 meters and cautiously reconstructs an open sacred area with a temenos or portico, not a securely demonstrated roofed temple. Construction may have occurred in at least two phases, beginning in the late Republic, with possible restoration under Caracalla: [Parco Nazionale del Circeo, Ara di Circe](https://www.parcocirceo.it/Epun_dettaglio.php?id_pun=2033).

**Critical qualification:** Literary tradition associates Circe with the promontory, but architectural remains by themselves do not name the deity. Venus is another proposed recipient for parts of the sanctuary complex. The alleged Caracallan restoration and *Circes sanctissimae* inscription support continuing Circean identification but do not settle every building’s dedication.

Circeii was a politically contested Latin and Roman settlement. Dionysius of Halicarnassus attributes a royal colony there to Tarquinius Superbus and explains that tradition located the Sun’s daughter on the promontory; this is Roman antiquarian history, not contemporary documentation of the settlement’s foundation.

### Pharmacussae

**[Roman geographic testimony/regional cult]** Strabo 9.1.13 reports two small islands off Attica, on the larger of which visitors could see “the tomb of Circe.” Because Circe is otherwise immortal, scholars commonly understand “tomb” as a heroized shrine, local relic, or place-making claim. No accompanying priesthood or ritual calendar is preserved.

### Ritual personnel and offerings

No surviving evidence securely establishes:

- a distinct Greek priestly college of Circe;
- initiation mysteries;
- an annual named festival;
- standardized animal sacrifices;
- temple prostitution;
- human sacrifice;
- an oracle;
- a body of dedicated hymns;
- a widespread stock of Circean theophoric personal names.

The absence matters. Narrative pigs are transformed victims, not evidence of pig sacrifice to Circe. Homer’s description of her attendants and palace is mythic household imagery, not an account of historical temple staff.

### Magic and ordinary devotion

**[Roman literary]** Circe became an authoritative mythical predecessor for herbalism, erotic magic, and transformation. References in Virgil, Horace, Ovid, and Pliny show cultural familiarity, but literary citation is not automatically cult practice.

**[Negative finding]** The Greek Magical Papyri contain many transformations, solar invocations, Hecatean forms, and divine identifications, but Circe is not a prominent invoked deity in the standard corpus. Modern “PGM rites of Circe” generally combine ancient magical techniques with a modern choice of patron.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Late eighth–seventh centuries BCE: emergence in epic

**[Early]** Circe enters the record as a fully formed divine figure in the *Odyssey*: solar daughter, forest-house goddess, transformer, lover, host, and guide. Hesiod integrates her into a cosmic genealogy and, in the transmitted ending, into western political genealogy. The evidence does not reveal whether she arose from cult, folktale, poetic invention, or an eastern Mediterranean divine type.

### Sixth–fifth centuries BCE: cyclic genealogy and visual canon

**[Archaic/Classical]** The *Telegony* extends her relation with Odysseus into dynastic tragedy and immortality. Vase painting spreads the cup–wand–hybrid iconography across Greek markets, including an object excavated in Egypt. Sophocles adapts Telegonus’s patricide for tragedy.

There is still no evidence that Circe possessed a major civic cult in mainland Greece comparable to Athena, Hera, or Demeter.

### Third–first centuries BCE: western localization and ritual authority

**[Hellenistic]** Apollonius maps Aeaea into a western Mediterranean itinerary and gives Circe purification expertise. Parthenius and Lycophron proliferate erotic and genealogical variants. Italian communities and Greek writers increasingly attach Odyssean episodes to real Tyrrhenian geography.

The localization likely served several functions: explaining place-names, integrating Italy into prestigious Greek heroic geography, and supporting local identity. Whether Circe’s name created the promontory’s name or the place-name attracted the myth remains uncertain.

### First century BCE–third century CE: Roman peak

**[Roman]** This is the best-documented period for both local worship and literary expansion:

- Cicero attests worship at the colony.
- Strabo reports a temple, relic, roots, and a second shrine.
- Virgil makes Circe part of proto-Roman geography but avoids her dangerous shore.
- Ovid makes her the agent in the Scylla and Picus transformations.
- Roman genealogists derive Latinus and city founders from her.
- The summit sanctuary at Circeo appears to have monumental late-Republican and imperial phases.

Calling this the “peak” refers to density and variety of surviving evidence, not a known count of worshippers.

### Late antiquity: encyclopedic witch and allegorical figure

**[Late antique]** Nonnus supplies Poseidon as a partner and Faunus as a son. Mythographers systematize contradictory genealogies. Christian and philosophical allegorists interpret transformed men as souls degraded by pleasure. Fulgentius explicitly moralizes the Scylla episode through lust and prostitution.

### Medieval transmission

**[Medieval]** Circe survives through Latin Ovid, Virgil, mythographic handbooks, scholia, and moralized Ovids. She becomes an exemplar of *luxuria*, deceptive beauty, sorcery, and the reduction of rational men to beasts. Boccaccio’s *De mulieribus claris* treats her through rationalizing claims that she was a celebrated courtesan whose lovers became figurative beasts.

This is literary survival, not demonstrable continuous cult.

### Renaissance–Baroque transformation

**[Early modern]** Classical texts, printed mythographies, emblem books, opera, theater, and domestic painting make Circe a major European symbol. She can represent:

- sensual appetite overcoming reason;
- dangerous eloquence;
- female magical knowledge;
- heresy or Babylonian corruption;
- theatrical illusion;
- the sovereign artist who remakes bodies.

Alciato’s emblem tradition and successors explicitly turn her into a warning against prostitutes or sensual enslavement. Spanish Golden Age writers deploy her to police female sexuality and debate natural magic; see the study of Circe in [sixteenth-century Spanish literature](https://www.uhu.es/publicaciones/ojs/index.php/etiopicas/article/view/7844).

### Nineteenth–twentieth centuries

**[Modern reception]** Symbolists, Pre-Raphaelites, and decadent artists detach Circe from the crowded Homeric episode and foreground the solitary femme fatale. Literary counter-traditions begin granting her a speaking voice: Augusta Webster’s “Circe” (1870), for example, makes female desire itself the subject.

James Joyce’s “Circe” episode in *Ulysses* (1922) is a hallucinatory dramatic sequence of bodily, sexual, and social transformations rather than a literal retelling. Jean Giraudoux’s *La guerre de Troie n’aura pas lieu*, modern poetry, opera, and psychoanalytic criticism continue the pattern.

### Twenty-first century

**[Modern reinvention]** Madeline Miller’s *Circe* (2018) consolidates scattered myths—Prometheus, Scylla, the Minotaur, Daedalus, Medea, Odysseus, Telegonus—into a first-person developmental novel. Miller turns Circe’s *pharmaka* into hard-won personal craft, foregrounds exile and sexual violence, and changes the cyclic ending into a story of chosen mortality and self-authorship. It is deliberately modern synthesis, not recovery of a lost ancient biography. The author’s presentation describes the book’s focus on family rivalry and female strength: [Madeline Miller, *Circe*](https://madelinemiller.com/circe/).

Modern Hellenic polytheists and witchcraft practitioners sometimes honor Circe as a goddess of sorcery, herbs, transformation, independence, or liminality. Individual altars and devotional practice are documented online, but there is no reliable census or proof of institutional continuity from Circeii. Claims of an unbroken “Circean witch cult” are **modern inventions unless independent evidence is supplied**.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Occult and esoteric reception

Circe did not become a major named power in the canonical Solomonic grimoires, Hermetic planetary hierarchy, Golden Dawn grades, or Theosophical cosmology. Modern occult treatments generally derive her authority from Homer, Ovid, Renaissance allegory, modern goddess spirituality, and popular witchcraft—not from an ancient initiatory lineage.

Frequently encountered modern claims require qualification:

- **“Priestess of Hecate.” [Modern inference]** Diodorus makes Hecate her mother and teacher; Ovid calls her spells Hecatean. Neither supplies a historical office.
- **“Goddess of witchcraft.” [Modern systematization]** Ancient literature makes her a goddess who practices potent *pharmaka*, but no surviving archaic cult title assigns her jurisdiction over all witchcraft.
- **“Moon goddess.” [Modern invention or conflation]** Her father is solar, and Ovid grants her power to cloud celestial light. No ancient evidence makes the moon her core domain.
- **“Patroness of pigs.” [Modern extrapolation]** Pigs are transformed men in Homer, not her sacred animal in documented cult.
- **“Inventor of the magic circle.” [False etymological invention]** It rests on resemblance between Κίρκη and words for a ring/circle.
- **“Ancient feminist goddess.” [Modern interpretive tradition]** Feminist readings illuminate gendered reception and restore Circe’s perspective, but the label is not ancient.

### Literature, visual art, film, comics, and games

Major modern interventions include:

- Calderón’s *El mayor encanto, amor* (1635), a court spectacle in which love overcomes enchantment.
- La Fontaine’s “The Companions of Ulysses” (1690), moralizing transformation.
- John Keats, Nathaniel Hawthorne, and later Victorian poets, who develop Circean seduction and artistic enchantment.
- Augusta Webster’s “Circe” (1870), which voices frustrated female desire.
- Waterhouse’s two Circe paintings (1891–1892), foundational for the modern visual stereotype.
- Joyce’s “Circe” in *Ulysses* (1922), using metamorphosis as theatrical hallucination.
- Eudora Welty’s “Circe” (1949), Margaret Atwood’s Circe poems, and Carol Ann Duffy’s “Circe,” all shifting narrative authority.
- DC’s Circe, a recurring Wonder Woman adversary from 1949 onward, combines classical transformation with superhero sorcery.
- Miller’s *Circe* (2018), the most influential recent popular reconstruction.

A broad reception study describes the long movement from Homer’s ambiguous but substantially helpful goddess to witch, whore, temptress, and modern reclaimed voice: [“Back on Circe’s Island”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ramus/article/back-on-circes-island/34A1BDA172467FC0747F3C42D7B4ED4F). Irene Berti likewise traces the movement from goddess to witch to belle-époque femme fatale in [“Le metamorfosi di Circe”](https://rosa.uniroma1.it/rosa03/status_quaestionis/article/view/13143).

### Political and commercial use

Circe has occasionally supplied misogynistic shorthand for a woman who allegedly emasculates, deceives, or animalizes men. Conversely, feminist reception reclaims her as a figure of resistance to patriarchal narration. Neither use represents a continuous ancient theology.

“Circe” is now used commercially for perfumes, cosmetics, hotels, music, games, scientific instruments, and fictional AIs. Such naming depends upon associations with enchantment and transformation; it supplies no evidence about ancient belief.

No substantial, well-documented extremist movement centered on Circe emerged in the reviewed evidence. Generic neo-pagan or classical imagery should not be labeled extremist without organization-specific documentation.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Divine figure or folktale witch?

Older scholarship often sought a single origin: degraded goddess, eastern animal-mistress, death goddess, solar deity, or folktale witch. Modern analysis more often separates questions:

1. What functions does Circe perform within the *Odyssey*?
2. What earlier story patterns may the poet have adapted?
3. How did later texts transform her?

The “witch in the woods” folktale comparison explains the isolated house, food trap, animal transformation, magical helper, ordeal, and release. It does not establish a recoverable pre-Homeric tale word for word.

### Circe and Calypso

Both sing and weave on remote islands, receive Odysseus sexually, and help him depart. Analysts have variously treated them as narrative doubles, inherited death-goddesses, or independently composed episodes. The standard Odyssey commentary notes that their relationship and the history of Books 5 and 10 remain disputed. Parallel function is secure; common cultic origin is not.

### Eastern or Anatolian origin

The solar family’s far-eastern Colchian connections, Circe’s animals, and the proposed “hawk” name encouraged comparison with Near Eastern “Mistress of Animals” imagery and Cybele/Ishtar. This can be a useful comparative hypothesis. It becomes unsound when presented as a demonstrated chain from a named prehistoric goddess to Homer’s Circe. The evidence contains resemblance, not documentary transmission.

### Where is Aeaea?

Proposals include:

- Monte Circeo in Latium;
- the island of Ponza;
- Aeaea imagined near the sunrise;
- a purely fabulous island;
- shifting poetic geography subsequently anchored in Italy.

Homer’s route is not consistently reproducible on a modern map. Apollonius, Virgil, Strabo, and Ovid represent historical stages in western localization. Monte Circeo’s island-like appearance amid sea and marsh made it an especially persuasive site, but later localization cannot simply be projected into Homer.

### The *Theogony* ending

The lines making Circe mother of Latinus and Agrius—and in the transmitted text Telegonus—have been suspected of interpolation or secondary composition because they connect Greek heroes to western peoples. Even if secondary, they are ancient evidence for the development of Circe’s myth. “Interpolated” does not mean modern forgery.

### Sex, consent, and power

Ancient and modern readings diverge sharply. Homer’s sequence includes attempted incapacitation, an armed threat, a divinely prescribed oath, sexual union, and subsequent hospitality. Ancient allegory tends to center male self-control; modern feminist readings center Circe’s vulnerability, agency, exile, or Odysseus’s power. Responsible historical treatment distinguishes the poem’s actions from the ethical vocabulary imposed by each reception period.

### Cult versus literature

Farnell’s model of Greek cult emphasizes institutional evidence; Circe’s meager priestly and festival record contrasts with her extraordinary literary afterlife. Burkert treats her primarily within epic and magic rather than as a major civic goddess. LIMC’s iconographic material demonstrates extensive narrative recognition, not necessarily worship.

Strabo and Cicero prevent the opposite error: Circe was not exclusively fictional in ancient practice, because localized worship at Circeii is explicitly reported. The right conclusion is a small, geographically restricted cult coupled with enormous literary importance.

### Graves and Kerényi

Karl Kerényi’s mythological writing is influential reception and comparative interpretation, valuable for symbolic patterns but not a substitute for primary-source chronology. Robert Graves’s *The Greek Myths* frequently combines ancient sources with speculative ritualism, poetic intuition, and outdated matriarchal reconstruction. Claims found only in Graves require independent verification.

### State of the evidence

- The *Odyssey* and *Theogony* survive through long manuscript traditions plus ancient papyrus fragments.
- The *Telegony*, *Nostoi*, and Sophoclean play survive only in fragments, epitomes, quotations, and testimonia.
- Ancient painted pottery is dispersed among the British Museum, Metropolitan Museum, Louvre, Berlin Antikensammlung, and other collections.
- Monte Circeo preserves archaeological structures, but dedications and architectural attribution remain incomplete.
- TLG provides the fullest searchable Greek literary corpus; Perseus offers open Greek and translations; LIMC remains the standard iconographic starting point; CIL is essential for Latin inscriptions.
- The Theoi Circe index is a useful source locator but a modern compilation, not a critical edition. Its collected passages can be checked at [Theoi, “Kirke”](https://www.theoi.com/Titan/Kirke.html).

| **Fifth–sixth century CE** | Fulgentius Christianizes the Scylla story as an allegory of lust and prostitution. |

| **2018** | Madeline Miller’s *Circe* becomes the most culturally influential recent first-person reinvention. |

| **Present** | Circe remains active in literature, games, comics, art, feminist criticism, Hellenic polytheism, and modern witchcraft; no documented institutional continuity from the ancient cult survives. |
