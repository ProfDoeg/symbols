# La Sirène: Research Dossier

**Class:** In Haitian Vodou, La Sirène is a female **lwa**—a powerful, personalized spirit served through prayer, song, possession, offerings, and ritual—not ordinarily a creator-goddess. Depending on house and region, she may be classified within the Rada, Kongo, or a broader aquatic “nation,” and may also designate a queen or collectivity of siren spirits. Calling her simply a “goddess” is common in art writing but is less exact than *lwa*.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest presently verifiable published documentation of **La Sirène as a named Haitian lwa** is Melville J. Herskovits’s fieldwork from Mirebalais, published in *Life in a Haitian Valley* in **1937**. Neither Moreau de Saint-Méry’s colonial description of Saint-Domingue religion (1797–98) nor the earliest surviving Bois Caïman accounts names La Sirène. Older African water-spirit traditions and older European mermaid imagery are relevant antecedents, but no surviving eighteenth-century document securely proves that a Haitian lwa already called *La Sirène* existed under that name.

**Evidentiary labels used below:** **[EARLY ATTESTED]**, **[LATER ETHNOGRAPHIC]**, **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**, **[SYNCRETISTIC IDENTIFICATION]**, **[FOLKLORE/ORAL TESTIMONY]**, **[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**, and **[MODERN INVENTION OR UNVERIFIED CLAIM]**.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

La Sirène is the female sovereign or mistress of the sea’s depths in Haitian Vodou. She is conventionally represented as a beautiful woman with a fish’s tail, long hair, mirror, and comb. Her sphere includes seawater, underwater riches, beauty, luxury, music, song, eloquence, occult knowledge, dreams, and the dangerous attraction of the deep. She is regularly paired with **Agwe/Agwé Tawoyo**, the admiral or master of the sea, and with **La Baleine**, the Whale. Ethnographers variously describe her as Agwe’s consort, an aquatic form or close analogue of Ezili, and a member of an underwater court.

That description is a historical composite. Herskovits, Deren, Métraux, Rigaud, and subsequent Haitian and diasporic practitioners do not furnish one universal catechism. Vodou is organized through houses, inherited ritual lineages, locality, and revelation; attributes may therefore differ without one house’s version invalidating another.

### Names and spellings

- **La Sirène** — French, literally “the Mermaid” or “the Siren.” **[EARLY ATTESTED: 1937 publication]**
- **Lasirèn / Lasiren / Lasirenn** — Haitian Creole spellings reflecting the pronunciation of French *la sirène*. **[LATER ETHNOGRAPHIC/LIVING USAGE]**
- **Maîtresse La Sirène / Metrès Lasirèn** — “Mistress La Sirène,” an honorific rather than a distinct securely documented being. **[REGIONAL/HOUSE USAGE]**
- **La Sirène Diamant / Lasirèn Dyaman** — “Diamond Siren,” found in modern Haitian religious and artistic speech; whether this is an epithet, manifestation, or separate member of an aquatic collectivity varies by lineage. **[REGIONAL VARIANT; chronology insufficiently established]**
- **Mami Wata / Mammy Water / Mama Water** — West and Central African names for a spirit or class of water spirits. Equation with La Sirène is widespread in museum, popular, and some practitioner discourse, but it is not a demonstrated one-to-one survival of a single prehistoric deity. **[SYNCRETISTIC IDENTIFICATION/MODERN SCHOLARLY COMPARISON]**
- **Sao** — identified as “the siren” in some twentieth-century Dahomean/Fon comparative literature and later Haitian commentary. The equation *Sao = La Sirène* needs to be treated as comparative ethnology, not a securely demonstrated transfer of a named Fon deity to Saint-Domingue. **[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**
- **Maman Dlo / Manman Dlo** — “Mother of Water,” used for related mermaid or water-woman traditions in Martinique, Guadeloupe, and Guyana. Cognate in theme, not automatically the same ritual person. **[REGIONAL COMPARISON]**
- **Yemọja/Yemoja, Yemayá, Iemanjá** — Yoruba and Afro-American orisha traditions associated with water and maternity. Their equation with La Sirène is a late comparative or syncretistic move, not evidence that La Sirène is simply a Haitian spelling of Yemoja. **[SYNCRETISTIC IDENTIFICATION]**

### Etymology

The immediate etymology is secure: French **sirène**, absorbed into Haitian Creole as **sirèn**. The word supplied a European mermaid category already familiar through heraldry, marine folklore, Catholic visual culture, printed illustration, and colonial vocabulary.

This says nothing by itself about the origin of the religious being. A French name may cover African-derived ritual conceptions. Conversely, resemblance to African water spirits does not prove that every later feature came intact from Dahomey.

*Mami Wata* is normally explained as a West African adaptation of English or pidgin **Mammy/Mama Water**. Proposals deriving it from indigenous African phrases exist, but no alternative has displaced the English/pidgin derivation in the principal art-historical literature. Moreover, “Mami Wata” often denotes a flexible category or collectivity rather than one transregional goddess.

### Classification changes

- Herskovits and Métraux record La Sirène among Haitian spirits of the waters. **[EARLY/LATER ETHNOGRAPHIC]**
- Deren closely associates her with Ezili’s aquatic aspect. **[LATER ETHNOGRAPHIC SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- Later handbooks often assign her unambiguously to Rada. Some practitioners instead stress Kongo affiliations or multiple siren nations. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- Commercial occult writing frequently calls her a universal “sea goddess,” patroness of psychic intuition or “the subconscious.” Those formulations may be modern interpretations unless supported by a named Haitian lineage. **[MODERN REINTERPRETATION]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Before 1937: antecedents and absences

Moreau de Saint-Méry’s *Description topographique, physique, civile, politique et historique de la partie française de l’isle Saint-Domingue* (published 1797–98) contains the best-known colonial account of *vaudoux*. It is hostile, external, and written after the colonial society it describes had begun to collapse. It does **not** securely name La Sirène. **[DOCUMENTED ABSENCE]**

The Bois Caïman ceremony traditionally placed in August 1791 is first described in surviving print by Antoine Dalmas in 1814, not in a contemporary ritual transcript. David Geggus and other historians disagree over which details can be recovered, including the animal sacrifice, oath, officiants, and later national elaborations. None of the early Bois Caïman evidence names La Sirène. Claims that she personally presided, received a human sacrifice, or formed part of a “pact” are unsupported. **[MODERN INVENTION OR UNVERIFIED CLAIM]**

Atlantic antecedents nevertheless existed:

- Fon, Ewe, Yoruba, Kongo, and other African societies possessed water vodun, river spirits, sea powers, royal pythons, and aquatic ancestors.
- European mermaid imagery had circulated along African coasts through ships, carved prows, heraldry, prints, trade goods, and Christian art.
- Afro-Atlantic communities could recombine these forms without preserving a written genealogy.

These establish a historical field of possibility, not a documentary first appearance of the Haitian lwa. **[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**

### Principal published witnesses

1. **Melville J. Herskovits, *Life in a Haitian Valley* (1937).**  
   Based on Mirebalais fieldwork. This is the earliest securely identifiable published ethnographic witness to La Sirène by name located for this dossier. Herskovits places her among Haitian water spirits and within the lived religion of rural communities. It proves that the named lwa was established by the 1930s; it does not date her origin. **[EARLY ATTESTED]**

2. **Maya Deren, *Divine Horsemen: The Living Gods of Haiti* (1953).**  
   Based on fieldwork begun in the 1940s. Deren documents the marine ritual complex of Agwe, La Sirène, Ezili, and La Baleine, including songs, aquatic offerings, possession, and vèvè. Her influential interpretation makes La Sirène resemble or overlap with the marine Ezili. Deren also records the legendary rivalry between Agwe and Ogoun over Ezili/La Sirène. Her account is invaluable participant-observation but includes interpretive system-building. **[LATER ETHNOGRAPHIC]**

3. **Alfred Métraux, *Le Vaudou haïtien* (1958; English translation 1959).**  
   Métraux describes La Sirène as Agwe’s mistress or wife, living in the depths, with long hair and a mirror. He records the widespread underwater-initiation narrative: people carried below the water may return with occult or healing knowledge. **[LATER ETHNOGRAPHIC/FOLKLORE]**

4. **Milo Rigaud, *La Tradition voudoo et le voudoo haïtien* (1953) and *Vè-Vè: Diagrammes rituels du Voudou* (1946/1950s; expanded editions later).**  
   Rigaud’s diagrams helped canonize printed forms of the vèvè. His plates are evidence for twentieth-century learned and artistic systematization, not proof that every line of a published vèvè is ancient or universal. **[LATER CANONICAL SYSTEMATIZATION]**

5. **Maya Deren’s film footage, released posthumously as *Divine Horsemen: The Living Gods of Haiti* (1985).**  
   The footage is visual evidence for dance, possession, altars, and ceremony in mid-century Haiti; editorial assembly occurred decades later. **[LATER VISUAL ETHNOGRAPHY]**

6. **Karen McCarthy Brown, *Mama Lola: A Vodou Priestess in Brooklyn* (1991).**  
   This documents a Haitian transnational house rather than attempting a universal pantheon. It is methodologically important because it shows that lwa identities are mediated through family, migration, ritual inheritance, and personal histories. La Sirène is not its central spirit. **[DIASPORA ETHNOGRAPHY]**

7. **Henry John Drewal, ed., *Sacred Waters: Arts for Mami Wata and Other Divinities in Africa and the Diaspora* (2008).**  
   This comparative corpus includes Marilyn Houlberg on Haitian water spirits and extensive material on the diverse African Mami Wata complex. It strongly supports comparison but also demonstrates why “Mami Wata,” “Yemoja,” and “La Sirène” must not be flattened into one timeless goddess. **[MODERN SCHOLARSHIP]**

8. **Deborah O’Neil and Terry Rey, “The Saint and Siren” (2012).**  
   This is the crucial historical study of the association between **St Philomena** and **Lasyrenn** at Bord de Mer de Limonade. The authors document a shrine tradition at least approximately 125 years old, pilgrimage, local identity, ecclesiastical opposition, and feminine religious power. **[REGIONAL SYNCRETISM]**

### Oral corpus

Songs are primary evidence even where their precise date cannot be established. The best-known family of refrains joins La Sirène and La Baleine—for example, the line often transcribed “Lasirèn, Labalèn, chapo m tonbe nan lanmè” (“La Sirène, La Baleine, my hat fell into the sea”). Its circulation is well established, but claims that it originated in a particular century require evidence not presently available. **[FOLKLORE/LITURGICAL ORAL TRADITION]**

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

Securely recurrent twentieth-century attributes include:

- the sea and underwater world;
- submerged treasure, wealth, luxury, and prosperity;
- beauty, erotic magnetism, adornment, and refined taste;
- music, singing, and sometimes artistic inspiration;
- occult knowledge received through dreams, possession, or underwater initiation;
- danger, disappearance, drowning, madness, or estrangement from ordinary society.

La Sirène is not merely benevolent or malevolent. Like water, she may enrich, teach, fascinate, possess, remove, or destroy. The common commercial reduction “money-drawing mermaid” preserves one attribute while stripping away much of the ritual relationship.

### Form and identifying objects

- **Woman-fish/mermaid:** now the dominant form. **[LATER CANONICAL ICONOGRAPHY]**
- **Long, carefully dressed hair:** widely reported by Métraux and later sources.
- **Mirror:** one of her most stable identifiers. It can signify beauty, doubled worlds, divination, or the threshold between surface and depth. The psychological explanation “self-knowledge” is primarily modern unless a practitioner supplies it within a named lineage.
- **Comb:** an instrument of grooming and alluring beauty; also given modern allegorical meanings such as “untangling problems.” **[CORE OBJECT; VARIABLE INTERPRETATION]**
- **Jewels, pearls, shells, perfume, fine fabric, champagne:** appropriate to beauty and underwater riches; exact offerings vary by house.
- **Trumpet:** linked to her musical or heraldic manifestation in some modern iconography and practitioner accounts. It is less consistently documented than mirror and comb. It should not be projected backward as an original African attribute without a dated object or text. **[REGIONAL/LATE ATTRIBUTE]**
- **Snake:** central to the famous African Mami Wata chromolithograph tradition but not a universal Haitian La Sirène emblem. **[MAMI WATA ICONOGRAPHY; OCCASIONAL TRANSFER]**

### Color, day, number, and offerings

Internet tables assign blue, sea-green, white, silver, pink, seven, Thursday, Friday, or Saturday to La Sirène. Such correspondences are house-dependent and sometimes originate in late occult manuals. No single color-day-number table is demonstrably universal.

Marine offerings may be placed in a small boat and sent or submerged at sea, especially in the broader Agwe complex. Deren’s documentation makes this one of the better-attested ritual forms. Mirrors, combs, perfume, jewelry, sweets, cakes, champagne, fish, and white or blue fabrics occur in later descriptions. **[LATER ETHNOGRAPHIC/REGIONAL]**

### “Singers are her children”

La Sirène’s association with voice, song, musicians, and singers is well represented in modern Haitian religious discourse. The stronger proposition that **all singers are literally her children** is an oral or lineage-specific formulation rather than a rule established in the early ethnographies. Calling a gifted singer “a child of La Sirène” may signify patronage, spiritual inheritance, possession, or affinity; these are not necessarily interchangeable. **[FOLKLORE/LIVING TRADITION]**

### Important images

- **Published vèvè:** Rigaud’s twentieth-century plates furnish a major printed record. They must be compared with actual temple-floor drawings, which vary.
- **Rigaud Benoit, *La Sirene* (1962), oil on board:** a securely dated landmark of Haitian religious painting.
- **Vodou flags and sequined banners:** twentieth- and twenty-first-century workshops often show the mermaid with mirror, comb, sequins, fish, ships, and Agwe.
- **Mami Wata snake-charmer image:** African Mami Wata artists adapted a European chromolithographic image of a female snake charmer, itself connected with late nineteenth-century performance publicity. It profoundly influenced African Mami Wata representation but is not the origin of every African water spirit or of Haitian La Sirène.

### Stella Maris

The Virgin Mary’s title **Stella Maris**, “Star of the Sea,” supplies an obvious Catholic maritime analogue, and Marian prints circulate in Vodou environments. Yet the most securely studied local Catholic pairing for La Sirène is **St Philomena at Bord de Mer de Limonade**, not a universal equation with Stella Maris. Claims that Stella Maris is *the* canonical Catholic image of La Sirène should therefore be labeled **[REGIONAL OR MODERN SYNCRETISM, NOT UNIVERSAL]**.

## 4. Genealogy and relations with divine beings

La Sirène has no securely attested birth narrative comparable to a written epic genealogy. Statements that she is the daughter of named Fon deities are comparative constructions or local teachings unless accompanied by a precise source.

### Principal relations

- **Agwe/Agwé Tawoyo:** spouse, consort, or female partner in the marine court. This is the strongest and most widely documented relationship.
- **La Baleine:** aquatic companion, counterpart, or closely associated lwa; songs invoke them jointly. Their precise kinship is not fixed.
- **Ezili Freda:** resemblance, overlap, or identification in Deren and later descriptions; both concern beauty, luxury, love, and refinement. Other houses keep them distinct.
- **Mami Wata:** comparative or syncretistic identity; in some modern usage La Sirène is “the Haitian Mami Wata,” while critical scholarship treats Mami Wata as a variable African and diasporic complex.
- **Yemoja/Yemayá/Iemanjá:** parallel sea or water mother in Yoruba-derived traditions. Similarity and modern interreligious exchange do not establish genealogical identity.
- **St Philomena:** locally intertwined cult at Bord de Mer de Limonade.
- **Stella Maris/Virgin Mary:** possible or modern maritime Marian analogy; not a universally documented equivalence.

## 5. Major myths and narrative complexes

### The underwater abduction and initiation

**[FOLKLORE, DOCUMENTED IN TWENTIETH-CENTURY ETHNOGRAPHY]**

La Sirène takes, invites, or draws a person beneath the water. Time under water may not correspond to time on land. The person may be instructed in healing, divination, music, occult knowledge, or material success. If returned, the initiate may possess extraordinary gifts but may also be changed, socially estranged, mentally disturbed, mute for a period, or bound by taboos.

Métraux records the basic narrative complex. Its analogues are widespread across Africa and the Atlantic, but resemblance does not prove a single point of origin. The story functions as an account of vocation, possession, exceptional talent, disappearance, illness, and transformed identity.

### The singer or musician chosen by La Sirène

**[LIVING ORAL TRADITION]**

A singer’s remarkable voice may be described as La Sirène’s gift. Dreams of water, mermaids, mirrors, or underwater music can be interpreted as signs of her claim. The recipient may owe service or offerings in return. Modern retellings often universalize this into “all singers are her children”; the sources support patronage more securely than the universal rule.

### La Sirène and Agwe’s underwater court

**[LATER CANONICAL ETHNOGRAPHIC FORM]**

La Sirène and Agwe inhabit a splendid realm beneath the sea, rich in treasure and populated by aquatic spirits. Agwe governs navigation, ships, sailors, and the open sea; La Sirène embodies the depths, beauty, attraction, wealth, and hidden knowledge. Their relation models a royal or admiral’s court more often than an extended narrative cycle.

### La Sirène, Ezili, Agwe, and Ogoun

**[RECORDED LEGEND, Deren 1953]**

Deren reports a triangular configuration: La Sirène is sufficiently identified with Ezili that Agwe’s rivalry with Ogoun over Ezili also implicates La Sirène. The episode articulates the opposition of sea and fire and the overlapping erotic alliances of Ezili. It should not be converted into a universally canonical marriage myth.

### The dangerous mermaid

**[FOLKLORE/ATLANTIC COMPARATIVE MOTIF]**

Coastal narratives describe a beautiful water woman who attracts people, grants wealth, demands fidelity, or causes drowning. Some are specifically about La Sirène; others concern unnamed mermaids, Maman Dlo, Mami Wata, or local water spirits. Attribution must therefore be made story by story.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

La Sirène does not possess a centralized church or a universally recognized chief temple. She is served within **ounfò** and family houses, at private altars, and at the sea. The institutional unit is normally the ritual lineage under an **oungan** or **manbo**, not a separate “Church of La Sirène.”

### Ritual forms

- songs naming La Sirène, La Baleine, Agwe, and other marine lwa;
- vèvè drawn in cornmeal or other powder;
- possession, whose embodied style varies by house;
- marine pilgrimage or service at the shoreline;
- offerings placed on an altar or sent to sea in a decorated boat;
- mirrors, combs, perfume, jewelry, fabric, drink, sweets, and marine objects;
- dreams and divination used to determine whether the lwa requests service.

Deren’s account of an Agwe ceremony is especially important: a vessel of offerings is dispatched onto or into the sea, dramatizing delivery to the underwater court.

### Priesthood

La Sirène has no securely documented exclusive hereditary priesthood. Oungans and manbos may serve her within broader Vodou responsibilities. A person described as her “child” is not automatically a priest. Initiation, possession, inherited service, and personal devotion are distinct statuses.

### Vèvè

Printed vèvè attributed to La Sirène commonly employ symmetrical marine, heart, star, fish, or serpentine motifs. Rigaud’s plates are historically influential, but a vèvè is a ritual drawing tradition rather than a fixed logo. Differences between houses are evidence, not corruption.

### Bord de Mer de Limonade

The strongest documented local cult center is the northern coastal village of **Bord de Mer de Limonade**, where Lasyrenn and St Philomena became intertwined. O’Neil and Rey describe a shrine attracting weekly visitors and thousands around the early-September feast. The association has periodically been opposed by Catholic authorities. This is a historically situated local cult, not proof that all Haiti identifies La Sirène with Philomena.

### Ouidah and Dahomey

The python temple at Ouidah, the royal palace traditions of Abomey, and Fon water vodun are essential to the broader history from which Haitian Vodou developed. They do not, however, supply a securely documented eighteenth-century temple specifically dedicated to a being named La Sirène. Modern Mami Wata shrines in Benin and Togo are valid contemporary traditions but cannot be used unchanged as photographs of pre-deportation religion.

### Sacrifice and contested allegations

Animal offerings belong to Vodou’s documented ritual economy, varying by lwa and ceremony. No reliable source located for this dossier documents **human sacrifice to La Sirène**. Colonial, missionary, occupation-era, sensationalist, and horror accounts frequently alleged cannibalism and human sacrifice in Vodou; those claims require case-specific corroboration. The absence of evidence for sacrifice to La Sirène is a positive finding.

Nor is “temple prostitution” documented as an institution of her cult. Modern sexualized descriptions of Mami Wata or La Sirène concern beauty, desire, spirit marriage, gender, and exchange; they are not evidence of organized sacred prostitution.

## 7. Historical evolution

### African and European antecedents, before 1791

West and Central African water-spirit systems, Fon/Ewe vodun, Kongo aquatic cosmologies, Yoruba river and ocean powers, and European mermaid images all preceded Haitian La Sirène. **[BACKGROUND, NOT IDENTITY]**

### Saint-Domingue, c. 1697–1804

Enslaved Africans and their descendants formed new ritual communities under violent colonial conditions. Moreau de Saint-Méry documents *vaudoux* but not La Sirène. Bois Caïman belongs to the Revolution’s religious memory, but no early evidence connects La Sirène to it. **[DOCUMENTED ABSENCE]**

### Nineteenth-century Haiti

The named lwa is poorly documented in accessible nineteenth-century print. This reflects the archive’s hostility and incompleteness, not proof of absence from practice. At Bord de Mer, the St Philomena–Lasyrenn cult appears to have developed by approximately the late nineteenth century. **[REGIONAL EVIDENCE]**

### 1896–1942: suppression and ethnological recovery

The Catholic Church encouraged rejection of Vodou in 1896. The most destructive phase was the state-supported **1939–42 anti-superstition campaign**, often called the *campagne des rejetés*, involving forced oaths, destruction or burning of ritual objects, and attacks on sacred sites. These campaigns targeted Vodou collectively; no evidence shows a La Sirène-specific proscription.

At the same time, Haitian Indigenist intellectuals and the Bureau d’Ethnologie began collecting and defending material that church and state actors were destroying.

### 1937–1958: ethnographic fixation

Herskovits, Deren, Rigaud, and Métraux transformed a living and variable oral tradition into a published corpus. They preserved irreplaceable evidence but also helped stabilize “pantheon,” vèvè, and attribute lists that later readers sometimes treat as timeless doctrine.

### 1960s–1980s: visual proliferation

Haitian painters and flag makers increasingly represented La Sirène as a mermaid with mirror and comb. Rigaud Benoit’s 1962 painting is a securely dated example. Tourism, collecting, migration, and the international art market amplified her image.

### 1987–2003: religious-rights transition

Haiti’s 1987 Constitution protected religious freedom. President Jean-Bertrand Aristide’s decree of 4 April 2003 formally recognized Vodou as a religion and permitted qualified religious leaders to seek state recognition. This was not the invention of the religion or of La Sirène; it changed Vodou’s legal standing.

### Present

La Sirène remains a living lwa in Haiti and Haitian diasporic communities. She is also served or represented in transnational, eclectic, and revived New Orleans settings. Contemporary insecurity and social crisis in Haiti have not ended Vodou practice; recent reporting describes both continued stigma and renewed reliance on Vodou communities.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Haitian and diasporic art

La Sirène is among the most visually marketable lwa because mermaid imagery is immediately recognizable. Paintings, sequined flags, sculpture, murals, and commercial prints may be devotional, aesthetic, tourist-oriented, or several at once. The existence of a market does not make an object religiously inauthentic, but provenance matters.

### Mami Wata exhibitions

Drewal’s scholarship and major exhibitions established the water spirit as a central figure in African Atlantic art history. This widened recognition of La Sirène while sometimes encouraging audiences to imagine Mami Wata as a single global goddess. Drewal’s own comparative corpus instead demonstrates plurality and historical borrowing.

### New Orleans

The historical archive of nineteenth-century Louisiana Voodoo—including newspapers and the Louisiana Writers’ Project interviews—does not securely establish La Sirène as a major named lwa of the Marie Laveau era. Robert Tallant’s *Voodoo in New Orleans* (1946) is sensationalistic and derived partly from interviews gathered decades after the remembered events.

Carolyn Morrow Long shows that the Louisiana Writers’ Project interviewed seventy elderly Black New Orleanians; the testimony was recorded without audio equipment, sometimes rewritten into stereotyped dialect, and often concerned events fifty or sixty years earlier. It remains important but must be handled critically.

Explicit service to La Sirène in modern New Orleans is better explained by late twentieth- and twenty-first-century Haitian, Cuban, Yoruba-derived, occult, and commercial interchange. The Voodoo Spiritual Temple, La Source Ancienne, botanicas, tarot, spiritual merchandise, and contemporary books participate in this revival. Present-day practice is real present-day religion, but it should not be retrojected into the nineteenth century. **[MODERN REVIVAL/SYNCRETISM]**

### Occult and New Age reinterpretation

Modern manuals describe La Sirène as:

- ruler of the subconscious;
- patroness of manifestation and glamour;
- universal mermaid goddess;
- archetype of feminine sexuality;
- spirit for money-drawing, psychic ability, or artistic success.

These may be meaningful contemporary practices, but Jungian, New Age, and correspondence-table language is not found in the earliest Haitian documentation. **[MODERN INVENTION/REINTERPRETATION]**

The *New Orleans Voodoo Tarot* and subsequent internet occultism have materially shaped the English-speaking image of La Sirène. Tarot assignment is modern and is not an inherited Haitian liturgical structure.

### Commercial use

Candles, baths, oils, dolls, jewelry, prints, and “La Sirena” money products are sold in botanicas and online. Some derive from living religious economies; others use the mermaid name without accountable lineage. Product existence documents reception, not the antiquity of the claim printed on its label.

## 9. Historiography and disputed questions

### Is La Sirène Mami Wata?

Three positions must be separated:

1. **Identity claim:** La Sirène is the Haitian form of Mami Wata.
2. **Historical-family claim:** Both participate in an African Atlantic complex of water spirits, mermaid imagery, commerce, beauty, wealth, and danger.
3. **Independent-development claim:** Haitian La Sirène arose from locally recombined African aquatic spirits and European mermaid imagery, while modern Mami Wata comparison came later.

The second is best supported. Direct identity may be valid within a particular modern lineage, but the documentary chain is incomplete.

### Is she Yemoja?

No early Haitian evidence establishes that La Sirène is simply Yemoja. Yemoja is a named Yoruba orisha with a distinct textual, ritual, and genealogical history. Yemayá and Iemanjá developed their own Cuban and Brazilian forms. Their equation with La Sirène reflects comparison, contact, and modern interreligious practice. **[SYNCRETISTIC IDENTIFICATION, NOT ORIGINAL IDENTITY]**

### Is she Ezili?

Deren’s presentation supports a strong overlap between La Sirène and the marine aspect of Ezili. Other classifications distinguish them. The difference cannot be resolved by declaring one side “canonical”: it is evidence of house-level and interpretive variation.

### Does she derive from Stella Maris?

Marian maritime imagery plausibly contributed to her Catholic-facing visual world, but a direct derivation is not demonstrated. The locally documented Philomena association is stronger. “La Sirène is Stella Maris” is therefore too categorical.

### Is the mirror ancient?

The mirror and comb are well documented in twentieth-century Haitian accounts and imagery. Mirrors are also central to African Mami Wata arts and European mermaid conventions. Their precise route into Haitian ritual cannot presently be established. Calling the mirror an unchanged Dahomean survival exceeds the evidence.

### Was she present at Bois Caïman?

No surviving early source names her. The earliest printed Bois Caïman narrative dates to 1814, and the ceremony’s later historiography is highly politicized. A La Sirène role is **unestablished**.

### Human sacrifice, sexual rites, and “demonic” character

No reliable evidence establishes human sacrifice or institutional sacred prostitution in her cult. Christian anti-Vodou literature has often redescribed lwa as demons; that is a historically datable polemical classification, not Vodou’s own classification. Contemporary Pentecostal demonization of Mami Wata belongs to the history of reception.

### Problems in the source base

- Vodou remained overwhelmingly oral and ritual.
- Colonial records were written by slaveholders, police, missionaries, and hostile observers.
- Sacred knowledge is selectively disclosed.
- Suppression destroyed objects and temples.
- Early ethnographers generalized from particular regions and informants.
- Printed vèvè books encouraged later standardization.
- Internet compilations recycle unsourced color, day, number, family, and offering lists.
- “Dahomean origin” is often asserted more precisely than the surviving evidence permits.

## 10. Chronology

| Date | Attestation or historical event |
|---|---|
| Before 1500 | Multiple African societies possess aquatic spirits and deities; European mermaid traditions also exist. These are antecedents, not attestations of La Sirène. |
| Fifteenth–nineteenth centuries | European ships, prints, heraldry, trade goods, and performance imagery circulate mermaid motifs along African coasts. |
| 1697–1804 | Saint-Domingue colonial period; African-derived religions are recomposed under slavery. No named La Sirène document has been securely identified. |
| 1791 | Bois Caïman and the northern insurrection. No early source names La Sirène. |
| 1797–98 | Moreau de Saint-Méry publishes his description of colonial Saint-Domingue and *vaudoux*; no secure La Sirène attestation. |
| 1814 | Dalmas publishes the earliest surviving account commonly cited for Bois Caïman. |
| Late nineteenth century, approximately | St Philomena–Lasyrenn devotion develops or is already active at Bord de Mer de Limonade. |
| c. 1885 onward | The European snake-charmer chromolithograph later adapted by African Mami Wata artists enters circulation. |
| 1896 | Catholic campaign urging rejection of Vodou. |
| 1915–34 | U.S. occupation; Vodou is criminalized and sensationalized while Haitian Indigenism grows. |
| 1937 | Herskovits publishes *Life in a Haitian Valley*: earliest secure published named Haitian attestation located here. |
| 1939–42 | State-supported Catholic anti-superstition campaign destroys sacred objects and sites. |
| 1946 | Tallant publishes *Voodoo in New Orleans*, an influential but sensationalized account based partly on Louisiana Writers’ Project material. |
| 1953 | Deren publishes *Divine Horsemen*, documenting La Sirène’s marine ritual complex and relation to Agwe and Ezili. |
| 1958–59 | Métraux publishes French and English editions of his major ethnography, including the underwater-teaching tradition. |
| 1962 | Rigaud Benoit paints *La Sirene*. |
| 1970s | Expanded publication and circulation of Milo Rigaud’s vèvè diagrams contribute to iconographic standardization. |
| 1985 | Deren’s Haitian footage is edited and released as *Divine Horsemen*. |
| 1987 | Haitian Constitution protects religious freedom; Drewal’s major article advances Mami Wata art history. |
| 1990 | Miriam Chamani establishes the Voodoo Spiritual Temple in New Orleans, part of a modern transnational revival. |
| 1991 | Karen McCarthy Brown publishes *Mama Lola*. |
| 2003 | Haiti formally recognizes Vodou as a religion by presidential decree. |
| 2008 | Drewal’s *Sacred Waters* supplies the most extensive comparative art-historical corpus on Mami Wata and diasporic water spirits. |
| 2012 | O’Neil and Rey publish their study of St Philomena and Lasyrenn at Bord de Mer. |
| 2020s | La Sirène remains a living lwa and a major subject of Haitian art, diaspora practice, online instruction, commercial spirituality, and contested cross-traditional identification. |
