# The Gede: Research Dossier

**Class:** In Haitian Vodou, the Gede (Haitian Creole: **Gede**; French spellings **Guédé, Gédé**; English ethnographic spelling **Ghede**) are a potentially innumerable **family or nation of lwa**—spirits of the dead associated simultaneously with death, sexuality, fertility, healing, children, social satire, and the cemetery. “Gede” can denote the collective, a ritual division, an individual manifestation, or part of an individual spirit’s name. They are not demons in Vodou classification. Some houses distinguish the Gede from the cemetery-governing **Bawon/Barons**; others identify or overlap them extensively.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest presently identifiable Haitian printed source explicitly naming Gede is Antoine Innocent’s French-language novel *Mimola, ou l’histoire d’une cassette* (Port-au-Prince, 1906). Because this is literary evidence, it proves that the name and associated belief circulated by 1906, not when the cult began. The earliest sustained, securely contextualized ethnographic documentation is Melville J. and Frances Herskovits’s 1934 fieldwork in Mirebalais, published in *Life in a Haitian Valley* (1937). An apparently cognate West African **Gede vodun** and the **Gedevi** population of the Abomey plateau were independently documented by Auguste Le Hérissé in 1911. The historical connection between these West African and Haitian materials is strong but reconstructive: no surviving eighteenth-century Saint-Domingue document explicitly traces a Gede cult across the Atlantic.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** contemporary documentary, material, or closely contextualized ethnographic evidence.
- **[LE] Later ethnographic/canonical elaboration:** well-documented twentieth- or twenty-first-century Vodou teaching or practice.
- **[RV] Regional or lineage variant:** attested in particular houses, regions, or communities but not universal.
- **[SR] Scholarly reconstruction:** a historical, linguistic, or comparative inference rather than an explicit traditional statement in an early source.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** association with a Catholic saint or another spirit-image; not necessarily an assertion that the beings are ontologically identical.
- **[F] Folklore or oral legend:** a recorded traditional narrative whose historical occurrence cannot be independently demonstrated.
- **[MR] Modern reception or retelling.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported popular claim.**

Vodou is predominantly oral and lineage-based. A missing printed myth is therefore not proof that a tradition does not exist; conversely, a claim repeated on modern occult websites is not early evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The collective identity

**[EA/LE]** Herskovits recorded Gede in Mirebalais as a powerful judge of death: even where Bawon Samdi has marked someone, Gede may refuse the death if it is undeserved. Maya Deren’s *Divine Horsemen* (1953), Alfred Métraux’s *Le Vaudou haïtien*/*Voodoo in Haiti* (1958–59), and subsequent Haitian and diasporic ethnographies describe a collective recognizable by cemetery symbolism, nasal speech, black or purple dress, funerary parody, obscenity, phallic dancing, clairin, tobacco, and hot pepper. Deren’s influential formulation, “corpse and phallus; king and clown,” is an ethnographer’s synthesis, not a surviving ancient title.

Gede are commonly described as:

- **the dead as a collective spiritual family**;
- **lwa who govern, receive, escort, or speak for the dead**;
- **healers who can decline a premature death**, especially that of a child;
- **tricksters and truth-speakers** whose license permits ridicule of authority and sexual pretension;
- **figures of regeneration**, because the grave and reproductive sexuality are treated as adjoining rather than contradictory domains.

The family is sometimes called a **fanmi Gede** and sometimes a **nanchon Gede** (“Gede nation”). Benjamin Hebblethwaite argues that both classifications preserve something historically important: the Gede are internally a proliferating family but may also memorialize a displaced West African people and ritual tradition. His 2021 study catalogues more than forty named Gede and analyzes twenty-six recorded Gede songs. [Hebblethwaite, “The Gede Rite in Haiti”](https://academic.oup.com/mississippi-scholarship-online/book/42146/chapter-abstract/356227126)

### Spellings and language

- Haitian Creole: **Gede**, plural normally unmarked.
- French: **Guédé**, **Gédé**, **les Guédés**.
- Older English-language ethnography: **Ghede**, sometimes **Guede**.
- Fon/Gbe-related forms in the historical literature: **Gede**, **Gedevi**.
- **-vi** in Gbe languages means “child” or “descendant”; **Gedevi** can therefore be glossed “children/descendants of Gede.”

No historical connection to Greek **Hades** is supported by linguistic or transmission evidence. The resemblance is accidental; proposed Greek, Latin, or European occult derivations are **[MI]** unless accompanied by evidence unavailable in the present record.

### African etymology and its limits

**[EA/SR]** Le Hérissé’s *L’Ancien Royaume du Dahomey* (1911) records Gede as a vodun and the Gedevi as inhabitants of the Abomey plateau. Later historical traditions describe Gede as a protective divinity represented by a large standing stone and call the Gedevi “children of Gede.” [Le Hérissé bibliographic record](https://ehrafworldcultures.yale.edu/cultures/fa18/documents/005)

A modern Beninese study describes a sacred forest, **Gedezun**, at Gbannyikɔn/Bohicon, containing a temple of Gedè and related vodun, under a **Gedenɔ̀**, “chief priest of Gedè.” It interprets these cults as honors paid to the earlier masters of the land upon which the Alladaxɔnu dynasty established Dahomey. [University of Abomey-Calavi study](https://bec.uac.bj/uploads/publication/068fb50c842cad5d138d1f2108ff621d.pdf)

Two competing reconstructions must remain separate:

1. **Gede-first reconstruction [SR]:** Gede was the ancestral or territorial vodun of a pre-Dahomean population, hence *Gede-vi*, “Gede’s children.”
2. **Ethnonym-first or institutional reconstruction [SR]:** “Gede/Gedevi” designated an autochthonous population or status group associated with the earth and burial; the Haitian spirit family developed from their displaced mortuary identity.

Later oral accounts disagree about whether Dahomean kings captured, adopted, or reassigned Gede, and even about which king did so. A French study concludes explicitly that “l’origine du culte de Gede demeure énigmatique”—“the origin of the Gede cult remains enigmatic”—because the versions do not agree. [Study of the Gede traditions](https://files01.core.ac.uk/download/pdf/52835245.pdf)

The often-repeated statement that the “entire Gedevi population” was sold to Saint-Domingue is too absolute. Dahomean conquest, enslavement, and Atlantic deportation make Gedevi participation in the formation of Haitian Vodou historically plausible, but neither shipping records nor demographic evidence demonstrate total deportation.

### Principal names and titles

The list is open-ended. New names may arise through local dead, lineage revelation, possession, songs, jokes, or the multiplication of functions.

- **Papa Gede / Papa Guédé** — “Father Gede”; sometimes the collective’s paternal manifestation, sometimes a particular psychopomp, sometimes identified with Bawon Samdi. **[LE/RV]**
- **Gede Nibo / Nibo / Nimbo** — among the best-attested individual Gede; associated with untimely or violent death, the young dead, erotic ambiguity, healing, divination, and speech for unrecovered dead. **[LE/RV]**
- **Gede Doub / Doubye / Double** — associated in Métraux-era descriptions with “second sight.” **[LE]**
- **Gede Mazaka / Masaka / Maza Lakwa** — extensively represented in songs; precise personality varies by lineage. **[LE/RV]**
- **Gede Linto** — miracle-worker or childlike/elderly figure in later descriptions. **[RV]**
- **Gede Loray** — “Gede Thunder.” **[RV]**
- **Gede Oussou/Wonsou/Rounsou** — several orthographic forms may conceal separate lineage identities. **[RV]**
- **Gede Ti Malis** — incorporates the folkloric trickster-name Ti Malis. **[RV/F]**
- **Brav Gede** — cemetery guard or vigilant Gede. **[RV]**
- **Gede Nouvavou**, **Gede Kriyòl**, **Gede Drivayè**, **Gede Pikan**, **Gede Ramase**, **Gede Ti Pis Lakwa**, **Mòpyon Lakwa**, **Jipitè Lakwa**, and many others — attested in modern song or local lists, not members of a universally fixed pantheon. **[RV/LE]**

The **Barons/Bawon**—Bawon Samdi, Bawon Lakwa, Bawon Simityè, Bawon Kriminèl, and local forms—may be presented as Gede, rulers of Gede, a related but distinct cemetery group, or offices occupied by the first male burial in a cemetery. **[RV]** Grann Brijit is correspondingly wife of Bawon, mother or queen of the Gede, or female owner of the cemetery.

### Catholic image-associations

**[SI]** Papa Gede or Bawon may be represented by chromolithographs of **Saint Gerard Majella**, **Saint Expeditus**, **Saint Martin de Porres**, or other black-clad or cross-bearing saints. There is no single universal pairing. Desmangles emphasizes that Vodouisants may use a saint’s picture as the visible face or ritual support of a lwa without knowing or adopting the saint’s Catholic biography. The image association is thus not proof that Gede “is really” Gerard or Patrick. [Study of Vodou/Catholic image associations](https://haitianconnection.org/wp-content/uploads/2017/09/syncretismvodou.pdf)

The November festival’s convergence with All Saints’ and All Souls’ Days is a genuine Catholic-Vodou historical conjunction, but whether it should be called disguise, synthesis, parallel observance, or appropriation remains debated.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### West African evidence

**[EA] 1911, Dahomey:** Le Hérissé recorded both Gede traditions and Gedevi historical geography. His evidence came from the recently conquered Kingdom of Dahomey, but it was filtered through a French colonial administrator and printed decades after the events narrated.

**[EA/SR] 1931–38:** Melville and Frances Herskovits conducted fieldwork in Dahomey in 1931 and published *Dahomey: An Ancient West African Kingdom* in 1938. Their work helps document the organization of vodun, royal ancestral cult, territorial deities, and the social memory of conquered peoples. It does not establish that every Haitian Gede trait—top hat, one-lensed glasses, sexual clowning—already existed in Dahomey.

Maupoil’s *La Géomancie à l’ancienne Côte des Esclaves* (1943), Le Hérissé, Mercier, and later Beninese oral histories establish a broad Fon/Gbe religious world in which land, ancestors, conquest, divination, and the incorporation of conquered peoples’ vodun were closely connected. Gede’s Haitian form nevertheless reflects creolization in Saint-Domingue and independent Haiti.

### Haitian printed and ethnographic corpus

**[EA] 1906 — Antoine Innocent, *Mimola*.** A French-language Haitian novel, initially serialized and then published in book form. It is the earliest located printed Haitian text explicitly naming Gede. As a novel, it is evidence for contemporary knowledge and discourse, not a transcript of ritual. A digitized 1906 edition survives. [Open Library/Internet Archive record](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL20555275M/Mimola_ou_L%27histoire_d%27une_cassette_Petit_tableau_de_m%C5%93urs_locales)

**[EA] 1928 — Jean Price-Mars, *Ainsi parla l’oncle*.** Price-Mars established Vodou as a religion and Haitian folklore as legitimate ethnographic subjects. He did not supply a complete Gede mythology, but his work changed the conditions under which Gede could be studied rather than dismissed as “superstition.” [Digital edition](https://www.classiques.uqam.ca/classiques/price_mars_jean/ainsi_parla_oncle/ainsi_parla_oncle_preface.html)

**[EA] 1934/1937 — Melville J. Herskovits, *Life in a Haitian Valley*.** Based on three months’ fieldwork in Mirebalais in 1934. Herskovits documented the cult of twins and the dead, Gede’s justice, relations between Gede and Bawon, offerings, dance, and possession. HRAF identifies 1934 as the field date and rates the volume highly as primary ethnography. [HRAF record](https://ehrafworldcultures.yale.edu/cultures/sv03/documents/001) His silent film footage also preserves a 1934 **banda** dance, although the catalog does not identify it exclusively as a Gede rite. [Smithsonian film record](https://collections.si.edu/search/detail/ead_component%3Asova-hsfa-1977-01-ref2?print=yes)

**[EA/LE] 1947 — Laura Boulton recordings and transcript.** A Vodou ceremony recorded in Haiti includes liturgy, songs, and speech by possessed participants, including Gede. The transcription now belongs to the Indiana University Archives of Traditional Music corpus and was edited in *Vodou Songs in Haitian Creole and English*. [Corpus description](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt14bt8rx)

**[LE] 1950 — Milo Marcelin, *Mythologie vodou (Rite Arada)*.** A foundational Haitian compilation of ritual songs and theological classifications. Its songs recur in later Max Beauvoir collections, demonstrating circulation rather than a single-author literary invention.

**[LE] 1953 — Maya Deren, *Divine Horsemen: The Living Gods of Haiti*.** Based on fieldwork begun in 1947. Its chapter “Ghede—Corpse and Phallus; King and Clown” remains the fullest classic portrayal of Gede’s death/sexuality polarity. Deren records ritual action, songs, possession, a child-healing episode, the sunglasses, nasal voice, obscenity, and the grave/womb symbolism. Her structural comparisons—particularly Gede as Legba transformed by death—are her own interpretive synthesis.

**[LE] 1958/1959 — Alfred Métraux, *Le Vaudou haïtien* / *Voodoo in Haiti*.** Based substantially on UNESCO-era Marbial research and extensive consultation with Haitian practitioners and intellectuals. Métraux documents Gede possession, obscenity, cemetery rites, individual names, and the annual cycle. His work remains indispensable but is not a Vodou scripture.

**[LE] 1991/2001 — Karen McCarthy Brown, *Mama Lola: A Vodou Priestess in Brooklyn*.** A long-term collaborative ethnography of Alourdes/Mama Lola’s Haitian and Brooklyn religious world. It documents Gede in a lineage and diaspora setting, where sexual candor, humor, healing, and the realities of migration are inseparable.

**[LE] 2008 — Max Beauvoir’s song and liturgy collections.** These preserve a large practitioner-curated corpus. They should not be treated as a universally binding “Vodou Bible”; their authority is substantial but situated.

**[LE] 2012 — Hebblethwaite and Bartley, *Vodou Songs in Haitian Creole and English*.** Critical bilingual edition bringing together songs collected by Jacques Roumain, Werner Jaegerhuber, Price-Mars, Marcelin, Boulton, and later recordists.

**[LE] 2021 — Benjamin Hebblethwaite, *A Transatlantic History of Haitian Vodou*.** Its Gede chapter combines historical linguistics, West African political history, fieldwork, and twenty-six songs recorded by Rasin Bwa Kayiman. Subjects include children, Gede Nibo, Bawon Kriminèl, sex work, AIDS, evangelical conflict, obscenity, and healing. [Book record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv20hcrkg)

### What colonial sources do not show

Moreau de Saint-Méry’s 1797 description of the *Vaudoux* dance and serpent cult is crucial evidence for colonial Afro-Creole religion, but it does **not securely name Gede**. The Bois Caïman evidence of 1791 likewise concerns revolutionary assembly, oath, sacrifice, and competing reconstructions of the officiants; no contemporary document securely places Gede at Bois Caïman. Assigning Gede a specific role there is **[MI]** unless a lineage explicitly presents it as later sacred history, in which case it is **[F/LE]**, not contemporary 1791 documentation.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Death, fertility, and the boundary

**[EA/LE]** Gede govern the transition from living body to dead ancestor, but they are not simply embodiments of annihilation. Their sexuality and laughter insist that death belongs to the same cycle as conception and birth. The cosmological formulation “tomb and womb” is particularly associated with Deren.

Gede may:

- judge whether a death is timely or deserved;
- escort or receive the dead;
- reveal information from the dead;
- speak for persons whose bodies were lost or unrecovered;
- guard graves and cemetery boundaries;
- heal persons near death;
- protect children;
- diagnose hidden causes of illness;
- expose lies or hypocrisy;
- prophesy, joke, insult, demand payment, or correct misconduct during possession.

These functions vary. No central ecclesiastical authority fixes a universal portfolio.

### Possession persona

A Gede possession is commonly marked by some combination of:

- a high, congested, or nasal voice, evoking a corpse whose nose has been plugged;
- cotton in the nostrils;
- face whitened with powder or flour;
- dark or purple clothing;
- top hat, undertaker’s coat, cane, cigar, pipe, or cigarettes;
- dark glasses, sometimes with one lens missing;
- exaggerated hip and pelvic movements;
- sexual songs and jokes;
- consumption or bodily application of peppered clairin.

**[LE]** The one-lensed glasses are often interpreted as liminality: one eye toward the living and one toward the dead, or impaired corpse-sight combined with supernatural sight. The practical explanation preserved in some performance accounts is that the corpse is unaccustomed to the bright realm of the living. No early West African source has been found for this accessory. It is therefore a Haitian development documented securely in twentieth-century performance, not an “ancient Dahomean symbol.”

### Banda

**[EA/LE]** The **banda** is both a rhythmic/dance complex and a defining Gede performance. Pelvic thrusting, hip rotations, mock copulation, and comic reversals belong to its ritual vocabulary. Herskovits filmed a banda in 1934. The dance is not reducible to entertainment: it embodies the Gede conjunction of death, reproductive power, ridicule, and social release.

### Sexual speech

**[LE]** Gede sexual language ranges from double entendre to explicit descriptions of genital acts and fluids. It can ridicule prudery, expose adultery, challenge elite respectability, comment on sexuality and disease, or simply delight the congregation. Hebblethwaite’s recorded song corpus includes oral sex, sex work, genital secretions, lice, AIDS, and **betiz** (“obscenity/vulgar talk”). Sanitized accounts that call Gede merely “cheerful” erase a defining part of the record.

Gede Nibo is sometimes described as effeminate, gender-crossing, bisexual, or associated with *masisi* identities. **[RV/LE]** Such descriptions have ethnographic and practitioner support in some communities, but sweeping claims that Nibo is universally “the gay lwa” collapse varied Haitian sexual categories and lineage teachings into modern identity labels.

### Colors, substances, days, and offerings

Commonly attested correspondences include:

- **Colors:** black, purple/violet, white; sometimes red in particular forms.
- **Day:** Saturday for Bawon/Gede in many houses; 1–2 November for the collective festival.
- **Places:** cemetery, grave, cemetery cross, crossroads at or near burial grounds.
- **Food:** roasted or salted maize, cassava, peanuts, bread, black coffee, cooked foods, and lineage-specific dishes.
- **Drink:** clairin, often steeped with hot peppers; rum; coffee.
- **Other offerings:** tobacco, candles, coins, flowers, perfume or Florida Water.
- **Animals:** black chickens and goats occur in twentieth-century ethnographic accounts; prescriptions vary by house and ceremony.
- **Symbols:** cross, coffin, grave, skull, phallus, cane, top hat, eyeglasses.

There is no secure evidence for a universally sacred Gede plant, planet, or number. The frequently cited **twenty-one peppers** belongs to later ritual recipes and practitioner accounts, not a demonstrated ancient numerology.

### Vèvè and images

**[EA/LE]** Gede/Bawon vèvè characteristically organize crosses, coffins, graves, and symmetrical flourishes around a vertical axis. Variation is normal; vèvè are transmitted, redrawn, and elaborated by ritual specialists rather than governed by a single canonical chart. A cross-centered Gede vèvè was published by Deren, and later drapo Vodou often depict Gede through sequined crosses, coffins, skulls, or dark-glassed figures.

The Birmingham Museum of Art preserves a Haitian **Vodou Flag or Banner (Guede)**, demonstrating the movement of Gede imagery from ritual commissions into museum collections. [Museum object](https://www.artsbma.org/collection/vodou-flag-or-banner-guede/)

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Competing family structures

1. **Bawon Samdi and Grann Brijit as parents [LE/RV].** Widely reported in later ethnography and practitioner teaching: Bawon is father/king, Brijit mother/queen, and the Gede are their innumerable children.
2. **Gede above or distinct from Bawon [EA/RV].** Herskovits’s Mirebalais account sharply distinguishes just Gede from a more dangerous Bawon who may seek a death; Gede can refuse to dig the grave.
3. **Bawon as the principal Gede [LE/RV].** Deren observed that many practitioners completely identified Gede and Bawon Samdi, while others maintained only a slight distinction.
4. **Bawon as cemetery office [RV].** In some teachings the first man buried in a cemetery becomes its Bawon and the first woman its Brijit, making the titles local offices rather than a single universal couple.
5. **Gede Nibo as adopted son [F/RV].** A later story says Nibo was a handsome youth who died violently and was adopted by Bawon and Brijit. No early source fixes the details or identifies a historical youth.

None should be silently substituted for the others.

### Every documented divine interaction

Vodou has few long narrative myths comparable to Greek epics. Most interactions occur in ritual order, songs, possession testimony, and genealogical teaching.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The first dead

**[F/LE]** Papa Gede is sometimes said to be the first human being who died, or the corpse of that first man. This makes him the opener of the road to the ancestral realm. No securely dated colonial text contains the story, and it is absent from the presently located African Gede evidence. It should be treated as a later Haitian oral explanation of office, not primordial scripture.

### Gede Nibo’s violent death

**[F/RV]** Nibo is said to have been a beautiful young man murdered or killed prematurely. Bawon Samdi and Grann Brijit adopt him after death; he therefore protects persons who die young, violently, anonymously, or before their bodies can be recovered. Versions disagree about his age, manner of death, sexuality, and exact relationship to the Barons. The story’s ethical center—making a socially unfinished death speak—is more stable than its plot.

### The just refusal of death

**[EA/F]** Herskovits records a Mirebalais teaching rather than a continuous tale: Bawon may desire or arrange someone’s death, but Gede, who must bury the victim, refuses if the person does not deserve to die. This establishes Gede as a limit upon arbitrary death. It conflicts productively with traditions that make Bawon and Gede the same entity.

### The healer of the child

**[EA as ethnographic testimony; F as miracle claim]** Deren reports that a gravely ill girl, unresponsive to medical, herbal, and ritual treatment, was placed on a replica tomb during a strong Gede ceremony. Chickens and a black goat were sacrificed. A possessed manbo, speaking as Gede, anointed the child with goat’s blood and a life-giving fluid represented as semen despite the medium’s female body; the child survived. The event is evidence that participants understood Gede as capable of returning the generative life he had consumed. It does not permit independent medical verification of cause or cure.

This episode is the strongest classic source for the often-repeated statement that Gede heal and protect children. Deren explicitly calls Gede “guardian of children.” [Digitized excerpt](https://www.scribd.com/document/635026538/Untitled)

### The grave as womb

**[SR grounded in ritual symbolism]** Deren interprets Gede’s phallus, corpse-persona, and cemetery as a single cycle: the cosmic abyss is both tomb and womb. This is not a separately narrated Haitian creation myth. It is an interpretive reconstruction from ritual behavior, songs, and symbolism.

### The multiplying dead

**[LE/RV]** A common theological principle is that the Gede family continually grows because human beings continue to die. Some honored or spiritually forceful dead may acquire Gede names or identities. This explains the family’s unstable size and locally generated members better than closed modern “pantheon lists.”

### Gede as social clown

**[LE]** Gede humiliates pretension by parodying priests, foreigners, elites, sexual propriety, and sometimes the congregation itself. Deren observed that white visitors—especially those displaying puritan embarrassment—could provoke stronger obscenity. The clowning is not mere comic relief: it is ritualized speech from beings whom death has placed beyond normal shame and rank.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Cult centers and material places

Gede has no single Haitian headquarters analogous to an ancient state temple. The primary institutions are:

- **ounfò/peristil** temples and domestic altars;
- **lakou**, extended religious-family compounds;
- cemetery crosses and tombs;
- the **Grand Cemetery of Port-au-Prince**;
- Drouillard cemetery serving Cité Soleil;
- regional cemeteries, crossroads, markets, and household altars;
- diasporic temples in New York, Montreal, Miami, Boston, and New Orleans.

The central cemetery cross is commonly treated as Bawon’s locus; Brijit may receive service at a grave, mound, or adjacent cross. The first burial of the relevant sex may supply the local Bawon or Brijit in some lineages.

### Fèt Gede

**[LE/SI]** Fèt Gede is observed principally on **1 and 2 November**, coinciding with the Roman Catholic feasts of All Saints and All Souls. Some services extend to 3 November or occupy a broader November season.

Typical practices include:

- cleaning and visiting graves;
- offering coffee, food, candles, flowers, clairin, and tobacco;
- pouring drink upon graves or cemetery crosses;
- wearing black, white, and purple;
- white-powdering the face;
- singing Gede songs and playing banda rhythms;
- processions from neighborhoods or temples;
- possession by Gede or Bawon;
- divination, healing advice, prophecy, discipline, and comic obscenity;
- sacrifice in temple contexts, followed by preparation or distribution of meat.

The observance is simultaneously mourning, reunion, public carnival, ancestral service, and a demonstration that death has not severed social relations.

### Cité Soleil and Drouillard

**[LE/RV]** “The Gede of Cité Soleil” is best understood as an intensely visible urban ritual formation, not a separately attested ancient Gede species. Drouillard cemetery serves communities whose dead include people lost to poverty, political violence, gang conflict, disaster, and inadequate burial. Processions move through Cité Soleil’s narrow streets toward the cemetery; possession becomes increasingly common near the graves.

A 1994 report quotes the Cité Soleil oungan Thenmyl Selem describing Gede’s groin-rotating dance, nasal vulgar speech, and pepper placed in the eyes or on the genitals of possessed people. His shrine combined Bawon’s cross with dolls, a damaged Christ statue, a black baby doll smoking a pipe, a melted television, and a toy shark—an important example of urban sacred assemblage rather than a fixed iconographic canon. [Archived report](https://nara-media.s3.amazonaws.com/presidential-libraries/clinton/foia/2006-0885-F-Seg-1/2006-0885-F-Seg-1-PDF/Box_055/42-t-12092985-20060885F-Seg1-055-004-2015.pdf)

Photographs from 2018 document devotees around Bawon Samdi’s tomb at Cité Soleil cemetery, faces powdered white and bodies dressed in Gede colors. [AP photographic report](https://avax.news/pictures/330392)

A 2025 Haitian report records continued observance at Drouillard, with tomb cleaning, candles, drums, food, coffee, clairin, possession, and strong participation by young people. [Gazette Haïti](https://www.gazettehaiti.com/index.php/node/12978)

### The pepper test

**[LE/RV]** Possessed people may drink very hot peppered clairin or rub it upon sensitive skin. Participants may understand the apparent immunity as proof that the Gede, rather than the ordinary human, is acting. Degree and method vary; spectacular journalistic photographs should not be mistaken for a universal requirement.

### Healing and children

**[EA/LE]** Gede’s authority over death makes them logical petitioners when a child or adult is near death: if the assigned time has not arrived, Gede may release or restore the patient. Ritual treatment may coexist with biomedical treatment, herbalism, baths, sacrifice, prayer, or divination. Deren’s child-healing account is the foundational published example; contemporary practice continues to describe the Gede as especially attentive to children.

### Sacrifice and allegations of human sacrifice

**Animal sacrifice [EA/LE]:** chickens, goats, pigs, or other animals may be offered according to house, means, and divinatory instruction. The animal commonly becomes communal food. Deren specifically recorded chickens and a black goat in a Gede healing ceremony.

**Human sacrifice [absence of evidence]:** No reliable source documents human sacrifice as a normative Gede ritual. Colonial and occupation-era “Voodoo cannibalism” literature often converted criminal cases, rumor, racial fantasy, or accusations of sorcery into claims about Vodou generally. Such allegations cannot be transferred to Gede without specific evidence. Bois Caïman’s reported pig sacrifice does not constitute evidence of human sacrifice, nor does it establish Gede’s presence.

**Temple prostitution:** No institution of Gede “temple prostitution” has been established. A report that Grann Brijit was “formerly a ritual prostitute” belongs to a particular scholarly/informant genealogy and should not be generalized into an institution. Sexual performance, sex work mentioned in songs, and cult prostitution are separate categories.

### Clergy and offices

Oungan, manbo, oungenikon, drummers, initiates, flag-bearers, and cemetery ritual specialists may all serve Gede. There is no exclusive centralized Gede priesthood in Haiti. In Benin, by contrast, the title **Gedenɔ̀** is reported for the chief priest of a surviving Gedè shrine.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the seventeenth century: Abomey plateau

**[SR from later oral history]** Gedevi communities occupied parts of the Abomey plateau before the consolidation of Dahomey. They were remembered as “owners of the earth” and were connected with territorial and mortuary responsibilities. Exact dates and ethnic classification remain disputed.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: conquest and Atlantic displacement

**[SR]** Aja-Fon dynastic expansion absorbed, subordinated, dispersed, or enslaved Gedevi populations. Mortuary and earth offices may have become an imposed but sacralized social specialization. Captives from the Bight of Benin entered Saint-Domingue in large numbers. The Gede complex plausibly emerged from this history, but its specifically Haitian clothing, Catholic calendar, speech, humor, and family structure developed in the Caribbean.

### 1791 and the Haitian Revolution

Vodou rites, oaths, healing, divination, and political communication participated in revolutionary society, but claims that Bois Caïman founded all Haitian Vodou or that Gede was specifically invoked there exceed the contemporary evidence. Later revolutionary memory can legitimately place the ancestral dead within national sacred history; that is **[F/LE]**, not a recovered 1791 transcript.

### Nineteenth century

Afro-Haitian religion survived under a state formally oriented toward Catholicism and under recurrent criminalization of *sortilèges*, assemblies, and healing practices. Gede almost certainly circulated orally, but named nineteenth-century documentation remains thin. This archival silence reflects elite hostility, oral transmission, and the selectivity of observers; it cannot establish absence.

### 1906–1930s: emergence into print and ethnography

*Mimola* (1906) supplies the earliest located named literary evidence. Price-Mars’s indigénisme made African-derived religion a legitimate element of Haitian national culture. Herskovits’s 1934 fieldwork then produced the first sustained ethnographic dossier.

### 1941–42: the anti-superstition campaign

**[EA]** Catholic clergy, supported by President Élie Lescot’s government and police, destroyed ritual objects, cut sacred trees, compelled oaths renouncing lwa, and attacked ounfò. Gede shrines and paraphernalia were vulnerable as part of Vodou generally, although surviving reports rarely inventory destruction spirit by spirit. Métraux later described the campaign as the context for UNESCO-supported ethnographic attention at Marbial. [Métraux’s Marbial article](https://www.persee.fr/doc/jsa_0037-9174_1953_num_42_1_2404?pageId=T1_137)

### 1947–59: classic documentation

Deren, Boulton, Métraux, Marcelin, Rigaud, and Haitian collaborators produced the corpus from which most international descriptions still derive. Their accounts agree on cemetery symbolism, obscene humor, dance, possession, and the life/death conjunction but differ on genealogy and classification.

### 1957–86: Duvalierism

**[EA/MR]** François Duvalier strategically adopted the visual rhetoric of Bawon Samdi—black suit, dark glasses, grave voice—and used Vodou-associated symbolism within authoritarian nationalism. This did not make Duvalier Bawon or Gede in Vodou theology, nor does it show that Vodou as a whole supported the regime. Priests and practitioners occupied every political position, including resistance. Popular culture’s fusion of “Papa Doc,” Bawon, zombies, and the Tonton Macoutes is partly historical political theater and partly foreign sensationalism.

### Diaspora and New Orleans

Haitian migration carried Gede rites into New York, Miami, Montreal, Boston, and New Orleans. In New Orleans, the evidence requires a firm distinction:

- Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century Louisiana Voodoo developed from local African, Afro-Creole, Catholic, medicinal, and later Spiritualist currents.
- The Louisiana Writers’ Project interviews and Robert Tallant’s *Voodoo in New Orleans* preserve important twentieth-century testimony but do not establish a historic, continuously named Gede cult under Marie Laveau.
- Tallant’s narrative is riddled with sensationalism and uncritical folklore.
- Publicly named Gede worship in modern New Orleans is largely associated with Haitian influence and the late-twentieth-century Voodoo revival.

Carolyn Morrow Long concludes that organized nineteenth-century New Orleans Voodoo had been heavily suppressed and that many modern public groups represent revival and recombination rather than unbroken institutional continuity. The Voodoo Spiritual Temple was founded in 1990; La Source Ancienne and botanica-based practice also incorporated Haitian lwa. [Long’s research profile](https://carolynmorrowlong.com/about-me/)

**[EA/LE] 2005–06:** At Big Chief Allison “Tootie” Montana’s jazz funeral, Bruce “Sunpie” Barnes performed Bawon Samdi/Gede in a procession joining Haitian Vodou, jazz-funeral practice, and Mardi Gras Indian memory. Richard Brent Turner and Anne Hastings analyzed the event as “The Gede in New Orleans,” not as proof that identical rites had persisted unchanged from Marie Laveau’s century. [Article index](https://aio.therai.org.uk/advanced-search/etFFABHXlCPXRoqQv1pYiWfSLtU6Ld0B?page=4)

### 1987–2003: legal change

The 1987 Haitian Constitution guaranteed freedom of religion. On 4 April 2003, President Jean-Bertrand Aristide’s decree recognized Vodou as “a religion in its own right” and allowed its religious leaders to seek state recognition. [Text of the decree](https://www.haitianaute.com/2021/11/arrete-presidentiel-du-4-avril-2003-de.html)

### Present

Fèt Gede remains publicly visible in Haiti and the diaspora. Practice continues amid Protestant condemnation, economic crisis, gang violence, migration, commercialization, museum collection, and social media. The living tradition is neither a museum survival nor a single standardized church.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Ethnographic film and art

Maya Deren’s footage, posthumously edited into the film *Divine Horsemen: The Living Gods of Haiti* (1985), made possession imagery internationally influential. It is documentary art assembled after Deren’s death, not an unmediated recording of an entire rite.

Haitian painters, flag makers, and sculptors depict Bawon and Gede through crosses, skulls, mirrored glasses, discarded machinery, dolls, coffins, and urban debris. The **Atis Rezistans**/Grand Rue artists made death imagery and recycled urban matter central to a contemporary Haitian aesthetic; this is living artistic theology and political ecology, not “primitive” survival. A major review notes monumental Bawon sculpture and assemblage practices in Port-au-Prince. [Review of *In Extremis*](https://www.caareviews.org/reviews/2480)

### Music

Vodou sacred songs remain a primary theological medium. Rasin bands place Gede language in recordings about poverty, sexuality, AIDS, evangelical hostility, and political life. Rasin Bwa Kayiman’s *Guede* (2004), analyzed by Hebblethwaite, is a modern recorded corpus rooted in ritual performance rather than a pop invention.

### Literature

Gede appears or provides symbolic structure in Haitian and diasporic literature, including Frankétienne’s visually vèvè-like writing, René Depestre’s *Hadriana dans tous mes rêves* (1988), and works dealing with zombies, revolution, sexuality, and the ancestral dead. Literary Gede should be analyzed as reception rather than silently quoted as ritual doctrine.

### Popular culture

Bawon Samdi—often mislabeled “Papa Gede”—has become a stock character in films, comics, television, role-playing games, and videogames. Typical alterations include:

- making him a solitary “voodoo god of death”;
- treating all Gede as zombies;
- recasting Vodou as necromancy;
- replacing lineage ritual with European ceremonial magic;
- confusing Haitian Vodou with Louisiana hoodoo, Santería, Palo, or invented “voodoo dolls.”

These are **[MR/MI]** unless a work explicitly engages practitioner sources. The commercial top-hatted skull owes more to twentieth-century Bawon iconography and entertainment than to the older West African Gede evidence.

### Occult and neopagan appropriation

Unlike Egyptian or Greco-Roman gods, Gede had little established role in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, or classical European grimoires. Claims that Gede belongs to an ancient Hermetic planetary hierarchy are **[MI]**. Late-twentieth- and twenty-first-century occult manuals sometimes detach Papa Gede or Bawon Samdi from Haitian houses and offer solitary “invocations.” These are modern Western esoteric adaptations, not documented historic Vodou procedure.

Some non-Haitian practitioners have entered legitimate lineages through long-term relationships and initiation; ethnicity alone does not decide authenticity. The evidentiary distinction is institutional and historical: accountable lineage transmission differs from self-authorization through books or social media.

### Tourism and commerce

New Orleans “voodoo shops,” Haitian art markets, festival photography, and online spirit merchandise create new public Gede images. Marie Laveau’s House of Voodoo, opened in 1978, and comparable businesses belong to modern spiritual commerce, not Marie Laveau’s personal nineteenth-century establishment. Commercial presence can support living practice, exploit it, or do both.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### African survival versus Haitian creation

Herskovits emphasized African “retentions”; later scholarship emphasizes transformation, power, and creolization. The strongest current position is not that Gede is either purely African or invented in Haiti. The name, historical memory, mortuary functions, and possible Gedevi connection have West African foundations; the documented Haitian family is a Caribbean formation.

Andrew Apter cautions that both practitioners’ claims about “Ginen” and scholars’ ethnic maps can be ideological as well as historical. His work argues for African generative principles without reducing Vodou to unchanged tribal survivals. [Apter, “On African Origins”](https://anthrosource.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1525/ae.2002.29.2.233)

### Who are the Gedevi?

They have been variously described as Yoruba, proto-Yoruba, autochthonous plateau people, earth owners, conquered subjects, or a ritual status group. The sources are late and politically interested. Dahomean dynastic histories could absorb indigenous ancestors to legitimate rule; colonial ethnographers could over-stabilize fluid identities. “Gedevi-Yoruba” is therefore a reasoned modern classification, not an uncontested self-description preserved from antiquity.

### Family or nation?

Métraux-era systematization often places Gede within or after Rada/Petwo sequences. Practitioners may call them a family, a nation, a rite, or all three at different levels. Treating these as mutually exclusive categories imposes an artificial taxonomy on a flexible ritual system.

### Gede versus Bawon

This is the clearest internal dispute. Herskovits distinguishes them strongly; Deren reports both identity and slight distinction; later accounts often place the Barons at the head of the family. No harmonization adequately preserves the evidence. House and region must be specified.

### Syncretism

Older scholarship often proposed that enslaved Africans concealed lwa “behind” Catholic saints. Desmangles instead emphasizes **symbiosis**: Catholic pictures, prayers, and feast days may be incorporated into an African-derived system without the lwa losing their identity. More recent scholarship further observes that Catholic-African synthesis had already occurred in the Kingdom of Kongo before Atlantic deportation. Thus not every Catholic element originated as Haitian camouflage.

### Sex, gender, and interpretation

Deren read Gede’s sexuality cosmologically; later feminist and queer scholarship examines possession as room for gender crossing and speech otherwise socially constrained. Both approaches illuminate the evidence, but neither converts every obscene joke into a fixed doctrine of sexual liberation. Gede can disrupt norms and still operate inside particular households with their own restrictions.

### The politics of death

Scholars have connected Gede’s popularity to slavery, revolutionary ancestry, state violence, dictatorship, AIDS, earthquake death, migration, and urban precarity. These are historically grounded interpretive contexts, but claims that Gede was “invented by Duvalier” are false: the printed and ethnographic record predates his presidency by decades.

### Archives and losses

Important collections include:

- the Melville J. and Frances S. Herskovits papers at the Schomburg Center;
- Herskovits’s 1934 film at the Smithsonian Human Studies Film Archives;
- Laura Boulton materials at Indiana University’s Archives of Traditional Music;
- Maya Deren papers and film materials;
- Musée du Panthéon National Haïtien and Haitian ethnological collections;
- Musée du quai Branly and other collections holding Haitian ritual objects;
- Fowler Museum and private drapo collections;
- Beninese royal-palace, shrine, and oral-history records.

Losses resulted from tropical deterioration, political upheaval, the 2010 earthquake, private dispersal, anti-Vodou destruction, and the removal of ritual objects into foreign collections.

## 11. Sources: assessment and conclusions

The recoverable history does not support a biography of a single god. It supports the history of a **collective religious institution of the dead**, continually remade through enslavement, burial, song, possession, migration, political terror, healing, and humor.

The most defensible synthesis is:

1. A West African Gede/Gedevi complex connected with Abomey, land, conquest, ancestors, and mortuary offices is securely documented only from the early twentieth century but preserves older historical memory.
2. The Haitian name is printed by 1906 and thoroughly documented from 1934 onward.
3. The Haitian Gede are neither a transparent copy of a single Dahomean vodun nor a twentieth-century invention.
4. Gede’s defining Haitian traits—cemetery rule, reproductive sexuality, clowning, nasal corpse-speech, banda, healing, children, peppered clairin, dark glasses, and the November feast—form a coherent twentieth-century and living complex, though individual traits vary by house.
5. The relationship between Gede, Bawon Samdi, Grann Brijit, and Gede Nibo is genuinely variable; no single genealogy supersedes all others.
6. No reliable evidence supports normative human sacrifice, ancient Hermetic Gede worship, derivation from Greek Hades, or an unbroken named Gede cult under Marie Laveau.
7. Cité Soleil’s Gede practice represents a major contemporary urban expression shaped by poverty, violent death, communal memory, and the Drouillard cemetery—not a separate timeless pantheon.
8. New Orleans’ publicly named Gede rites are best documented as Haitian-diasporic and revival-era formations, although they operate within a much older Louisiana African-Creole religious landscape.
9. Gede’s obscenity is not incidental: it is a religious language through which the dead mock status, expose the body’s universality, and affirm life at the grave.
10. Absence of a published myth is not absence of living knowledge. It is, however, a limit on what can responsibly be called universally canonical.
