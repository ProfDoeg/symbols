# Simbi: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** In Haitian Vodou, **Simbi is a family or class of lwa**, often individualized as named members such as Simbi Dlo, Simbi Andezo, and Simbi Makaya. They are especially associated with fresh water, springs, serpents, esoteric knowledge, healing, and ritual power. In Kongo-speaking Central Africa, the cognate **simbi** (plural **bisimbi/basimbi**) denotes a category of local or nature spirits rather than one universally individualized god.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The oldest securely dated documentary background is seventeenth-century Kikongo lexicography and missionary documentation from the Kingdom of Kongo. The *Vocabularium Latinum, Hispanicum et Congense*, compiled principally at Mbanza Kongo around 1648 and preserved in Joris van Gheel’s 1652 manuscript, records the linguistic world in which *simbi*-related terms occur. The manuscript is now Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale di Roma, Fondo Minori 1896, MS Varia 274. It does not furnish a Haitian Simbi myth or prove that every later Haitian attribute was already present. For **Simbi as a Haitian lwa**, the earliest secure, presently located published attestation is Melville J. Herskovits’s fieldwork from Mirebalais, conducted in 1934 and published in *Life in a Haitian Valley* in 1937, pp. 151–52. Earlier colonial descriptions of Saint-Domingue, including Moreau de Saint-Méry’s 1797 account, document African-derived dances and religion but have not yielded a secure named Simbi attestation.

**Core historical finding:** The name and much of the underlying category are demonstrably **Kongo/West-Central African**, not specifically Dahomean/Fon. Haitian Vodou as a whole contains major Fon and other West African strata, but Herskovits’s *Dahomey*, Le Hérissé, Maupoil, and other major Fon corpora do not establish a corresponding Fon vodun named Simbi. Calling Simbi “the Dahomean vodun behind the Haitian lwa” conflates the wider formation of Vodou with this particular spirit family.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Simbi as category and individual

**[Securely attested: twentieth-century Haitian ethnography]** Haitian sources use *Simbi* in two overlapping ways:

1. as a collective family of related lwa;
2. as an apparently singular spirit when a song, possession account, or ritual description does not specify an epithet.

This is not merely inconsistent terminology. Vodou names can designate a family, a ritual “point,” or a locally individualized manifestation. Different houses may therefore treat two Simbi names as separate lwa, related “points” of one lwa, or members of one family. No centralized Vodou authority fixes a universal list.

Spellings include **Simbi**, **Sim’bi**, and older French or English transcriptions. Kikongo singular **simbi** contrasts with plural **bisimbi** or **basimbi**, the prefixes *bi-* and *ba-* marking plurals in different Bantu noun-class treatments. Haitian Creole normally uses *Simbi* without preserving that grammatical distinction.

### Etymology

**[Secure linguistic relationship; exact derivation disputed]** The Haitian name is overwhelmingly connected with Kikongo and neighboring Kongo-language forms *simbi/bisimbi*. Wyatt MacGaffey’s comparative study treats the Kongo–Haiti relationship as strong but warns that similar labels do not make the two religious categories perfectly identical.

A frequently repeated etymology derives *simbi* from Kikongo **-simba**, “hold, grasp, keep, preserve.” This is semantically plausible and supported by later dictionaries, but it should not be treated as a demonstrated one-step derivation without dialectal and historical qualification. Popular claims that the word simply means “guardian” compress several related expressions and later interpretations into a dictionary gloss.

### Principal Haitian names

- **Simbi Dlo** — Haitian Creole, “Simbi of the water.” **[Later canonical ethnographic designation]** A water-centered manifestation associated with springs, rivers, pools, and aquatic ritual space.
- **Simbi Andezo / Andejo / Yandezo** — orthography varies. **[Securely attested by 1950s ritual documentation]** The name occurs in songs, a vèvè, and Odette Mennesson-Rigaud’s documented Petwo service. The popular etymology “of two waters,” often expanded to mean the meeting of salt and fresh water, is **not securely demonstrated** by the early published sources and may be folk analysis.
- **Simbi Makaya / Makaia** — **[Securely attested twentieth-century Haitian tradition]** A Simbi associated with the Makaya complex, leaves, medicines, secret knowledge, and forceful magical work. *Makaya* is both a Kongo-related ritual term and a Haitian place-name; treating either meaning as its exclusive origin goes beyond the evidence.
- **Simbi Anpaka**, **Simbi Ganga**, **Simbi Gran Simba**, **Simbi La Flambo**, **Simbi Twa Zile**, and other names occur in practitioner lists and local liturgies. **[Regional or lineage variants]** Their identities and relationships cannot be standardized from the available comparative record.
- **Simbi nan dlo** — “Simbi in the water,” a recurrent song formulation.
- **Simbi nan lapli** — “Simbi in the rain.” **[Folklore/modern oral-liturgical formulation]** Rain belongs naturally to an expanded fresh-water domain, and modern summaries call Simbi ruler of rainfall, but no early primary text located here establishes “Simbi in the Rain” as an ancient, universally separate lwa.

### Classification

- **Kongo:** *simbi* is a locality or nature spirit, often attached to water, rocks, ravines, pools, territory, fertility, or an extraordinary dead person. **[Ethnographically attested category]**
- **Haiti:** Simbi is a lwa or family of lwa. Many are placed in Kongo, Petwo, Makaya, or mixed ritual series; assignments differ by house. **[Regional/lineage variation]**
- **Popular English occultism:** “water god,” “snake god,” “demon,” or “elemental.” **[Modern systematization]** These labels are not neutral translations of either *simbi* or *lwa*. “Demon” is especially misleading unless reporting hostile Christian polemic.
- **Gender:** Many sources use masculine pronouns for particular Haitian Simbis, but the wider Kongo and Haitian categories are not securely restricted to one sex. Claims of a universally male Simbi or a fixed female “Simbi Yandezo” are lineage-specific or modern.

### Catholic and other identifications

**[Syncretistic identifications; variable and non-canonical]** Modern tables variously associate Simbi manifestations with **Saint Andrew**, **Saint Raphael**, **Moses striking the rock**, or the **Three Magi/Three Kings**. These are not interchangeable ancient identities:

- Saint Andrew associations appear especially with Simbi Andezo in practitioner traditions, possibly aided by the name’s sound.
- Saint Raphael, an archangel linked with healing and water in the Book of Tobit, supplies an iconographically suitable image in some houses.
- Moses striking water from the rock expresses Simbi’s mastery of springs and concealed water.
- The Three Kings express wisdom, foreign origin, precious gifts, and—especially in modern explanation—mastery of secret or herbal arts.

No evidence located in colonial Saint-Domingue sources fixes any one of these as Simbi’s original Catholic counterpart.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Kongo antecedents

The 1652 *Vocabularium Congense* is the oldest surviving Bantu dictionary. It was assembled through collaboration involving the Kongo-born cleric Manuel de Roboredo and Capuchin missionaries and copied by Joris van Gheel. Its value is linguistic and contextual: it demonstrates an early written Kikongo vocabulary, but later quotations of phrases such as *isimba ia nsi* must be checked against manuscript foliation or the heavily revised 1928 Van Wing–Penders edition before being used as direct seventeenth-century definitions of the spirit category.

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century Kongo missionary and ethnographic records supply the fuller religious descriptions later synthesized by Janzen and MacGaffey. In these sources, bisimbi may inhabit springs, streams, pools, rocks, gullies, and politically or agriculturally important places. Some are understood as exceptional dead persons transformed into enduring territorial powers. Ritual experts serving them are described as *banganga simbi*.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** MacGaffey’s “Twins, Simbi Spirits, and Lwas in Kongo and Haiti” is the most focused comparative treatment. It argues for historically meaningful continuities but rejects trait-matching that strips words and practices from their local structures.

### Colonial Saint-Domingue

Moreau de Saint-Méry’s *Description… de la partie française de l’isle Saint-Domingue* (1797) is indispensable for colonial dance, racial classification, ritual suppression, and the broader environment from which Haitian Vodou emerged. It does **not**, in the searchable record consulted, name Simbi. Likewise, the surviving materials concerning the August 1791 Bois Caïman gathering do not securely name Simbi.

**Finding:** Claims that Simbi presided at Bois Caïman, authorized the Haitian Revolution, or appears in an original Bois Caïman liturgy are **modern invention or unsupported retrospective tradition**, unless a lineage supplies a separately datable source. David Geggus’s treatment of the fragmentary and conflicting Bois Caïman evidence provides no basis for inserting Simbi.

### Haitian ethnography and ritual records

- **Melville J. Herskovits, *Life in a Haitian Valley* (1937), pp. 151–52.** Fieldwork in Mirebalais in 1934. **[Earliest securely located Haitian publication]** Records Simbi within lived Haitian religion rather than a literary mythology.
- **Maya Deren, *Divine Horsemen: The Living Gods of Haiti* (1953).** Based on fieldwork beginning in 1947. **[Ethnographic-literary synthesis]** Places Simbi among aquatic lwa and emphasizes possession, ritual movement, and the relational structure of the pantheon. Her language is influential but sometimes poetic rather than a verbatim indigenous creed.
- **Milo Rigaud, *La tradition voudoo et le voudoo haïtien* (1953), pp. 207–18.** Contains Odette Mennesson-Rigaud’s “Service pour Simbi (sur rite Pétro).” **[Primary ritual documentation]** This is the richest early published description of a service specifically for Simbi and is more evidentially important than later internet lists.
- **Alfred Métraux, *Le vaudou haïtien* (1958; English 1959), especially pp. 267 and 310–12 in later English pagination.** **[Canonical ethnography]** Describes the Simbi as guardians of springs and marshes, presents songs, and reproduces or discusses Simbi ritual material.
- **Milo Rigaud, *Ve-Ve: Diagrammes rituels du Voudou* (first published 1940s; later editions).** **[Learned-priestly systematization grounded in practice]** Important for published vèvè traditions, but Rigaud’s diagrams should not be mistaken for an exhaustive, universally standardized canon.
- **Robert Farris Thompson, *Flash of the Spirit* (1983).** **[Modern comparative reconstruction]** Connects Haitian Simbi with Kongo bisimbi and interprets Haitian *pakèt kongo*, including a 1979 “Simbi-of-the-Leaves” packet, within Atlantic Kongo visual history.
- **Wyatt MacGaffey, “Twins, Simbi Spirits, and Lwas in Kongo and Haiti” (2001/2002).** **[Modern critical comparison]** The essential corrective to simplistic claims of unchanged African survival.
- **Benjamin Hebblethwaite and collaborators, linguistic and song studies.** **[Modern historical-linguistic reconstruction]** Document Kongo vocabulary in Kongo–Petwo–Makaya traditions and preserve songs such as invocations of Simbi Makaya and Andezo.

### Named sources that do not establish Simbi

Herskovits’s *Dahomey* (1938), Le Hérissé’s work on Dahomey, Maupoil’s *La géomancie à l’ancienne Côte des Esclaves*, Mercier’s Fon studies, the Ouidah python-temple record, and Abomey palace traditions illuminate Fon vodun, serpent religion, divination, and the West African constituents of Haitian Vodou. They do not supply a demonstrated Fon deity named Simbi.

A.G. Ellis’s *The Ewe-Speaking Peoples* (1890) is a colonial-era synthesis whose ethnic categories and generalizations require caution. It cannot establish a Simbi genealogy merely because it documents water or serpent divinities.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Water, locality, and serpents

**[Secure Kongo and Haitian continuity at the categorical level]** Springs, deep pools, rivers, marshes, rocks, and other liminal localities are Simbi’s clearest domain. Haitian descriptions frequently call the Simbi guardians of springs and marshes. A Simbi may manifest as, inhabit, or be symbolized by a snake.

“Water snake” is therefore defensible as an iconographic and visionary form, but “Simbi is one single cosmic serpent” is not. That formulation merges the Simbi family with Danbala and erases local manifestations.

### Magic, medicine, and secret knowledge

**[Secure twentieth-century Haitian evidence]** Simbi is repeatedly associated with occult knowledge, herbal medicine, healing, divination, and the transmission or guarding of ritual secrets. Simbi Makaya concentrates these associations. The Kongo background is structurally compatible: local spirits authorize medicines and ritual experts, while *nganga* denotes a healer-diviner or ritual specialist.

The common phrase “guardian of sorcerers” requires qualification. Métraux and later writers associate Simbi with *bòkò* and magical specialists, but *sorcerer* imports a hostile English distinction. Simbi may guard healers, diviners, priests, or practitioners of aggressive magic according to context. There is no evidence that every Simbi is exclusively or primarily the patron of harmful sorcery.

### Rain

**[Later expansion/folklore]** Since rain is fresh water and sustains springs, modern practitioners and manuals extend Simbi’s domain to rainfall. The popular phrase “Simbi in the rain” is plausible within living theology but is not securely recoverable as an early Kongo or colonial Haitian title. Treat it as a modern oral-liturgical expression unless a specific song performance and date are cited.

### Fire and “hot” Simbis

Some Haitian houses classify particular Simbis, especially Makaya-related forms, as “hot,” forceful, or fiery. **[Regional ritual classification]** This is not a contradiction of aquatic identity: Vodou’s “hot/cool” language can describe ritual temperament rather than physical element. Modern occult charts that divide Simbis into four Western elements are **modern invention**.

### Vèvè

A published vèvè for Simbi Andezo appears in Métraux, attributed to an oungan named Abraham. It uses the linear, symmetric ritual graphic vocabulary of Haitian vèvè. **[Secure twentieth-century visual record]** Variants drawn by other priests are equally authentic to their houses; there is no single ancient or universally binding Simbi seal.

Vèvè are drawn on the ground, traditionally with substances such as cornmeal, flour, ash, brick powder, or coffee, to mark and invite the lwa during service. Commercial “sigils” detached from those ritual settings may reproduce a real diagram but change its function.

### Colors, offerings, days, and numbers

Published and online tables disagree extensively. Blue, green, white, red, and black are assigned to different Simbi manifestations; offerings may include water, cane syrup, rum, herbs, fish-associated objects, perfumes, or house-specific foods.

**Finding:** No early corpus supports one universal Simbi color, weekday, number, or menu. These are lineage liturgies or modern occult correspondences, not safe pan-Vodou facts.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

Simbi has no single birth narrative, parents, spouse, or offspring comparable to a literary pantheon’s genealogy. Kongo bisimbi are a category with multiple origin accounts; some may be primordial locality spirits, while others are extraordinary dead persons transformed into territorial beings. Haitian Simbis are related principally through family resemblance, ritual nation, and shared domain rather than a stable family tree.

### Every recoverable named divine interaction

The early corpus supplies very few narrative “god-meets-god” episodes. Most relationships are liturgical co-presence or scholarly comparison, not myths.

- **Danbala Wèdo — shared serpent and aquatic symbolism; sometimes juxtaposed in ritual space.** Source: Maya Deren, *Divine Horsemen* (1953), followed by later ethnography. **Tier: later canonical ethnographic comparison.** No early narrative of combat, marriage, or conversation was located.
- **Ayida Wèdo — indirect association through the aquatic-serpentine complex.** Source: comparative twentieth-century Vodou descriptions. **Tier: learned systematization.** No discrete early interaction is documented.
- **Ezili Dantò — modern sources sometimes call Simbi Makaya her husband.** The claim circulates in recent summaries and is sometimes attributed broadly to Vodou tradition, but a reliable early primary passage was not located. **Tier: regional/modern oral claim; not established as pan-Vodou canon.**
- **Ti Jean Petwo — sometimes named alongside Simbi Makaya as Ezili Dantò’s husbands.** Same evidentiary problem. **Tier: regional/modern systematization.**
- **Legba — precedes access to other lwa in the general ceremonial order, including a Simbi service.** Source: standard Haitian liturgical descriptions in Deren and Métraux. **Tier: securely attested ritual ordering, not a Simbi-specific narrative interaction.**
- **Simbi Dlo, Simbi Andezo, and Simbi Makaya — jointly invoked as “Three Simbis” or “Three Kings” in some modern services.** Source: practitioner liturgies and commercial ritual literature, especially late twentieth- and twenty-first century. **Tier: modern lineage practice/systematization.** The triad is not demonstrably an ancient fixed trinity.
- **Mami Wata — both are sometimes grouped as African-diasporic water spirits.** Source: modern comparative and popular writing. **Tier: modern scholarly/popular comparison.** No Haitian primary narrative involving both was located.
- **Agwe — shares an aquatic domain, although Agwe is characteristically maritime and Simbi fresh-water/local.** Source: twentieth-century pantheon descriptions. **Tier: learned classification.** No discrete interaction found.
- **Saint Andrew, Saint Raphael, Moses, and the Three Magi — iconographic or devotional correspondences, not mythological encounters between independent beings.** **Tier: syncretistic identification.**

**Absence-of-evidence finding:** No securely attested early narrative corpus has Simbi fighting, seducing, judging, deceiving, or rescuing another named lwa. Lists that manufacture such episodes from pantheon membership are unsupported.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The spirit in the spring

**[Kongo ethnographic tradition]** Bisimbi inhabit or control extraordinary springs, pools, stones, ravines, and streams. Communities approach such places with restrictions and offerings because disturbing the spirit can cause sickness, infertility, drought, or social disorder, while correct relations can yield fertility, healing, and protection.

This is a recurrent narrative pattern rather than one fixed scripture. Local versions name different spirits and founders. Its function is territorial and political as well as religious: the spirit legitimates rights over land and water and the authority of the ritual expert who communicates with it.

### The transformed extraordinary dead

**[Kongo regional variant]** Some accounts identify a simbi as an exceptional person who died or crossed into the spirit world and became an enduring local power. The Mbola traditions, for example, connect a named transformed being with healing medicines. This should not be universalized into “all bisimbi are ancestors”; Kongo classifications vary.

### Simbi and hidden knowledge

**[Haitian canonical oral pattern]** Simbi inhabits water or a secluded natural site and possesses knowledge inaccessible to ordinary people. A practitioner who approaches correctly may receive medicine, divination, clairvoyance, songs, or magical capacity. Improper intrusion risks disorientation, illness, disappearance, or spiritual capture.

This is documented as belief, ritual expectation, and possession theology rather than as one stable prose myth. Later occult writers often turn it into a standardized initiation tale in which Simbi tests an aspirant and awards psychic powers. That fixed story-form is **modern elaboration**.

### Simbi Andezo’s service

Odette Mennesson-Rigaud’s “Service pour Simbi (sur rite Pétro)” documents a concrete ritual cycle rather than a literary legend: preparations, invocations, songs, offerings, possession, and service under Petwo forms. **[Secure primary ritual documentation, 1953]** It establishes that Simbi worship was elaborated and institutionally embedded, not merely a name in pantheon lists.

### The Three Kings

**[Syncretistic/modern lineage complex]** Some practitioners honor Simbi Dlo, Andezo, and Makaya as a triad called the Three Kings, relating them to the Magi. The association emphasizes mastery, gifts, herbs, secrets, and sometimes three differentiated kinds of water or power.

No colonial or early ethnographic source located here establishes this as a primordial Kongo trinity. Commercial “Three Kings rituals” are **modern ritual products**, even when practitioners have received the underlying triad through genuine house tradition.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Kongo sacred sites

Kongo bisimbi are attached to particular landscape features rather than necessarily housed in monumental temples. Springs, pools, rivers, rocks, caves, and ravines function as shrines. Authority belongs to custodians and *banganga* who know restrictions, medicines, and the spirit’s history.

The political importance of such sites can be substantial: a locality spirit may validate settlement, landholding, chiefly legitimacy, fertility, and communal identity.

### Haiti

Simbi is served in the **ounfò/peristil**, at household altars, and at natural water sites. A service may include:

- salutation through songs and drums;
- tracing a vèvè;
- water placed in basins or vessels;
- leaves and medicinal preparations;
- candles, drinks, food, and lineage-specific offerings;
- possession, in which the lwa “rides” a devotee;
- requests for healing, divination, protection, or ritual knowledge.

**[Secure ethnographic framework; details variable]** There is no known ancient central Temple of Simbi, national priesthood, or universal annual festival. Ritual authority rests in houses and lineages under oungan, mambo, and other specialists.

Animal sacrifice is documented in Haitian Vodou generally and may occur in services determined by a house. **No reliable evidence located establishes human sacrifice to Simbi.** Colonial and nineteenth-century accusations of Vodou human sacrifice belong to a broader racialized polemical tradition and cannot be repeated as fact without case-specific evidence.

Likewise, no documentary basis was found for “temple prostitution” in Simbi worship.

### Dahomey and Ouidah

The Ouidah python temple and Abomey palace cults document important West African serpent and royal religious traditions. **[Secure but separate evidence]** Their existence does not prove that Haitian Simbi descends from the Ouidah python vodun. Similar serpent imagery may have facilitated Haitian recombination, but the name and closest category point to Kongo-speaking Africa.

### New Orleans

Historical Louisiana Voodoo possessed strong serpent symbolism, especially the “Grand Zombi,” and had Central African as well as West African roots. Yet Robert Tallant’s *Voodoo in New Orleans* (1946), the Louisiana Writers’ Project interviews, and Carolyn Morrow Long’s historical work do not securely establish Simbi as a named major lwa in nineteenth-century New Orleans.

**Finding:** A distinct historical “New Orleans Simbi cult” is unproven. Simbi’s conspicuous presence in present-day New Orleans is better documented in the **late twentieth- and twenty-first-century revival**, when Haitian Vodou, Cuban traditions, African American conjure, tourism, and new religious organizations interacted.

Tallant remains useful as evidence of twentieth-century storytelling about New Orleans Voodoo, but his sensationalism, embellishment, and dependence on retrospective interviews prevent uncritical use. The Writers’ Project interviews are nearer to primary testimony but were recorded decades after many alleged events.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the seventeenth century

**[Modern reconstruction]** Local spirit traditions certainly predate their written recording, but no securely dated pre-European inscription names Simbi. Archaeological landscapes may be consistent with long-standing water and locality cults, yet attributing a particular ancient feature to bisimbi without inscription or continuous testimony is speculative.

### 1640s–seventeenth century: Kongo documentation

Missionaries and Kongo collaborators produced dictionaries, catechisms, and reports in a Christianizing kingdom. These sources preserved vocabulary while also filtering local categories through Latin-Christian concepts. Missionaries sometimes destroyed ritual objects and opposed practices they classified as idolatry.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: forced Atlantic transfer

**[Secure historical setting; reconstruction of religious transmission]** Enslaved Kongo- and Angola-region people were transported to Saint-Domingue and other American colonies. Words, ritual offices, medicines, territorial-spirit concepts, and water-spirit traditions entered creolizing religious communities. The transfer was not preservation in isolation: Kongo materials interacted with Fon, Yoruba, Aja, Catholic, colonial, and locally generated forms.

### Late eighteenth century and the Haitian Revolution

The historical record documents African-derived oaths and ritual mobilization, most famously the contested Bois Caïman accounts. It does not securely name Simbi. Assigning Simbi a direct revolutionary role is therefore unsupported.

### Nineteenth century

Vodou persisted under recurrent legal, clerical, and elite hostility. Simbi worship probably continued within oral and house traditions, but the surviving published record is thin. This documentary silence must not be mistaken either for absence of worship or proof of every later claim.

### 1930s–1950s: ethnographic visibility

Herskovits, Deren, the Rigauds, and Métraux made Simbi visible in published ethnography. Their books are indispensable but do not constitute timeless canon: each records particular informants, regions, houses, translations, and authorial theories.

The 1941–42 Catholic-state **campagne antisuperstitieuse**, often called *Operation Nettoyage*, destroyed drums, altars, ritual objects, and sacred sites and coerced renunciations. Simbi was affected as part of Vodou generally; no Simbi-specific suppression order has been located.

### 1960s–1980s: diaspora and comparative reconstruction

Migration spread Haitian houses through North America. Scholars increasingly identified Kongo elements that earlier Dahomey-centered models had minimized. Thompson’s 1983 work made Simbi prominent in Atlantic visual and religious comparison.

### 1990s–present: revival, internet religion, and commercialization

New Orleans organizations including Miriam Chamani’s Voodoo Spiritual Temple and Sallie Ann Glassman’s La Source Ancienne incorporated Haitian and Cuban elements into a self-conscious local revival. Scholars such as Carolyn Morrow Long distinguish this from uninterrupted nineteenth-century institutional Voodoo.

Online manuals now publish fixed colors, sigils, saint correspondences, “Three Kings” rites, and solitary invocations. Some transmit practitioner traditions; others combine Vodou with ceremonial magic, New Age elemental systems, or commerce. Their historical claims require independent checking.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Occult and esoteric literature

Simbi was not a major named figure in Renaissance Hermetism, the *Key of Solomon*, the Golden Dawn, or classical European grimoires. Claims that Simbi possesses a medieval European seal or belongs to an ancient grimoire hierarchy are **modern inventions**.

Late twentieth-century and contemporary occult books recast Simbi as:

- an “elemental” of water;
- a patron of psychic powers;
- a serpent current;
- a spirit for rain magic;
- a guardian of magicians;
- one member of an initiatory Three Kings triad.

These can be valid descriptions of particular modern religious practices, but they are not evidence for ancient Kongo or colonial Haitian doctrine.

### Art and material culture

Haitian flags, paintings, bottles, vèvè drawings, and *pakèt kongo* provide modern visual forms. Thompson documented a 1979 packet identified as “Simbi-of-the-Leaves,” important evidence that Simbi Makaya symbolism participated in conscious Kongo-style material creativity.

Commercial reproductions often present one vèvè as “the ancient symbol of Simbi.” That is misleading: published vèvè are datable Haitian ritual drawings with house-level variation.

### Literature, music, film, and games

Simbi remains far less standardized in mass media than Baron Samedi, Ezili, or “voodoo dolls.” Uses of “Simbi Makaya” in music and fantasy commonly emphasize mysterious water magic. The German metal band Die Apokalyptischen Reiter included a track titled “Simbi Makya” on *The Divine Horsemen* (2021). **[Modern artistic reception]** This is not a liturgical source.

### Neopagan and solitary practice

Internet practitioners sometimes detach Simbi from initiated Vodou and present the lwa as universally accessible. Haitian practitioners frequently contest this and emphasize lineage, divination, and proper introduction. The dispute is contemporary and internal to living religion; scholarship cannot declare one theology metaphysically true, but it can distinguish historically transmitted house practice from recently constructed solitary manuals.

### Political and extremist appropriation

No well-documented extremist movement centered on Simbi was found. Vodou more generally has been used politically—by revolutionary memory, Haitian statecraft, anti-Vodou propaganda, and racial nationalism—but attributing those developments personally to Simbi would be unwarranted.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Dahomey-centered versus Kongo-centered models

Early Afro-Haitian scholarship often emphasized Dahomey because names such as Vodou, Danbala, Legba, and other institutional parallels were conspicuous. Simbi is a major counterexample: its closest linguistic and categorical parallels are Kongo.

Luc de Heusch’s 1989 “Kongo in Haiti” broadly contrasted a Dahomean Rada complex with a Kongo-related Petwo complex. Later scholarship warns that this is a heuristic, not a clean ethnic map: both rites are creolized, and individual spirits move between ritual series.

Andrew Apter further criticized simple origin tables, arguing that claims about “Africa” and “Creole” are themselves part of Vodou’s historical production of authority and knowledge.

### Survival versus creolization

Robert Farris Thompson stressed recognizable Kongo continuities in gesture, graphic form, medicine, packets, and cosmology. MacGaffey accepts comparison but insists on reconstructing the differing matrices in which a word or practice operates. The responsible conclusion is neither “unchanged African survival” nor “pure Haitian invention,” but historically constrained transformation.

### Text versus secrecy

Much Simbi knowledge is oral, initiatory, local, and deliberately restricted. Published ethnography therefore underrepresents practice. At the same time, secrecy cannot function as proof for an undated historical assertion. “An initiate told me” is evidence of a contemporary claim; it is not automatically evidence of antiquity.

### Vèvè standardization

Rigaud’s and Métraux’s diagrams helped stabilize public expectations of what a vèvè should look like. Scholars must distinguish:

- a diagram observed or supplied by a named practitioner;
- its publication by an ethnographer;
- later copying as a supposedly universal emblem.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- **“Simbi is originally a Dahomean/Fon python god.”** Unsupported; the strongest name/category evidence is Kongo.
- **“Simbi is one god with one fixed personality.”** Contradicted by family and lineage usage.
- **“Simbi Andezo certainly means the junction of two waters.”** Popular but linguistically unproven.
- **“The Three Kings are an ancient Kongo Simbi trinity.”** Not established before modern Haitian/diasporic practice.
- **“Simbi took part in Bois Caïman.”** No secure source.
- **“Marie Laveau historically served Simbi.”** No reliable nineteenth-century evidence located.
- **“Every snake spirit in Haiti, Dahomey, Kongo, and New Orleans is Simbi.”** Comparative flattening.
- **“Vodou or Simbi worship involved human sacrifice.”** Colonial/sensational allegation without Simbi-specific documentary proof.
- **“A single internet vèvè is Simbi’s unchanged African seal.”** Anachronistic.
- **“Simbi belongs to medieval European demonology.”** Modern invention.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before European documentation:** Local Kongo water, territorial, and extraordinary-dead spirit traditions exist, but no securely dated written occurrence can yet be assigned.
- **c. 1648:** A Latin–Spanish–Kikongo vocabulary is compiled at Mbanza Kongo under Manuel de Roboredo and Capuchin auspices.
- **1652:** Joris van Gheel copies the surviving *Vocabularium Latinum, Hispanicum et Congense*. **[Earliest secure documentary background]**
- **Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries:** Kongo- and Angola-region captives are forcibly carried into Atlantic colonies, including Saint-Domingue. **[Historical mechanism for transmission]**
- **1791:** Bois Caïman traditions become attached to the outbreak of the Haitian Revolution. No secure Simbi name occurs in the surviving early record.
- **1797:** Moreau de Saint-Méry publishes his major description of colonial Saint-Domingue. It documents African-derived practices but supplies no located Simbi attestation.
- **1890:** A.G. Ellis publishes *The Ewe-Speaking Peoples*, later influential in comparative discussions but not evidence for a Fon Simbi.
- **1928:** Van Wing and Penders publish a substantially mediated edition of the seventeenth-century Kikongo dictionary as *Le plus ancien dictionnaire bantu*.
- **1934:** Herskovits conducts fieldwork in Mirebalais.
- **1937:** *Life in a Haitian Valley* publishes the earliest securely located Haitian ethnographic references to Simbi.
- **1938:** Herskovits publishes *Dahomey*. Its value concerns Fon religion generally; it does not establish a Dahomean Simbi.
- **1941–42:** Catholic and state-backed antisuperstition campaigns destroy Vodou material culture and coerce renunciation.
- **1946:** Robert Tallant publishes *Voodoo in New Orleans*, a highly influential but sensationalized synthesis drawing partly on Louisiana Writers’ Project material.
- **1947 onward:** Maya Deren undertakes Haitian fieldwork.
- **1953:** Deren publishes *Divine Horsemen*; Milo Rigaud publishes *La tradition voudoo et le voudoo haïtien*, including Odette Mennesson-Rigaud’s dedicated “Service pour Simbi.”
- **1958:** Métraux publishes *Le vaudou haïtien*.
- **1959:** English translation, *Voodoo in Haiti*.
- **1974:** Janzen and MacGaffey publish *An Anthology of Kongo Religion*, making translated Lower Kongo primary testimony more accessible.
- **1979:** A Haitian *pakèt kongo* identified as “Simbi-of-the-Leaves” is made and later discussed by Thompson.
- **1983:** Thompson publishes *Flash of the Spirit*, strongly advancing the Kongo comparison.
- **1989:** Luc de Heusch publishes “Kongo in Haiti: A New Approach to Religious Syncretism.”
- **1990:** Miriam Chamani establishes the Voodoo Spiritual Temple in New Orleans; the modern revival increasingly combines Louisiana memory with Haitian and other Afro-Caribbean traditions.
- **1992:** The *New Orleans Voodoo Tarot* by Louis Martinié and Sallie Ann Glassman helps circulate a consciously modern New Orleans esoteric synthesis.
- **2001/2002:** MacGaffey publishes “Twins, Simbi Spirits, and Lwas in Kongo and Haiti.”
- **2002:** Andrew Apter publishes “On African Origins: Creolization and Connaissance in Haitian Vodou.”
- **Twenty-first century:** Digital practitioner networks disseminate Simbi vèvè, Three Kings rites, saint correspondences, rain associations, and solitary invocations. Their antiquity varies and often cannot be established.
- **2021:** Die Apokalyptischen Reiter release the musical track “Simbi Makya,” an example of global artistic reception.
- **2026:** Simbi remains a living family of lwa in Haitian and diasporic Vodou; practice remains decentralized, lineage-dependent, and incompletely represented by published sources.

### Final assessment

The fullest defensible reconstruction is not a biography of one serpent god. It is the history of a **Kongo category of locality and water spirits transformed into a Haitian family of lwa**, differentiated by house, rite, and epithet. Springs, snakes, medicine, secrecy, and ritual authority are strongly documented. Simbi Andezo and Simbi Makaya are securely present by mid-twentieth-century ritual documentation; their fixed modern definitions are less secure. The Three Kings and rain complexes belong to living and modern reception but cannot presently be projected unchanged into Africa or colonial Haiti. New Orleans possesses a genuine Central African religious inheritance, yet a named nineteenth-century Simbi cult there remains unproven; the explicit New Orleans Simbi is chiefly part of the modern revival.
