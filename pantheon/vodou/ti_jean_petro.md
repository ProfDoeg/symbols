# Ti Jean Petro: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A male **lwa**—a spirit or “mystery” (*mistè*) served in Haitian Vodou—principally associated with the **Petwo** rites and, in some classifications, the Congo-Zandor complex. He is not securely attested as a pre-Atlantic Dahomean *vodun*.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation of Ti Jean Petro by name:** Melville J. Herskovits’s Haitian fieldwork in Mirebalais in 1934, published in *Life in a Haitian Valley* (1937). Herskovits records the Petwo complex and material subsequently cited specifically for Ti Jean Petro by Maya Deren. The clearest early printed description is Deren’s *Divine Horsemen* (1953), which calls Ti Jean the son of Dan Petro, says that he has one foot or none, and describes his extraordinary ability to climb trees. Alfred Métraux independently records the one-legged arboreal dwarf in *Le Vaudou haïtien* (1958), p. 76.
>
> **Critical distinction:** The **Don/Dom Pèdre dance**, documented in colonial Saint-Domingue from 1768, is earlier than any known attestation of **Ti Jean Petro as a named lwa**. The shared word *Petro/Petwo* does not by itself prove that Ti Jean is the colonial ritualist Pierre “Dom Pedro,” his son, or his apotheosis.

## Evidentiary labels

- **Securely attested early material:** Contemporary or near-contemporary records, or early ethnography based on identified fieldwork.
- **Later ethnographic elaboration:** Twentieth-century descriptions presented as living tradition but not demonstrated in colonial records.
- **Regional or house variant:** A teaching documented for a particular locality, temple, lineage, or practitioner.
- **Learned systematization:** An ethnographer’s or theologian’s attempt to make a coherent pantheon from variable practice.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** A historical or comparative hypothesis rather than an indigenous narrative directly recorded from practitioners.
- **Modern invention or unsupported circulation:** A claim appearing only in recent occult, commercial, or crowdsourced sources, without traceable earlier documentation.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

Ti Jean Petro belongs to the “hot” Petwo field of Haitian Vodou. Petwo is not a tidy pantheon with a universally fixed membership. It is a complex of rites, rhythms, ritual temperatures, inherited spirits, local families, and classifications that vary between regions and houses. Early ethnographers commonly opposed “cool,” protective Rada lwa to “hot,” forceful Petwo lwa, but later scholarship treats this binary as a useful ritual contrast rather than a complete taxonomy.

Ti Jean’s best-attested characteristics are:

- small or dwarfish stature;
- one foot, one leg, or no feet;
- extraordinary mobility in trees, particularly palms;
- association with Petwo force, fire, magic, and violent possession;
- kinship with Dan Petro in one influential account;
- marriage or companionship with Marinette in another;
- participation with Marinette and Chanprèl in a Petwo fire song.

The widespread internet description “snake-lwa” derives largely from Maya Deren’s interpretation of the legless tree-climber as a riddle whose answer must be “a snake.” It is not an independently documented possession statement that Ti Jean universally manifests as a serpent.

### Names

| Form | Meaning or status | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Ti Jan Petwo** | Modern Haitian Creole spelling: “Little Jean/John Petwo” | Linguistically regular modern form |
| **Ti Jean Petro / Ti-Jean Pétro** | French and older ethnographic spelling | Herskovits, Deren, Métraux and later literature |
| **Ti Jean Pied-Sec / Ti Jan Pye Chèch** | “Little Jean Dry-Foot” | Later ethnographic form; frequently associated with Marinette Bwa Chèch |
| **Ti-Jean-Pied-Fin** | “Little Jean Thin-Foot” | Later variant; sometimes treated as equivalent to Pied-Sec |
| **Ti Jean Zandor / Prince Zandor** | Ti Jean associated with, or made chief of, the Zandor/Congo-Savanne group | Later ethnographic and lexicographic systematization |
| **Ti Jean Dantor / Frè Ti Jan** | A Ti Jean of the Dantor family | Living regional/house traditions; relationship to Ti Jean Petro is variable |
| **Petro-Je-Rouge / Petro-e-Rouge** | “Petro Red-Eyes” | Modern lists sometimes make this an epithet of Ti Jean; older sources often treat *je-rouge* more generally as a classifying Petwo epithet. Identity is not secure |
| **Jean-Philippe Petro** | Name assigned in later narratives to the founder of Petwo | Not found in Moreau de Saint-Méry’s original 1768 account; later harmonization |
| **Tinyó** | Dominican spirit sometimes compared with Ti Jean | Comparative identification; not demonstrated to be simply the same spirit |

*Ti* is Haitian Creole for French *petit*, “little.” *Jan/Jean* is the ordinary name John. The name is therefore manifestly creolized and Christian-era, not a preserved Fon divine name.

### Is “Petro” Spanish, Portuguese-Kongo, or *dan* “snake”?

Three hypotheses must be kept separate:

1. **Colonial Don Pèdre derivation — securely attested historical substrate.** Moreau de Saint-Méry says that in 1768 a Black man of Petit-Goâve, whom he regarded as of Spanish origin, introduced the violent *danse à Don Pèdre*. This is the oldest printed explanation of the ritual name.

2. **Kongo *Dom Pedro* derivation — modern archival reconstruction.** Newly studied 1768–69 judicial papers call the leader Pierre “Dom Pedro.” Kate Ramsey’s and Crystal Nicole Eddins’s historical work connects him and his movement with West-Central African, especially Lemba, political-religious concepts. *Dom*, rather than Spanish *Don*, fits Portuguese-Christian naming in Kongo. The documents do not call this man Ti Jean Petro.

3. **Deren’s *dan* = serpent hypothesis — learned systematization.** Deren proposed that *Dan Petro* might contain Fon *Dan/Danh*, “serpent,” rather than *Don*. She used Ti Jean’s lack of feet and tree-climbing to complete a serpent riddle. This is ingenious comparative interpretation, not a demonstrated etymology.

Andrew Apter’s critique is important: a French-Christian name such as “Little John Petro” cannot simply be projected backward as an unchanged pre-contact African divinity. Ti Jean’s formation is better understood as creolization, even if some of his traits draw upon Kongo or broader African spirit complexes.

### Catholic identifications

- **Saint John the Baptist:** Strongly represented in contemporary Ti Jean Dantor service and in living houses that celebrate Ti Jean on 24 June. It is less securely demonstrated for every form called Ti Jean Petro.
- **Saint Anthony the Great or Saint Anthony the Hermit:** Luc de Heusch reports this comparison while noting that the same saint may represent Simbi. It is therefore a local or unstable correspondence, not a universal equivalence.
- **Saint Saviour:** Appears on modern websites but was not verified in the principal ethnographic sources examined.
- **Archangel Raphael:** A Dominican association attached to Tinyó; it should not automatically be transferred to Haitian Ti Jean Petro.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The 1768 colonial record: Petwo, but not yet Ti Jean

Moreau de Saint-Méry’s *Description topographique… de la partie française de l’isle Saint-Domingue* and his essay on dance report that in 1768 a Black man at Petit-Goâve introduced a dance analogous to *Vaudoux* but faster and more violent. Participants allegedly mixed crushed gunpowder into *tafia*. Moreau says the dance caused deaths and excited spectators into a shared crisis. Authorities prohibited it because it disturbed public order.

This proves that a **Don Pèdre/Dom Pedro ritual-dance** existed by 1768. It does not attest:

- the name Ti Jean;
- a one-legged spirit;
- a tree-dwelling lwa;
- Dan Petro’s paternity;
- Marinette as Ti Jean’s wife;
- a connection to Ezili Dantor;
- pepper as Ti Jean’s particular emblem.

Recently examined judicial records substantially revise Moreau’s hostile summary. Between 1768 and 1769 Judge Joseph Ferrand de Beaudière investigated Pierre “Dom Pedro” and followers active around Petit-Goâve, Jacmel, and Léogâne. Dom Pedro reportedly preached approaching freedom, opposed overseers’ use of the whip, and encouraged the enslaved to turn the whip against abusive *commandeurs*. Forty-two people were arrested. Some were still imprisoned in 1773. The same records connect the movement with ritual payments, tests of endurance, herbal and protective knowledge, and anti-slavery sedition. The whip belongs securely to **Dom Pedro’s colonial movement**; evidence making it Ti Jean Petro’s personal ritual attribute is lacking.

### Herskovits, 1937

Herskovits and Frances Herskovits conducted fieldwork in Mirebalais in 1934. *Life in a Haitian Valley* records locally variable lists of lwa and Petwo rites rather than a standardized national canon. Deren later cites Herskovits, p. 191, in support of Ti Jean’s unusual body and arboreal ability.

This is the earliest presently identifiable secure ethnographic attestation of Ti Jean Petro by name. Because the book reports twentieth-century oral religion, it cannot independently date the lwa to 1768.

### Maya Deren, 1953

Deren’s *Divine Horsemen: The Living Gods of Haiti*, based on Haitian work beginning in 1947, supplies the first extended interpretive treatment:

- Dan Petro is “father of the Petro tribe of loa.”
- Ti Jean Petro is Dan Petro’s son.
- Ti Jean has one foot, or perhaps no feet.
- He climbs trees almost immediately upon appearing.
- Deren interprets him as “obviously a snake.”
- She groups Dan and Ti Jean Petro, Congo Savanne, and Congo Zandor as Petwo analogues of ancestral and life-giving serpent principles.
- Elsewhere she calls Marinette Ti Jean Petro’s wife.

These equations are a mixture of recorded information and Deren’s comparative interpretation. Her suggested comparison with the one-legged Aztec Tezcatlipoca, and her speculation about Amerindian sacrificial influence, belong to mid-century comparativism and have not become established historical conclusions.

### Milo Marcelin/Rigaud song corpus

A song published in Milo Rigaud’s mid-century collection and re-edited by Benjamin Hebblethwaite, Musset Apolon, and Tahiri Jean-Baptiste contains:

> *Marinèt Bwa Chèch … Limen dife.  
> Ti Jan … Limen dife.  
> Chanprèl … Limen dife.*

“Marinette Dry-Wood … light the fire. Ti Jean … light the fire. Chanprèl … light the fire.”

This is strong liturgical evidence for Ti Jean’s association with fire and for his joint invocation with Marinette and Chanprèl. It does not specify that the singer means Ti Jean Petro rather than a locally understood Ti Jean within the Petwo sequence; the Petwo rhythm and companions make that identification highly probable.

### Alfred Métraux, 1958

In *Le Vaudou haïtien*, p. 76, Métraux reports that Ti Jean Petro is sometimes imagined as a one-legged dwarf living or moving in trees. His treatment locates the figure among creole-named Petwo spirits, some of whom may preserve African spirit types under Haitian names.

Métraux also records Ti-Jean-Pied-Sec as Marinette’s husband: a small red-clothed man who travels by hopping on one foot and climbs palms to fall upon and kill passersby when he requires food. This is an ethnographic legend, not a report of actual homicide or sacrifice.

### Luc de Heusch, 1989

De Heusch’s “Kongo in Haiti: A New Approach to Religious Syncretism” argues that Petwo is strongly Kongo rather than Dahomean. He compares:

- Ti Jean Pied-Sec with Kongo Nkita nature/ancestral spirits;
- dwarfish or physically atypical Petwo beings with Kongo *mhaka* and *baka* complexes;
- the ritual role of dwarfs and people with club feet in Kimpasi institutions;
- Congo Savanne/Zandor with West-Central African traditions.

This is a sophisticated comparative reconstruction, not evidence for a West African god literally named Ti Jean Petro.

### Other requested corpora: negative findings

- **Herskovits’s *Dahomey* (1938):** No secure Ti Jean Petro entry was found. This supports, rather than weakens, the conclusion that Ti Jean under this name is Haitian/creole.
- **Maupoil, Le Hérissé, Mercier, Ouidah python cult and Abomey records:** These document Fon *vodun*, Dan/Dangbe, Fa, royal cult, and related institutions. They do not securely attest Ti Jean Petro.
- **Bois Caïman documents:** No early Bois Caïman account names Ti Jean Petro. Assigning the 1791 ceremony to Ti Jean, Marinette, or Ezili Dantor is retrospective reconstruction.
- **Karen McCarthy Brown’s *Mama Lola*:** Valuable for variation, inheritance, and the Ezili complex, but it does not establish a universal Ti Jean Petro genealogy.
- **New Orleans sources:** Robert Tallant, Ina Fandrich, Carolyn Morrow Long, and Louisiana Writers’ Project materials do not provide convincing evidence for an independently transmitted nineteenth-century New Orleans cult of Ti Jean Petro.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Secure and relatively early attributes

**One-legged or footless form.** This is the most stable physical marker in Deren and Métraux. Variants say one foot, one leg, a dried or thin foot, or no feet.

**Trees and palms.** Ti Jean climbs trees with paradoxical agility. In the darker legend recorded by Métraux, he waits atop palms and drops on travelers. “He lives in trees” is therefore defensible as later ethnographic tradition; a particular exclusive sacred species is not.

**Fire.** The Petwo song naming Ti Jean with Marinette and Chanprèl repeatedly commands “light the fire.” Marcelin also places Ti Jean Petro among “divinities of fire.” Modern houses describe him as eating, dancing in, or manipulating fire. The association is secure; claims that he is an abstract “god of the element fire” are modern taxonomic language.

**Red.** Métraux’s Pied-Sec is red-clothed. Red belongs broadly to Petwo heat and force. Yellow is reported in some present-day Ti Jean Dantor service. Red-and-black systems are common online but should be treated as house-specific unless documented in a lineage.

**Magic and dangerous force.** Early ethnographers often described Petwo through the moralizing categories “malevolent,” “demonic,” or “black magic.” Practitioners more commonly distinguish hot, fast, strict, defensive, aggressive, or dangerous operations. Ti Jean’s association with magic is old ethnographically; “patron of black magicians” is a sensationalized modern formulation.

### Possession

A Petwo possession attributed to Ti Jean may involve:

- abrupt, forceful or violent motion;
- hopping or impaired-foot performance;
- climbing or gestures toward trees;
- engagement with fire;
- biting or self-directed violence in a Ti Jean Zandor episode reported by Métraux.

Such possession behavior is ritual evidence for how communities embody the lwa, not proof of a fixed visual anatomy.

### Pepper, gunpowder, whip, and cane

- **Pepper:** Hot peppers are widely used in Petwo food and ritual preparations. No early source examined establishes pepper as Ti Jean Petro’s exclusive emblem or offering. Label: **general Petwo association, later transferred to Ti Jean**.
- **Gunpowder:** Secure for the 1768 Don Pèdre dance in Moreau’s account and for broader Petwo fire practice. It is not securely a unique Ti Jean attribute.
- **Whip:** Secure in the newly studied Dom Pedro judicial material: Pedro challenged the plantation whip and encouraged its reversal against abusive overseers. It is not yet securely documented as an implement carried by Ti Jean Petro.
- **Cane or stick:** Modern houses give Ti Jean a cane during possession; one northern or Dantor form wears a hat and uses a cane. Colonial “Dom Pedro” also became a title for ritualists said to carry a large stick and whip. Direct continuity is plausible but unproved.

### Vèvè and visual art

No single universally authoritative vèvè can be established. Vèvè vary by house and may belong to Ti Jean Dantor rather than Ti Jean Petro narrowly defined. Internet diagrams frequently circulate without provenance.

A documented modern artwork is Eviland Lalanne’s sequined banner *Ti Jan Pero [Ti Jean Petro]*, exhibited in discussions of Haitian sequin art. Such banners materialize a late-twentieth- or early-twenty-first-century visual reception, not an ancient iconographic canon.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Competing genealogies

1. **Son of Dan Petro — later ethnographic systematization.** Deren states this explicitly in 1953. It is the best-known genealogy in academic literature.

2. **Son of Ezili Dantor — regional/house variant.** Present-day Dantor lineages call Ti Jean Dantor Ezili Dantor’s son or only child. Some modern sources transfer this parentage to Ti Jean Petro without demonstrating that all houses equate the forms.

3. **Lover or husband of Ezili Dantor — regional variant.** Other accounts make Ti Jean Dantor Dantor’s husband or lover. The contradiction should not be harmonized: spirit kinship in Vodou is relational and house-specific.

4. **Husband or lover of Marinette — later ethnographic tradition.** Deren and Métraux associate Marinette with Ti Jean Petro/Pied-Sec as wife or companion.

5. **Apotheosis, son, or spiritual heir of Dom Pedro — modern reconstruction.** Some writers propose that Ti Jean is the deified Pierre Dom Pedro, his biological son, or a ritual successor. No colonial document establishes any of these identities.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

Ti Jean Petro possesses no surviving scripture or stable epic cycle comparable to an ancient Mediterranean god. His mythology exists as ritual description, possession, song, genealogy, and short legends.

### The riddle in the tree

**Later ethnographic core:** Ti Jean has one foot or none yet climbs trees with remarkable speed.

**Deren’s development:** Deren turns the paradox into a riddle: what has no feet but climbs trees? A snake. She consequently interprets Ti Jean as a serpent and makes him the son of Dan Petro, whose name she relates speculatively to *Dan*, serpent.

**Assessment:** The bodily paradox is attested; the mandatory answer “snake” is Deren’s exegesis. Later encyclopedias often erase that distinction.

### The palm-tree predator

Métraux records Ti-Jean-Pied-Sec as a small red-clothed one-legged being who climbs palms and, when hungry, throws himself on passersby to kill and consume them.

This is **folklore recorded ethnographically**, not evidence that worshippers historically killed humans for him. It belongs to the wider imagery of dangerous forest spirits, cannibal lwa, night societies, and colonial fears of maroons.

### Ti Jean and the fire

The Petwo song places Ti Jean between Marinette Bwa Chèch and Chanprèl, each followed by *Limen dife*, “Light the fire.” Modern possession accounts elaborate this into fire-eating, walking or dancing in fire, and healing through fire.

The song is the secure core. Specific miracles belong to oral testimony and individual houses.

### Ti Jean as Dom Pedro reborn

Modern retellings say that Don Juan Felipe Pedro died and became Ti Jean Petro, or that Ti Jean is his son or spiritual heir. The version is historically possible as apotheosis but is undocumented in the eighteenth-century papers. “Juan Felipe/Jean-Philippe” itself is not in Moreau’s account and requires a traceable later source.

### Ti Jean Dantor

In southern and diasporic temples, Ti Jean Dantor is playful, fiery, youthful, and closely attached to Ezili Dantor. He may climb coconut trees, smoke two cigarettes, wear yellow, and be served on 24 June.

This living complex should not be silently merged with Deren’s footless son of Dan Petro. Some houses identify or escort the forms together; others distinguish Ti Jean Dantor, Ti Jean Petro, Jean Laurent, Ti Kita, and related spirits.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Institutional setting

Ti Jean is served within family inheritance, temple lineages, and Petwo ceremonies rather than through an ancient state temple. No excavated temple uniquely dedicated to him is known. His ritual record consists primarily of:

- songs;
- possession;
- vèvè;
- altars and Catholic images;
- fire ceremonies;
- offerings defined by individual houses;
- oral instructions transmitted through initiation.

### Ritual service

Attested or plausibly documented elements include Petwo rhythms, fire, strong drink, red or yellow cloth, hats, canes, cigarettes, and fiery preparations. Their exact allocation varies.

Modern commercial sources list mutton, rum, cinnamon, anise, sugarcane, tobacco, peppers, gunpowder, and perfumes. These should not be assembled into a universal recipe. In Vodou, correct service depends upon lineage and divination; published lists often conflate Ti Jean Petro with Ti Jean Dantor or generalized Petwo service.

### Sacrifice

Animal sacrifice is historically documented in Vodou generally and in Petwo ceremonies. No reliable source was found showing a distinctive sacrificial species universally required by Ti Jean Petro.

Claims of **human sacrifice** fall into three categories:

- the palm-tree cannibal legend;
- Moreau’s allegation that gunpowder-tafia caused deaths during the Don Pèdre dance;
- colonial and sensationalist accusations against Vodou.

None documents institutional human sacrifice to Ti Jean Petro. Deren’s broad comparativist discussion of human sacrifice and maize deities is not Haitian archaeological evidence.

### Suppression

The 1685 *Code Noir* required Catholic baptism and excluded public African religious practice. Colonial authorities repeatedly dispersed enslaved gatherings. The Don Pèdre dance was specifically prohibited after authorities associated it with disorder and ideas “contrary to public peace.”

Post-independence anti-superstition campaigns, especially the Catholic *campagnes anti-superstitieuses* of 1896 and 1941–42, destroyed ritual objects and attacked Vodou institutions generally. No documented suppression order specifically naming Ti Jean Petro was located.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1768: African antecedents, not Ti Jean himself

Fon, Ewe, and Kongo peoples carried serpent cults, nature-spirit complexes, possession, divination, sacred trees, healing institutions, and initiatory societies into Saint-Domingue. These are historical matrices. There is no known African inscription, shrine record, royal list, or early ethnography naming Ti Jean Petro.

The strongest proposed ancestry is now **Kongo/Lemba**, not a particular Dahomean god. De Heusch’s Nkita/Kimpasi comparison is suggestive. Deren’s Dan-serpent model reflects the Dahomean side of Haitian religious synthesis but is not a genealogy demonstrated by Fon sources.

### 1768–73: Dom Pedro movement

The Petwo name enters the record through the ritual and anti-slavery movement around Pierre “Dom Pedro.” Its violent reputation was produced both by ritual heat and by colonial authorities’ fear of organized enslaved people. The whip, intoxicating gunpowder-tafia, secrecy, protective objects, and preaching of freedom belong to this episode.

### 1791 and the Revolution

Petwo later became strongly associated with rage, revolt, fire, and the Haitian Revolution. Deren interpreted it as a New World religious answer to slavery. The ritual song *Vive la Liberté* was reportedly retained in Petwo service.

No surviving 1791 Bois Caïman document names Ti Jean Petro. Claims that Ti Jean personally presided there are late invention.

### 1934–58: ethnographic fixation

Herskovits, Deren, Marcelin/Rigaud, Courlander, and Métraux transformed local oral knowledge into a print corpus. This period supplies nearly all foundational statements about Ti Jean:

- son of Dan Petro;
- one-legged or footless;
- tree-climber;
- associated with fire;
- husband of Marinette;
- related to Zandor;
- potentially serpent-like.

Because these scholars worked in different regions and relied on different informants, contradictions are evidence of Vodou’s local organization, not errors to be erased.

### 1960s–90s: structural and Africanist reinterpretation

Haitian and foreign scholars debated whether Petwo was:

- a primarily creole creation;
- Dahomean serpent religion transformed;
- Kongo/Lemba ritual reconstructed in Haiti;
- an amalgam whose “African origin” cannot be reduced to one source.

De Heusch’s Kongo thesis and Apter’s critique of simple African survivals are especially relevant to Ti Jean.

### Twenty-first century

Ti Jean remains a served lwa in Haiti and its diaspora. Online temples, videos, shops, occult encyclopedias, and social media now circulate blended profiles. These often combine Ti Jean Petro, Ti Jean Dantor, Ti Jean Zandor, Ti Jean Quinto, Tinyó, and the folktale hero Ti Jean.

Living service is genuine contemporary evidence, but it does not automatically prove the antiquity of every asserted correspondence.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### New Orleans

The assertion that Ti Jean Petro is a traditional “New Orleans Voodoo snake deity” is traceable in readily available form to Denise Alvarado’s *Voodoo Hoodoo Spellbook* (2009) and derivative tourism websites. It reproduces the Deren-derived “son of Dan Petro/snake” profile.

No convincing nineteenth-century Louisiana newspaper, court record, Writers’ Project interview, Tallant passage, Marie Laveau document, or early New Orleans liturgy was found naming Ti Jean Petro. The safest conclusion is:

> **Modern Louisiana occult reception is documented; an old, independent New Orleans cult is not.**

Tallant is useful evidence for twentieth-century folklore about New Orleans Voodoo but is notoriously unreliable in detail. Marie Laveau was a historical practitioner, not an incarnation or priestess of Ti Jean Petro, and should appear here only as part of later commercialization that blends Haitian and Louisiana traditions.

### Occult and commercial literature

Judika Illes’s *Encyclopedia of Spirits* (2009) presents four possibilities: Ti Jean as Don Petro, his son/heir, Ezili Dantor’s son/lover, or an African spirit renamed in Haiti. The book usefully preserves contradictions but supplies no primary evidence resolving them.

E. A. Koetting’s *The Spider and the Green Butterfly*, online possession forums, spell books, perfumes, candles, and statues represent **modern occult appropriation and commerce**, not historical Vodou canon. A child-Saint-John statue sold as “Ti Jean Petro” actually describes the Dantor form, illustrating commercial conflation.

### Literature

Dean Koontz’s horror novel *Darkfall* (1984) uses “Ti Jean Pie” or Ti Jean Pied-Fin as a supernatural ally of a murderous bokor. This materially reinforces the popular image of Ti Jean as a “black magic demon,” but is fiction shaped by the horror genre.

Derek Walcott’s *Ti-Jean and His Brothers* is based on the widespread Caribbean folktale hero “Little John.” It is not securely about Ti Jean Petro and should not be cited as a myth of the lwa.

### Visual art and music

Haitian sequined banners such as Eviland Lalanne’s *Ti Jan Pero* demonstrate the lwa’s continuing visual life. Recorded Vodou music and recent streaming releases continue to pair Ti Jean with Ezili or Dantor. These are living reception and devotion, not evidence for colonial iconography.

### Political appropriation

Petwo as a whole has been made a symbol of revolutionary rage, guerrilla struggle, and national resistance. That interpretation has historical roots in Dom Pedro’s documented anti-slavery activity but can become anachronistic when every individual Petwo lwa is assigned a personal role in the Revolution.

No credible evidence was found for a distinctive modern extremist movement centered on Ti Jean Petro.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### African survival versus creole creation

Herskovits emphasized African cultural continuities but also documented great variation. Apter shows that Herskovits’s own lists include spirits with unmistakably creole or European names, including Ti Jean Petro. Consequently, “African roots” must refer to structures, ritual techniques, or spirit types—not necessarily an African god of the same name.

### Dahomean versus Kongo origins

Deren’s serpent reading encourages comparison with Dahomean Dan/Dangbe. De Heusch instead emphasizes Kongo Nkita, *mhaka*, Kimpasi, and Zandor/Congo Savanne. Recent archival work on Dom Pedro and Lemba makes the Kongo interpretation historically stronger for the Petwo movement.

The evidence supports a layered conclusion: Petwo developed in Saint-Domingue from multiple African and creole materials, with a strong West-Central African historical component. Ti Jean’s personal name and documented form are Haitian.

### Dom Pedro’s identity

Older scholarship repeated Moreau’s “Black man of Spanish origin.” Recent judicial documents give Pierre “Dom Pedro,” place him within Saint-Domingue’s enslaved population, and reveal anti-slavery organization. *Dom* plausibly reflects Portuguese-Kongo rather than Spanish usage.

Still unestablished:

- that he was a Kongo priest;
- that he belonged formally to Lemba;
- that he was Jean-Philippe;
- that he became Ti Jean Petro;
- that Ti Jean was his biological child.

### One leg: African spirit, maroon memory, or serpent?

Three explanations coexist:

- Kongo spirit and ritual-complex parallels: de Heusch;
- serpent riddle: Deren;
- memory of maimed maroons or plantation punishment, including proposed comparison with the rebel Colas Jambes Coupées: modern scholarly hypothesis.

None is proved exclusively.

### Problems in the evidence

Vodou was transmitted largely through song, possession, household inheritance, and initiation. Written records were usually made by hostile colonial officials, missionaries, outsiders, or ethnographers. House secrecy and regional variation mean absence from print is not proof of absence from practice. Conversely, late oral testimony cannot by itself establish an eighteenth-century date.

### Critical scholarship

- Luc de Heusch, “Kongo in Haiti: A New Approach to Religious Syncretism” (1989).
- Andrew Apter, “On African Origins: Creolization and Connaissance in Haitian Vodou” (2002), together with his critique of Herskovitsian syncretism.
- Crystal Nicole Eddins, work on ritual rebels and the Haitian Revolution, including the newly recovered Dom Pedro judicial record.
- Kate Ramsey, *The Spirits and the Law*, for the political history of suppression and criminalization.
- David Geggus and later Bois Caïman scholarship, for the distinction between contemporary documentation and revolutionary mythmaking.

### Final assessment

The reconstructable Ti Jean Petro is not an ancient Dahomean god carried intact to Haiti. He is a **Haitian creole lwa whose first secure individual documentation is twentieth-century**, although he belongs to a Petwo historical field attested in Saint-Domingue from 1768.

His early ethnographic core is narrow but vivid: a forceful Petwo spirit, small and one-legged or footless, extraordinarily agile in trees, linked with Dan Petro, Marinette, Zandor, fire, and dangerous magical power. His fuller modern profile—snake-dragon, revolutionary god, patron of “black magic,” universal son of Ezili Dantor, Saint John, New Orleans deity, wielder of whip and pepper—is a layered combination of regional practice, scholarly inference, occult synthesis, and commercial repetition.

The most historically important correction concerns Don Pedro. The colonial person was neither securely “a Spaniard” nor demonstrably Ti Jean. Archival evidence instead reveals Pierre “Dom Pedro” as an organizer whose rites, opposition to plantation whipping, and promises of freedom alarmed colonial officials. This history helps explain the political temperature of Petwo, but the transformation from Dom Pedro’s movement to Ti Jean Petro remains an **unproven tradition of apotheosis**, not a documented succession.
