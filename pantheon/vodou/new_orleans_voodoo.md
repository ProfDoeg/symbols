# New Orleans Voodoo (the spirits): Research Dossier

> **Class:** A historically changing **collective of spirits/divinities**, not one divine being and not a single closed pantheon. In Haitian Vodou they are chiefly **lwa** (“spirits,” “mysteries”); in Fon and related West African traditions, **vodun** are divinities, powers, or spirit-beings; historical Louisiana Voudou preserved only a fragmentarily documented set of named spirits alongside ancestors, saints, magical powers, and deified or quasi-deified human figures.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** For the tradition’s West African antecedents, the bilingual *Doctrina Christiana* in Spanish and the language of Allada/Ardra, printed in **1658**, is the earliest substantial linguistic document bearing on the religious culture later called Vodun. The earliest securely documented named cult directly continuous with the dossier is the **Dangbé sacred-python cult at Whydah/Ouidah**, reported by seventeenth-century European writers and described in detail by Willem Bosman from observations made before his Dutch account appeared in **1704**. For colonial Saint-Domingue, Moreau de Saint-Méry’s description, published in **1797** but reporting eighteenth-century practice, is the foundational printed description of the *vaudoux* dance and serpent-centered rite. For New Orleans itself, the earliest presently identifiable explicit newspaper evidence is the Louisiana Gazette’s **16 August 1820** report, “Idolatry and Quackery,” concerning nighttime meetings and the worship of an “African deity.” Congo Square dances were independently witnessed by Benjamin Latrobe in **1819**, but he did not identify them as Voudou. Claims of a fully documented Louisiana pantheon before 1820 therefore exceed the evidence.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** contemporary or near-contemporary text, object, image, or institutional record.
- **[CE] Later canonical elaboration:** broadly established within a living tradition but documented later than the claimed origin.
- **[RV] Regional or house variant:** true of a locality, lineage, *sosyete*, temple, or informant, not necessarily universal.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** an ethnographer’s, priest’s, theologian’s, or occultist’s ordering of fluid practice.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** historically formed association, not an assertion that two figures were always identical.
- **[FL] Folklore or retrospective testimony:** culturally consequential but not contemporary corroboration.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or demonstrably modern reconfiguration.**
- **[U] Unestablished:** asserted, but the available evidence does not permit verification.

“New Orleans Voodoo,” “Haitian Vodou,” and West African “Vodun” are retained as separate historical formations. Similar names do not prove unchanged identity across the Atlantic.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The collective

**[CE/RV] Haitian Vodou.** The beings ordinarily served are the **lwa** (older French and English spelling *loa*), also called **mistè** (“mysteries”), **lespri** (“spirits”), or, in older ethnographies, “gods.” Practitioners generally distinguish the remote creator **Bondye**—from French *Bon Dieu*, “Good God”—from the lwa, who mediate particular domains and relationships. Lwa can be inherited, attached to a family or locality, associated with an African “nation,” revealed to a priest or house, or understood as an ancestor elevated after death. Consequently, no universally binding census exists. “The 401 lwa” or “1,001 lwa” is a formula of plenitude, not a fixed catalogue.

**[EA/CE] West African Vodun.** In Fon, Ewe, Gun, Xweda, and neighboring Gbe-speaking settings, forms such as **vodun/vodu/vodũ** designate divine or spiritual powers and sometimes the cult devoted to them. The word’s deeper etymology is debated; glosses such as “spirit,” “deity,” “mystery,” or “that which cannot be fully known” convey usage better than a single certain derivation. It did not originally name one standardized “religion of Dahomey.”

**[EA/MSR] Louisiana Voudou.** Nineteenth-century sources use *Voudou, Voudoux, Voodoo, Voudoo,* and related spellings both for the religion and its participants. Modern scholars often reserve **Voudou** or **Louisiana Voodoo** for the historical Louisiana complex and **Vodou** for Haiti. Historical New Orleans practice was an Afro-Creole religion shaped principally by West-Central African and Senegambian populations, later Haitian/Saint-Dominguan migration, Catholic practice, Native American materia medica, and Anglo-American conjure. Carolyn Morrow Long’s concise historical synthesis emphasizes this plural formation rather than a simple transplant from Haiti ([64 Parishes](https://64parishes.org/entry/voudou)).

### Major names and historical transformations

- **Vodun / Vodou / Voudou / Voodoo.** **[EA]** West African *vodun* is attested before the Haitian and Louisiana formations. French colonial *vaudoux* and related spellings were applied to Saint-Domingue rites. English *voodoo* later became both the usual Louisiana spelling and a derogatory metaphor for irrationality or fraudulent technique. That metaphor is a modern secular afterlife, not a description of the religion.

- **Lwa / loa.** **[CE]** Haitian Creole plural and singular are both *lwa*. Derivation from French *loi* (“law”) is often proposed, but historical-linguistic work treats spirit names and ritual vocabulary as a composite archive of Gbe, Kikongo, Yoruba, French, and Creole forms rather than assuming a single French origin. Hebblethwaite’s linguistic survey stresses that songs and rite names preserve some of the most substantial evidence of African origins ([University of Florida PDF](https://ufdcimages.uflib.ufl.edu/AA/00/02/55/96/00001/Hebblethwaite_Spirit_Migration.pdf)).

- **Rada.** **[EA/MSR]** Derived from **Arada/Allada**, the West African kingdom and slave-trade ethnonym. In Haiti, Rada lwa are conventionally “cool” or *dous* and treated as old/root spirits. This is ritual classification, not proof that every Rada lwa came unchanged from Allada.

- **Petwo/Petro.** **[CE/contested]** The “hot” Haitian rites and spirits. Explanations include a founding ritualist called Don Pedro and substantial Kongo/Lemba origins. No single origin theory accounts securely for every Petwo name and practice.

- **Nago/Anago.** **[EA/CE]** A regional ethnonym for Yoruba-speaking peoples, retained in Haitian spirit classification and especially associated with the Ogou complex.

- **Kongo, Ibo, Wangol, Siniga, Makaya, Gede.** **[CE/RV]** “Nations” or ritual groupings whose names can preserve African ethnonyms or later Haitian cult organization. Their membership varies by house.

### Specifically Louisiana names

- **Li Grand Zombi / Le Grand Zombi / Grand Zombi.** **[FL/MSR]** Later Louisiana accounts treat this as a supreme or major spirit and often as a serpent. *Zombi* plausibly reflects Kikongo **nzambi**, “god/divine power,” rather than the modern cinematic walking corpse. Yet contemporary nineteenth-century evidence establishing one fixed theology is weak. The famous claim that Marie Laveau owned a snake named Zombi is retrospective and cannot presently be substantiated.

- **Papa Lébat / Papa Labat / Liba / LaBas / Laba Limba.** **[FL/MSR]** Remembered as a trickster, gatekeeper, or opening spirit. Comparison with Haitian/Fon **Legba** is strong in function and sound, though exact Louisiana forms were recorded late. The occasionally repeated claim that the name is “unequivocally Yoruba” is erroneous: Legba is especially characteristic of Gbe traditions, while Yoruba **Èṣù-Elégbára** is related through long regional contact and comparison, not simple identity.

- **Blanc Dani / Monsieur Danny.** **[FL/MSR]** A poorly documented Louisiana divine name compared with West African **Dan/Dangbé** and Haitian **Danbala**. Some later interpretations conflate Blanc Dani, Grand Zombi, and a serpent power. The Louisiana evidence does not demonstrate that practitioners universally made those equations.

- **Onzancaire / On Sa Tier and other names in late collections.** **[FL/U]** Scattered recollections preserve names whose spellings and identities are unstable. They should not be inflated into a complete pantheon.

- **Marie Laveau.** A historical person, not securely a nineteenth-century goddess. **[FL/MI]** After her death she became an ancestor-like recipient of petitions, offerings, grave markings, pilgrimage, art, ritual address, and tourist devotion. Her modern cult therefore belongs to the reception history of the spirits, not to an invented divine biography.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### West Africa

1. **The 1658 *Doctrina Christiana*.** **[EA]** Printed in Spanish and the language of Ardra/Allada, it is the earliest important linguistic witness for the cultural region. Because it is a missionary catechism, it filters local categories through Christian questions; it is not a neutral pantheon list. Its existence and importance are discussed in recent historical work on Christianity and Vodun formation ([Taylor & Francis](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/0144039X.2022.2101598)).

2. **Seventeenth-century Whydah reports.** **[EA, hostile outsider source]** Charles Chaulmer (1661), Olfert Dapper (1668), and John Ogilby (1670) report coastal religious practices. They are early, but frequently derivative and framed through “idolatry.”

3. **Willem Bosman, Dutch text 1704.** **[EA]** Bosman supplies the earliest detailed published description of Whydah’s serpent cult: a creator remote from ordinary sacrifice, subsidiary divine powers, and the sacred snake invoked in crises. His account is a European commercial observer’s report, not an indigenous scripture.

4. **Des Marchais/Labat, observations of 1725, publication 1730–32.** **[EA]** Describes the Whydah royal procession for the serpent and the initiation or dedication of female ritual personnel. The Ouidah Dangbé temple is thus not a recent tourist fabrication; its cult was already reported in the seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries. A scholarly review notes its documented seventeenth-century antiquity ([Persée](https://www.persee.fr/doc/jafr_0037-9166_1975_num_45_1_1769_t1_0232_0000_3)).

5. **Royal Dahomean reorganization, ca. 1740 onward.** **[EA through later oral histories/MSR]** Traditions credit **Hwanjile**, *kpojito* of King Tegbesu, with importing or elevating Mawu and Lisa and reorganizing the royal religious hierarchy. This was a political centralization of diverse cults, not the creation of all Vodun. Modern historical synthesis places it in Tegbesu’s reign, 1740–74 ([Cambridge chapter](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/2FCBF92A767FD8DE3615602F589C326E/9781009256155AR.pdf/Rituals__Runaways__and_the_Haitian_Revolution.pdf?event-type=FTLA)).

### Saint-Domingue and Haiti

1. **Moreau de Saint-Méry, *Description topographique…*, 1797.** **[EA, retrospective colonial witness]** Describes an assembly called *vaudoux*, a serpent kept in a box, a presiding king and queen, oath-taking, possession-like states, dance, and consultation. The description is indispensable but should not be treated as a transcript of every Saint-Dominguan rite. Moreau wrote as a white colonial lawyer and defender of the colonial order.

2. **Bois Caïman accounts.** **[contested]** No eyewitness record from August 1791 survives. Antoine Dalmas’s history, published **1814**, is the earliest known narrative source; Hérard Dumesle (1824) and Céligny Ardouin (later nineteenth century) reshape the story. Léon-François Hoffmann argued that the canonical ceremony is a developing national myth; David Geggus concluded that a religious ceremony involving a pig sacrifice probably occurred but that Boukman’s precise role, the famous prayer, blood-drinking, and the ceremony’s causal relation to the revolt are progressively elaborated. The sources place political planning before or alongside religious oath-making, not conclusively as its product ([Hoffmann](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ecreo_0708-2398_1990_num_13_1_1307); [Geggus summary](https://rasanblaj.ht/library/geggus-marronagevoodoosaint-1992?lang=en); [Hurbon collection](https://www.macrocosme.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/Linsurrection-des-esclaves-de-Saint-Domingue-auteur-laennec-hurbon.pdf)).

3. **Nineteenth-century codes, prosecutions, and polemics.** **[EA]** These document practices through hostile law: divination, ritual objects, dances, healing, animal sacrifice, and gatherings. Haitian law prohibited many popular ritual practices from **1835 to 1987**, creating a record of what authorities attempted to control ([University of Chicago Press](https://press.uchicago.edu/ucp/books/book/chicago/S/bo10454972.html)).

4. **Ethnographic corpus.**

   - Melville J. Herskovits, *Life in a Haitian Valley* (1937) and *Dahomey* (1938): comparative ethnography, influential but inclined to organize practice into African “retentions.”
   - Bernard Maupoil, *La Géomancie à l’ancienne Côte des Esclaves* (1943): extensive study of Fa divination, oral narratives, Legba, and the coastal pantheon; invaluable but itself a colonial-era ethnographic arrangement ([bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/La_g%C3%A9omancie_%C3%A0_l_ancienne_co%C3%82te_des_es.html?id=k3pGAAAAMAAJ)).
   - Maya Deren, *Divine Horsemen* (1953): participant-observation from 1947 onward, especially possession, ritual movement, and major lwa; its chapter sequence itself systematizes Legba, Gede, Danbala, Simbi, Agwe, Ogou, and Ezili ([full text](https://www.multiple-secularities.de/media/deren_1953_divine_horsemen.pdf)).
   - Alfred Métraux, *Le Vaudou haïtien* (1958; English 1959): broad mid-century synthesis of history, temple organization, initiation, magic, possession, and persecution ([Persée review](https://www.persee.fr/doc/caoum_0373-5834_1959_num_12_48_3976_t1_0422_0000_1)).
   - Milo Rigaud, *La Tradition voudoo et le voudoo haïtien* and *Vèvè*: influential priestly/learned systematization, especially diagrams. Rigaud’s drawings are documentation of particular mid-century forms, not proof that every reproduced internet vèvè is ancient.
   - Leslie Desmangles, *The Faces of the Gods* (1992): interprets Vodou-Catholic relations as a creole “symbiosis,” not a mere disguise laid over unchanged African gods ([UNC Press](https://uncpress.org/9780807843932/the-faces-of-the-gods/)).
   - Karen McCarthy Brown, *Mama Lola* (1991): life history and ritual ethnography centered on the Brooklyn Haitian priestess Alourdes and her family spirits; evidence for one transnational lineage, not a universal catechism.
   - Patrick Bellegarde-Smith and Claudine Michel, eds., *Haitian Vodou: Spirit, Myth, and Reality* and related collections: Haitian and diasporic approaches correcting the older tendency to treat believers solely as ethnographic objects.
   - Rachel Beauvoir-Dominique and Max Beauvoir: priestly and ethnographic documentation, especially songs, rites, and the multiplicity of spirit nations.
   - Laënnec Hurbon: historical sociology of persecution, state power, Protestant/Catholic relations, and Vodou’s changing public status.

### Louisiana corpus

1. **Benjamin Latrobe’s journal, 1819.** **[EA]** Records hundreds of enslaved people dancing on Sundays at Congo Square, with drums, dress, bells, shells, and distinct groups. It attests public African-derived music and dance, but not, by itself, a ceremony for Grand Zombi or Marie Laveau.

2. **Louisiana Gazette, 16 August 1820, “Idolatry and Quackery.”** **[EA, hostile press]** The earliest explicit New Orleans notice presently recoverable describes illegal night meetings and “idolatrous worship of an African deity.” Arrests followed. Its language shows official alarm but supplies little reliable theology ([Notre Dame Historical Review](https://sites.nd.edu/historical-review/files/2025/05/1.1-Full-Journal-1.pdf)).

3. **Police reports, newspapers, and court reports, 1820s–1870s.** **[EA, institutionally hostile]** These record raids, mixed gatherings, women leaders, drums, dances, food, alcohol, charms, and St John’s Eve assemblies. A New York Historical Society finding aid identifies an 1824 police report mentioning Marie Laveau ([NYU finding aid](https://findingaids.library.nyu.edu/nyhs/ms1944_new_orleans_collection/contents/aspace_ref172_e8o/)).

4. **St John’s Eve press accounts, 1869–75.** **[EA but sensationalized]** The first known published description dates only to 1869. Reports multiply in 1870, 1872, and 1875, describing waterside gatherings, fires, bathing, dancing, food, songs, and a presiding “queen.” Some supposedly Louisiana descriptions were copied from Moreau de Saint-Méry’s Haitian account. This textual recycling is a major warning against treating newspaper detail as independent eyewitness corroboration ([University of Georgia thesis](https://getd.libs.uga.edu/pdfs/busby_kendra_m_201505_ma.pdf)).

5. **Louisiana Writers’ Project files, late 1930s–early 1940s.** **[FL/EA for memory]** Roughly seventy elderly Black New Orleanians were interviewed about late nineteenth-century practice, especially Marie Laveau, Dr John, queens, charms, songs, and ceremonies. The interviews document remembered tradition and the 1930s politics of recollection, not direct observation of the 1840s. Interviewer race, prompting, transcription, and editorial selection affected what survives. The Robert Tallant archive includes these transcripts and his working materials ([New Orleans City Archives](https://archivesnolalibrary.as.atlas-sys.com/repositories/3/archival_objects/69303)).

6. **Robert Tallant, *Voodoo in New Orleans* (1946).** **[MI mixed with archival fragments]** Tallant popularized genuine interview material but combined it with unattributed borrowing, invented dialogue, sensational narrative, and claims contradicted by records. He knowingly portrayed Marie Laveau’s father as a rich white planter although LWP materials identified him as a free man of color involved in real estate and slave trading. Tallant is evidence for twentieth-century reinvention, never a safe uncorroborated source for nineteenth-century ritual ([Louisiana History review](https://carolynmorrowlong.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/11/Louisiana-History-2008.pdf)).

7. **Carolyn Morrow Long, *Spiritual Merchants* (2001) and *A New Orleans Voudou Priestess* (2006).** **[MSR]** Long combines sacramental registers, notarial files, newspapers, court documents, directories, archival interviews, and material-culture research. Her work establishes the sharpest division presently possible between the documented Marie Laveau and the literary-tourist figure ([University Press of Florida](https://floridapress.org/9780813032146/a-new-orleans-voudou-priestess/); [University of Tennessee Press](https://utpress.org/9781572331105/spiritual-merchants/)).

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

There is no collective humanoid portrait of “the spirits.” Their most characteristic shared mode is **manifestation through service and possession**.

### Ritual manifestation

**[CE]** A successful possession is commonly described as a lwa “mounting” a human “horse.” Speech, posture, taste, clothing, gesture, and action identify the possessing spirit. Possession is therefore living iconography: Ogou may demand rum, a saber, military bearing, or political speech; Gede may wear black and purple, sunglasses, and enact obscene comedy; Ezili Freda may request perfume, jewelry, and refined foods; Danbala may move serpent-like, climb, whistle, or avoid ordinary speech. Deren’s “divine horsemen” formulation made this image famous, but it records a practitioner metaphor rather than inventing it.

### Vèvè

**[CE/RV]** A **vèvè** is a ritually drawn emblem, normally traced on the ground with cornmeal, flour, ash, brick dust, coffee grounds, or other powder. It invites, honors, and locally identifies a lwa. Designs vary by house and draftsman. Published plates, especially those associated with Milo Rigaud, encouraged the false modern idea that each lwa owns one invariant occult “sigil.” The Library of Congress’s Rigaud/Keller collection explicitly preserves discussion of vèvè and Beauvoir’s assessment of Rigaud’s treatment ([LOC finding aid](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/service/gdc/gdcfindingaidpdfs/af022001/af022001.pdf)).

### Principal Haitian spirit complexes

- **Legba.** **[CE]** Gate, threshold, speech, access, and ritual opening. His permission is normally sought first. Old-man imagery is especially Haitian Rada; fiery or youthful crossroads forms belong to other rites and should not all be collapsed into one person.
- **Danbala Wèdo and Ayida Wèdo.** **[CE/SI]** Serpent, sky, ancestral continuity, rainbows, purity, cooling power. White is prominent. Their comparison with West African Dan/Dangbé/Aido-Hwedo is historically strong, but Haitian relationships are creolized.
- **Ogou family.** **[CE/RV]** Iron, warfare, labor, technology, politics, masculine authority, and revolutionary memory. Multiple Ogou are not merely epithets of one universally fixed god.
- **Ezili family.** **[CE/RV]** Love, luxury, maternity, anger, protection, grief, sexuality, and political violence in contrasting manifestations such as Ezili Freda and Ezili Dantò.
- **Agwe and Lasirèn.** **[CE]** Sea, ships, wealth, music, beauty, and the dangerous underwater world.
- **Azaka/Kouzen Zaka.** **[CE]** Agriculture, peasant labor, thrift, jealousy, and rural social identity.
- **Loko and Ayizan.** **[CE]** Priesthood, initiation, ritual authority, sacred trees, and transmission.
- **Gede and the Barons.** **[CE/RV]** Death, cemeteries, sexuality, fertility-through-decay, healing, satire, and social truth-telling.
- **Marasa.** **[CE]** Sacred twins whose doubleness exceeds ordinary arithmetic; Deren’s formula “two and two equals five” describes the twins plus their unifying power.
- **Simbi family.** **[CE/RV]** Water, springs, magic, communication, and in some modern houses technological mediation.

Colors, foods, days, saints, and songs vary. Internet tables that assign one exact color, weekday, planet, tarot card, and crystal to every lwa are generally **[MI/occult systematization]** unless supported by a named house or ethnography.

### Louisiana attributes

**[EA/FL]** The surviving record emphasizes collective dancing, drumming, songs, water rites, spirit consultation, healing, divination, protection, coercive magic, and charms more than elaborate deity biographies. Snakes appear persistently but ambiguously—as living ritual animals, symbols of Grand Zombi or Blanc Dani, and sensational press shorthand.

**Gris-gris.** The term refers to prepared protective, coercive, healing, luck, love, or harmful packets and objects, with West African and Islamic/Senegambian antecedents and later Catholic and commercial ingredients. It is not the name of the religion.

**Hoodoo distinction.** **[MSR]** Hoodoo/conjure/rootwork is primarily a repertoire of African American practical magic and healing; Louisiana Voudou was a communal religion with spirits, leaders, songs, dances, and calendar rites. They overlapped heavily. By the early twentieth century, as organized Louisiana Voudou declined, “Voodoo” was increasingly used for individual hoodoo services and commercial spiritual products. Treating them as eternally identical obscures that historical transition.

## 4. Genealogy and relations among divine beings

### Why no single family tree exists

Vodou has no universally authoritative scripture or central hierarchy. “Family” may mean African origin, ritual nation, complementary pair, marriage enacted in possession, a house’s inherited grouping, or a scholarly reconstruction. West African royal theology, Haitian temple practice, and Louisiana memory must therefore be charted separately.

### West African learned and royal configurations

**[LS based on oral traditions recorded in the twentieth century]** Herskovits’s Dahomean scheme places **Nana Buluku** before the paired creator **Mawu-Lisa**. Mawu and Lisa generate or govern divisions represented by sky/thunder, earth, and sea powers; **Legba** mediates divine communication; **Aido-Hwedo** supports or shapes the world. This is influential but not an untouched “ancient African Bible.” Hwanjile’s eighteenth-century state cult elevated Mawu-Lisa over local vodun, while local shrines retained other genealogies and priorities.

### Haitian relational complexes

- Danbala and Ayida Wèdo are spouses or complementary serpent powers.
- Agwe and Lasirèn are paired in many houses.
- Loko and Ayizan are paired as guardians of priesthood and initiation.
- Freda is linked with Ogou in erotic and ritual narratives, but the particular Ogou and emotional outcome vary.
- Gede Nibo, Bawon Samdi, Grann Brijit, and other cemetery powers form shifting kinship and authority structures.
- The Marasa precede or outrank ordinary persons and, in some ritual sequences, are served with the ancestors and the dead.

### Discrete documented divine interactions

The corpus is oral, performative, and house-variable; there can be no demonstrably exhaustive list of every interaction ever narrated. ## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Creation through Mawu-Lisa and Aido-Hwedo

**[LS/RV]** In the best-known Dahomean reconstruction, Nana Buluku produces Mawu and Lisa; the paired powers order creation, while the serpent Aido-Hwedo assists and supports the world. Legba receives mediation and communication. Herskovits recorded this in 1930s Dahomey and made it internationally canonical. The historical core is a set of West African oral cult traditions; the polished genealogy is a twentieth-century ethnographic presentation shaped by the royal elevation of Mawu-Lisa beginning in the eighteenth century. It cannot be projected unchanged into seventeenth-century Whydah or every Haitian house.

### Dangbé, ancestor and protector of Whydah

**[EA plus oral variants]** Dangbé is the sacred python and benefactor of the Xweda/Houéda. Origin traditions variously make the python an ancestral protector encountered during migration, a battle helper, or an eponymic ancestor. All agree more securely on Xweda ownership of the cult than on one origin episode. Following Dahomey’s conquest of Whydah in 1727, the conquerors retained the serpent cult for political as well as religious reasons. Ouidah research identifies the cult as originally lineage-based and subsequently civic, while priestly control remained with particular families ([IRD study](https://horizon.documentation.ird.fr/exl-doc/pleins_textes/pleins_textes_7/carton06/35121.pdf)).

### Bois Caïman

**[contested historical legend]** The received story says that in August 1791 enslaved conspirators assembled at Bwa Kayiman; Boukman led prayer and oath-taking; a priestess sacrificed a black pig, its blood bound the participants, and the ceremony inaugurated the uprising.

The evidentiary layers are:

1. **[EA]** A coordinated uprising began in northern Saint-Domingue in August 1791.
2. **[later near-contemporary]** Dalmas, 1814, supplies the earliest known religious-sacrificial account.
3. **[nineteenth-century elaboration]** Dumesle and Ardouin add or reshape speeches, actors, and national significance.
4. **[modern national canon]** Boukman’s famous Creole prayer, Cécile Fatiman’s standardized role, Ezili Dantò possessing the priestess, an exact date of 14 August, and direct causation of the Revolution are not equally secured by early evidence.
5. **[MSR dispute]** Hoffmann treats the received account principally as myth formation; Geggus judges a pig-sacrifice ceremony probable but denies proof that Vodou supplied the rebellion’s organization or original political decision. Hurbon argues that even if individual canonical details are late, religion cannot be severed from the insurgents’ social world.

The proper conclusion is neither “everything happened exactly as later told” nor “nothing happened”: an oath-bearing religious gathering is plausible and probably attested indirectly; its now-canonical script is layered.

### Makandal

**[EA/FL]** François Makandal, executed in 1758, was a maroon organizer and poisoner in colonial records. Later traditions turn him into a prophetic Vodou priest who escaped execution or survived spiritually. “Makandal the revolutionary houngan” is a powerful Haitian memory but not a title securely used in the surviving trial record. His legend became a template linking African knowledge, poison, metamorphosis, and liberation.

### The lwa crossing the Atlantic

**[CE/MSR]** Ritual songs and prayers often locate the spirits in **Ginen**—Africa, ancestral depth, or the spiritual homeland “beneath the water.” Service retrieves, renews, or calls them across this divide. This is not one fixed migration epic; it is a ritual geography encoding enslavement, death, ancestry, and continuity.

### Louisiana’s serpent sovereign

**[FL/U]** Later accounts narrate Grand Zombi as the great serpent or supreme African power served by New Orleans Voudous. The likely linguistic bridge from Kikongo *Nzambi* is strong; the full mythic portrait is not. Tallant and tourism converted a fragmentary sacred name into a stable character with a biography and visual brand.

### Marie Laveau’s legendary cycle

**[documented person plus folklore]** Repeated stories make Laveau an immortal queen, owner of a snake called Zombi, hairdresser who extracted elite women’s secrets, political fixer who controlled judges, miracle-worker who freed prisoners, and ageless woman secretly replaced by a daughter. Long’s archival work establishes a free woman of color, devout Catholic, partner of Christophe Glapion, mother, property holder, healer and prison visitor, and a woman publicly remembered as a Voudou leader. It does not substantiate the espionage system, universal political control, pet serpent, or seamless replacement by “Marie II.” The doubling legend probably condensed several women, daughters, and successive ritual leaders.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### West African institutions

**[EA/CE]** Vodun cults were historically organized through royal, civic, lineage, and shrine institutions rather than one church. Priests and priestesses underwent training, restrictions, possession disciplines, and custodianship of shrines. The Royal Palaces of Abomey embodied the integration of kingship, ancestors, conquest, and vodun. Hwanjile’s religious reorganization helped legitimate Tegbesu’s contested succession.

**Dangbéxwé, Ouidah.** The python temple is the best-documented surviving named sanctuary relevant to Louisiana serpent traditions. Its present structure has changed, but the cult site and civic veneration are early-attested. Killing a python was historically prohibited and could provoke ritual or political punishment.

**Fa divination.** **[EA/LS]** A trained diviner reads configurations generated through nuts or chains and interprets a corpus of signs, narratives, prescriptions, and sacrifices. Maupoil’s 1943 volume is the classic documentary source, but it freezes a living oral system at one historical moment.

**Human sacrifice in Dahomey.** **[EA, scale contested]** Royal Dahomey conducted documented human sacrifices, especially in royal funerary and annual customs, commonly using captives or condemned persons. European witnesses, abolitionists, diplomats, and colonial advocates often exaggerated numbers and framed the practice to serve political agendas; complete denial is equally untenable. The sacrifices belonged particularly to royal-ancestral state ritual and warfare, not to every domestic Vodun ceremony and not to Haitian or Louisiana Vodou as such.

### Haitian service

A temple may be called **ounfò/hounfor**; its central post is the **poto mitan**, around which spirits descend and participants dance. A male priest is an **oungan/houngan**, a female priest a **manbo/mambo**. Initiatory ranks and titles vary.

A service may include:

1. Catholic prayers or songs;
2. salutes to cardinal directions and ritual authorities;
3. drawing vèvè;
4. libations and offerings;
5. sequenced drum rhythms and songs for different nations;
6. dance;
7. possession;
8. consultation, healing, prophecy, rebuke, or blessing by the manifested lwa;
9. animal sacrifice where required;
10. communal cooking and distribution of consecrated food.

**Animal sacrifice.** **[CE]** Chickens, goats, pigs, cattle, and other animals may be offered according to the lwa and the economic capacity of sponsors. Killing and cooking are ritually regulated; much meat becomes communal food. It should neither be concealed nor confused with human sacrifice.

**Human sacrifice and ritual murder.** **[contested/alleged]** No normal Haitian Vodou liturgy documented by the principal ethnographers prescribes human sacrifice. The **Bizoton affair of 1863–64**, in which several people were convicted of killing and allegedly eating a child, became the central colonial proof-text for “Voodoo cannibalism.” The trial occurred under political pressure, relied on disputed confessions, and was amplified by British diplomat Spenser St John. Ramsey emphasizes conflicting reports and the affair’s role in producing anti-Vodou law and representation. It cannot prove a general cult practice; neither can the weak record determine every factual detail of the alleged murder.

**Offerings and sacred matter.** Foods, rum, water, coffee, perfumes, candles, flowers, cloth, metal implements, and Catholic images serve particular lwa. Material correspondences are relational and house-specific, not a universal occult periodic table.

### New Orleans

#### Congo Square

**[EA]** Colonial and American authorities allowed or confined enslaved people’s Sunday markets and dances to designated spaces. A municipal ordinance in 1817 restricted enslaved dancing to Sundays and approved sites. Latrobe’s 1819 description records 500–600 people in culturally differentiated music-and-dance groups. Congo Square was an incubator of African-derived performance and exchange; later claims that it was specifically “Grand Zombi’s chosen haunt” first appear in much later guidebooks and are **[FL]**, not Latrobe’s testimony. A turn-of-the-century guide did repeat that claim while acknowledging legal suppression ([Library of Congress scan](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/public/gdcmassbookdig/picayunesguideto00newo/picayunesguideto00newo_bw.pdf)).

#### St John’s Eve

**[EA from 1869 onward/FL for earlier details]** Celebrated on the night of 23 June, the eve of John the Baptist’s feast, it combined Catholic calendar, midsummer water symbolism, bathing, fires, dance, food, and leadership by Voudou queens. Sites included Lake Pontchartrain and waterways connected with Bayou St John. The best-known 1872 report describes wishes made while adding wood to a fire and songs addressed to “Mamzelle Marie.” Because the earliest published account is only 1869 and reporting was aggressively sensational, claims about exact antebellum liturgy remain uncertain.

#### Leadership

Nineteenth-century Louisiana Voudou appears unusually visible through female ritual leaders—Marie Laveau, Sanité Dédé, Betsey Toledano, and others named in records or recollection. “Queen” could denote real religious authority, popular reputation, press invention, or all three. There was no documented citywide episcopal institution.

#### Gris-gris and healing

Packets could contain roots, herbs, bones, hair, nails, cloth, written prayers, coins, animal material, or manufactured products. Practitioners provided protection, luck, love, uncrossing, vengeance, healing, and legal aid. Catholic candles, psalms, saint images, and blessed objects could coexist with African-derived materials. Long traces the twentieth-century shift from individually prepared objects to mass-produced oils, powders, candles, and labeled curios.

#### Suppression

Voudou was not generally outlawed under one statute bearing its name. Authorities used laws governing enslaved assembly, nighttime meetings, racial mixing, public order, fraud, medicine, and dance. Police raids repeatedly detained women and broke up ceremonies. During the Civil War occupation and Reconstruction-era racial anxiety, enforcement and hostile reporting intensified. This indirect legal method makes “Voodoo was legal” and “Voodoo was banned” both misleading without qualification.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1658: oral and archaeological past

**[U/MSR]** The constituent traditions are older than their documentation, but exact antiquity cannot be assigned to named myths merely because they were later called “ancient.” Archaeology, language, dynastic tradition, and comparative ritual establish deep regional roots without producing a pristine original pantheon.

### 1658–1727: coastal documentation

Missionary and travel accounts begin recording Allada and Whydah categories. The Dangbé python cult is securely visible. Sources are overwhelmingly European and interested in conversion or trade.

### 1727–1791: Dahomean centralization and Atlantic dispersion

Dahomey conquered Allada and Whydah, retained and reorganized conquered cults, and expanded participation in Atlantic slavery. Hwanjile and Tegbesu’s elevation of Mawu-Lisa illustrates that “traditional pantheons” were already products of political change. Enslaved Fon, Xweda, Aja, Yoruba, Kongo, and many others carried ritual languages and practices to Saint-Domingue and Louisiana.

### 1791–1804: revolution and creolization

Saint-Domingue’s African-derived rites helped create trust, healing, oath, and collective identity, but surviving evidence does not justify making Vodou a centralized revolutionary command system. Refugees—white, free colored, and enslaved—moved first to Cuba and then, especially in 1809–10, to New Orleans, profoundly reshaping its Francophone and Afro-Creole population.

### 1804–1860: Haitian consolidation; Louisiana emergence

Haiti’s isolation from Rome and weakness of parish infrastructure allowed African-derived and Catholic practices to develop in close interaction. In New Orleans, Congo Square, Haitian immigration, local Kongo/Senegambian traditions, Catholic confraternal life, healing, and charms formed a distinct Voudou. The 1820 newspaper report and later police records provide the first explicit local evidence.

### 1860–1915: concordat, persecution, and the high visibility of Louisiana Voudou

Haiti’s 1860 Concordat restored institutional Catholic power. Laws and clerical campaigns targeted “sortilèges” and “superstition.” In New Orleans, St John’s Eve celebrations attracted reporters and tourists; Marie Laveau became the public emblem of the religion. Her death in 1881 shifted reporting toward memorial legend. Organized Voudou declined under policing, Protestant and Catholic pressure, racial segregation, professional medicine, and dispersal of ritual communities.

### 1915–34: US occupation and the exoticizing gaze

US occupation journalism and fiction popularized zombies, cannibalism, drums, and despotic sorcery. Simultaneously, Haitian **indigénisme**, especially Jean Price-Mars’s *Ainsi parla l’oncle* (1928), reclassified Vodou as religion and national culture rather than mere superstition.

### 1930s–1950s: ethnographic canon formation

Herskovits, Maupoil, Deren, Métraux, Rigaud, and the Haitian Bureau of Ethnology documented spirits and rites. Their books preserved otherwise vulnerable material but also created the modern printed pantheon. In Louisiana, LWP interviews captured fading memories; Tallant converted them into a sensational narrative.

### 1939–42: Haitian anti-superstition campaign

The Catholic hierarchy, with state assistance, demanded public oaths renouncing Vodou, destroyed ritual objects and sanctuaries, and represented lwa as Satanic. The principal intense campaign occurred in 1941–42, with earlier campaigns in the 1890s and 1910s. Hurbon emphasizes the campaign’s association with elite “civilizing” projects and notes Jacques Roumain’s contemporary opposition ([Hurbon](https://classiques.uqam.ca/contemporains/hurbon_laennec/statut_du_vodou_hist_anthropo/statut_du_vodou_hist_anthropo_texte.html)).

### 1957–86: Duvalierism

François Duvalier appropriated Vodou symbols, cultivated a supernatural persona, and incorporated selected oungans into rural political control. This was state patronage and coercive appropriation, not rule by “the Vodou church.” After Jean-Claude Duvalier’s fall in 1986, **dechoukaj** violence targeted people perceived as regime collaborators, including ritual specialists.

### 1970s–present: heritage, diaspora, and revival

New Orleans’s Historic Voodoo Museum dates to 1972 ([museum site](https://voodoomuseum.com/)). Haitian immigration and initiates of Haitian Vodou, Cuban-derived religions, Spiritual Churches, and African traditions produced new communities. Miriam Chamani founded the Voodoo Spiritual Temple in 1990; Sallie Ann Glassman’s La Source Ancienne and Island of Salvation botanica introduced Haitian-derived service and visual art.

Scholars disagree over continuity. Some practitioners understand present work as the survival or renewal of Louisiana tradition. Jeffrey Anderson argues that historical Mississippi Valley Voodoo probably ceased as an intact living religion after the 1940s and that present “New Orleans Voodoo” is an emerging reconstruction primarily drawing on Haitian and West African systems ([Nova Religio](https://online.ucpress.edu/nr/article-abstract/26/4/33/196051/Mississippi-River-Valley-VoodooA-Living-Tradition?redirectedFrom=fulltext)). This does not make contemporary practice unreal; it dates its institutional lineage.

### Legal recognition in Haiti

President Jean-Bertrand Aristide’s decree of **4 April 2003** recognized Vodou as a religion in its own right and allowed recognized priests to perform legally consequential ceremonies such as marriages and baptisms subject to regulation ([decree text](https://www.haitianaute.com/2021/11/arrete-presidentiel-du-4-avril-2003-de.html)). Recognition did not end social hostility, especially from some Catholic and evangelical communities.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Occult and esoteric appropriations

**[MI]** Nineteenth- and twentieth-century European and American occult writers extracted “voodoo” from living communities and recast it as black magic, sexual primitivism, secret societies, or a transferable spell system. Later ceremonial magicians assigned lwa fixed planetary, tarot, cabalistic, and color correspondences. Unless documented in a named Vodou lineage, those charts are modern occult syntheses.

**[MI]** The commercial “voodoo doll” pierced with pins is largely a Euro-American magical and entertainment composite. Figurines and packet-objects occur in African diasporic practice, including objects manipulated for healing or coercion, but the mass-market pin-doll sold as the defining emblem of Vodou is not an ancient universal rite.

### Literature, film, television, and games

Works such as William Seabrook’s *The Magic Island* (1929), *White Zombie* (1932), later zombie cinema, James Bond’s *Live and Let Die*, Anne Rice’s fiction, Disney’s *The Princess and the Frog*, and *American Horror Story: Coven* materially shaped the international image. They combine Haitian zombies, Louisiana Voudou, hoodoo, demonic magic, and invented ritual. Their images are evidence for reception, not for earlier religious practice.

Marie Laveau’s portrayal as an immortal witch or combat magician is especially modern. It has largely displaced the archival woman—Catholic, free woman of color, healer, family head, prison visitor, and reputed ritual leader.

### Tourist Voodoo

New Orleans now supports museums, walking tours, botanicas, ritual shops, ghost tours, altars, festivals, and souvenir commerce. These exist on a continuum:

- practitioner-run religious institutions;
- heritage interpretation;
- mixed commercial-religious spaces;
- entertainment relying on horror stereotypes;
- generic occult retail labeled “Voodoo.”

A 2022 ethnography examined Congo Square, the Historic Voodoo Museum, Voodoo Authentica, museums, botanicas, tours, and an initiation, finding divergent presentations from spiritually meaningful heritage to the deliberate blending of history and ghost-story fiction ([Kennesaw State thesis](https://digitalcommons.kennesaw.edu/art_etd/4/)). “Tourist Voodoo” is therefore not one fraud, but its market rewards certainty, spectacle, dolls, curses, and a simplified Marie Laveau.

### Modern political and cultural uses

Vodou has served Haitian nationalism, Black cultural recovery, dictatorship, revolutionary memory, art, music, and diaspora community-building. It has also been scapegoated as the cause of poverty, dictatorship, earthquakes, and political failure. Such claims reproduce the nineteenth-century theory that African religion obstructs “civilization”; Ramsey’s archival work shows that this rhetoric repeatedly justified coercion.

## 9. Historiography and contested-material audit

### Origin: “Dahomean religion transplanted intact”

**Finding: rejected in simple form.** **[MSR]** Fon/Dahomean elements are fundamental, especially in Rada vocabulary and spirits, but Kongo, Yoruba/Nago, Igbo, Senegambian, Catholic, Masonic, French magical, and locally generated Haitian elements are also documented. Louisiana’s demographic foundation was even more heavily West-Central African and Senegambian than the standard “Dahomey-to-Haiti-to-New Orleans” diagram suggests.

### Etymology: Vodou means simply “god”

**Finding: approximate, not exhaustive.** Gbe *vodun* can denote a deity or spiritual power, but precise etymological claims such as “the unknowable,” “to draw water,” or one literal root should be presented as disputed unless tied to a linguistic study.

### Syncretism: saints were merely disguises

**Finding: inadequate.** **[SI/MSR]** Coercive Catholicization encouraged correspondences, but practitioners also became Catholics and developed genuine ritual relationships with saints, sacraments, prayers, images, and feast days. Desmangles’s “symbiosis” model better describes simultaneous identification and difference. Saint Peter is not timelessly identical to Legba, nor Mater Dolorosa to Ezili Freda; these are historically situated associations that differ by image and house.

### Bois Caïman

**Finding: probable ritual gathering; canonical details uneven.** The sacrifice has earlier support than many named participants and speeches. No contemporary 1791 eyewitness document establishes the familiar complete narrative. The claim that “Bwa Kayiman” means “house of an imam” and proves an Islamic ceremony is **[MI]** folk etymology unsupported by the early documentation.

### Human sacrifice and cannibalism

**Finding:** Royal Dahomean human sacrifice is documented, although numbers are contested. Haitian Vodou has no established normative human-sacrifice rite. The Bizoton prosecution concerns an alleged crime and compromised trial, not proof of ordinary worship. New Orleans newspapers’ “orgy,” sacrifice, and cannibal language must be independently corroborated; it often repeats Atlantic anti-Black polemic.

### Temple prostitution

**Finding: not established.** Some European descriptions sexualized female initiation, ritual marriage to a deity, and priestly residence. No reliable evidence supports a general institution of “temple prostitution” in Fon Vodun, Haitian Vodou, or Louisiana Voudou. Sexual relations, exploitation, and gendered authority existed as social facts, but the specific institution is unproven.

### Tallant

**Finding: indispensable reception source, unreliable sole historical authority.** His access to LWP files preserves clues, but his inventions and racialized sensationalism require claim-by-claim checking. Material appearing only in Tallant should be labeled **[MI/U]**.

### Li Grand Zombi

**Finding: name plausibly old; full modern character insecure.** The Kikongo comparison with *Nzambi* is persuasive. Identification with Danbala, Dangbé, Blanc Dani, or Marie Laveau’s snake is possible at the level of historical convergence but not proved as a single nineteenth-century doctrine.

### Papa Lébat

**Finding: likely Legba-related; details late.** Gatekeeper/trickster functions and the name support comparison. A fully specified Louisiana Legba with Haitian iconography, saint, colors, weekday, and vèvè is usually a modern reconstruction.

### Congo Square as a Voodoo temple

**Finding: overstatement.** It was a regulated market-and-dance ground where African-derived practices circulated. Some participants may have been Voudou practitioners. Latrobe did not document it as a dedicated temple or record Grand Zombi worship there.

### Continuous New Orleans Voodoo

**Finding: disputed.** Long allows survival, transformation, and revival; Anderson emphasizes rupture and reconstruction. Present practitioners’ religious legitimacy does not depend on proving unbroken institutional descent, but historical claims of continuity require lineage evidence.

### Archives and losses

Principal evidence survives in:

- New Orleans Public Library/City Archives;
- Louisiana Writers’ Project and Robert Tallant papers;
- Historic New Orleans Collection;
- Louisiana State Museum;
- Archdiocese of New Orleans sacramental registers;
- New Orleans notarial and conveyance records;
- Tulane special collections and newspapers;
- Library of Congress and American Folklife Center;
- Haitian Bureau of Ethnology;
- Musée du Panthéon National Haïtien and private temple collections;
- Musée de l’Homme/Institut d’Ethnologie collections associated with Maupoil;
- royal-palace and local-cult traditions in Abomey and Ouidah.

Loss resulted from slavery’s documentary violence, deliberate secrecy, newspaper sensationalism, decaying oral transmission, police seizure, Haitian anti-superstition burnings, tropical deterioration, political upheaval, commercial dispersal, and collectors removing sacred objects from their communities.
