# Belial: Research Dossier

**Class:** Hebrew abstraction and condemnatory epithet; in late Second Temple Jewish texts, a named evil angel, adversarial spirit, or prince of darkness; in Christian reception, a name or analogue of Satan/Antichrist; in early-modern ritual magic and modern demonolatry, a king or ruling demon. Belial is not securely attested as an ancient god with a cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Hebrew **בְּלִיַּעַל** (*bĕliyyaʿal*), “worthlessness,” “wickedness,” “destruction,” or “that which brings no benefit,” in pre-exilic material preserved in the Hebrew Bible. The precise date of the oldest passage is disputed because the surviving Masoretic manuscripts are medieval and the biblical compositions underwent transmission and editing. The earliest securely datable physical witnesses to a **personal supernatural Belial** are the Dead Sea Scrolls, principally manuscripts copied during the late second and first centuries BCE.

**Central historical development:** a Hebrew common noun and idiom—especially “sons of *bĕliyyaʿal*,” meaning worthless or lawless people—was progressively reanalyzed and personified. By the Qumran corpus, Belial is the supernatural ruler of the present age of wickedness and commander of the lot of darkness. Jewish and Christian apocalyptic writings developed overlapping but non-identical figures called Belial or Beliar. Medieval and Renaissance demonology then detached the name from much of its apocalyptic context and made Belial an individual infernal king with appearance, offices, legions, and eventually a sigil.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Names and scripts

- **בְּלִיַּעַל — *bĕliyyaʿal*, beliyya‘al.** Biblical Hebrew common noun, later proper name.
- **בְּנֵי־בְלִיַּעַל — *bĕnê-bĕliyyaʿal*.** Literally “sons of worthlessness/destruction”; an idiom for wicked, lawless, rebellious, or socially destructive men. The feminine **בַּת־בְּלִיַּעַל**, “daughter of worthlessness,” occurs in Hannah’s self-description of what she is not in 1 Samuel 1:16.
- **Βελίαρ — *Beliar*.** The best-attested Greek form in 2 Corinthians 6:15 and common in the *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs*. It probably reflects interchange or alteration of the final liquids *l/r*, not a separate being.
- **Βελίαλ — *Belial*.** Greek transliteration found in parts of the textual tradition and later literature.
- Further manuscript spellings include **Beliam, Belian, Beliab, Belias, Berial**. These are normally transmission variants rather than independently attested beings.
- Latin and vernacular forms include **Belial, Beliall, Beliar, Bélial, Beliale**.

### Etymology

**[Secure early usage; etymology disputed]** The traditional and still common explanation analyzes *bĕliyyaʿal* as **בְּלִי** (*bĕlî*, “without”) plus a form of **יעל** (*y-ʿ-l*, “to benefit, be useful”): “without worth/use,” hence “worthlessness.” The ordinary biblical contexts support a semantic field of uselessness, wickedness, destructiveness, and lawlessness, but the exact morphology is not certain. The word occurs twenty-seven times in the Hebrew Bible according to the standard count. A detailed philological survey lists the occurrences and shows that Greek translators usually rendered the meaning rather than treating the word as a name. [Piquer Otero’s philological study](https://revistaseug.ugr.es/index.php/meahhebreo/article/download/12499/10714/36224)

Competing proposals include:

- **“Without rising”:** *bĕlî yaʿal* or *bĕlî ʿālâ*, interpreted as something from which one does not rise—ruin, death, or Sheol. This has been invoked especially for “torrents of *bĕliyyaʿal*” in Psalm 18:5/2 Samuel 22:5.
- **“Without a yoke”:** rabbinic **בלי עול**, *bĕlî ʿol*. Sanhedrin 111b and related interpretation connect *bĕnê bĕliyyaʿal* with people who cast off the “yoke of Heaven.” This is an exegetical wordplay, not demonstrably the historical derivation.
- **Underworld term:** T. K. Cheyne connected it with a “land of no return” and ultimately with Mesopotamian underworld language.
- **Belili:** older comparativist scholarship proposed alteration of the name of the Mesopotamian goddess Belili. This has never won secure philological demonstration and should not be presented as Belial’s proven divine origin.
- **“Lord of the forest,” “lord without a master,” “without master”:** modern occult folk etymologies produced by dividing the word into forms resembling *baʿal* (“lord”) and other words. They are not supported by the Hebrew spelling or ancient evidence.

The appropriate conclusion is therefore narrower than many occult genealogies: “worthlessness/wickedness” is a strong contextual gloss; the word’s exact formation remains disputed; derivation from a forgotten Canaanite or Mesopotamian god is unproved. The range of historical proposals is preserved in the older but useful [Jewish Encyclopedia discussion](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/2805-belial), while the modern reference summary notes proposals from “without worth,” “never to rise,” and “swallow.” [Woolstenhulme, *Belial/Beliar*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781444338386.wbeah11046)

### Titles and classifications

**Hebrew Bible — securely attested early material**

Belial is not called an angel, demon, god, prince, or personal enemy of YHWH. It is an abstraction or quality in expressions concerning:

- worthless or wicked persons;
- conspirators and rebels;
- sexual aggressors at Gibeah;
- Eli’s sacrilegious sons;
- false witnesses against Naboth;
- an ungenerous thought that would deny aid to the poor;
- destructive counsel, speech, or calamity;
- the “torrents of *bĕliyyaʿal*,” parallel to death.

**Dead Sea Scrolls — late Second Temple sectarian systematization**

Belial receives titles or functional descriptions including:

- leader or ruler of the “lot of darkness”;
- **Prince of Darkness**;
- **angel of darkness**;
- **angel of enmity/hostility**;
- **angel of the pit/destruction**;
- ruler of wickedness;
- commander of the spirits of his lot;
- enemy of the Sons of Light;
- cosmic opponent of the Prince of Light/Michael;
- a created being whose temporary authority remains subordinate to Israel’s God.

The Qumran evidence does not create metaphysical equality between God and Belial. God is said to have created or appointed the powers and has fixed Belial’s destruction. The dualism is therefore ethical, angelic, and apocalyptic, but not an ultimate dualism of two uncreated gods. A concise German scholarly treatment gives the relevant titles and passages: 1QS 3:20–21; 1QM 13:10–11; 17:5–6; CD 4:12–18; 4Q286; and 11QMelchizedek. [Deutsche Bibelgesellschaft, “Belial/Beliar”](https://www.die-bibel.de/stichwort/14912)

**Jewish and Christian pseudepigrapha — canonical or para-canonical elaboration depending on community**

Belial/Beliar is:

- a spirit of deception;
- source or ruler of impurity, anger, fornication, and lawlessness;
- enemy of God’s elect;
- apocalyptic opponent of a priestly or messianic deliverer;
- in the *Ascension of Isaiah*, ruler of this world, associated with Samael and manifested through a Nero-like lawless king.

**Christian scripture**

In 2 Corinthians 6:15, **Βελίαρ** is the antithesis of Christ: “What concord has Christ with Beliar?” The name functions as Satan or the supreme evil power, although the verse does not provide a biography, hierarchy, or visual description. The critical reading **Βελίαρ** is supported by major witnesses; later English Bibles often print “Belial.” [Greek text and variants](https://www.bibliaplus.org/en/commentaries/236/cambridge-greek-testament-for-schools-and-colleges-commentary/2-corinthians/6/15)

**Early-modern demonology**

Belial becomes an individual demon-king, created after Lucifer, outwardly beautiful and persuasive, commanding infernal legions and granting rank, influence, and political favor.

**Modern demonolatry**

Belial is variously interpreted as a patron of autonomy, sovereignty, self-mastery, transgression, earth, material power, or “living without a master.” These attributes are largely twentieth- and twenty-first-century reinterpretations, not translations of the biblical name and not evidence for an ancient cult.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Hebrew Bible

**[Secure early usage; non-personal]** The corpus contains twenty-seven occurrences. Important examples include:

- **Deuteronomy 13:13–14:** “sons of *bĕliyyaʿal*” lead a town into worship of other gods.
- **Deuteronomy 15:9:** a “thought of *bĕliyyaʿal*” refuses aid as the sabbatical year approaches.
- **Judges 19:22; 20:13:** the assailants at Gibeah are “sons of *bĕliyyaʿal*.”
- **1 Samuel 1:16:** Hannah denies being a “daughter of *bĕliyyaʿal*.”
- **1 Samuel 2:12:** Eli’s sons are “sons of *bĕliyyaʿal*”; the following narrative specifies sacrificial corruption and sexual misconduct.
- **1 Samuel 25:17, 25:** Nabal is characterized through the wordplay between his name, “fool,” and *bĕliyyaʿal*.
- **1 Samuel 30:22:** wicked and worthless members of David’s force.
- **2 Samuel 16:7; 20:1:** Shimei and Sheba use or receive the designation in political conflict.
- **2 Samuel 22:5 / Psalm 18:5:** “torrents of *bĕliyyaʿal*,” an image of deadly chaos or destruction.
- **1 Kings 21:10, 13:** false witnesses who enable Naboth’s judicial murder.
- **Nahum 1:11; 2:1:** destructive or wicked counsel/person.
- **Proverbs 6:12; 16:27; 19:28:** socially destructive people and speech.
- **2 Chronicles 13:7:** rebels associated with Jeroboam.

Nothing in these passages securely establishes a named supernatural personality. English translations retaining “Belial” can obscure this fact.

### Septuagint and Greek Jewish Bible

**[Hellenistic translation history]** The Septuagint normally translates the semantic force:

- **παράνομος**, “lawless”;
- **ἀνομία/ἀνόμημα**, “lawlessness”;
- **λοιμός**, “pestilent person/pest”;
- other contextual renderings.

The Old Greek traditions therefore mostly understood a quality or human type, not a demonic proper name. Some later recensions and literalizing translators transliterated the Hebrew, helping make “Belial” look like a name. Piquer Otero argues that variations within Samuel-Kings may also preserve developing perceptions of cosmic or supernatural negativity rather than a single uniform translation policy. [Study and textual evidence](https://revistaseug.ugr.es/index.php/meahhebreo/article/view/12499)

### Dead Sea Scrolls

**[Earliest securely datable physical evidence for the personalized being]**

The principal manuscript, **1QM**, was among the seven Cave 1 scrolls discovered in 1947. Its script is normally dated to the late first century BCE, approximately 30–1 BCE, although the composition incorporates earlier materials and its literary history remains debated. Cave 4 fragments 4Q491–497 preserve related war texts. The Israel Museum describes the work as a seven-stage eschatological conflict between the Sons of Light, led by the Prince of Light/Michael, and the Sons of Darkness, headed by Belial. [Israel Museum, *War Scroll*](https://dss.collections.imj.org.il/war)

Major passages:

- **1QS 1:16–2:8, Community Rule:** entrants renounce “all the men of the lot of Belial”; priests curse Belial and his lot during the covenant ceremony.
- **1QS 3:13–4:26, Treatise on the Two Spirits:** the Prince of Lights and Angel of Darkness govern opposed lots. The relationship between this Angel of Darkness and Belial is made explicit across the sectarian corpus, though the surviving wording should not always be flattened into a single title-list.
- **CD 4:12–18, Damascus Document:** Belial is “unleashed against Israel”; his three nets are fornication, wealth, and defilement of the sanctuary.
- **1QM 1, 13–14, 17:** Belial commands the lot of darkness; God made him “for the pit” or destruction; his rule is temporary; his army is defeated in the final war.
- **1QH/Thanksgiving Hymns:** the speaker describes attacks, snares, or domination associated with Belial and his spirits.
- **4Q174, Florilegium:** an eschatological time of Belial and conflict with his lot.
- **4Q390, Pseudo-Moses:** “dominion of Belial” structures a period of apostasy.
- **4Q286, Blessings and Curses:** Belial is associated with the angel of the pit.
- **11Q11, Apocryphal Psalms:** Belial appears in an exorcistic or protective context and is aligned with Satanic/hostile power.
- **11Q13, 11QMelchizedek:** Melchizedek executes divine judgment against Belial and “the spirits of his lot.” Psalm 82 is applied to this heavenly judgment. [Study of 11Q13’s scriptural interpretation](https://czasopisma.kul.pl/ba/article/download/12449/11515)

The *War Scroll*’s surviving column 13 calls Belial an “angel of malevolence/hostility,” locates his dominion in darkness, and presents God as the one who made him and fixed his destruction. [Translation of 1QM](https://preteristarchives.org/1qm-4q491-496-the-war-scroll/)

### 1 Enoch, Jubilees, and Tobit

- **1 Enoch:** **[Absence finding]** The surviving Ethiopic book and its Aramaic fragments contain the Watchers, Asael/Azazel, Semihazah, evil spirits, and eschatological punishment but do not make Belial a major named actor. The dossier should not transfer the deeds of Azazel, Semihazah, or Satan to Belial.
- **Jubilees:** **[Later Second Temple elaboration]** “Beliar” language occurs chiefly in moral-apocalyptic expressions such as “spirit of Beliar” and “sons of Beliar.” Mastema, however, is the principal named adversarial prince in the narrative. Equating Mastema and Belial is tempting because Qumranic roles overlap, but *Jubilees* does not simply give every deed of Mastema to Belial.
- **Tobit:** **[Absence finding]** Asmodeus is the named demon in the story; Raphael binds him. Belial is not a character. Later demonological catalogues that place Asmodeus and Belial in the same hierarchy do not retroactively put Belial into *Tobit*.

### Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs

**[Jewish core with substantial Christian transmission and interpolation; dating disputed]** The work survives principally in Greek manuscripts, with versions in Armenian, Slavonic, and other languages. Scholars disagree over how much derives from a Jewish composition of the late Second Temple period and how much took shape under Christian editing, usually in the second century CE or later.

Beliar appears throughout as the ruler or source of deceit and moral corruption:

- **Testament of Reuben:** sexual impurity and the spirits of deception are associated with Beliar.
- **Testament of Levi:** priestly righteousness and the eschatological priest oppose Beliar’s power.
- **Testament of Judah:** two spirits or inclinations contend; greed and fornication place people under Beliar.
- **Testament of Issachar:** simplicity and honest labor resist Beliar.
- **Testament of Dan:** anger and falsehood belong to Beliar; Satan/Beliar terminology overlaps.
- **Testament of Naphtali:** disorder and apostasy align with Beliar.
- **Testament of Benjamin:** the righteous person cannot be mastered by Beliar; Beliar will ultimately be cast into eternal fire.

The work describes a “spirit of error of Beliar,” a “kingdom of Beliar,” and the “sword of Beliar.” These are apocalyptic and moral images, not references to a sanctuary or organized cult. An older critical translation and introduction remains accessible in [R. H. Charles’s edition](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/8/8b/The_Testaments_of_the_twelve_patriarchs_%28IA_testamentsoftwel00char%29.pdf).

### Sibylline Oracles

**[Jewish-Christian composite; late accretion]** In Christian portions of the Sibylline tradition, Beliar appears as an eschatological deceiver or Antichrist, sometimes arriving from the line of the Sebastenoi and associated with the Nero-redivivus expectation. The books were composed and edited in stages from the Hellenistic period into late antiquity; no “Sibylline” occurrence should be cited without identifying the particular book and Christian or Jewish stratum.

### Ascension of Isaiah

**[Composite Jewish-Christian apocalypse, approximately late first to early third century CE]** Beliar is one of the work’s most developed forms:

- he hates Isaiah for exposing Samael and foretelling the descent of the Beloved;
- he inspires or works through Isaiah’s accusers;
- he is “the great ruler, the king of this world”;
- in the *Testament of Hezekiah* portion, he descends in the likeness of a lawless human king, recognizably modeled on Nero, “the slayer of his mother”;
- he performs signs, persecutes the faithful, and is defeated at the manifestation of the Beloved.

The text associates Beliar closely with **Samael**, but whether the names designate one being, allied beings, or overlapping titles varies with passage and recension. The complete translated text is available at [Early Christian Writings](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/text/ascension.html); its composite structure and dating are summarized by [Early Jewish Writings](https://www.earlyjewishwritings.com/ascensionisaiah.html).

### New Testament

**[Early Christian canonical attestation]** In 2 Corinthians 6:15:

> τίς δὲ συμφώνησις Χριστοῦ πρὸς Βελίαρ;  
> “What concord is there between Christ and Beliar?”

The verse belongs to 2 Corinthians 6:14–7:1, a unit whose relation to its context is debated. Proposals include Pauline composition, a fragment of another Pauline letter, non-Pauline interpolation, or Christian reworking of Jewish dualistic exhortation. Whatever its literary history, the textual opposition is explicit: Christ and Beliar represent mutually exclusive allegiances. The verse does not say Beliar is Christ’s brother, equal, or biological opposite.

### Nag Hammadi and “Gnostic Belial”

**[Limited and disputed identification]** The Nag Hammadi codices, copied in Coptic in the fourth century but preserving works usually composed in the second through fourth centuries, do not present a unified “Gnostic pantheon.” Their authoritative modern edition is the Coptic Gnostic Library. [Brill, Coptic Gnostic Library Online](https://scholarlyeditions.brill.com/cglo/)

A name rendered **Belias** occurs in some transmission or translation of the *Apocryphon of John* in an underworld or archontic list. This may reflect the Belial name-family, but Belial is not a central creator, demiurge, or archon across the Nag Hammadi corpus. In particular:

- Belial is not Yaldabaoth;
- he is not Saklas merely because both are hostile rulers;
- he is not Samael in every Gnostic source;
- Barbelo is unrelated despite superficial similarity of sound;
- the extensive mythology of Sophia and the archons cannot properly be retold as a “Belial myth.”

The claim that Belial belongs to a distinct, well-attested “Gnostic pantheon” therefore overstates the evidence. At most, the name participates marginally in the broad late-antique circulation of Jewish and Christian demonological names.

### Rabbinic and mystical Jewish literature

**[Rabbinic reinterpretation, not a demon biography]** Sanhedrin 111b and Sifre Deuteronomy 93 explain *bĕnê bĕliyyaʿal* as people **בלי עול**, “without the yoke” of Heaven. Midrashic treatments continue the biblical moral category. Rabbinic literature normally does not develop Belial into a leading demon comparable to Samael, Ashmedai, or later Lilith.

**[Absence finding]** No securely established major Belial narrative has been identified in the foundational Hekhalot writings, *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh*, or the main body of the *Zohar*. These corpora contain vast angelological and demonic materials, but resemblance in theme is not occurrence. Claims that *Sefer Raziel* supplies Belial’s sigil or ancient ritual are especially suspect: the circulating *Sefer Raziel* is a medieval compilation drawing on earlier materials, while the standard Goetic Belial seal belongs to a different manuscript tradition.

### Pseudo-Dionysius and Aquinas

**[Absence finding]** Pseudo-Dionysius’ *Celestial Hierarchy* systematizes nine angelic orders, and Thomas Aquinas develops a scholastic account of angels and fallen angels. Neither supplies Belial’s later Goetic rank, appearance, sigil, or legions. Later occult writers sometimes place named demons as inversions of Dionysian angelic orders, but this is a learned post-medieval synthesis. Weyer’s statement that Belial rules spirits formerly of the orders of Virtues and Angels is not a quotation from Pseudo-Dionysius or Aquinas.

### Qur’an, hadith, tafsir, and al-Ṭabarī

**[Absence finding]** **Belial/Beliar is not a Qur’anic name.** The Qur’an’s principal adversarial figures are Iblīs and al-Shayṭān; later Islamic traditions contain shayāṭīn, jinn, and named rebellious beings, but these should not be silently equated with Belial. No canonical hadith dossier or established account in al-Ṭabarī makes Belial an Islamic demon. Modern lists that call Belial a jinn or an Islamic king of demons generally import European grimoire material into an Islamic-looking framework.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains by period

**[Biblical]** Worthlessness, lawlessness, destructiveness, rebellion, corrupt speech, sacrilege, sexual violence, false witness, and social disorder.

**[Qumran]** The present dominion of wickedness; temptation; apostasy; impurity; hostile spirits; persecution of the elect; leadership of the Sons of Darkness; eschatological war.

**[Pseudepigraphal and early Christian]** Deception, passions, sexual sin, anger, idolatry, persecution, Antichrist-like kingship, and opposition to the messiah or “Beloved.”

**[Early-modern grimoire]** Political and social advancement, favor from friends and enemies, dignities and offices, truthful answers when constrained, and command of legions.

**[Modern invention or reinvention]** Personal sovereignty, independence, earth, material manifestation, shadow work, sexual liberation, anti-authoritarianism, and “breaking chains.” These themes may be meaningful within living modern practice but are not ancient attributes.

### Appearance

**[Ancient absence]** No securely identified ancient image of Belial survives. Qumran manuscripts are textual, not cult statues or illustrated demonologies. There is no agreed ancient physiognomy, animal, weapon, color, planet, or emblem.

**[Weyer and Goetia]** Belial appears as **two beautiful angels seated in a fiery chariot** in the *Ars Goetia* tradition; Weyer’s earlier text has a beautiful angel in a fiery chariot. His beauty and pleasant speech disguise falsity.

**[Modern sigil]** The familiar lineal “seal of Belial” derives from *Lemegeton* manuscripts and their printed descendants. It is not found in the Hebrew Bible, Dead Sea Scrolls, New Testament, Nag Hammadi codices, or ancient inscriptions.

### Numbers, directions, elements, and correspondences

- **68:** Belial’s number in the *Ars Goetia* list.
- **22:** his position in Reginald Scot’s English presentation of Weyer’s catalogue, reflecting the different order and number of spirits.
- **80 legions:** Weyer/Goetic command figure.
- **North, earth, particular planets, metals, colors, incense, enn, zodiacal degree:** mostly modern occult tabulations or specific ritual systems. They are not stable across manuscripts and should be cited only to the modern author assigning them.
- The modern chant or “enn” commonly circulated for Belial belongs to modern demonolatry, not to the *Lemegeton* or any ancient source.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy and hierarchy

Belial has no parents, consort, children, or siblings in the Hebrew Bible or Dead Sea Scrolls.

At Qumran he is a created angelic adversary with a “lot,” army, and subordinate spirits. His strongest structural counterpart is the Prince of Light, usually identified with Michael, but the texts do not make them brothers.

Weyer reports that some say Belial was created immediately after Lucifer and is “father and seducer” of those who fell. This is a Renaissance demonological report, not biblical genealogy. The Goetic tradition repeats it.

Modern sources sometimes assign Belial consorts such as Naamah or Lilith, or place him among four cardinal rulers with Lucifer, Leviathan, and Satan. These configurations belong to particular modern occult systems, not a continuous ancient genealogy.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **YHWH/God — creation and sentence:** God made or appointed Belial for the pit, permits his temporary dominion, supports the Sons of Light, and fixes Belial’s annihilation. **Source:** 1QM 13–14, late Second Temple period. **Tier:** securely attested Qumran systematization.
- **Michael / Prince of Light — eschatological opposition:** Michael’s lot and angelic forces oppose Belial and his lot in the final war. **Source:** 1QM 13 and 17; comparison with 1QS 3. **Tier:** securely attested Qumran apocalyptic tradition.
- **Melchizedek — judgment and deliverance:** Melchizedek announces liberty, atones for his lot, judges Belial and his spirits, and delivers the righteous from Belial’s hand. **Source:** 11Q13 II, first century BCE manuscript. **Tier:** regional/sectarian Qumran variant.
- **Mastema — functional association, not a narrated meeting:** 1QM 13 uses language of hostility/*maśṭēmâ* in describing Belial; later comparison with *Jubilees* encouraged equation of Belial and Prince Mastema. No surviving narrative stages a meeting between two distinct characters called Belial and Mastema. **Tier:** ancient functional overlap; exact identification is modern scholarly reconstruction.
- **Satan — identification or overlapping designation:** 11Q11 and later Christian literature align Belial with Satanic power; the Peshitta renders Beliar in 2 Corinthians 6:15 as Satan. No canonical text narrates Belial meeting Satan as a separate being. **Tier:** late Second Temple/early Christian syncretistic identification.
- **Samael — association or identification:** Beliar and Samael are closely linked in the *Ascension of Isaiah*; Isaiah’s exposure of Samael provokes Beliar. Recensional complexity prevents a universal claim that the two names are always identical. **Source:** *Ascension of Isaiah* 1–5. **Tier:** late Jewish-Christian apocalyptic elaboration.
- **The Beloved/Christ — persecution and final defeat:** Beliar opposes the revealed descent and mission of the Beloved; at the final manifestation the Beloved rebukes the powers through which Beliar acted and destroys the godless. **Source:** *Ascension of Isaiah* 3–4. **Tier:** early Christian apocalyptic elaboration.
- **Christ — absolute opposition:** Christ and Beliar have no concord. **Source:** 2 Corinthians 6:15, first century CE. **Tier:** canonical Christian attestation.
- **Messianic/priestly deliverer — destruction of Beliar’s dominion:** a righteous priestly or messianic figure binds or overthrows Beliar’s power. **Source:** several *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs*. **Tier:** Jewish-Christian pseudepigraphal elaboration; wording and Christian layers disputed.
- **Lucifer — creation-order relationship:** Belial was allegedly created immediately after Lucifer. **Source:** Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* (1577), repeated in the *Ars Goetia*. **Tier:** early-modern accretion.
- **Solomon — coercion and imprisonment:** Solomon compels Belial and the other spirits, confines them in a brazen vessel, and casts it into a Babylonian lake or pit. **Source:** Weyer’s catalogue and *Lemegeton* tradition. **Tier:** early-modern Solomonic legend.
- **Bileth — shared imprisonment and rank:** Bileth is first and Belial second among the kings confined in the brazen vessel. **Source:** Weyer/Scot. **Tier:** early-modern accretion.
- **Asmoday/Asmodeus — shared imprisonment and rank:** Asmoday is third after Bileth and Belial in the vessel episode. **Source:** Weyer/Scot. **Tier:** early-modern accretion.
- **Satan in Milton — council subordinate/associate:** Belial sits among the fallen angels and advises Satan’s infernal council against renewed open war. **Source:** Milton, *Paradise Lost* I–II (1667/1674). **Tier:** literary reinvention.

**Absence findings:** No ancient text documents Belial meeting Azazel, Semihazah, Lilith, Iblīs, Barbelo, Sophia, Yaldabaoth, Raziel, Raphael, Dante’s demons, or the named demons of most modern hierarchies. Later charts placing them together are systematizations, not recovered myths.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The word that became a being

**[Secure early core → late personification]** The earliest recoverable “story” is linguistic rather than biographical. Biblical writers used *bĕliyyaʿal* for persons or acts that threatened covenantal and social order. The idiom “son of X” made an abstraction behave grammatically like a parent or owner: a “son of worthlessness” is one characterized by worthlessness. Later readers encountering “sons of Belial” could understand Belial as the leader or father of such people.

The Septuagint’s semantic translations show that personification was neither automatic nor complete in Hellenistic Judaism. Qumran then supplies the decisive evidence: “lot of Belial,” “dominion of Belial,” Belial’s spirits, his eschatological army, and direct curses make him a named supernatural agent. This is development, not proof that every biblical occurrence secretly named the same demon.

### War of the Sons of Light and Sons of Darkness

**[Qumran sectarian apocalypse]** The *War Scroll* describes a final conflict staged in cycles. Israel’s faithful, the Sons of Light, organize as a sacral army with priests, trumpets, banners, weapons, liturgy, and angelic assistance. Their human enemies—variously Edom, Moab, Ammon, Philistia, the Kittim, and apostate Jews—belong to the lot of darkness. Behind them stands Belial.

The battle alternates: the Sons of Light prevail three times, Belial’s lot three times, and divine intervention decides the seventh engagement. Michael and the holy angels support the light; Belial and his spirits support darkness. Priestly prayers curse Belial, declare him created for destruction, and anticipate the end of his dominion.

The work is not a historical report of an executed war and not a liturgy of Belial-worship. It is an apocalyptic military rule or idealized scenario belonging to the Qumran community’s self-understanding. Whether it was intended as an operational manual, eschatological liturgy, scriptural war exegesis, or literary combination remains debated.

### Belial’s three nets

**[Qumran moral-polemical myth]** CD 4:12–18 says Belial is loose against Israel and catches people in three nets:

1. fornication;
2. wealth;
3. defilement of the sanctuary.

The passage converts moral and legal disagreement into cosmic warfare. Belial’s traps explain why other Israelites violate the sect’s interpretation of marriage, property, and temple purity. The “nets” are not physical cult objects.

### Melchizedek’s judgment over Belial

**[Qumran regional variant]** 11QMelchizedek interprets the Jubilee law, Isaiah 61, Psalm 82, and other scriptures eschatologically. Melchizedek proclaims release, judges heavenly powers, and rescues those belonging to his lot from Belial. Belial’s spirits are defendants or enemies in the heavenly assize.

This is one of the clearest named divine-being interactions involving Belial. Its polarity resembles Michael versus Belial but should not be harmonized automatically: 11Q13 foregrounds Melchizedek, while 1QM foregrounds the Prince of Light/Michael.

### Beliar in the Testaments

**[Jewish-Christian moral apocalypse]** In the *Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs*, human psychology, ethics, and eschatology interlock. Spirits of truth and error compete within human conduct; passions such as lust, anger, envy, and greed open a person to Beliar. Patriarchal exhortation teaches descendants to resist him through simplicity, compassion, chastity, and fear of God. A future priestly or messianic savior ends Beliar’s rule, after which deception ceases.

The apparent unity is complicated by Christian passages predicting Jesus-like salvation. Some scholars reconstruct Jewish source materials later Christianized; others regard the extant work’s Christian literary form as fundamental. Belial’s role must therefore be attributed to individual passages, not to a hypothetical lost original without qualification.

### Beliar-Nero in the Ascension of Isaiah

**[Early Christian apocalypse]** Beliar, ruler of this world, becomes manifest in the likeness of a matricidal king. The allusion is to Nero, whose killing of Agrippina and persecution of Christians generated the Nero-redivivus legend. He performs wonders, demands allegiance, persecutes the faithful, and is destroyed by the returning Beloved.

This is not a claim that the historical Nero literally bore the personal name Belial. It is an apocalyptic identification: imperial persecution is interpreted as Beliar’s earthly manifestation.

### Solomon and the brazen vessel

**[Early-modern accretion]** Weyer and the *Lemegeton* transmit a tale in which Solomon gathers seventy-two spirit-kings and their innumerable legions into a brazen vessel sealed by divine power. The vessel is cast into a Babylonian lake or pit. Babylonians later break it open expecting treasure, releasing the spirits; the spirits return to former places, while Belial enters an image and gives oracles.

This story draws on the broad Solomonic magical tradition but is not found in Kings or Chronicles. It provides the narrative frame that turns Belial from an apocalyptic adversary into a conjurable spirit.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Ancient cult

**[Negative material finding]** There is no securely identified ancient temple, altar, priesthood, festival, cult statue, dedicatory inscription, theophoric personal name, pilgrimage center, sacred animal, or sacrificial calendar belonging to Belial.

The biblical “sons of Belial” do not constitute a religious fraternity. The men of Gibeah, Eli’s sons, Naboth’s accusers, and political rebels are condemned human beings, not devotees of a god called Belial.

Milton accurately exploits this absence when he says of Belial, “To him no temple stood / Or altar smoked,” then poetically argues that Belial is nevertheless honored whenever priests become corrupt. That is literary theology, not archaeological evidence.

### Human sacrifice and sexual rites

**[Contested-material finding]**

- No ancient source documents human sacrifice to Belial.
- No ancient source documents temple prostitution in a cult of Belial.
- Eli’s sons misuse sacrificial portions and have sex with women at the sanctuary entrance, but 1 Samuel calls the sons *bĕnê bĕliyyaʿal* as condemnation; it does not describe sacrifice or sex offered to a deity named Belial.
- Weyer says Belial accepts “sacrifices, gifts, and offerings” before giving answers, but this is a sixteenth-century demonological catalogue. It is not evidence of an Israelite cult.
- Modern claims that Belial “requires blood” derive from particular occult manuals or practitioner testimony. They are modern ritual traditions, not ancient continuity.

### Qumran ritual opposition

The Community Rule’s covenant ceremony curses Belial and his lot. The *War Scroll* prescribes prayers and battle liturgies against him. These are rituals involving Belial as an enemy, comparable to exorcistic naming of a hostile power—not worship.

### Conjuration

**[Early-modern ritual magic]** In Weyer and the *Ars Goetia*, Belial must be constrained by divine names and ritual authority. He is said to lie unless compelled, to demand offerings, and to bestow dignities and favor. The magician is not presented as Belial’s worshipper but as an operator invoking superior divine authority.

The *Lemegeton*’s ritual apparatus includes a seal for the spirit, protective circles, divine names, conjurations, threats, and a license to depart. Modern “working with” or worshipping Belial reverses much of this coercive framework.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### I. Biblical Israel and Judah

*Bĕliyyaʿal* denotes ruinous character, conduct, speech, or calamity. It is not demonstrably a divine name. The passages preserve different social settings—tribal violence, monarchic polemic, wisdom instruction, prophetic denunciation—and should not be forced into a single demonology.

### II. Hellenistic translation and semantic instability

Greek translators generally render the meaning, but transliterations and recensional differences gradually permit the word to be read as a name. Apocalyptic dualism, developed angelology, imperial oppression, and sectarian boundary-making provide the environment in which moral abstraction becomes supernatural ruler.

### III. Qumran, approximately second–first centuries BCE

Belial reaches his first fully documented personhood. He governs a cosmic-social faction, commands spirits, tempts Israel, and opposes the elect. Yet he remains a creature under God’s sovereignty. Qumran gives the figure a domain, army, period of rule, named opponents, and eschatological end.

### IV. Jewish and Christian pseudepigrapha, first century BCE–third century CE

The *Testaments*, Sibylline materials, and *Ascension of Isaiah* distribute the name across moral psychology, messianic expectation, imperial critique, and Antichrist speculation. Beliar may overlap with Satan, Samael, Mastema, or a future tyrant, but the equations vary by text.

### V. Early Christianity

Second Corinthians canonizes the Christ/Beliar opposition in a compact formula. Patristic readers generally treat Belial as Satan, the devil, Antichrist, or lawlessness personified. Belial never becomes a separately defined universal article of Christian doctrine.

### VI. Rabbinic Judaism and Islam

Rabbinic interpretation primarily returns to the moral-linguistic sense, especially “without the yoke” of Heaven. Jewish demonology develops other names more extensively.

Islamic scripture and mainstream tradition do not adopt Belial as a standard named being. Iblīs and al-Shayṭān occupy the relevant adversarial roles. Assertions that Belial has a continuous Islamic cult or fixed jinn identity are unsupported.

### VII. Medieval Latin Christianity

The Vulgate often retains **Belial**, encouraging readers to hear a proper name. Homiletic and moral literature uses “sons of Belial” for heretics, rebels, criminals, or enemies. Such usage could have political and persecutory consequences, but it is rhetoric of stigmatization rather than evidence for worship of Belial.

Pseudo-Dionysian angelic hierarchy and scholastic demonology supplied categories later occult authors could populate with biblical demon names. They did not themselves create the Goetic Belial profile.

### VIII. Renaissance demonology

Johann Weyer’s **Pseudomonarchia Daemonum**, appended to the 1577 edition of *De praestigiis daemonum*, gives the earliest printed form of Belial’s now-familiar demonic office. Weyer reports that Belial:

- was created after Lucifer;
- seduced those who fell;
- appears as a beautiful angel in a fiery chariot;
- speaks pleasantly but lies unless divinely constrained;
- grants dignities, senatorial offices, and favor;
- rules eighty legions from the former orders of Virtues and Angels;
- participated in Solomon’s brazen-vessel episode.

Weyer included the catalogue within a work attacking credulous magic and witch persecution; its entries should not automatically be treated as Weyer’s devotional beliefs. [Latin and English text](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm); [parallel digital edition](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/pseudomonarchia/read/)

### IX. The *Lemegeton* and printed Goetia

The seventeenth-century *Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis* adapts Weyer and related spirit catalogues. Its first book, the *Ars Goetia*, expands the list to seventy-two and numbers Belial **68**. It gives him two beautiful angelic forms in a fiery chariot and supplies a seal. The manuscript tradition is early modern; the book is not a work by the biblical Solomon. [Critical transcription and manuscript discussion](https://www2.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm)

### X. Milton and literary personhood

In *Paradise Lost* (1667; revised 1674), Belial becomes a vividly individuated fallen angel:

- outwardly graceful and dignified;
- inwardly false, lustful, slothful, and cowardly;
- a superb rhetorician able to make “the worse appear / The better reason”;
- advocate of “ignoble ease” at Pandemonium’s council.

Milton fuses the biblical “sons of Belial,” especially Eli’s sons, with the Renaissance fallen angel. In *Paradise Regained* Belial also participates in infernal deliberation concerning temptation. Milton’s portrait materially shaped later English perceptions. [Scanned scholarly edition of *Paradise Lost*](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/6/65/Milton%27s_Paradise_lost_%28IA_miltonsparadiselmilton%29.pdf)

### XI. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century occult reception

Grimoire revivals, French demonological encyclopedias, English occult publishing, and Aleister Crowley’s edition of the *Goetia* spread Belial’s seal and rank. Later correspondence tables increasingly assigned planets, directions, elements, colors, and psychological meanings absent from the manuscripts.

### XII. Contemporary demonolatry

Modern demonolaters may invoke or venerate “King Belial” as a real spiritual intelligence, deity-like patron, or psychological archetype. Frequently reported modern themes include autonomy, anti-authoritarianism, political influence, wealth, earth, sexuality, and severe personal transformation.

S. Connolly’s *Modern Demonolatry* and *Daemonolatry Goetia* helped standardize an earth-element association, “enns,” and devotional rather than coercive approaches. These are modern religious constructions. They should neither be ridiculed as unreal nor misrepresented as ancient Hebrew practice. No reliable demographic count establishes the scale of Belial-specific worship.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature

- **Milton, *Paradise Lost*:** the single most influential literary Belial: beautiful, eloquent, corrupt, and politically cautious.
- **Dante:** **absence finding.** Dante’s *Commedia* profoundly influenced Western hell imagery, but it does not provide the major named Belial portrait sometimes attributed to it. Dante belongs to the background of infernal reception, not Belial’s direct dossier.
- Later horror and fantasy increasingly use “Belial” as a ready-made name for a demon, fallen angel, secret order, or monstrous force, often without reference to Qumran.

### Film and popular media

Notable uses include:

- *Basket Case* (1982), where Belial is the name of Duane Bradley’s violently separated conjoined twin; this is grotesque horror naming, not a version of the ancient figure.
- *The Prophecy* series and assorted possession films use Belial as an infernal proper name.
- Comics, manga, anime, role-playing games, and video games repeatedly portray Belial as a demon lord, often adding gender, family, elemental powers, or political kingdoms invented for the franchise.
- Games such as *Diablo III* present a “Lord of Lies,” creatively combining the Goetic liar with an original fantasy cosmology.

These works have made “Belial” visually more concrete than any ancient tradition ever did.

### Psychological and New Age readings

Belial may be interpreted as:

- Jungian “shadow”;
- rejected autonomy;
- ego freed from religious authority;
- the danger of persuasive rationalization;
- material embodiment or root-level power.

Such readings are twentieth- or twenty-first-century interpretations. Jung did not establish an ancient Belial archetype with a fixed meaning.

### Political use and stigmatization

“Children/sons of Belial” has long served as a label for religious opponents, rebels, political enemies, alleged heretics, and criminals. The phrase’s biblical prestige allowed speakers to turn contingent opponents into embodiments of cosmic disorder. Each use must be studied in its own polemical setting.

No evidence found in the dedicated search supports a stable Belial-centered extremist organization of historical importance. Individual occult or Satanic symbols may be appropriated by fringe actors, but general claims of organized extremist “Belial cults” require case-specific evidence.

### Commercialization

Belial’s Goetic seal appears on jewelry, tattoos, clothing, candles, ritual tools, album art, and online devotional products. Modern products often advertise invented ancient pedigrees, especially claims that the seal is Hebrew, Kabbalistic, Sumerian, or used by Solomon. The seal’s demonstrable documentary home is the early-modern *Lemegeton* tradition.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Belial already a deity or demon in the Hebrew Bible?

The older mythological school pointed to “torrents of Belial,” Sheol imagery, Mesopotamian “land of no return,” and the proposed goddess Belili. The conservative linguistic position treats every biblical use as abstract or adjectival. A cautious synthesis is preferable:

- no biblical passage unmistakably introduces a named demon;
- some poetic passages personify destruction in language capable of later mythologization;
- Qumran’s personal Belial is not created from nothing but represents a substantial conceptual development.

### How did personification occur?

Proposed mechanisms include:

1. grammatical reanalysis of “sons of Belial” as followers of a named leader;
2. personification of wickedness, death, or chaos;
3. growth of Jewish angelology and cosmic dualism;
4. sectarian polarization;
5. influence from Iranian dualistic patterns.

Iranian influence is possible in the broad development of Second Temple apocalyptic dualism but cannot be reduced to “Belial is Ahriman.” The precise verbal derivations proposed by older scholarship are speculative.

### Is Belial the same as Satan?

There is no timeless answer.

- Hebrew Bible: no; *bĕliyyaʿal* is not the narrative ha-satan of Job or Zechariah.
- Qumran: roles overlap, and some texts approach identification, but terminology remains varied.
- 2 Corinthians and Syriac interpretation: effectively yes, or at least the supreme power opposed to Christ.
- *Ascension of Isaiah*: Beliar overlaps strongly with Samael and Antichrist/Nero.
- Grimoires: Belial and Satan/Lucifer may be separate ranked spirits.

### Beliar and Antichrist

Some apocalyptic sources treat Beliar as the supernatural power manifested in an eschatological tyrant; others use him as Satan. Calling him “the Antichrist” without specifying the source erases this distinction.

### Qumran dualism

Belial is powerful but created. Consequently, Qumran does not teach two equal eternal gods. Scholarly comparisons with Zoroastrian dualism concern structure and historical influence, not identity.

### Gnostic identification

Belial’s inclusion in a “Gnostic pantheon” is marginal and often exaggerated. Nag Hammadi evidence for a Belias-like name does not justify identifying him with the Demiurge, every archon, or Samael in all texts. The once-common habit of treating “Gnosticism” as a single religion with a single pantheon is itself contested; modern scholarship emphasizes distinct Sethian, Valentinian, Hermetic, Thomasine, and other writings.

### Primary evidence and preservation

- Biblical Hebrew is preserved principally in medieval Masoretic codices, with earlier manuscript evidence from Qumran.
- Qumran manuscripts are held mainly by the Israel Antiquities Authority and the Shrine of the Book/Israel Museum; photographs and metadata are available digitally.
- The *Testaments* survive through a complex multilingual Christian transmission.
- The *Ascension of Isaiah* survives complete principally in Ethiopic, with Greek fragments and Latin and Slavonic witnesses.
- Nag Hammadi texts survive in fourth-century Coptic codices discovered in 1945.
- Weyer’s catalogue survives in sixteenth-century print.
- The *Lemegeton* survives in seventeenth- and eighteenth-century manuscripts, notably British Library and related collections.

### Useful modern scholarship

Essential orientations include:

- Theodore J. Lewis, “Belial,” *Anchor Bible Dictionary*.
- B. Otzen, “*Bĕliyyaʿal*,” *Theological Dictionary of the Old Testament*.
- Guinevere T. Woolstenhulme, “Belial/Beliar,” *Encyclopedia of Ancient History*.
- Karel van der Toorn, Bob Becking, and Pieter W. van der Horst, eds., *Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible*.
- John J. Collins on Qumran apocalypticism.
- Maxwell J. Davidson, *Angels at Qumran*.
- R. H. Charles and later critical work on the *Testaments* and *Ascension of Isaiah*.
- Jeffrey Burton Russell’s historical studies of the Devil.
- Henry Ansgar Kelly, *Satan: A Biography*.
- Bernard Bamberger, *Fallen Angels*.
- Birger Pearson, Kurt Rudolph, Bentley Layton, David Brakke, Marvin Meyer, and Michael Williams for the diversity and historiography of “Gnosticism.”
- Joseph H. Peterson’s manuscript-based transcriptions for early-modern grimoires.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Belial was an ancient Phoenician god worshipped at Sidon: unestablished.
- Belial was a Babylonian storm god: unestablished.
- The name certainly means “lord of the forest”: false folk etymology.
- The Goetic seal is ancient Hebrew or Solomonic archaeology: false.
- Belial appears throughout 1 Enoch: false.
- Belial is the demon Asmodeus in *Tobit*: false.
- Belial is a Qur’anic jinn: false.
- Belial has a continuous cult from biblical antiquity to modern demonolatry: unsupported.
- Ancient Israelites sacrificed humans to Belial: unsupported.
- Pseudo-Dionysius or Aquinas assigned Belial eighty legions: false.
- Dante supplied the standard Belial character later used by Milton: unsupported; Milton’s direct portrayal is his own biblical and demonological synthesis.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Documentary event |
|---|---|
| First millennium BCE | Hebrew compositions use **בְּלִיַּעַל** for worthlessness, wickedness, destruction, rebellious people, and destructive forces. No unambiguous named demon appears. |
| Third–first centuries BCE | Septuagint translators usually render the word semantically as lawlessness, wickedness, or a pestilent person; transliteration varies by book and recension. |
| Second–first centuries BCE | Qumran compositions personify Belial as leader of the lot of darkness, tempter of Israel, commander of hostile spirits, and eschatological enemy. |
| Late second–first century BCE | Copies of the Community Rule, Damascus Document, War traditions, hymns, blessings/curses, and Melchizedek text preserve Belial’s sectarian roles. |
| Approximately 30–1 BCE | Principal surviving 1QM *War Scroll* manuscript copied in an early Herodian hand. |
| First century BCE–second century CE | Jewish and Christian pseudepigrapha develop Beliar as spirit of error, ruler of passions, and apocalyptic enemy; dating of individual *Testaments* strata disputed. |
| Mid-first century CE | 2 Corinthians 6:15 places Christ and Beliar in absolute opposition. |
| Late first–early third century CE | Composite *Ascension of Isaiah* identifies Beliar with the ruler of this world and a Nero-like eschatological tyrant, closely associated with Samael. |
| Second–fourth centuries CE | Belias-like material enters an archontic list in a version of the *Apocryphon of John*; Belial remains marginal within the diverse writings later preserved at Nag Hammadi. |
| Third–sixth centuries CE | Rabbinic interpretation explains *bĕliyyaʿal* through “without the yoke” of Heaven; no comparably elaborate rabbinic demon biography emerges. |
| Fifth–sixth centuries CE | Pseudo-Dionysius systematizes angelic orders but does not provide the later Goetic Belial profile. |
| Seventh century onward | Qur’an and formative Islamic tradition develop Iblīs and shayṭān traditions without adopting Belial as a standard figure. |
| Thirteenth century | Aquinas systematizes angelic and demonic nature without assigning Belial a distinctive rank or legend. |
| Medieval period | Latin Christian rhetoric uses “sons of Belial” for morally or politically condemned people; Solomonic magical traditions develop independently. |
| 1577 | Johann Weyer publishes *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* with Belial as a beautiful infernal king commanding eighty legions. |
| 1584 | Reginald Scot’s *Discoverie of Witchcraft* makes the catalogue available in English. |
| Seventeenth century | *Lemegeton/Ars Goetia* lists Belial as spirit 68, gives him two beautiful angelic forms in a fiery chariot, and transmits his seal. |
| 1667/1674 | Milton’s *Paradise Lost* creates the influential eloquent, beautiful, slothful, and morally corrupt fallen angel Belial. |
| Nineteenth century | Biblical philology debates “worthlessness,” “without rising,” Sheol, Belili, and underworld derivations; occult revivals republish demon catalogues. |
| 1904 | Crowley and Mathers’ printed *Goetia* helps popularize Belial’s seal and ritual profile in Anglophone occultism. |
| 1945 | Nag Hammadi codices discovered; later scholarship reveals the diversity of the writings misleadingly grouped under a single “Gnostic religion.” |
| 1947 | Cave 1 discovery brings 1QM and 1QS to modern scholarly attention, radically strengthening the documented pre-Christian history of personal Belial. |
| 1955 | Eleazar Sukenik’s posthumous publication helps establish the *War Scroll* text. |
| Late twentieth century | Historical studies by Russell, Kelly, Bamberger, Collins, Davidson, and others separate biblical, Qumranic, Christian, and grimoire layers. |
| Late twentieth–early twenty-first century | Horror, fantasy, games, comics, metal music, ceremonial magic, and demonolatry proliferate new Belial images. |
| 2006–present | Modern demonolatry publications and online communities increasingly treat Belial as a devotional patron of autonomy, earth, sovereignty, and transformation—documented modern developments, not recovered ancient cult. |
| 2026 | Belial remains simultaneously a biblical translation problem, a major Qumranic adversary, a Christian name for supreme evil, a Goetic king, a literary character, and an object of living modern esoteric practice. |
