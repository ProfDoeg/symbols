# Azrael: Research Dossier

**Class:** Angel; specifically the divinely commissioned angel of death in medieval and later Islamicate angelology. In modern Jewish, Christian, occult, literary, and popular reception he is variously called an archangel, psychopomp, personification of death, angel of judgment, or—incorrectly in relation to the early evidence—a demon.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The consonantal angel-name **ʿZRʾL** occurs on late-antique Jewish Aramaic incantation bowls from Sasanian Mesopotamia, approximately the sixth–seventh centuries CE, but those bowls do **not** identify that angel as the angel of death. The earliest presently secure identification of **ʿIzrāʾīl/Azrael by name with the Qur’anic Angel of Death** is in early Islamic exegetical tradition, most clearly the surviving *Tafsīr* of Muqātil ibn Sulaymān (d. 767), commenting on Qur’an 32:11. The Qur’an itself, conventionally dated to the early seventh century, says only **مَلَكُ الْمَوْتِ, malak al-mawt**, “the Angel of Death,” and never uses the name Azrael. Stephen Burge’s source study remains fundamental because it separates the late-antique name, the Islamic identification, and the much later Ethiopic *Apocalypse of Peter* recension.[Burge, “‘ZR’L, The Angel of Death and the Ethiopic Apocalypse of Peter”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0951820710364880)

## Evidentiary labels used

- **[Early attested]** securely attested before or during the first Islamic centuries.
- **[Later canonical elaboration]** authoritative or widely accepted within a religious tradition, but later than its foundational scripture.
- **[Regional variant]** geographically or linguistically restricted tradition.
- **[Learned systematization]** exegetical, theological, cosmographical, or mystical organization of earlier material.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** explicit or inferred equation with a figure from another tradition.
- **[Medieval/early-modern accretion]** later narrative, magical, or iconographic development.
- **[Folklore]** orally transmitted or popular narrative whose documentary witness is later than the story’s alleged setting.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** a historical conclusion rather than a traditional assertion.
- **[Modern invention]** a recent claim, attribute, ritual, or fictional character without support in the older sources.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

**[Early attested]** Qur’an 32:11 commands Muhammad to say: “The Angel of Death who has been entrusted with you will take you; then to your Lord you will be returned.” The Arabic is *malak al-mawt alladhī wukkila bikum*. The verse gives a function, subordination, and destination, but no proper name, appearance, genealogy, rank, weapon, color, feast, or cult.[Qur’anic Arabic Corpus, 32:11](https://corpus.quran.com/translation.jsp?chapter=32&verse=11)

**[Later canonical elaboration]** Islamic commentary identified this office-holder as **ʿIzrāʾīl** or **ʿAzrāʾīl**. He extracts or receives souls only on God’s command. This is a much better description of the mature Islamic figure than “god of death”: Azrael possesses no independent sovereignty over mortality and is neither worshipped nor propitiated as a deity.

### Names and forms

- Arabic: **عزرائيل**, vocalized **ʿIzrāʾīl**, **ʿAzrāʾīl**, and sometimes **ʿAzrāyīl**.
- Persian: **عزرائیل**, *ʿEzrāʾīl/ʿAzrāʾīl*.
- Ottoman and modern Turkish: **Azrâil/Azrail**.
- Urdu: **عزرائیل**, *ʿIzrāʾīl*.
- Malay and Indonesian: **Izrail**, **Izra’il**, **Malaikat Izrail**.
- Hebrew-form comparisons: **עזריאל**, *ʿAzriʾel/Azriel*, and **עזר־אל**, “El/God has helped.”
- Geʿez Ethiopic recension: commonly transliterated **Ezrāʾel/Ezrael**.
- European forms: Azrael, Azraël, Azraele, Azrail, Asrael.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The likeliest linguistic background is the Northwest Semitic root ע־ז־ר, *ʿ-z-r*, “help,” plus *ʾēl*, “God”: “God has helped” or “help of God.” Comparable biblical personal names include Azarel/Azriel. Etymology alone, however, does not prove identity: a person, protective angel, and death angel may bear cognate names without being the same literary figure.[Biblical Hebrew lexical entry](https://lex.ibc.oarc.science/en/w/5832/)

**[Disputed]** The spellings do not establish derivation from Ezra the scribe, and no early source transforms Ezra into the angel of death. Nor is Azrael etymologically identical with **Azazel** (עזאזל), the wilderness recipient or being in Leviticus 16 and fallen angel in Enochic reception, or with **ʿAzāzīl**, an Islamic name sometimes assigned to Iblīs. Conflations among Azrael, Azazel, Azazil, Samael, and Raziel are generally secondary.

### Titles

- **Malak al-mawt**—“Angel of Death”: the sole Qur’anic singular title.
- **Malāʾikat al-mawt / rusulunā**—“angels of death/Our messengers”: collective executors inferred from Qur’an 6:61, 7:37, 8:50, 16:28–32, and 47:27.
- Persian **fereshte-ye marg**, Turkish **ölüm meleği**, Urdu **malak-ul-maut**: vernacular translations.
- “Archangel”: common in comparative and modern writing, but not a Qur’anic technical rank.
- “Psychopomp”: a modern comparative-religion classification, useful because he conveys souls but not a traditional Arabic title.
- “Angel of grief,” “angel of bereavement,” or patron of grief counsellors: **[Modern invention]**, characteristic of late twentieth- and twenty-first-century New Age angel spirituality, not Qur’an, hadith, or medieval tafsīr.

### Classification by tradition

- **Islam:** an obedient angel commissioned to take souls; frequently one of four great angels with Jibrīl, Mīkāʾīl, and Isrāfīl in later schemes.
- **Rabbinic Judaism:** no canonical angel of death named Azrael. The Talmudic Angel of Death is normally anonymous, though Samael becomes associated with death in later materials.
- **Christianity:** no biblical or orthodox dogmatic archangel Azrael. Named appearances are apocryphal, literary, folkloric, or modern.
- **Ethiopic Christian apocrypha:** Ezrael is an angelic punisher in the Ethiopic *Apocalypse of Peter*.
- **Gnostic corpora:** no securely attested Azrael in the Nag Hammadi codices or the major heresiological catalogues.
- **Modern occultism:** usually a benevolent psychopomp or angel of transformation; occasionally recast as a “death current” spirit or demon. These are modern constructions.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Late-antique Aramaic evidence

**[Early attested]** Jewish Babylonian Aramaic incantation bowls from approximately the sixth–seventh centuries contain forms of the name ʿZRʾL among lists of angels invoked for protection. C. D. Isbell’s *Corpus of the Aramaic Incantation Bowls* and Joseph Naveh and Shaul Shaked’s *Amulets and Magic Bowls* publish relevant examples.

What the evidence establishes:

1. The name existed in a Jewish-Aramaic angelic repertoire before or around Islam’s rise.
2. It could be invoked protectively.
3. It circulated in Mesopotamia, the region in which Jewish, Christian, Mandaean, Iranian, and emerging Islamic traditions interacted.

What it does **not** establish:

1. That this ʿZRʾL was the Angel of Death.
2. That the later Islamic narrative had already formed.
3. That Azrael was a canonical Jewish archangel.
4. That the bowls constitute a public cult.

### Biblical and Second Temple corpora

**[Absence of evidence]** The Hebrew Bible and Septuagint contain destroying or death-bearing angels but no death angel named Azrael. Relevant precursors include the “destroyer” in Exodus 12:23; the angel striking Israel in 2 Samuel 24 and 1 Chronicles 21; and the angel of YHWH killing the Assyrian army in 2 Kings 19:35/Isaiah 37:36. These are functional analogues, not Azrael attestations.

**[Absence of evidence]** Azrael is not named in:

- 1 Enoch;
- Jubilees;
- Tobit;
- the securely identified Dead Sea Scrolls;
- the New Testament;
- the principal early Christian apocalypses in their earliest surviving forms.

These corpora supply a populated angelic world—Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, Uriel, Asael/Azazel, angelic watchers, punishers, and guides—but do not document the named being Azrael.

### Rabbinic texts

**[Early and later rabbinic parallels]** The Mishnah, Babylonian and Palestinian Talmuds, and classical midrashim discuss **מלאך המות, malʾakh ha-mavet**, the Angel of Death, usually anonymously. Babylonian Talmud Avodah Zarah 20b describes the death angel standing above the dying with a drawn sword and a drop of gall; the sight causes death and decomposition. Bava Batra 16a connects the satan, evil inclination, and Angel of Death. Sukkah 53a tells how Solomon tried to save two attendants by sending them away, thereby conveying them to the place appointed for their deaths.

**[Important negative finding]** These are not named-Azrael passages. Later popular compilations sometimes replace the anonymous Angel of Death with “Azrael,” retrospectively importing the Islamic name.

The Sukkah 53a story is the closest old analogue to “The Appointment in Samarra”: Solomon sees the Angel of Death looking troubled, sends the endangered men to Luz, and learns that this was where Death was required to take them. The tale’s structure is rabbinic; “Baghdad,” “Samarra,” the merchant, and the servant belong to later retelling.[Sukkah story and Maugham comparison](https://www.tatanka.com/topic/parables/appointment_in_samarra.html)

### Qur’an

**[Securely attested foundational material, c. 610–632]**

- Qur’an 32:11: one commissioned “Angel of Death.”
- 6:61: plural divine messengers take the soul.
- 8:50 and 47:27: angels strike the faces and backs of hostile unbelievers at death.
- 16:28–32: angels receive the wicked reproachfully and the righteous with peace.
- 39:42: God takes souls at death and during sleep.
- 79:1–2 is often interpreted as angels violently drawing out some souls and gently releasing others, though the verse does not name Azrael.

**[Learned systematization]** Commentators reconciled the apparent grammatical plurality by making Azrael the chief and the other angels his assistants: God is the ultimate cause, Azrael performs the seizure, and subordinate angels handle the soul. Al-Qurṭubī’s harmonization is summarized in *The Study Quran*.[Qur’an 6:61](https://quran.com/en/6/61) [Study Quran discussion](https://thequran.love/wp-content/uploads/2025/03/thestudyquran-4.pdf)

### Muqātil ibn Sulaymān

**[Earliest secure named identification, eighth century]** Muqātil ibn Sulaymān (d. 150 AH/767 CE), author of the earliest substantially complete surviving Qur’anic commentary, identifies the Angel of Death in Qur’an 32:11 as ʿIzrāʾīl. This makes his commentary the safest terminus for the developed name-function union. It is later than the Qur’an but early in tafsīr history.

### Hadith

**[Later canonical elaboration]** The major Sunni hadith collections call the figure “the Angel of Death,” not Azrael. The most famous encounter is Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī 1339/3407 and Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2372: God sends the Angel of Death to Moses; Moses strikes him and damages an eye; God restores it and offers Moses added years equal to the hairs covered by his hand on an ox; Moses chooses death near the Holy Land.[Bukhārī 1339](https://sahibukhari.com/bukhari/1339)

The hadith’s authority in Sunni Islam does not make “Azrael” part of its text. Later authors identify the anonymous angel with Azrael. Commentators disagree whether Moses failed to recognize an angel appearing as a man, acted in self-defense, or struck an assumed bodily form rather than the angel’s essential nature.

**[Later canonical elaboration]** Another canonical hadith cycle personifies Death as a ram slaughtered between Paradise and Hell after judgment, signifying the end of mortality. Medieval interpreters sometimes associated this figure with the Angel of Death; others maintained the distinction between the creature “Death” and the angel who administers death.

### Early tafsīr, *qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ*, and cosmography

**[Medieval elaboration]** Al-Ṭabarī (d. 923), al-Thaʿlabī (d. 1035), al-Kisāʾī, al-Masʿūdī, al-Maqdisī, al-Qurṭubī (d. 1273), Ibn Kathīr (d. 1373), and later compilers transmit expanding stories about the Angel of Death.

The central Adam narrative says God sent angels to bring earth for Adam’s body. Earth appealed to God for protection; Gabriel, Michael, and sometimes Isrāfīl withdrew, but Azrael obeyed despite Earth’s plea. Because he could take the clay from which human life was made, God appointed him to take human souls. The story is absent from the Qur’an. Its versions differ in the sequence of angels, wording, and whether the appointment is reward, consequence, or proof of fitness.

A University of Chicago publication of early Arabic papyrus material concerning Adam reports the tradition that ʿIzrāʾīl succeeded after Gabriel, Michael, and Isrāfīl failed, and was consequently given the task of drawing souls from bodies.[Early Arabic papyrus discussion](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/oip75.pdf)

### Al-Suyūṭī

**[Learned systematization, fifteenth century]** Jalāl al-Dīn al-Suyūṭī’s *al-Ḥabāʾik fī akhbār al-malāʾik* compiles traditions about angels, including the Angel of Death, from Qur’an, hadith, tafsīr, ascetic lore, and earlier collections. Stephen Burge’s edition and study demonstrates how heterogeneous reports were gathered without thereby acquiring equal authenticity.[Burge, *Angels in Islam* material](https://ghayb.com/wp-content/uploads/2021/07/Angels-In-Islam-Suyuti.pdf)

### Jewish mystical and magical texts

**[Medieval/early-modern accretion]** The *Hekhalot* corpus, *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh*, and Zoharic literature contain many similarly formed angel names. **Raziel** is not Azrael. **Azriel** may appear in later Kabbalistic angel lists, but no secure early Zoharic passage makes him the Islamic-style Angel of Death.

*Sefer Raziel* is a medieval Hebrew magical compilation, probably assembled in Ashkenaz around the thirteenth century from earlier materials. Modern websites frequently mine its angel lists and then supply Azrael with a seal, color, planet, or invocation. Such details must be verified manuscript by manuscript; they are not an ancient, standardized Azrael cult.[Background on *Sefer Raziel*](https://legrandgrimoire.com/guides/kabbalah/rituals-inspired-by-sefer-raziel-hamalach/)

### Ethiopic *Apocalypse of Peter*

**[Regional Christian accretion]** The Greek *Apocalypse of Peter* originated in the second century, but the name Ezrael occurs in the much later Ethiopic recension, whose relevant manuscripts are early modern, often dated around the fifteenth–sixteenth centuries. Ezrael appears repeatedly as an angel administering eschatological punishments. Burge argues that Islamic influence on Ethiopic transmission is plausible; therefore, it is methodologically unsound to cite “Azrael in the Apocalypse of Peter” as a second-century attestation.

### Gnostic texts and heresiologists

**[Absence of evidence]** Searches of the Nag Hammadi corpus, including the major Robinson and Meyer translations, produce no secure Azrael. Nor do the surviving accounts of Irenaeus, Hippolytus, or Epiphanius document him as an aeon, archon, demon, or angel in a historical Gnostic school. The Gnostic Archive’s corpus index is useful for checking the translated collection.[Nag Hammadi Library](https://gnosis.org/naghamm/nhl.html)

This absence matters. Modern “Gnostic Azrael” systems combine the Islamic angel with modern occult cosmologies; they are not recoveries of a Nag Hammadi being. Azrael should therefore be cross-referenced with a Gnostic pantheon only as a later comparative or modern syncretistic figure.

### Christian scholastic angelology

**[Absence of evidence]** Pseudo-Dionysius’ *Celestial Hierarchy* supplies nine angelic orders but does not name Azrael. Thomas Aquinas discusses angelic nature, cognition, embodiment, and ministry without recognizing Azrael as a canonical archangel. Later attempts to place him among Dominions, Thrones, or Archangels are modern or occult taxonomies.

### Grimoires

**[Absence of evidence]** Azrael is not one of the seventy-two spirits of the *Ars Goetia*. No stable Azrael office appears in the seventeenth-century *Lemegeton* or in the core *Book of Abramelin*. Confusion commonly arises with Raziel, Azazel, and strings of divine or angelic names. Joseph Peterson’s manuscript-based edition of the *Lemegeton* permits direct checking.[*Lemegeton* critical web edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm)

### Dante and Milton

**[Absence of evidence]** Azrael does not appear as a named character in Dante’s *Commedia* or Milton’s *Paradise Lost*. Their importance is contextual: both decisively shaped Western images of angels, demons, and death, which modern illustrators later projected onto Azrael. Claims that Dante included Azrael generally concern modern derivative fiction or fan-created continuities.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain

**[Qur’anic core]** The Angel of Death takes the human soul at its appointed time and returns it toward God. He does not decide the time of death.

**[Later canonical elaboration]** Azrael supervises assistants, distinguishes righteous from wicked souls, and participates in the soul’s transition into *barzakh*. The gentle or terrifying mode reflects Qur’anic passages about plural angels and later hadith descriptions.

**[Mystical elaboration]** Sufi authors make Azrael an embodiment of the divine attribute that severs attachment between spirit and body. In Ibn ʿArabī and later Akbarian interpretation, angelic forms correspond to spiritual functions; ʿAbd al-Karīm al-Jīlī says the death angel may appear according to the soul’s dominant inner form. This is metaphysical anthropology, not ordinary physical description.

### Form and powers

**[Early material]** No Qur’anic description exists.

**[Medieval accretion]** Reports give him:

- cosmic size, spanning the heavens or having Earth like a small object before him;
- four faces or multiple faces;
- thousands of wings;
- innumerable eyes and tongues corresponding to living creatures;
- a register, tree, tablet, or leaves bearing mortal names;
- helpers assigned to believers, unbelievers, land, sea, and other creatures;
- a beautiful appearance to the righteous and a terrifying appearance to the wicked;
- a sword, spear, whip, hooks, or instruments of extraction in particular texts.

These descriptions are not mutually consistent. The familiar claim of “four faces, 4,000 wings, and a body covered with eyes and tongues” is a composite of medieval reports popularized by nineteenth- and twentieth-century encyclopedias. It must not be presented as Qur’anic.

### Iconography

There is no ancient, universally standardized Azrael icon. Islamic manuscript art generally portrays angels as winged, haloed, often youthful and richly clothed. An Ottoman/Persian *ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt* manuscript in Topkapı Palace, H.408, fol. 43b, contains an “Angel of Death” image dated approximately 1525–1575.[A&AePortal catalogue](https://aaeportal.com/images/68722/the-angel-of-death)

The oldest surviving manuscript of al-Qazwīnī’s *Wonders of Creation*, completed at Wāsiṭ in 1280, contains more than 400 images and establishes the visual setting in which later Angel-of-Death images circulated, though not every winged angel in it is Azrael.[Library of Congress manuscript record](https://www.loc.gov/resource/gdcwdl.wdl_08962/?sp=12)

A sixteenth-century Ottoman manuscript of Aflākī’s Rumi hagiography depicts a handsome young Angel of Death attending Rumi’s final meeting. The Morgan catalogue confusingly glosses the figure as “ʿAzrāʾīl (Gabriel),” an example of how museum descriptions can preserve variant or mistaken identifications.[Morgan Library miniature](https://www.themorgan.org/collection/treasures-of-islamic-manuscript-painting/49)

The black-robed skeleton with scythe is Western late-medieval Death iconography, not early Islamic Azrael. Modern art often merges the two.

### Associations not securely established

No early source securely assigns Azrael:

- a planet;
- zodiac sign;
- weekday;
- liturgical feast;
- sacred animal or plant;
- canonical color;
- number;
- temple emblem;
- stable sigil.

Modern associations with cream, white, black, purple, Scorpio, Saturday, lilies, skulls, or grief crystals are **[Modern invention]** unless tied to a specifically named modern practitioner.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other beings

Azrael has no attested parents, spouse, children, siblings, or birth story in foundational Islamic sources. Calling the great angels “brothers” is devotional or literary language, not genealogy.

### Documented interactions

Each entry below concerns a discrete scene rather than mere membership in the same angelic class.

- **God/Allah — command and appointment.** God entrusts the Angel of Death with taking souls. Qur’an 32:11, early seventh century. **[Securely attested foundational material]**
- **God/Allah — restoration and renewed commission.** After Moses strikes the Angel of Death, the angel reports to God; God restores his eye and sends him back. *Ṣaḥīḥ al-Bukhārī* 1339/3407; *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* 2372, compiled ninth century. **[Later canonical elaboration; angel unnamed]**
- **Earth/al-Arḍ — taking Adamic clay.** Earth appeals to God’s protection; Azrael nevertheless takes clay for Adam. *Qiṣaṣ*, tafsīr, and cosmographical traditions, early medieval onward. **[Medieval accretion]**
- **Jibrīl/Gabriel — sequential mission.** Gabriel first attempts or refuses to take earth for Adam; Azrael later succeeds. Early Islamic Adam traditions. **[Medieval accretion]**
- **Mīkāʾīl/Michael — sequential mission.** Michael likewise returns without clay; Azrael succeeds. Same cycle. **[Medieval accretion]**
- **Isrāfīl — sequential mission.** Some versions include Isrāfīl among the unsuccessful angels. Same cycle. **[Regional/textual variant]**
- **Adam — creation and death.** Azrael obtains the earth forming Adam and in later narratives attends or extracts Adam’s soul. Arabic Adam apocrypha and *qiṣaṣ*. **[Medieval accretion]**
- **Moses/Mūsā — combat and negotiation.** Moses damages the death angel’s eye; the angel returns after consulting God. Bukhārī/Muslim. **[Later canonical elaboration; identification as Azrael is later]**
- **Abraham/Ibrāhīm — revelation of forms.** In later eschatological lore Abraham asks to see how the angel appears to believer and unbeliever and is shown beautiful and terrifying forms. **[Medieval accretion]**
- **Solomon/Sulaymān — appointed-death tale.** Solomon questions an anonymous death angel and attempts to move endangered men; his action brings them to their appointed place. Babylonian Talmud Sukkah 53a; Islamic analogues later substitute Azrael. **[Rabbinic parallel; later syncretistic naming]**
- **Rumi — welcoming death.** Aflākī’s *Manāqib al-ʿārifīn*, fourteenth century, narrates the death angel’s arrival at Rumi’s final illness; later versions name and picture him as Azrael. **[Sufi hagiography]**
- **Death personified — mastery or distinction.** Some medieval cosmologies describe Death as a terrible created entity placed under Azrael’s control; others identify Azrael with Death. **[Conflicting learned systematizations]**
- **Subordinate angels of death — command hierarchy.** Tafsīr reconciles Qur’an 32:11’s singular with 6:61’s plural by placing helpers under Azrael. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Satan/Iblīs — no secure narrative interaction.** They share later angelological narratives but no foundational Azrael–Iblīs episode is established. Claims that Azrael and Azazil are brothers are unsupported.
- **Samael — equation rather than episode.** Some modern Jewish and occult works equate the two because both are called angels of death. No early text records them meeting or establishes identity. **[Modern syncretistic identification]**
- **Azazel — no secure interaction.** Similar spelling has produced modern conflation, but Azazel’s Enochic and Levitical histories are separate.
- **Raphael — occasional modern equation.** Gustav Davidson and later popular angel dictionaries report conflicting equations; no early source securely identifies Azrael with Raphael. **[Modern systematization]**
- **Ezrael of the Ethiopic Apocalypse — probable reception relationship.** This is likely an adaptation of the Islamicate death angel rather than a second being interacting with him. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Appointment as Angel of Death

**[Medieval Islamic narrative]** God determines to create Adam and sends the great angels to obtain earth. Earth adjures each messenger by God not to harm it. Gabriel, Michael, and sometimes Isrāfīl retreat out of compassion. Azrael answers that obedience to God overrides Earth’s plea and gathers soil of different colors and qualities. Human diversity is sometimes explained from this mixed earth. God appoints Azrael over death because he proved able to separate earthly material from its place and would therefore be able to separate souls from bodies.

The early core is the collection of Adam’s clay; Azrael’s named role and the moral rationale vary. Some tellings praise firmness; others make the office a burdensome consequence. The story also explains why humans fear him while he remains God’s obedient servant.

### Taking souls

**[Qur’anic core plus medieval elaboration]** God fixes every lifespan. Azrael receives notice through a tablet, a command, a falling leaf from a cosmic tree, or the fading of a written name. Assistants loosen the soul; Azrael takes it. The righteous soul departs gently and is greeted with peace, while the wicked soul is pulled violently. The Qur’an supports differentiated receptions but not the leaf, tree, detailed tools, or proper name.

### Moses strikes the Angel of Death

**[Canonical hadith; later named identification]** The death angel comes to Moses. Moses strikes him and damages an eye. The angel complains to God that he has been sent to someone unwilling to die. God restores him and offers Moses an extended life measured by hairs beneath his hand on an ox. When Moses learns death must still follow, he accepts it and asks to die near the Holy Land.

Later rationalizations say the angel arrived without identifying himself, assumed a vulnerable human form, or came before Moses had been asked to consent. Critics, including some modern Muslim reformists, question the narrative’s theological coherence; hadith traditionalists defend its sound chain and interpretive possibilities. The actual hadith never says “Azrael.”

### Abraham sees the two forms

**[Medieval eschatological lore]** Abraham asks the Angel of Death to reveal the form used for believers; he sees beauty and says this alone would reward the righteous. He then sees the horrific form used for unbelievers and says its sight alone would suffice as punishment. Versions vary in the angel’s color, smell, weapons, and number of faces.

### Solomon cannot evade destiny

**[Rabbinic and Islamicate folklore]** In the Talmudic version, Solomon sends two men to Luz after noticing the Angel of Death’s concern; they die where the angel had been ordered to take them. In Islamic versions, a man frightened by the angel’s gaze asks Solomon to have the wind carry him to India; the angel had wondered why the man was in Jerusalem when death was appointed in India.

Somerset Maugham’s 1932 play *Sheppey* relocated the pattern to Baghdad and Samarra. John O’Hara used “Appointment in Samarra” as his 1934 novel’s title. Calling the Maugham text an ancient Sufi tale is therefore imprecise: the fatal-appointment motif is old, but that wording and geography are modern literary recasting.

### Rumi and Azrael

**[Sufi hagiography]** Aflākī’s account of Rumi’s last days presents the Angel of Death as a beautiful visitor whom the saint recognizes and welcomes. The story signifies willing union rather than defeat. Later Ottoman illustration and popular biographies make the figure explicitly Azrael.

### Nasir Khusraw and visionary encounter

**[Sufi/Ismaili reception]** A testament attributed to Nāṣir-i Khusraw describes a dream encounter with the death angel warning of approaching death. The episode belongs to visionary and ethical literature, not biographical evidence in the modern sense.

### Azrael as physician

**[Palestinian folklore]** J. E. Hanauer’s *Folk-lore of the Holy Land* records a story in which Azrael lives as a human physician while retaining his office. His son learns that Azrael standing at a patient’s head means death, at the feet recovery; comic stratagems temporarily thwart him. This is late-recorded folk narrative, not scripture.[Hanauer text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Folk-lore_of_the_Holy_Land/Azrael)

### End of Death

**[Canonical hadith plus disputed interpretation]** After final judgment, Death is brought as a ram and slaughtered between Paradise and Hell, and both communities are told that existence is now everlasting. Some interpreters treat the ram as Death personified; a minority assimilates it to the death angel. The sources do not require Azrael himself to be killed.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### No institutional cult

**[Documented absence]** There is no known ancient or medieval temple dedicated to Azrael, no priesthood, civic festival, sacrificial calendar, temple economy, oracle, or pilgrimage center. Islam’s prohibition on worshipping angels precludes calling Islamic respect for Azrael a cult in the Greco-Roman sense.

No credible evidence associates Azrael with human sacrifice, temple prostitution, or sacrificial killing. Claims to that effect normally confuse him with Azazel, Moloch, or modern fictional death cults.

### Devotional and magical uses

**[Late-antique magic]** Aramaic bowls invoking a similarly named angel are household apotropaic objects, not temple worship.

**[Medieval Islam]** Belief in the Angel of Death forms part of angelology and eschatology. Deathbed prayers, Qur’anic recitation, remembrance of death, and funeral rites address God rather than Azrael. Stories about him function as admonition.

**[Folk practice]** Vernacular prayers sometimes ask God to make the encounter with ʿIzrāʾīl gentle. Such language acknowledges his office; it is not worship of the angel.

**[Modern occultism]** Individual practitioners now invoke Azrael for necromancy, grief work, “shadow work,” past-life visions, or transition rituals. These practices are recent and decentralized. A 2022 online ritual using a black candle, incense, mirror, and sigil is evidence for contemporary practice, not antiquity.[Contemporary practitioner report](https://wizardforums.com/threads/exploring-past-lives-with-azrael.1782/)

The *Lemegeton* and *Abramelin* do not supply an authentic ancient “seal of Azrael.” Commercial sigils marketed today generally derive from modern artists or automatic-writing systems.

## 7. Historical evolution

### c. 500–700: late-antique angel-name

The consonantal ʿZRʾL appears in Mesopotamian Jewish magical texts. Function uncertain; death association unproved.

### c. 610–632: Qur’anic office

The Qur’an presents one commissioned Angel of Death alongside plural soul-taking angels. No proper name.

### Eighth century: named identification

Muqātil ibn Sulaymān’s tafsīr identifies the Qur’anic office-holder as ʿIzrāʾīl. Traditions attributed to early authorities expand his role in Adam’s creation.

### Ninth–tenth centuries: hadith and historical-exegetical consolidation

Bukhārī and Muslim canonize the Moses–Angel of Death encounter without the name. Al-Ṭabarī and other exegetes collect creation, death, and eschatological narratives.

### Eleventh–fourteenth centuries: narrative expansion

*Qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ*, cosmographies, preaching literature, Sufi works, and tafsīr give the angel forms, assistants, cosmic size, registers, and individualized encounters. Al-Qazwīnī’s cosmography establishes an influential illustrated angelic world.

### Fourteenth–sixteenth centuries: Sufi and visual elaboration

Aflākī’s Rumi hagiography and its Ottoman reception make the Angel of Death beautiful and welcome to the saint. Persian, Arab, Ottoman, and Indo-Islamic manuscripts visualize the figure in locally conventional angelic dress.

### Fifteenth–sixteenth centuries: Ethiopic Christian reception

Ezrael enters the Ethiopic *Apocalypse of Peter* as an eschatological punisher, probably in a Christian environment already exposed to Islamic angelology.

### Early modern Jewish and Christian reception

European Orientalist, Christian Kabbalist, and magical literature increasingly treats Azrael as a generally “Hebrew,” “Jewish,” or “biblical” archangel, although the documentary chain is primarily Islamicate. Samael, Azriel, Azazel, and Raziel become confused.

### Nineteenth century: literary globalization

European poetry and Orientalist folklore collections popularize a solemn or compassionate Azrael. Henry Wadsworth Longfellow’s “The Reaper and the Flowers” uses Azrael as the angel who gathers children. Jean Delville’s Symbolist “Azraël” imagery associates the name with fin-de-siècle mysticism.

### Twentieth century: encyclopedic and fictional synthesis

General angel dictionaries—especially Gustav Davidson’s 1967 *A Dictionary of Angels*—assemble claims from unequal sources into compact entries. Davidson is useful as an index but frequently suppresses chronology and source quality; it should not be treated as a primary authority.

The name migrates into novels, comics, film, games, fantasy angelologies, and occult manuals. The scythe-bearing “Azrael” increasingly resembles the European Grim Reaper.

### Twenty-first century

Azrael exists simultaneously as:

1. the traditional Islamic name of the Qur’anic Angel of Death;
2. a disputed name rejected by Muslims who restrict themselves to Qur’an and authenticated hadith;
3. a Sufi symbol of divinely ordained transformation;
4. a New Age helper for grief and transitions;
5. an occult spirit;
6. a fictional title for assassins, angels, antiheroes, cats, demons, and weapons;
7. a militant nom de guerre.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature

- **Longfellow, “The Reaper and the Flowers” (1839):** Azrael takes children gently; a major English-language contribution to the compassionate image.
- **Maugham, *Sheppey* (1932):** the Baghdad–Samarra fatal-appointment parable, with Death rather than a historically sourced named Azrael.
- **John O’Hara, *Appointment in Samarra* (1934):** title derived from Maugham.
- **G. K. Chesterton, “Lepanto” (1911):** invokes Azrael within a polemical Christian representation of Ottoman Islam.
- **Milton and Dante:** not Azrael sources; their demonological and angelic imagery influenced later reception only.

### DC Comics

**[Modern invention]** Denny O’Neil and Joe Quesada introduced Jean-Paul Valley as Azrael in *Batman: Sword of Azrael* no. 1, October 1992. “Azrael” is a conditioned assassin’s mantle associated with the Order of St. Dumas, divine vengeance, and a flaming sword—not an adaptation of a particular medieval Azrael myth. Valley later replaced Bruce Wayne during *Knightfall* and adopted an increasingly armored, violent Batman persona.[DC history](https://www.dc.com/blog/2016/04/18/the-dctv-secrets-of-gotham-ep-2-18-pinewood)

A 1995–2003 solo series ran for 100 issues. Michael Lane became another Azrael in 2009. *Gotham* made Theo Galavan an Azrael; the 2022 *Sword of Azrael* miniseries revisited Jean-Paul Valley’s conditioning and religious violence.[DC, *Sword of Azrael* 2022](https://www.dc.com/comics/sword-of-azrael-2022/sword-of-azrael-1)

DC occasionally refers to a literal celestial Azrael, but its dominant Azrael is a human vigilante or inherited office. This is commercial Christian-gothic reinvention.

### Marvel and Disney corporate reception

**[Modern invention]** Marvel’s Azrael predates Disney’s ownership of Marvel. An Angel of Death named Azrael first appeared in *Venus* no. 14, cover-dated 1951, created by Bill Everett. Later Marvel stories treat him as a heavenly enforcer pursuing those who cheat death. The character therefore became “Disney” intellectual property only through the Walt Disney Company’s 2009 acquisition of Marvel; he was not created by Walt Disney or classical Disney animation.[Marvel character record](https://marvel.fandom.com/wiki/Azrael_%28Earth-616%29)

If “Disney’s Azrael” means the *Lucifer* television character, that identification is also imprecise: *Lucifer* derives from DC/Vertigo and is controlled by Warner Bros. Its Azrael, portrayed by Charlyne Yi, is Lucifer’s sister and the Angel of Death. It is a modern female personification, not a scriptural representation.

No independently verified classical Walt Disney animated Azrael corresponding to the angel was found. Gargamel’s cat Azrael belongs to *The Smurfs*, created by Peyo, not Disney.

### Other mass-media reinventions

- **The Smurfs:** Gargamel’s cat Azrael, introduced in Peyo’s comics, turns the death angel’s name into a comic villain’s pet.
- **Kevin Smith’s *Dogma* (1999):** Azrael is a fallen Muse/demonic antagonist, unrelated to Islamic orthodoxy.
- **Games and anime:** *Darksiders*, *BlazBlue*, *Gundam SEED*, and numerous role-playing systems use the name for martial or destructive characters.
- **Warhammer 40,000:** Azrael is Supreme Grand Master of the Dark Angels; the name signals angelic militancy, not psychopomp function.
- **Film *Azrael* (2024):** the title applies to a human protagonist in post-apocalyptic horror rather than a historical angel tradition.

### New Age spirituality

**[Modern invention]** Contemporary guides describe Azrael as a tender grief counsellor who comforts mourners, hospice workers, and the dying; assign him cream-colored energy, crystals, prayers, and signs; and recommend “working with” him through meditation. This extends the older gentle psychopomp but is not documented in medieval Islam. A 2026 commercial guide openly presents him as patron of grief and carers, illustrating the current form of the tradition.[Modern spiritual guide](https://archangelireland.com/blogs/news/archangel-azrael-the-complete-guide)

### Political and militant appropriation

Ayyūb Fāliḥ Ḥasan al-Rubayʿī, an Iraqi Shiʿi militia commander, adopted **Abū ʿAzrāʾīl**, “Father of Azrael,” and gained social-media celebrity while fighting Islamic State forces from 2014 onward. The name weaponizes Azrael’s popular identity as inexorable death. Reports documented violent imagery and a video allegedly showing mutilation of a burned corpse.[United States Institute of Peace profile](https://iranprimer.usip.org/blog/2021/nov/10/profiles-pro-iran-militias-iraq) [Al Jazeera report](https://www.aljazeera.com/amp/news/2015/8/28/video-of-celebrity-iraq-fighter-slicing-body-goes-viral)

This is documented political-military appropriation, not a cult of Azrael.

## 9. Contested-material pass

### Etymology and origin

- **Secure:** ʿZRʾL is morphologically compatible with “God has helped.”
- **Uncertain:** whether the late-antique bowl angel is genealogically the immediate source of Islamic ʿIzrāʾīl.
- **Unsupported:** derivation from Ezra the scribe.
- **False conflation:** Azrael = Azazel or Azazil on spelling alone.

### Jewish origin versus Islamic formation

The name has a plausible Jewish-Aramaic prehistory, but the identity “named chief Angel of Death Azrael” is first securely formed in Islamic exegesis. Calling him simply “the Jewish Angel of Death” erases both the anonymous rabbinic death angel and the Islamic evidence.

### “Canonical in Islam”

Two usages conflict:

1. The **office** is indisputably Qur’anic.
2. The **name** is post-Qur’anic and absent from the authenticated hadith texts.

Traditional exegetes widely accept ʿIzrāʾīl; modern Salafi writers often reject the name as unverified *isrāʾīliyyāt*. The appropriate historical conclusion is neither “Azrael is absent from Islam” nor “the Qur’an names Azrael,” but: the office is scriptural and the name is an early, durable exegetical identification.[Modern Sunni objection](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/40671) [Turkish Islamic encyclopedia](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/azrail)

### Syncretism

- With the anonymous Jewish Angel of Death: functional comparison, not automatic identity.
- With Samael: later Jewish/occult equation.
- With Raphael: occasional modern angel-dictionary equation.
- With Ethiopic Ezrael: probable Islamicate influence.
- With the Grim Reaper: modern visual fusion.
- With Gnostic archons: modern invention.

### Human sacrifice, sexual rites, temples

No evidence. Assertions generally result from confusing Azrael with Azazel or importing generic “death god” motifs.

### Suppression

No documented campaign suppressed an Azrael cult because no institutional cult is known. Modern theological criticism targets the reliability of the **name**, not belief in the Qur’anic Angel of Death.

### Occult antiquity

No ancient or medieval standardized “Azrael sigil,” planetary correspondence, invocation rite, or Goetic office has been established. Rituals advertised as ancient are usually twentieth- or twenty-first-century constructions.

### Neopagan worship

Individual angelolatry and occult practice exist online, but there is no reliable census or evidence of a large organized Azrael-centered religion. The safest classification is small-scale, decentralized modern practice.

## 10. Historiography and scholarly debate

A. J. Wensinck’s *Encyclopaedia of Islam* article systematized the classical Muslim material but belongs to an older Orientalist reference tradition. Modern work by Stephen Burge improves the picture through close attention to manuscript recensions, hadith, tafsīr, and cross-religious transmission.

John MacDonald’s studies of late Islamic eschatology examine the developed Angel of Death traditions. Jane Idleman Smith and Yvonne Haddad treat Azrael within Muslim death and resurrection beliefs. Christian Lange studies the personnel and geography of Islamic hell. Leigh Chipman compares Adam-creation narratives in Judaism and Islam.

For Jewish antecedents, the incantation-bowl editions of Isbell, Naveh, and Shaked are more probative than modern angel dictionaries. The *Jewish Encyclopedia* entry is useful as a witness to turn-of-the-twentieth-century synthesis but freely combines rabbinic and Arabic lore and should not be used to backdate the composite figure.[*Jewish Encyclopedia*, “Death, Angel of”](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/5018-death-angel-of)

Gustav Davidson’s *Dictionary of Angels* is immensely influential in popular culture but methodologically hazardous: it aggregates scriptures, grimoires, folklore, poetry, and earlier encyclopedias without consistently distinguishing their dates or authority. Claims traceable only to Davidson require renewed source verification.

Jeffrey Burton Russell’s histories of the Devil, Henry Ansgar Kelly’s *Satan: A Biography*, and Bernard Bamberger’s *Fallen Angels* clarify why Azrael should not be collapsed into Satanic demonology. Azrael is not a fallen angel in Islamic tradition.

Birger Pearson and Kurt Rudolph’s work on Gnosticism, together with the Robinson and Meyer Nag Hammadi editions, provides the basis for the negative conclusion: historical Gnostic evidence for Azrael is lacking.

### Surviving evidence and repositories

- Incantation bowls: dispersed among museums and collections; published through specialist corpora.
- Qur’anic and tafsīr manuscripts: widely dispersed; Muqātil’s text survives through later manuscript transmission.
- Al-Qazwīnī manuscripts: Library of Congress, Topkapı Palace, British Museum, Ashmolean, and other collections.
- Ethiopic *Apocalypse of Peter*: known through late manuscripts and critical editions; the manuscript date must not be confused with the second-century Greek work.
- Rumi hagiographic imagery: Morgan Library and other Ottoman/Persian manuscript collections.

### Discredited or misleading claims still circulating

1. “Azrael is named in the Qur’an.”
2. “Azrael is the Jewish biblical Angel of Death.”
3. “The second-century *Apocalypse of Peter* names Azrael.”
4. “Azrael appears in Dante, Milton, 1 Enoch, or the Nag Hammadi codices.”
5. “Azrael and Azazel are the same being.”
6. “A standard ancient sigil and color system survives.”
7. “The Appointment in Samarra is verbatim an ancient Sufi text.”
8. “The Grim Reaper’s scythe is traditional Islamic Azrael iconography.”
