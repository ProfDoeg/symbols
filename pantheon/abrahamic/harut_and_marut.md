# Harut and Marut: Research Dossier

**Class:** A paired collective of angels in the dominant Qurʾanic reading; alternatively two human kings in a minority reading, and fallen or punished angels in much later narrative tradition. They are not gods, jinn, or demons in the Qurʾān, although later polemic, folklore, and occult literature sometimes treats them as fallen angels, demonic magicians, or spirits.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Qurʾān 2:102, in Arabic, conventionally assigned to the Medinan period of Muḥammad’s proclamation, approximately 622–632 CE. The verse names **Hārūt and Mārūt at Babylon**, says that they taught no one without warning, “We are only a trial, so do not disbelieve,” and associates what people learned from them with separating husband and wife. It does **not** say that the pair rebelled, committed sexual sin, killed anyone, became demons, were suspended by their feet, or were imprisoned in a well.

**Evidentiary key:** **[Early attestation]** Qurʾān or securely pre-Islamic comparative material. **[Early exegesis]** reports attributed to first- and second-century AH authorities but preserved in later compilations. **[Canonical elaboration]** widely transmitted Qurʾanic exegesis or theology, not additional Qurʾanic revelation. **[Regional variant]** localization or form peculiar to a region. **[Medieval accretion]** qiṣaṣ, cosmography, poetry, or folklore. **[Modern reconstruction]** scholarly historical or comparative inference. **[Modern invention]** a recent claim lacking an identifiable premodern source.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The pair in the Qurʾān

Arabic: **هَارُوت وَمَارُوت**, vocalized **Hārūt wa-Mārūt**. Persian: هاروت و ماروت. Turkish: Hârût ve Mârût. Older European forms include Harut/Marut, Harout/Marout, and Hārūt/Mārūt.

In the received reading of Qurʾān 2:102, the phrase is:

> وَمَا أُنْزِلَ عَلَى الْمَلَكَيْنِ بِبَابِلَ هَارُوتَ وَمَارُوتَ  
> *wa-mā unzila ʿalā l-malakayni bi-Bābila Hārūta wa-Mārūta*  
> “and what was sent down upon/to the two angels at Babylon, Hārūt and Mārūt.”

The names are indeclinable foreign proper names in the traditional grammatical analysis. The received **malakayn**, “two angels,” is the overwhelmingly dominant recitation. A minority reading attributed to Ibn ʿAbbās, al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, Abū al-Aswad al-Duʾalī, and al-Ḍaḥḥāk vocalized the same consonantal ductus as **malikayn**, “two kings.” That reading turns Hārūt and Mārūt into human rulers or magicians rather than angels, but it is not one of the canonical mass-transmitted readings. Al-Ṭabarī records it and rejects it in favor of “two angels.” The grammatical data and principal dispute are summarized by the [Quranic Arabic Corpus](https://corpus.quran.com/treebank.jsp?chapter=2&token=18&verse=102) and in recent work on the verse’s history of interpretation.[Modern discussion](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/17/8/876)

The Qurʾān gives them no epithets. “The two angels of Babylon,” “teachers of magic,” “fallen angels,” and “angels in the well” are descriptive titles coined by commentators, scholars, or storytellers. Of these, only “the two angels at Babylon” closely reproduces the received Qurʾanic wording.

### Etymology

**Iranian derivation — [Modern scholarly reconstruction].** Paul de Lagarde in 1866 connected Hārūt and Mārūt with Avestan **Haurvatāt** (“Wholeness,” “Completeness”) and **Amərətāt/Ameretat** (“Immortality”), two Amesha Spentas associated respectively with waters and plants. Middle Persian produced **Hordād/Harwadād** and **Amurdād**, while a Sogdian glossary preserves forms transliterated **hrwwt** and **mrwwt**, remarkably close to Hārūt and Mārūt. Armenian tradition has the paired plant-name *Haurot-Maurot*. These intermediate forms make the comparison substantially stronger than a chance resemblance between the Arabic and full Avestan forms.[Encyclopaedia Iranica: Hordād](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/hordad/) [Encyclopaedia Iranica: Hārūt and Mārūt](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/harut-and-marut/)

The identification nevertheless explains principally the **names**, not the Qurʾanic story. Haurvatāt and Ameretat are beneficent, grammatically feminine Iranian divine entities, not Babylonian male angels who teach prohibited magic. No extant Zoroastrian text supplies the Qurʾanic episode. The Turkish *İslâm Ansiklopedisi* therefore judges the name-relationship generally accepted but stresses that the connection does not extend securely beyond onomastics.[TDV İslâm Ansiklopedisi](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/harut-ve-marut)

**Arioch and Marioch — [Disputed reconstruction].** Slavonic 2 Enoch names Arioch and Marioch as guardians of Enoch’s books or of the earth; related forms were reportedly recognized in Aramaic Enoch material from Qumran. Their rhyming names have been compared with Hārūt and Mārūt. The functions and narratives, however, do not match closely. They should not be identified as the Qurʾanic pair without qualification.

**Vedic Maruts — [Rejected or weak comparison].** The Sanskrit **Maruts**, a group of storm deities, resemble “Mārūt” in form only. Neither pairing, function, Babylonian setting, nor transmission path supports identifying one Qurʾanic angel with the Vedic collective. This is a comparativist resemblance, not a documented genealogy.

**Semitic derivations:** Various attempts have linked the names to Arabic roots, to the Amorite god Ḥauron, or to other Near Eastern beings. No proposal commands evidence comparable to the Iranian name-pair, and none has established a pre-Qurʾanic figure already bearing both Arabic names.

### Classification through time

- **Qurʾān, seventh century — [Early attestation]:** two angels under the received reading; their warning makes them instruments of a divine test rather than straightforward tempters.
- **Minority exegetical reading — [Early exegesis]:** two human kings, **malikayn**, or two ordinary Babylonian magicians.
- **Classical tafsīr — [Canonical elaboration]:** either obedient angels teaching discriminative knowledge by divine command, or angels who fell into sin and were punished.
- **Ibn Ḥazm and marginal interpretations — [Learned systematization]:** devils or members of jinn-like tribes, proposed partly to preserve angelic impeccability.
- **Persian, Arabic, and Turkish literary tradition — [Medieval accretion]:** paradigmatic sorcerers, often suspended in the “well of Babylon.”
- **Modern occult commerce — [Modern invention or adaptation]:** spirits or “demonic angels” whose supposed grimoire can be used to summon entities. No such category is present in Q 2:102.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Qurʾān 2:102

The passage defends Solomon against an accusation of unbelief:

1. People followed what the devils recited or promulgated concerning Solomon’s kingdom.
2. Solomon did not disbelieve; the devils did, by teaching people magic.
3. The syntax then associates this material with “what was sent down to the two angels at Babylon, Hārūt and Mārūt.”
4. The pair taught no one before warning that they were a **fitna**, a trial or temptation, and that the learner must not disbelieve.
5. People learned from them what could separate a man from his wife.
6. Such knowledge could harm no one except by God’s permission.
7. Its purchasers knew that it harmed rather than benefited them and forfeited their share in the hereafter.

The verse is syntactically compressed. The particle **mā** in “what was sent down” was read affirmatively by most exegetes but as a negation by some: “and [magic] was not sent down to the two angels.” The negative reading exonerates the angels entirely and makes Hārūt and Mārūt either a parenthetical identification or human teachers. Other interpreters distinguish knowledge of magic, taught for recognition and defense, from its illicit practice. These are exegetical solutions, not explicit narrative details in the verse.

No surviving manuscript yet permits the names to be pushed securely before the Qurʾanic proclamation. Early Qurʾan manuscripts attest the textual tradition of Sūrat al-Baqara, but a dossier-specific claim that a particular seventh-century folio containing verse 2:102 is the oldest physical witness requires manuscript-by-manuscript verification. The conservative statement is therefore that the Qurʾanic text is the earliest secure literary attestation.

### Early tafsīr and transmitted reports

**Mujāhid b. Jabr (d. 722), preserved by al-Ṭabarī — [Early exegesis].** Angels are astonished at human wickedness. God asks them to choose two representatives, and they choose Hārūt and Mārūt. The pair descend as judges, rule justly by day, and return to heaven at night. Al-Zuhara appears as a beautiful female litigant. They desire her and corrupt judgment in her favor. They subsequently lose the ability to ascend, obtain intercession from a righteous man, and choose temporary earthly punishment over eternal punishment. They are suspended upside down in Babylon. John C. Reeves prints and analyzes this recension from al-Ṭabarī.[Reeves, “Parascriptural Dimensions”](https://almuslih.org/wp-content/uploads/Library/Reeves,%20C%20-%20Harut%20and%20Marut.pdf)

**Kaʿb al-Aḥbār, Ibn ʿAbbās, Ibn ʿUmar, ʿAlī, al-Rabīʿ, and al-Suddī traditions — [Early exegesis of disputed transmission].** These differ over:

- whether all the angels objected to humanity or only Hārūt and Mārūt;
- whether two or three angels descended;
- whether they remained righteous for one day or a long period;
- whether the woman was human, Venus embodied, a Persian queen, or a litigant;
- whether the angels merely desired her or committed fornication;
- whether she demanded idolatry, murder, wine-drinking, disclosure of the divine name, or several of these;
- whether she was punished as Venus or rewarded for chastity;
- whether Idrīs or another righteous man interceded;
- whether punishment occurred at historical Babylon, “Babylon of Damāvand,” Mount Damāvand, “the West,” or another locality.

Reports placed in the mouth of Muḥammad occur in *Musnad Aḥmad* and related collections, especially through ʿAbd Allāh b. ʿUmar. Hadith critics noted weakness or displacement in their chains. Ibn Kathīr judged the fuller story more plausibly derived from Kaʿb al-Aḥbār than from Muḥammad; Ibn al-Jawzī treated prophetic and ʿAlī-attributed versions as fabricated. The strong conclusion is not that the story was absent from early Islam, but that **no sound Prophetic hadith establishes its details**.[TDV analysis](https://islamansiklopedisi.org.tr/harut-ve-marut)

### Al-Ṭabarī and later exegetes

Al-Ṭabarī (d. 923), *Jāmiʿ al-bayān*, preserves a large dossier under Q 2:102 rather than one harmonized account. He accepts the reading “two angels,” records rival syntactic interpretations, and transmits multiple narrative chains. His compilation is the principal archive for earlier Muslim variants.[Arabic text of al-Ṭabarī on 2:102](https://quran.ksu.edu.sa/tafseer/tabary/sura2-aya102.html)

Al-Thaʿlabī (d. 1035) and al-Kisāʾī’s *Qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ* tradition package the scattered reports into a more continuous “Tale of Hārūt and Mārūt,” commonly placed near Idrīs. Al-Baghawī (d. 1122) relates the choice of exemplary angels, names an optional third figure, ʿAzāʾīl, and describes the prohibitions against idolatry, killing, adultery, and wine.[Al-Baghawī on 2:102](https://quran.com/2%3A102/tafsirs/ar-tafsir-al-baghawi)

Al-Qurṭubī, Fakhr al-Dīn al-Rāzī, Qāḍī ʿIyāḍ, Ibn Ḥazm, Abū Bakr Ibn al-ʿArabī, Ibn Kathīr, and Shiʿi exegetes including al-Ṭūsī and al-Ṭabrisī scrutinized or rejected important parts of the fall narrative. Objections included weak transmission and the doctrine that angels do not deliberately disobey God.

### Persian tafsīr

The Persian *Tarjoma-ye Tafsīr-e Ṭabarī*, compiled for the Samanid Manṣūr b. Nūḥ in 362/963, provides the first full surviving Persian rendering. It transfers the punishment to a well in Mount Damāvand: the angels hang by their feet, tongues extended with thirst, water only a sword-blade away. Sorcerers visit them for instruction. Later Persian commentaries—Esfarāyenī’s *Tāj al-tarājem*, Surābādī’s tafsīr, Maybudī’s *Kashf al-asrār* (begun 1126), and Abū al-Futūḥ Rāzī’s *Rawḍ al-jinān*—supply further variants.[Iranica](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/harut-and-marut/)

### Jewish and Christian comparanda

These sources do not name the Qurʾanic pair and must not be treated as direct attestations.

- **Genesis 6:1–4 — [Early comparative substrate]:** “sons of God” take human women; it contains neither magic lessons nor Hārūt and Mārūt.
- **1 Enoch 6–16, third–first centuries BCE:** the Watchers descend, marry women, and teach forbidden arts; Asael/Azazel teaches weapon-making and adornment. Their imprisonment until judgment supplies a close narrative complex, not the names.
- **Jubilees 4:15; 5:1–11, second century BCE:** Watchers are initially sent to instruct humanity but transgress with women and are bound in the earth.
- **Dead Sea Scrolls:** Aramaic manuscripts from Qumran confirm circulation of Watcher traditions before Islam, but no known fragment names Hārūt and Mārūt.
- **2 Peter 2:4 and Jude 6, first century CE:** sinful angels are confined awaiting judgment.
- **Tobit:** Asmodeus kills Sarah’s husbands; Raphael overcomes him. This supplies marriage-destroying demonology but no Hārūt-Mārūt pair.
- **Talmud and early midrash:** contain debates over angels objecting to humanity and traditions about fallen angels, but no securely pre-Islamic Hārūt-Mārūt story.
- **Midrash of Shemḥazai and ʿAzael:** two angels challenge the creation of humanity, descend, fall through lust, and encounter a woman who ascends by the divine name. Its earliest extant written registration is in the eleventh-century *Bereshit Rabbati*, after the Qurʾān. It may preserve older material, but it cannot simply be declared the written source of Q 2:102. Reeves argues for a shared, fluid parascriptural environment rather than simple copying.[Corpus Coranicum](https://corpuscoranicum.de/de/verse-navigator/sura/2/verse/102/intertexts/769)
- **Hekhalot literature, Sefer Raziel, and Zohar:** related motifs include Enoch/Metatron, divine names, Aza/Azael, Naamah, and magical angelology. The *Zohar* presents Aza, Azael, and Naamah, not Hārūt and Mārūt.
- **Nag Hammadi texts and heresiologists:** no securely identified occurrence of the names Hārūt or Mārūt appears in the Nag Hammadi codices, Irenaeus, Hippolytus, or Epiphanius. “Gnostic origin” is consequently unestablished. Broader Gnostic and rabbinic debates about angels, Adam, and hostile powers are contextual parallels only.
- **Pseudo-Dionysius and Aquinas:** neither makes Hārūt and Mārūt part of Christian angelic hierarchy. Their angelologies matter to later comparison, not to the pair’s documentary corpus.
- **New Testament apocrypha:** preserve fallen-angel themes but no secure namesake pair.

### Grimoires

The *Lemegeton* or *Lesser Key of Solomon* and the *Book of Abramelin* do not securely list Hārūt or Mārūt among their spirits. Assertions that the pair belong to Goetic catalogues conflate Islamic legend with Western ceremonial magic. Islamic occult works such as texts attributed to al-Būnī provide the relevant environment for letter magic, divine names, talismans, and planetary operations, but a medieval work demonstrably titled *The Grimoire of Harut and Marut* has not been established from a dated manuscript.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and powers

**[Early attestation]** Their sole certain domain is knowledge designated **siḥr**, especially a means of causing discord between spouses. Their limitation is equally explicit: no harm occurs independently of God’s permission.

**[Exegetical elaboration]** The pair know the divine name or formula by which they ascend to heaven; teach magic and sometimes its countermeasures; judge human disputes; recognize licit from illicit operations; and warn every pupil before teaching.

**[Medieval folklore]** They become permanent custodians or involuntary teachers of the world’s magical arts. Persian dictionaries and poets use “Hārūt” metonymically for an unsurpassed magician or enchanting speech, music, eyes, or hair.

They have no securely attested sacred animal, plant, color, number, weekday, metal, weapon, or independent celestial body. Venus belongs to the woman al-Zuhara, not to the pair themselves. Attempts to assign them occult correspondences from modern tables are modern systematization.

### Form and iconography

The Qurʾān gives no physical description. Tafsīr assumes angelic bodies or temporary human appearance. A story attributed to al-Aʿmash describes them as enormous as mountains, chained from ankles to knees, hanging head downward with their heads close to the earth.

Their standard manuscript iconography is consequently narrative rather than cultic:

- two winged or gigantic male figures;
- suspended upside down in a narrow well, pit, or cavern;
- shackles or chains on the legs;
- sometimes a visitor or would-be magician above them;
- occasionally flames, water, or the Babylonian landscape.

A major surviving example is **“The Fallen Angels Harut and Marut in the Well at Babylon,”** fol. 66a of a manuscript of al-Qazwīnī’s *ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt*, probably Mosul, about 1300, British Library Or. 14140.[Art and Architecture ePortal object record](https://aaeportal.com/?id=68682) A later example cited in scholarship is a Persian *ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt* illustration dated approximately 1050 AH/1640 CE.

These are book illustrations, not icons installed for worship. No ancient Babylonian relief, statue, seal, or temple image can securely be identified as Hārūt and Mārūt.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other beings

No source gives Hārūt or Mārūt parents, children, consorts, siblings, mounts, or an independent rank in a named angelic choir. They function as a pair. The claim that they are “brothers” is a natural modern inference, not a stated Qurʾanic genealogy.

### Every documented named interaction

- **God/Allah — divine sender, tester, and limiter.** God sends or causes knowledge to be sent to the pair and limits its harmful effect. In tafsīr he tests them and sentences them after their failure. **Source:** Qurʾān 2:102, seventh century; al-Ṭabarī and later tafsīr, ninth–tenth centuries onward. **Tier:** early attestation / canonical elaboration.

- **Solomon (Sulaymān) — shared polemical context, but no direct meeting.** Q 2:102 acquits Solomon of unbelief while distinguishing the devils’ magic from what was sent to the pair. It never says Solomon met, commanded, imprisoned, or learned from them. **Source:** Qurʾān 2:102. **Tier:** early attestation; absence of interaction is explicit.

- **The devils (al-shayāṭīn) — parallel or syntactically connected teachers of magic.** They teach magic and falsely implicate Solomon. Whether their teaching is identical with, additional to, or contrasted with what was sent to the pair is disputed by translators. No individual devil is named. **Source:** Qurʾān 2:102. **Tier:** early attestation.

- **Al-Zuhara/Venus — object of desire and catalyst of their fall.** In Mujāhid’s recension she appears already as Venus in female form; elsewhere she is a mortal woman later transformed into the planet. She solicits or receives the ascent formula, induces one or more sins, escapes, or is punished/rewarded in heaven. **Source:** early reports preserved by al-Ṭabarī; al-Thaʿlabī and later qiṣaṣ. **Tier:** early exegesis / medieval elaboration.

- **Anāhīd/Bīdūkht — Iranian identification of the woman.** Some Persian or Iranizing recensions name the temptress Anāhīd; modern scholars compare her with the Iranian goddess Anāhitā and the planet Venus. She is not named in the Qurʾān. **Source:** Persian tafsīr and comparative scholarship. **Tier:** regional syncretism / modern reconstruction.

- **Idrīs — intercessor or chronological contemporary.** Some versions place the episode in Idrīs’s time and have him petition God on behalf of the fallen angels. Others use an anonymous righteous man. **Source:** al-Maqdisī, al-Thaʿlabī, and qiṣaṣ traditions. **Tier:** medieval elaboration.

- **ʿAzāʾīl — optional third angel.** In al-Kalbī/al-Baghawī-type versions, ʿAzāʾīl descends with Hārūt and Mārūt but repents or asks to be recalled before committing their sins. This figure is not necessarily the later Islamic Azrael, although names are often conflated. **Source:** al-Baghawī on Q 2:102. **Tier:** medieval variant.

- **Gabriel and Michael — objects of an exegetical denial, not companions.** One interpretation reads the passage against Jewish magicians who alleged that Gabriel and Michael brought magic to Solomon; the Qurʾanic statement instead exonerates Solomon and identifies Hārūt and Mārūt. No direct encounter between the four angels is recorded. **Source:** reports in al-Ṭabarī’s tafsīr. **Tier:** early exegetical polemic.

- **Shemḥazai, Aza/ʿUzza, and Azael — comparative counterparts, never narratively encountered.** Jewish fallen-angel stories resemble the later Muslim tale. No primary text stages a meeting between these figures and Hārūt or Mārūt. **Tier:** medieval Jewish parallel / modern comparative reconstruction.

- **Haurvatāt and Ameretat — proposed name ancestors, not interacting beings.** No text presents four separate figures meeting. **Tier:** modern linguistic reconstruction.

- **Iblīs — analogical comparator only.** Poets and theologians compare angelic pride toward humans with Iblīs’s refusal to honor Adam; no source records a direct Hārūt-Iblīs or Mārūt-Iblīs encounter. **Tier:** learned theological comparison.

- **Adam — catalyst through angelic debate, not direct interlocutor.** In some reports the angels criticize Adam or his descendants before God. The pair do not meet Adam in the preserved narrative. **Tier:** early exegetical elaboration.

No securely attested interaction exists with Raphael, Asmodeus, Metatron, Dante’s demons, the spirits of the *Lemegeton*, or any Gnostic archon.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The Qurʾanic teaching at Babylon

**[Early attestation].** Hārūt and Mārūt are at Babylon and possess transmitted knowledge of magic. They warn each pupil that they are a trial and forbid unbelief. Some pupils nevertheless learn how to divide spouses. Their power is subordinate to God.

The verse makes the human learner’s culpability central. It does not narrate why the angels are in Babylon or whether teaching is punishment, duty, or both. “Sent down” may modify the knowledge rather than the pair. Any full story of their descent is therefore interpretation.

### The angels’ challenge and fall

**[Early exegesis / canonical elaboration].** The angels observe humanity’s violence and disobedience and claim that angelic governors would behave better. God invites them to nominate representatives. Hārūt and Mārūt—sometimes with a third angel—receive bodily appetites and descend to judge humans.

A beautiful woman appears. Their desire compromises their judgment. In the fullest versions she offers sexual access on conditions that progressively violate divine commands: worship an idol, kill a witness or child, drink wine, or disclose the name that allows ascent. They initially choose wine as the seemingly least offense, but intoxication leads to idolatry, murder, and intercourse. Other recensions restrict their guilt to intention or attempted seduction.

The woman’s fate divides sharply:

- she learns the ascent formula and is rewarded for chastity by becoming Venus;
- she steals the formula and is punished by becoming Venus;
- she already is Venus in human guise and simply returns to the sky;
- she is a Persian queen named Bīdūkht or Anāhīd;
- she remains an unnamed litigant.

This instability shows that there was no single canonical fall narrative.

### Choice of punishment and the Babylonian well

God or an intercessor obtains for them a choice between punishment in this world and punishment in the hereafter. Knowing the hereafter to be everlasting, they choose earthly punishment. They are sent to Babylon, hung upside down by their feet, hair, or wings, and kept there until the Last Judgment. Their mouths may remain just beyond reach of water, a feature resembling the punishment of Tantalus and perhaps incorporated through late-antique or Iranian narrative transmission.

Magicians visit the well. The pair continue to warn visitors yet must teach those who persist. Al-Qazwīnī preserves a story in which al-Ḥajjāj orders the Jewish exilarch to guide a pious Muslim to the well; the visitor sees two mountain-sized beings in chains and faints when their bodies convulse at the divine name. This is medieval sacred geography, not archaeological testimony.

### Damāvand and “Babylon of Damāvand”

**[Persian regional variant].** The 963 Persian *Tafsīr-e Ṭabarī* relocates the prison to Mount Damāvand, already a site of demonic confinement in Iranian lore, most famously that of Żaḥḥāk. A locality was called **Bābel-e Donbāvand**, “Babylon of Damāvand,” permitting interpreters to retain the Qurʾanic place-name while Iranizing it. Ḥamd Allāh Mustawfī preserves both Babylonian and Damāvand associations.

### Isfahan legend

**[Late folklore; poorly documented].** Modern devotional and oral accounts sometimes state that the pair are hung in a well in **Isfahan**, particularly between sunset and evening prayer. A web fatwa preserves this formulation, but it supplies no early manuscript provenance.[Modern record of the Isfahan claim](https://eshaykh.com/quran-tafsir/haroot-and-maroot/)

The critical result is negative: the securely traceable classical localizations are historical Babylon, “Babylon of Damāvand,” Mount Damāvand, and an unspecified western place. Isfahan is not named in Q 2:102 and did not emerge in the consulted early tafsīr evidence. It should be labeled regional folklore unless an identifiable premodern Isfahani source is produced. It may reflect migration of the Damāvand legend or confusion with other Iranian sacred wells; this is an inference, not an established transmission history.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

There is no documented cult of Hārūt and Mārūt in the institutional sense.

- No temple, priesthood, festival, sacrifice, pilgrimage administration, or dedicatory inscription is securely associated with them.
- No Babylonian cuneiform text names them.
- No excavated Babylonian structure is securely their prison or sanctuary.
- A well at Babylon is today pointed out in local legend as their prison; this is post-Qurʾanic sacred geography, not proof that the legend existed in ancient Babylon. Modern conservation reporting records the continuing local identification.[Modern Babylon report](https://www.theartnewspaper.com/2022/03/24/babylon-is-coming-back-to-life-with-its-famed-ishtar-gate-to-be-restored-by-this-summer)
- Reports that sorcerers traveled to their well to obtain instruction belong to medieval narrative. They demonstrate belief in such consultation, not an archaeologically documented school or pilgrimage economy.
- “Hārūt” and “Mārūt” are not established as common theophoric personal names.

The pair did acquire a quasi-ritual position in magical imagination. Invoking their story could authorize a text as primordial Babylonian wisdom. Yet no verified ritual lineage from the Qurʾanic pair to a modern magical group is documented.

Claims involving human sacrifice, temple prostitution, a historical sex cult, or ritual killing are absent. Murder appears only inside the fall narrative, usually as the killing of a child or witness under intoxication.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before Islam

Watcher traditions involving angels, women, forbidden arts, and imprisonment circulated in Jewish apocalyptic literature from the Hellenistic period. Iranian Haurvatāt and Ameretat formed an older rhyming divine pair. These are separate streams; no pre-Islamic document yet combines their Iranian-looking names with the Watcher story and Babylonian magic.

### Seventh century

Qurʾān 2:102 provides the first secure combination: Hārūt and Mārūt, angels or possibly kings under a minority vocalization, Babylon, magic, moral warning, marital separation, and divine limitation.

### Eighth–tenth centuries

Exegetical reports construct narrative explanations. The pair become angelic critics of humanity, earthly judges, failed sexual beings, and punished prisoners. Al-Ṭabarī preserves competing reports rather than eliminating their contradictions. This period also produces theological resistance: if angels are impeccable, either the fall story is false, their sin was only inward, the reading is “two kings,” or the teaching itself was divinely authorized and therefore not rebellion.

### Tenth–twelfth centuries

Persian tafsīr relocates the story to Damāvand and integrates Iranian demon geography. Arabic and Persian qiṣaṣ anthologies turn fragments into a continuous legend. The woman becomes increasingly important as Zuhara/Venus, Anāhīd, a Persian princess, or a litigant. The well and inverted suspension become defining imagery.

### Thirteenth–fifteenth centuries

Al-Qazwīnī’s cosmography gives the story visual and geographical form. Rūmī repeatedly invokes the pair. In *Masnavī* V.620–623, intellect and spirit are imprisoned in clay like Hārūt and Mārūt in the dreadful pit; good and bad learn magic and its antidote from them, after being warned that the teaching is a test.[Rūmī text and translation](https://masnavi.net/3/50/eng/5/620/) Turkish, Persian, and Arabic literature makes the well a metaphor for the beloved’s chin and the angels’ suspension a metaphor for hair descending into it.

The Byzantine emperor John VI Kantakouzenos’s fourteenth-century anti-Islamic writing renders them **Arōt and Marōt**, showing transmission into Christian polemic.

### Early modern period

Illustrated cosmographies continue to depict the pair in the well. Ottoman divan poetry treats Hārūt as the archetypal magician, often surpassed by the beloved’s eyes or glance. This is literary reception, not worship.

### Eighteenth–twentieth centuries

European Romanticism absorbs them into the broader fallen-angel fashion. George Croly and Thomas Moore use the tradition; Moore’s *The Loves of the Angels* appeared in 1823. H. Rider Haggard’s *The Ivory Child* (1916) gives the names to African magicians, “Mr. Hare-root and Mr. Mare-root,” a colonial literary reinvention rather than preservation of Islamic tradition.

Modern Qurʾan commentators divide between accepting a restrained version, rejecting the fall as Isrāʾīliyyāt, or rationalizing the pair as human Babylonian sages. Muḥammad ʿAbduh and Rashīd Riḍā’s *Tafsīr al-Manār* minimizes the Venus legend and seeks an interpretation compatible with angelic impeccability and a demystified understanding of magic.[Study of *Tafsīr al-Manār*](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/12/9/734)

### Present

Hārūt and Mārūt remain living Qurʾanic figures in Muslim teaching about magic, culpable knowledge, and divine testing. The expansive fall narrative survives in sermons, children’s stories, Persianate poetry, television and online videos, while many Muslim scholars explicitly warn that its sexual and murderous details rest on weak or Isrāʾīliyyāt reports.

There is no demonstrable continuous worship or organized reconstructionist religion centered on them.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Modern occultism

A commercially circulating English work titled *The Forbidden Grimoire of Harut and Marut*, associated with the modern Egyptian occult writer **A. al-Toukhi**, presents spells, talismans, fumigations, and spirit operations as the pair’s magical inheritance. Its publication and translation belong to the modern occult market. No publicly documented manuscript history establishes it as the translation of an ancient “lost grimoire.” Claims that Hārūt and Mārūt personally authored it are **modern pseudepigraphy**.

Online occult communities recast the pair as summonable demons, djinn, Enochian angels, or Goetic spirits. These classifications conflict with Q 2:102 and cannot be traced to the *Lemegeton* or *Abramelin*. They are modern syncretism.

### Literature and art

- Persian literature: Ferdowsī likens extraordinary musical skill to Hārūt’s magic; Nāṣer-e Khosrow, Neẓāmī, and Rūmī employ the pair as images of enchantment, pride, embodiment, and punishment.
- Ottoman poetry: the “well of Babylon,” the beloved’s chin, hair, glance, and sorcery form a conventional image cluster.
- Thomas Moore, *The Loves of the Angels* (1823): Romantic fallen-angel adaptation.
- H. Rider Haggard, *The Ivory Child* (1916): comic-exotic appropriation of the names.
- Erik Madigan Heck, *Harut and Marut* (2016): contemporary fine-art title; an evocative modern reception rather than a traditional icon.
- Modern fantasy databases, games, and demonological websites increasingly present them as discrete combat entities or demons. Where no primary Islamic source is cited, such portrayals are creative adaptation.

Dante and Milton profoundly shaped the Western image of fallen angels, but neither makes Hārūt and Mārūt characters. Claims that they appear in *Inferno* or *Paradise Lost* are erroneous conflations.

### Politics and extremism

No major documented nationalist, extremist, or state cult centered on the pair emerged from the consulted record. Polemical uses do occur: critics use their story to attack Islamic angelology, while Muslim apologetic literature rejects the Iranian or Jewish comparisons as accusations of borrowing. These are modern confessional arguments, not additional primary evidence.

## 9. Historiography and disputed questions

### Did the story come from the Watchers?

Genesis, 1 Enoch, Jubilees, and later Shemḥazai-Azael traditions provide strong motif parallels: angels judge humanity, descend, desire women, reveal forbidden knowledge, and are bound until judgment. Yet no surviving pre-Islamic source presents the full Qurʾanic complex, and the closest Hebrew prose recension is preserved only in an eleventh-century compilation. “Derived directly from Midrash Abkir” is therefore too confident. A shared late-antique reservoir of Jewish, Christian, Iranian, and oral traditions is the more cautious reconstruction.

Reeves argues that the mature Muslim tale has an authentically Muslim literary identity while drawing on older scriptural and parascriptural building blocks. Patricia Crone similarly drew attention to the Book of Watchers but emphasized that the exegetes recognized the Watcher framework despite the Qurʾān’s brevity and Iranian-looking names.

### Are the names Zoroastrian?

The phonological chain Avestan **Haurvatāt–Ameretat**, Middle Iranian/Sogdian **Harwōt–Marwōt**, and Arabic **Hārūt–Mārūt** is the dominant scholarly explanation. Its weakness is narrative: beneficent guardians of water and plants do not resemble fallen Babylonian instructors in magic. The most economical theory is transfer of a paired name onto a different narrative complex, not survival of an intact Zoroastrian myth.

A related comparison identifies al-Zuhara/Anāhīd with the Iranian Venus deity Anāhitā. This may explain Persian names and planetary associations but is not proven for the Qurʾanic stage.

### Are they angels or kings?

“Two angels” is the received and contextually dominant reading. “Two kings” is genuinely old in exegesis but noncanonical as Qurʾanic recitation. It should not be dismissed as a modern rationalization, though modern commentators revived it because it avoids fallible angels.

### Did they fall?

The Qurʾān never calls them fallen. Their warning can be read as obedience. The “fallen angels” classification belongs to tafsīr, qiṣaṣ, comparative religion, and modern summaries. Calling them fallen without qualification silently converts one exegetical history into the Qurʾanic text.

### Is the complete fall story an authentic hadith?

No consensus-grade ṣaḥīḥ report establishes it. Versions transmitted as Prophetic reports have defective or disputed chains; significant critics traced them to Kaʿb al-Aḥbār or other non-Prophetic transmitters. Nevertheless, the narrative is historically important because it circulated widely and shaped literature, art, and belief.

### Is the Iranian punishment derived from Tantalus?

The unreachable water, perpetual thirst, and punishment for disclosing divine secrets resemble Tantalus closely. Iranica proposes “tantalization” as a classical accretion. Direct transmission has not been documented, so this remains comparative reconstruction, not a proven borrowing.

### Did they belong to Gnosticism?

No Nag Hammadi text or named heresiological catalogue securely attests them. Gnostic discussions of archons, Adam, revelation, and hostile angels form part of the late-antique environment, but assigning Hārūt and Mārūt to a “Gnostic pantheon” is modern taxonomic placement. It is not an ancient classification.

### Discredited or unsupported popular claims

- that Hārūt and Mārūt were ancient Babylonian gods: unsupported;
- that a cuneiform tablet records their magical school: unsupported;
- that they authored a surviving ancient grimoire: unsupported;
- that they appear in the *Lemegeton*, *Abramelin*, Dante, or Milton: unsupported;
- that Hārūt is the Amorite Ḥauron and Mārūt the god Martu: speculative fringe etymology;
- that the Sanskrit Maruts are the same beings: superficial comparison;
- that a modern Babylonian or Isfahani well is archaeologically verified as their prison: folklore;
- that the Qurʾān narrates their lust, fornication, murder, intoxication, or inverted suspension: false; those details are extra-Qurʾanic.

### Major modern studies

Gustav Davidson’s *Dictionary of Angels* is useful as a modern index but frequently compresses traditions and should not replace primary citations. The *Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible*, Jeffrey Burton Russell’s histories of the Devil, Henry Ansgar Kelly’s *Satan: A Biography*, and studies by Birger Pearson and Kurt Rudolph illuminate comparative angelology, demonology, and Gnosticism but contain no independent primary attestation of this pair.

The central documentary conclusion is firm: **the Qurʾān is the first secure witness to Hārūt and Mārūt by these names. Everything about their challenge to humanity, lust for Venus, intoxication, murder, hanging by the feet, imprisonment in a well, and localization in Damāvand or Isfahan belongs to the subsequent history of interpretation, literature, image, and folklore.**
