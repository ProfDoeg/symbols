# Metatron: Research Dossier

**Class:** Angelic being—variously an angel, archangel, “Prince of the Presence,” celestial scribe, heavenly priest, vice-regent, and transformed patriarch. Some mystical texts give him quasi-divine titles, but the sources consistently preserve his subordination to God, sometimes violently.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The name **Metatron**—Hebrew/Aramaic מטטרון or מיטטרון—is securely attested in late-antique Jewish Babylonian materials: the Babylonian Talmud and Jewish Aramaic incantation bowls, broadly fifth–seventh centuries CE. The precise priority of individual texts is disputed because the Talmud was compiled over time and bowls are often only archaeologically dated. Metatron is not named in the Hebrew Bible, Septuagint, Dead Sea Scrolls, 1 Enoch, Jubilees, Tobit, New Testament, Nag Hammadi codices, Qurʾan, or canonical hadith.

**Central evidentiary distinction:** Enoch’s translation to heaven is ancient; the named angel Metatron is late antique; their explicit identification is later still. The influential thesis that Enoch had always secretly been Metatron is a retrospective harmonization made possible above all by *3 Enoch*. Yakir Paz’s revisionist reconstruction places the emergence of Metatron as a proper angelic name in Babylonia and the first securely identifiable Enoch–Metatron synthesis in Palestinian sources of approximately the eighth century. That chronology remains debated, particularly by scholars who detect earlier Enochic traditions behind the surviving texts. [Yakir Paz, “Metatron Is Not Enoch”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/330961109_Metatron_is_Not_Enoch); [Philip Alexander on *3 Enoch*](https://www.earlyjewishwritings.com/3enoch.html).

## Evidentiary labels used

- **[Early antecedent]** Material predating the name Metatron that later traditions applied to him.
- **[Secure late-antique attestation]** A named occurrence in the Talmud, bowls, or comparably datable source.
- **[Hekhalot elaboration]** Material in the late-antique/early-medieval mystical corpus.
- **[Medieval Kabbalah]** Zoharic or other medieval Jewish mystical systematization.
- **[Early-modern occult reception]** Christian Cabala, ceremonial magic, or grimoire use.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A historical hypothesis, not an ancient identification.
- **[Modern invention or retelling]** A recent symbol, doctrine, artwork, or fictional character.
- **[Absence of evidence]** A searched corpus in which Metatron is not actually named.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orientation

Metatron is the pre-eminent angel of several—not all—streams of rabbinic, Hekhalot, and Kabbalistic literature. His recurrent offices are mediation of the divine name and presence, record-keeping, instruction, heavenly liturgy, priesthood, governance of angels or nations, and guidance of an earthly visionary. In *3 Enoch*, Rabbi Ishmael meets him at the entrance to the seventh palace; Metatron declares that he was once Enoch son of Jared. God transformed Enoch’s flesh into flame, enlarged him, clothed and crowned him, placed him on a throne, gave him celestial knowledge, and named him “the lesser YHWH.” Yet the text also recounts Metatron’s public punishment after Elisha ben Abuyah mistakes his seated posture for evidence of “two powers.” The paired narratives exalt him and police the boundary between him and God.

There is no single continuous “Metatron doctrine.” At least three complexes were combined unevenly:

1. a principal angel associated with Exodus 23:20–21 and the divine name;
2. the translated and increasingly exalted Enoch of apocalyptic literature;
3. functions elsewhere belonging to Michael, Yahoel, the Prince of the World, the heavenly high priest, or the celestial scribe.

Philip Alexander consequently described *3 Enoch*’s Metatron as a fusion of originally distinguishable traditions, especially Enoch, Yahoel/Lesser YHWH, and Michael-like material. [Alexander study](https://www.marquette.edu/maqom/Alexander.pdf).

### Names and spellings

- **מטטרון — Mṭṭrwn**, conventionally *Metatron* or *Meṭaṭron*: six-letter spelling.
- **מיטטרון — Myṭṭrwn**, conventionally *Mitatron* or *Meṭaṭron*: seven-letter spelling.
- **מיטטרון / מיטטרון** in Babylonian Aramaic contexts: orthographic variation is normal, and English forms such as *Mittron*, *Mitatron*, and *Metatron* should not automatically be treated as different beings.
- **חנוך — Ḥanokh/Enoch:** explicitly equated with Metatron in *3 Enoch* and related medieval traditions, but not in the Babylonian Talmud’s named Metatron passages.
- **נער — naʿar, “Youth”:** Metatron says that although he has seventy names, the King calls him “Youth” (*3 Enoch* 3). This can signal his relatively recent elevation, perpetual youth, or subordinate servant-status.
- **שר הפנים — sar ha-panim, “Prince of the Presence/Countenance”:** a major Hekhalot title. It denotes privileged access to the divine presence, not identity with God.
- **שר העולם — sar ha-ʿolam, “Prince of the World”:** applied to Metatron in some later materials, but the title has an independent history and is not everywhere his.
- **ספרא רבא — “great scribe”:** reflects the record-keeping function seen in *b. Ḥagigah* 15a and amplified in Hekhalot and Kabbalistic sources.
- **כהן גדול — “high priest”:** a heavenly priestly office in portions of the Hekhalot–*Shiʿur Qomah* complex.
- **שר התורה — “Prince of Torah”:** teacher, revealer, or guardian of heavenly Torah in later sections of *3 Enoch* and associated texts.
- **יהוה הקטן — YHWH ha-qaṭan, “the lesser YHWH”:** the most controversial epithet. In *3 Enoch* 12 and 48C, it is grounded in Exodus 23:21, “my name is in him.” It delegates divine authority without straightforwardly making Metatron an independent god.

The “seventy names” are not stable across manuscripts. They signify comprehensive authority corresponding to the traditional seventy nations and reflect the broader magical-mystical practice of accumulating powerful divine and angelic names. [*3 Enoch* text](https://scrollmapper.github.io/scrolls/extrabiblical/3-enoch/); [“Seventy Names of Metatron”](https://www.sefaria.org/Otzar_Midrashim%2C_Seventy_Names_of_Metatron).

### Etymology: unresolved

No etymology commands consensus. Andrei Orlov surveys at least nine proposals and correctly treats the name as an unresolved philological problem. [Orlov, “The Etymology of the Name Metatron”](https://www.marquette.edu/maqom/metatronname.html).

Principal proposals include:

- **Latin *metator***, “guide,” “forerunner,” “one who marks out a camp.”  
  **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** This was long attractive because Metatron is associated with the angel sent before Israel in Exodus 23:20 and because *metator* could describe a guide. Gershom Scholem objected that Metatron’s securely early functions do not make the derivation inevitable. Paz has revived a historically specific version: Palestinian Jews used *metator* as a functional designation for the Exodus angel; in Babylonia it became a proper name.

- **Greek μετὰ θρόνον, “beside/after the throne,” or unattested *metathronios*.**  
  **[Rejected or weak reconstruction]** It fits Metatron’s proximity to the throne but is linguistically defective: the supposed compound is not attested in the required form, and the throne association may be later than the name.

- **Greek μέτρον, “measure.”**  
  **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Jellinek and later Gedaliahu Stroumsa connected the name with measurement, especially Metatron’s association with *Shiʿur Qomah*, the “measurement of the [divine] body.” Semantically suggestive, but unproved.

- **Hebrew/Aramaic root מטר, “guard/watch.”**  
  **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** This yields something like “guardian” or “keeper of the watch.” The morphology is disputed.

- **Greek μετετέθη / μετέθηκεν, “was translated/transferred.”**  
  **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Charles Mopsik related the name to the Septuagint’s language for God’s “taking” or translating Enoch in Genesis 5:24 and Sirach 44:16. This elegantly fits Enoch but may explain a secondary Enochic interpretation rather than the name’s actual origin.

- **A deliberately opaque magical name.**  
  Philip Alexander and Scholem regarded this as plausible, noting similar formations in Hekhalot angelology and the suffix *-on* in names such as Sandalfon. This is an admission of uncertainty, not a positive derivation.

The six- and seven-letter spellings later generated mystical distinctions between two Metatrons, sometimes one primordial and one the transformed Enoch. **[Medieval learned systematization]** The orthography alone does not prove that two beings were originally intended.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Biblical and Second Temple antecedents

**Hebrew Bible, Genesis 5:21–24 — [Early antecedent].** Enoch “walked with God; then he was not, for God took him.” Nothing here says that he became an angel, received a new name, or was Metatron.

**Exodus 23:20–23 — [Early antecedent].** God sends an unnamed angel before Israel and warns the people to obey him “for my name is in him.” Later rabbinic and Hekhalot texts use this as the principal scriptural warrant for Metatron’s authority and divine-name-bearing status. Exodus itself does not name Metatron.

**Isaiah 63:9 — [Early antecedent].** The “angel of his presence” supplied language for *sar ha-panim*. It does not name Metatron.

**Daniel 7 and 10–12 — [Early antecedent].** The enthroned Ancient of Days, “one like a son of man,” and named angelic princes helped create the conceptual environment of exalted heavenly mediators. Equations with Metatron are later exegesis.

**Septuagint Genesis 5:24 — [Early antecedent].** Greek μετέθηκεν, “transferred him,” became important in later arguments about Enoch’s translation and, much later, Mopsik’s etymology. It contains no Metatron.

**1 Enoch, c. third century BCE–first century CE — [Early antecedent].** The composite Ethiopic work makes Enoch a heavenly traveler, scribe, revealer, and eschatological witness. In the *Book of Parables*, the relationship between Enoch and the “Son of Man” is textually and interpretively difficult. Metatron is never named. Similar functions do not prove identity.

**Jubilees 4:17–25, second century BCE — [Early antecedent].** Enoch writes heavenly signs and human deeds and is taken to Eden. This is a strong antecedent for the celestial-scribe motif, but not an attestation of Metatron.

**Dead Sea Scrolls — [Absence of evidence].** Aramaic Enoch manuscripts and texts featuring Melchizedek, Michael, heavenly priests, or exalted human speakers provide comparative evidence for mediatorial angelology. No surviving scroll names Metatron.

**Tobit, roughly third–second century BCE — [Absence of evidence].** Raphael identifies himself as one of seven angels who enter before divine glory. This supports the history of named archangels but not Metatron.

### The Babylonian Talmud

The Bavli reached literary form over the fifth–seventh centuries CE, though individual traditions may be older or younger.

**b. Sanhedrin 38b — [Secure late-antique attestation].** A *min*, or sectarian, challenges Rav Idit over Exodus 24:1. Rav Idit replies that the speaker is Metatron, “whose name is like the name of his Master,” citing Exodus 23:21. The interlocutor then asks why Metatron should not be worshipped; Rav Idit answers that Israel would not accept him even as a guide. This is simultaneously an exaltation and an anti-binitarian boundary text. [*Sanhedrin* 38b](https://www.sefaria.org/Sanhedrin.38b).

**b. Ḥagigah 15a — [Secure late-antique attestation].** Elisha ben Abuyah, “Aḥer,” enters paradise and sees Metatron seated, writing Israel’s merits. Since heavenly beings were conventionally imagined as standing, Aḥer concludes, “Perhaps there are two powers.” Metatron is struck with sixty fiery lashes to demonstrate his created and punishable status; he is then permitted to erase Aḥer’s merits. The episode does not call Metatron Enoch. It is the primary source for “Aḥer’s heresy,” not evidence that normative rabbinic Judaism worshipped Metatron. [*Ḥagigah* 15a](https://www.sefaria.org/Chagigah.15a).

**b. Avodah Zarah 3b — [Secure late-antique attestation, textual variant].** In a discussion of God’s daily activities, some textual witnesses or interpretations assign Metatron the teaching of children who died young. Other versions leave the activity to God. This is a good example of unstable transmission rather than a universally fixed office.

### Incantation bowls

**Jewish Babylonian Aramaic bowls, approximately fifth–seventh centuries CE — [Secure material attestation].** Metatron occurs on protective bowls among invoked angelic powers and bears titles such as “Great Prince” and “Great Prince of the throne/world.” These are physical artifacts written in ink on ceramic bowls, commonly buried inverted beneath thresholds or floors to bind demons and protect named clients. Nippur is the best archaeologically documented findspot, although many bowls now in collections lack reliable provenance. The bowls demonstrate practical invocation outside purely literary speculation; they do not demonstrate temples, public worship, or sacrifice to Metatron. [Montgomery, *Aramaic Incantation Texts from Nippur*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv5139kt); [Oriental Institute survey](https://isac.uchicago.edu/sites/default/files/uploads/shared/docs/Publications/SAOC/saoc71.pdf).

### *Reʾuyot Yeḥezqel* and related Palestinian material

The *Visions of Ezekiel* is often assigned a late-antique date, but its precise dating and redaction are disputed. It gives Metatron a prominent cosmological role without identifying him with Enoch. Older scholarship sometimes dated it as early as the third or fourth century; Paz’s textual reconstruction places securely recoverable Palestinian Metatron material around the early seventh century. The disagreement directly affects claims about the “first Metatron.”

### Hekhalot literature

The Hekhalot corpus survives chiefly in medieval manuscripts but contains strata assembled from late antiquity through the early Middle Ages. Its fluid textual transmission makes a single date misleading. Peter Schäfer’s *Synopse zur Hekhalot-Literatur* prints manuscript witnesses in parallel rather than manufacturing a single original text. The principal compositions include *Hekhalot Rabbati*, *Hekhalot Zutarti*, *Maʿaseh Merkavah*, *Merkavah Rabbah*, *Shiʿur Qomah*, and *Sefer Hekhalot/3 Enoch*. [Hekhalot corpus overview](https://hekhal.org/corpus/heikhalot/).

Metatron is not equally central in all of them. Some passages naming him appear to be additions; other hierarchical texts omit him. The absence prevents speaking of one uniform “Merkabah theology.”

### *3 Enoch* / *Sefer Hekhalot*

**Language:** Hebrew with Aramaic elements.  
**Surviving manuscripts:** medieval and textually divergent.  
**Date:** disputed. Philip Alexander proposed a fifth–sixth-century final redaction while acknowledging older and later material; Paz argues that the Enoch–Metatron synthesis is not securely attested until about the eighth century. It is safest to describe the surviving work as a layered late-antique/early-medieval composition rather than a transcript from Rabbi Ishmael’s lifetime.

Principal Metatron sections:

- Chapters 1–3: Metatron receives Rabbi Ishmael in heaven.
- Chapters 3–16: Metatron reveals that he was Enoch; hostile angels protest his elevation; he is transformed, crowned, enthroned, and appointed.
- Chapter 12: God calls him the “lesser YHWH.”
- Chapter 15: his flesh becomes flame, sinews fire, bones glowing coals, eyes torches, hair flames, and body cosmic in extent.
- Chapter 16: Aḥer sees him seated; Metatron is punished with fiery lashes. This rewrites the Talmudic episode inside the Enoch narrative.
- Chapters 17–40: he guides Ishmael through celestial hierarchies and cosmology.
- Chapters 41–48: he reveals cosmic, eschatological, and soul-related mysteries.
- Chapter 48C and related appendices: Enoch–Metatron receives wisdom, angels, treasuries, authority over the heavenly household, and a throne corresponding to God’s throne.

The work sometimes presents tension between a primordial, already powerful Metatron and Enoch’s historical transformation. That tension helped generate “two Metatrons” theories. [Complete online *3 Enoch*](https://scrollmapper.github.io/scrolls/extrabiblical/3-enoch/); [Odeberg/Alexander bibliographical discussion](https://digital-brilliance.com/contributed/Karr/Biblios/mmhie.pdf).

### Midrash, Targum, and medieval collections

Palestinian Targumic and midrashic materials identify Metatron with the angel guiding Israel, the advocate of Israel, the heavenly youth, or the minister of the tabernacle. Dating individual passages is difficult because printed compilations preserve late recensions and variant readings. Medieval commentators occasionally read “Metatron” where an older text probably had the loanword *metator*, “guide.” Such passages cannot automatically be used as early attestations.

### Zohar and Kabbalah

The main body of the *Zohar* appeared in late-thirteenth-century Castile, traditionally attributed to the second-century Rabbi Simeon bar Yoḥai but historically associated above all with Moses de León and his circle.

Its Metatron is:

- the “Youth” serving the Shekhinah;
- ruler of the lower worlds or heavenly household;
- head of a celestial academy;
- guardian or conductor of souls;
- servant represented by Abraham’s “elder servant” in Genesis 24;
- at times the heavenly tabernacle, vehicle, garment, or lower manifestation through which sefirotic power acts.

The Zohar does not simply repeat *3 Enoch*. It subordinates Metatron to the Shekhinah in some passages and treats him as a structural mediator between sefirotic divinity and the created worlds. One passage identifies the “Youth” serving the Shekhinah in the heavenly Temple; another calls Metatron the “elder of his house” who governs the divine household. [Zohar, *Beshalach*](https://www.zohar.com/zohar/Beshalach/chapters/34); [Zoharic Metatron source sheet](https://voices.sefaria.org/sheets/555819).

*Tiqqunei ha-Zohar*, probably late thirteenth or fourteenth century, makes still more fluid associations among Metatron, the middle pillar, the righteous one, and upper or lower Shekhinah. These are **[Medieval Kabbalistic systematizations]**, not proof that early rabbis equated Metatron with the Shekhinah. [*Tiqqunei Zohar* 60b](https://www.sefaria.org/Tikkunei_Zohar.60b.21).

### *Sefer Raziel*, magical texts, and grimoires

*Sefer Raziel ha-Malʾakh* is a medieval Hebrew-Aramaic compilation drawing on several earlier magical and cosmological works. Metatron appears in name-lists, cosmological formulae, and traditions of heavenly instruction. Its medieval compilation history rules out treating the printed work as a book literally transmitted from Adam.

The **Lemegeton** or *Lesser Key of Solomon*—a seventeenth-century English grimoire compilation—does not make Metatron a principal spirit of the *Ars Goetia*. Modern charts that place him over the entire work commonly import later Hermetic-Qabalistic correspondences.

The **Book of Abramelin**, surviving in early-modern manuscripts but presenting itself as a fourteenth-century narrative, is likewise not a primary source for the historical Jewish Metatron tradition. Some later editions and derivative magical systems add archangelic correspondences absent from the core narrative.

### Christian, Gnostic, and Islamic corpora

**New Testament and early Christian apocrypha — [Absence of evidence].** Metatron is not named. Comparisons with Christ, the Logos, the exalted Son of Man, or heavenly Enoch are modern comparative scholarship unless a later text explicitly makes the equation.

**Pseudo-Dionysius and Aquinas — [Absence of evidence].** Neither makes Metatron part of the standard Christian ninefold celestial hierarchy. Aquinas’s angelology cannot be cited as a Metatron source.

**Dante and Milton — [Reception by comparison, not attestation].** Neither gives Metatron a narrative role. Their celestial hierarchies and angelic epics are sometimes used to contextualize modern representations, but they are not links in Metatron’s textual biography.

**Nag Hammadi codices and heresiologists—Irenaeus, Hippolytus, Epiphanius — [Absence of evidence].** They document demiurges, archons, aeons, and exalted revealer figures but do not name Metatron. Claims that Metatron is an ancient “Gnostic god” are therefore modern syncretistic classification. Scholarly comparisons to Gnostic mediators may illuminate shared late-antique problems of transcendence; they do not establish identity.

**Qurʾan, canonical hadith, and early tafsir — [Absence of evidence].** The Qurʾanic Idrīs is often equated with Enoch in Islamic tradition, but neither the Qurʾan nor canonical hadith says that Idrīs became Metatron. Al-Ṭabarī transmits Enoch/Idrīs traditions without making Metatron a Qurʾanic angel. Modern claims that Qurʾan 19:57’s “high station” names Metatron are unsupported.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Offices

- **Celestial scribe:** records Israel’s merits in *b. Ḥagigah* 15a; later records deeds, decrees, or cosmic knowledge.
- **Guide and revealer:** escorts Rabbi Ishmael through heaven in *3 Enoch*.
- **Bearer of the divine name:** explicitly connected with Exodus 23:21.
- **Prince of the Presence:** enjoys exceptional access to God’s throne.
- **Vice-regent or governor:** appointed over princes, nations, treasuries, and the heavenly household in *3 Enoch* 10–12 and 48C.
- **Heavenly priest:** ministers at the celestial tabernacle in certain Hekhalot and *Shiʿur Qomah* traditions. [Orlov, “Priestly and Liturgical Roles”](https://www.marquette.edu/maqom/metatronpriest.html).
- **Teacher:** teaches dead children in one Talmudic tradition and Torah or celestial mysteries elsewhere.
- **Advocate for Israel:** a later midrashic development.
- **Psychopomp or soul-official:** supervises souls in some Zoharic texts.
- **Cosmic mediator:** in Kabbalah, channels or embodies lower-world divine governance.

### Form

*3 Enoch* gives the fullest transformation scene: Enoch’s body becomes fiery and enormous; he receives wings, countless eyes, garments of glory, a crown, and a throne. The imagery deliberately resembles the throne-bearing angelic orders and divine glory. It is literary visionary description, not evidence for a fixed artistic type.

Metatron is also called **Youth**, creating a tension between cosmic gigantism and youthful servant-status. In the Zohar he can be less a visible humanoid than a metaphysical garment, vehicle, tabernacle, or channel.

### Symbols and alleged correspondences

Secure early sources associate him with:

- writing implements or books;
- throne and crown;
- fiery lashes, body, wings, and eyes;
- heavenly curtain, palaces, and tabernacle;
- the divine name;
- Torah and celestial records.

No securely ancient source gives Metatron a standardized color, planet, weekday, metal, animal, plant, or weapon. Later occult tables assign him to Keter, the *Primum Mobile*, white brilliance, and the highest angelic order. These are **[Early-modern and modern Hermetic correspondences]**.

### Iconography and “Metatron’s Cube”

There is no securely identified ancient or medieval Jewish portrait of Metatron and no agreed iconographic attribute by which an archaeological figure can be recognized as him. Jewish incantation bowls sometimes carry schematic demons or protective figures, but a figure on a bowl cannot be labeled Metatron merely because his name appears in the inscription.

“**Metatron’s Cube**”—the modern diagram of thirteen circles joined by straight lines, often said to contain projections of the five Platonic solids—is **not attested in the Talmud, Hekhalot literature, Zohar, or classical Jewish manuscripts under that name**. Its exact naming history remains poorly documented, but its popular attribution belongs to twentieth-century or later sacred-geometry and New Age discourse. Claims that Enoch brought the diagram from heaven, that it was a Temple emblem, or that ancient Kabbalists used it for protection are **[Modern inventions unless a specific manuscript is produced]**.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Origin versions

1. **Primordial or pre-existing high angel:** Certain passages presuppose Metatron’s authority without an earthly origin.  
   **Tier:** Hekhalot elaboration.

2. **Enoch transformed:** God takes Enoch from the generation of the Flood and turns him into Metatron.  
   **Source:** *3 Enoch* 3–16 and 48C.  
   **Tier:** early-medieval synthesis; later dominant legend.

3. **Functional identity with the Exodus angel:** Metatron is the unnamed guide bearing God’s name.  
   **Sources:** *b. Sanhedrin* 38b and later exegesis.  
   **Tier:** secure rabbinic interpretation, not biblical naming.

4. **Identity or overlap with Michael:** Some Palestinian and medieval materials equate them; other texts, including *3 Enoch*, list Michael separately.  
   **Tier:** regional or textual variant.

5. **Two Metatrons:** Later writers distinguish a six-letter primordial Metatron from a seven-letter Enoch-Metatron.  
   **Tier:** medieval learned systematization.

Metatron has no securely attested parents, consort, biological offspring, sacred animal, or mount. The popular statement that Sandalphon is his “twin brother,” with Elijah transformed into Sandalphon as Enoch became Metatron, is a late analogical construction, not a stable doctrine of early Hekhalot literature.

### Documented interactions with named divine beings

Because manuscripts vary, the following records discrete interactions actually present in major sources rather than every name appearing in proximity.

- **YHWH / the Holy One, blessed be He** — God takes Enoch, transforms and crowns him, grants him seventy names and delegated authority, calls him “lesser YHWH,” and later orders or permits his punishment to refute Aḥer’s inference. *3 Enoch* 3–16, 48C; *b. Ḥagigah* 15a. **[Secure rabbinic core; Hekhalot elaboration]**
- **Shekhinah** — Metatron serves her in the heavenly Temple and functions as her attendant, garment, or lower-world vehicle. *Zohar*, *Beshalach* and related passages. **[Medieval Kabbalah]**
- **Enoch** — Explicit identity: Metatron tells Ishmael, “I am Enoch son of Jared.” *3 Enoch* 3. **[Early-medieval synthesis]**
- **Rabbi Ishmael** — Metatron receives him at the seventh palace, protects him from hostile angels, and reveals heavenly structures and mysteries. *3 Enoch* 1–48. **[Hekhalot narrative]**
- **Elisha ben Abuyah/Aḥer** — Aḥer sees Metatron seated and infers two divine powers; Metatron is lashed and receives authority to erase Aḥer’s merits. *b. Ḥagigah* 15a; expanded in *3 Enoch* 16. **[Secure rabbinic attestation; later elaboration]**
- **ʿUzzah, ʿAzzah, and ʿAzael** — These angels oppose Enoch’s elevation, asking why a human should enter heaven; God rebukes them, and they eventually acknowledge Metatron’s authority. *3 Enoch* 4–5. **[Hekhalot elaboration]**
- **Anafiel** — In some Hekhalot recensions Anafiel guards or admits the visionary and stands in a hierarchical relationship near Metatron; textual roles vary. **[Hekhalot variant]**
- **Michael** — Appears as a separate great prince in *3 Enoch*’s celestial hierarchy; elsewhere Michael and Metatron are identified or assigned the same Exodus-guiding and priestly functions. **[Conflicting late-antique/medieval variants]**
- **Gabriel** — Appears among the named princes in Metatron’s guided celestial revelation; later magical texts make Gabriel a subordinate recipient of Metatron’s commands. *3 Enoch* and medieval angelological compilations. **[Hekhalot; medieval accretion]**
- **Sammael** — Later angelological and magical texts sometimes place Sammael under Metatron’s authority or oppose them as representatives of holy and demonic governance. This is not a single early canonical combat myth. **[Medieval systematization]**
- **Prince of Torah** — Metatron receives or exercises the office in later *3 Enoch* materials; in other texts the Prince of Torah is a distinct angel invoked for learning. **[Hekhalot textual variant]**
- **Princes of the nations** — Metatron distributes crowns, authority, or judgment among seventy national princes. *3 Enoch* 10, 14, 30 and 48C-related material. **[Hekhalot elaboration]**
- **Dumah** — In Zoharic eschatology Metatron receives accounts concerning the dead from Dumah and presents them before his Master. **[Medieval Kabbalah]**
- **Sandalphon** — They occur together in later hierarchies as extremely exalted angels, but an early narrative interaction or sibling relationship is not securely attested. **[Absence of early interaction; later pairing]**
- **Jesus Christ / Logos** — No primary Jewish, New Testament, or patristic text stages a meeting or identifies them. Parallels are scholarly comparison. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**
- **Idrīs** — Later Islamic tradition identifies Idrīs with Enoch, but no securely early Islamic source continues the chain “Idrīs = Enoch = Metatron.” **[Modern syncretistic inference]**
- **Gnostic demiurge or aeons** — No Nag Hammadi narrative interaction is attested. **[Absence of evidence]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The translation and transformation of Enoch

**Early core:** Genesis says only that God “took” Enoch.  
**Second Temple elaboration:** 1 Enoch makes him traveler, scribe, witness, and recipient of heavenly secrets; Jubilees places him in Eden recording judgment.  
**Hekhalot culmination:** *3 Enoch* identifies him as Metatron.

In *3 Enoch*, humanity’s corruption causes the Shekhinah to withdraw. God chooses Enoch from among mortals. Three angels object to admitting a human into heaven, but God defends the choice. Enoch is equipped with wings and innumerable eyes; divine wisdom, Torah, secrets, and cosmic knowledge are opened to him. His dimensions expand, his body becomes fire, and he is enthroned at the door of the seventh palace. God crowns him and proclaims his authority to the heavenly household.

The story’s function is simultaneously anthropological and institutional: a human can cross the angelic boundary, yet only by divine election and transformation. It validates the Hekhalot revealer’s access while dramatizing resistance from established angels.

**Development dispute:** Older scholarship often read *3 Enoch* as preserving an ancient, continuous esoteric Enoch tradition. Paz argues instead for a late Palestinian synthesis of an originally Babylonian Metatron with Byzantine or Palestinian Enoch trajectories. The surviving evidence cannot prove that the named Metatron existed in the Second Temple period.

### The lesser YHWH

God places his name upon Metatron, citing Exodus 23:21. The title expresses delegated jurisdiction: Metatron is a visible, name-bearing vice-regent through whom the otherwise transcendent deity can govern and reveal.

The same literature limits the title. Metatron remains a servant, can be beaten, and is never portrayed as an uncreated rival equal to God. Calling him simply “the Jewish second god” erases the sources’ active boundary-policing. Conversely, denying the title’s startling force also distorts the evidence. The text deliberately approaches the boundary and then marks it.

### Aḥer and the “two powers”

In *b. Ḥagigah* 15a, Aḥer sees Metatron sitting and writing. He concludes that there may be “two powers in heaven.” Metatron receives sixty fiery lashes because he should have stood when Aḥer arrived, thereby preventing the error. The angel’s extraordinary privilege is real, but the punishment proves that he is subordinate.

*3 Enoch* 16 incorporates the episode after the vast exaltation narrative. This creates a theological counterweight: no matter how divine Metatron looks, he remains created and punishable.

The historical Aḥer’s exact beliefs cannot be recovered from this literary scene. The story is evidence for rabbinic anxiety about exalted mediators, not a verbatim trial transcript.

### Guide of Israel

Through Exodus 23, Metatron becomes the angel sent before Israel, bearing the divine name and sometimes leading the wilderness journey. *b. Sanhedrin* 38b uses this interpretation polemically: Israel refuses to accept even such an exalted angel in place of God’s presence. Later midrash and Zohar expand his role.

### Heavenly priest, tabernacle, and liturgist

Later Hekhalot and *Shiʿur Qomah* texts place Metatron in charge of the heavenly tabernacle, where he offers or supervises celestial worship. This may absorb older Michael traditions, since Michael is the heavenly high priest elsewhere. Orlov sees priestly antecedents in Enochic traditions; the precise direction of influence remains debated.

### Heavenly scribe and judge

The Talmud permits Metatron to sit solely to record Israel’s merits. *3 Enoch* expands the office into custody of archives, decrees, wisdom, secrets, and national princes. Zoharic sources add administration of souls and resurrection-related records. Later popular accounts that make him the author of an all-encompassing “Akashic record” are **[Modern New Age reinterpretation]**, not the wording of the early sources.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### No temple cult

No temple, altar, priesthood, civic festival, state cult, sacrificial calendar, or theophoric naming tradition dedicated to Metatron has been securely identified. “Heavenly high priest” describes his mythic office, not an earthly Metatron priesthood.

There is no evidence of:

- animal or human sacrifice to Metatron;
- temple prostitution associated with him;
- a city under his patronage;
- pilgrimage to a Metatron sanctuary;
- ancient statues or cult images commissioned for public worship.

The absence is significant: Metatron belongs primarily to interpretation, mystical ascent, angelology, and private protective magic rather than public cult.

### Practical invocation

The incantation bowls are the clearest material evidence. Clients used written formulas invoking God, angels, biblical verses, and coercive names against demons, illness, curses, and household afflictions. Metatron’s inclusion shows that his name was considered efficacious. It does not follow that clients regarded him as a god.

Hekhalot texts prescribe fasting, purity, recitation of names, seals, adjurations, and ritual preparation for ascent or revelation. Modern scholarship debates whether these describe regularly performed rituals, literary ideals, or both. Metatron may guard thresholds, supply passwords, introduce the visionary, or reveal names.

Medieval practical Kabbalah invokes him in amulets, angelic adjurations, and prayers for knowledge or protection. Rabbinic authorities’ attitudes toward such practices varied; their existence should not be equated with universal Jewish observance.

## 7. Historical evolution

### First millennium BCE: Enoch and mediatorial antecedents

Genesis supplied the cryptic translation of Enoch. Daniel, apocalyptic literature, and later Second Temple works developed named angels, heavenly books, enthroned agents, celestial priests, and human ascent. **Metatron is not yet named.**

### Third century BCE–first century CE: Enochic expansion

1 Enoch and Jubilees transformed Enoch into a scribe and revealer. These works supplied narrative building blocks later absorbed into Metatron, but there is no direct named chain.

### Third–seventh centuries CE: emergence of the name

Babylonian rabbinic discourse and incantation bowls provide the first firm named evidence. The Talmud associates Metatron with the divine name, Exodus’s guiding angel, celestial writing, and the danger of “two powers.” Bowls show practical invocation.

Palestinian texts subsequently give him cosmological and revelatory functions. Dating remains disputed.

### Seventh–ninth centuries: Enoch–Metatron synthesis

On Paz’s reconstruction, Palestinian circles combined Babylonian Metatron traditions with an exalted Enoch trajectory around the eighth century. The resulting complex lies behind *3 Enoch* and related materials. Other scholars date substantial portions earlier.

### Early Middle Ages: Hekhalot consolidation

Metatron becomes guide, throne attendant, ruler, scribe, priest, revealer, and bearer of the divine name. Manuscript fluidity prevents reconstructing a single canonical system.

### Twelfth–thirteenth centuries: German Pietists and early Kabbalah

Ḥasidei Ashkenaz and emerging Kabbalistic circles elaborated divine names, angelic hierarchies, *Shiʿur Qomah*, and two-Metatron distinctions. Some Jewish thinkers embraced Metatron as a key mediator; rationalists were more cautious about angelic speculation.

### Late thirteenth century onward: Zoharic reinterpretation

The Zohar integrated Metatron into a sefirotic universe, often as servant of the Shekhinah, ruler of the lower world, heavenly academy head, or cosmic garment. He remained powerful but ontologically below the divine sefirot in many passages.

### Renaissance–nineteenth century: Christian Cabala and ceremonial magic

Christian Kabbalists extracted angelic names and correspondences from Jewish materials. Later occult synthesis placed Metatron at **Keter**, the crown of the Tree of Life, and paired him with Sandalphon at Malkhut. The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn made this correspondence influential; Israel Regardie’s published Golden Dawn materials call Metatron the archangel of Kether and “Prince of Countenances.” [Golden Dawn correspondence](https://www.llewellyn.com/encyclopedia/term/kether); [Crowley-era table](https://hermetic.com/crowley/equinox/i/viii/eqi08024).

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries: globalized angel spirituality

New Age writers recast Metatron as an ascension master, healer, guardian of “indigo” or “crystal” children, regulator of vibrations, and user of a sacred geometric cube. These doctrines have no demonstrable basis in the Talmud or Hekhalot corpus. Contemporary practitioners do invoke Metatron, but reliable statistics on organized worship are unavailable. Practice is decentralized and usually belongs to angel spirituality, ceremonial magic, or eclectic occultism rather than a reconstructed ancient cult.

### Suppression and political use

No documented campaign specifically destroyed a historical Metatron cult, because no such cult is known. Rabbinic stories instead suppress a theological inference: Metatron cannot be treated as a second independent power. Christian and Islamic dominance affected Jewish mystical communities generally, but there is no securely documented forced conversion campaign aimed specifically at Metatron devotees.

No substantial, well-documented extremist political movement centered on Metatron was identified. Conspiracy literature sometimes folds “Metatron’s Cube,” Kabbalah, or Jewish angelology into antisemitic occult narratives; these are modern appropriations, not continuations of an ancient political cult.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Western esotericism

Golden Dawn-derived Hermetic Qabalah fixed a highly influential schema:

- Keter — Metatron;
- Malkhut — Sandalphon;
- Chayot ha-Qodesh — angelic order of Keter;
- Primum Mobile — cosmic sphere;
- brilliant white — color symbolism.

This systematic table is **[Early-modern/modern occult systematization]**. It draws on Jewish names but should not be projected unchanged into the Talmud or Zohar.

Theosophical, Thelemic, and later ceremonial-magical writers developed Metatron as the highest created intelligence, a bridge between the unmanifest and creation. Chaos-magical and New Age systems detach him further from Jewish textual constraints.

### “Metatron’s Cube”

The diagram is now marketed in jewelry, tattoos, meditation prints, crystal grids, and wellness products. It is commonly said to contain the five Platonic solids and to represent the blueprint of creation. The geometry can indeed be drawn so that familiar polygonal projections appear within it; the attribution to Metatron is historically separate from that mathematical fact.

No primary Jewish source used the named diagram. Descriptions of it as “ancient Kabbalah,” “the seal Metatron used to guard creation,” or “a symbol revealed to Enoch” are **[Modern invention]**.

### Literature and screen fiction

- **Philip Pullman, *His Dark Materials* (1995–2000):** Metatron is the Authority’s regent and principal antagonist, formerly a human named Enoch. Pullman preserves the human-to-angel transformation and vice-regency but turns the figure into an authoritarian usurper. This is among the most culturally influential modern reinventions.
- **Kevin Smith, *Dogma* (1999):** Alan Rickman’s Metatron is the “Voice of God,” a humorous but broadly recognizable development of the mediator who can communicate divine speech safely to humans.
- **DC/Vertigo, *Lucifer* and related comics:** Metatron appears within a reworked Judeo-Christian cosmic bureaucracy.
- **Television series *Supernatural*:** Metatron is God’s scribe, later an ambitious antagonist; the scribe motif derives loosely from *Ḥagigah* and Enochic tradition.
- **Shin Megami Tensei and associated games:** Metatron appears as an extremely powerful Law-aligned angel, often with metallic or mechanized visual design. This has strongly shaped popular gaming iconography.
- **Other games, anime, music, and comics:** the name frequently signals supreme angelic authority, machinery, cosmic geometry, or a near-divine final opponent. These are modern genre conventions.

### Milton and Dante

Modern summaries sometimes insert Metatron into the worlds of Dante or Milton because both authors depict complex celestial hierarchies. Metatron is not a major named character in either *Divine Comedy* or *Paradise Lost*. Claims to the contrary confuse later angel dictionaries with the poems themselves.

### Modern angel dictionaries

Gustav Davidson’s *A Dictionary of Angels* assembled Jewish, Christian, magical, literary, and occult notices without consistently distinguishing chronology or textual authority. It popularized identifications such as Metatron as the angel who stayed Abraham’s hand or destroyed Sennacherib’s army, though those deeds are attributed to different angels in different sources. Davidson remains valuable as an index to reception, not as proof of ancient consensus.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Continuity versus late synthesis

The central debate asks whether Metatron is the culmination of a continuous Second Temple Enoch tradition or a late synthesis.

- **Continuity models:** Hugo Odeberg and, more cautiously, Andrei Orlov emphasize lines from ancient Enochic priesthood, scribal activity, transformation, and heavenly mediation into *3 Enoch*.
- **Redaction-critical caution:** Philip Alexander accepts older materials but dates the received *3 Enoch* substantially later and sees several once-independent figures fused in Metatron.
- **Late-synthesis revision:** Yakir Paz separates Babylonian Metatron from Palestinian/Byzantine Enoch and dates their identifiable fusion to about the eighth century.

The disagreement partly reflects different standards: motifs can be old even when a named identification is late.

### “Two powers in heaven”

Alan Segal and later Daniel Boyarin used rabbinic “two powers” traditions to reconstruct contested Jewish ideas about principal angels, Logos figures, exalted patriarchs, and divine plurality. Metatron is crucial evidence, but the rabbinic narratives are polemical witnesses: they reveal both the plausibility and rejection of interpreting a heavenly vice-regent as a second deity.

### Michael, Yahoel, Melchizedek, and Christ

Comparative studies observe overlapping offices:

- Michael: Israel’s prince and heavenly high priest;
- Yahoel: divine-name-bearing angel and guide in the *Apocalypse of Abraham*;
- Melchizedek: exalted eschatological deliverer in 11QMelchizedek;
- Christ/Logos: heavenly mediator bearing divine authority.

These parallels support a shared history of mediatorial speculation but do not prove that the figures were originally identical. Alexander’s fusion theory remains a reconstruction, not an explicit confession in the sources.

### Dating and manuscript problems

No autograph of *3 Enoch* survives. Its medieval manuscripts contain reordered material, appendices, duplicated traditions, and divergent name lists. Odeberg’s 1928 edition was pioneering but sometimes reconstructed too confidently. Alexander’s translation in Charlesworth’s *Old Testament Pseudepigrapha* remains standard in English; Schäfer’s synoptic work transformed study of the wider Hekhalot corpus by preserving manuscript plurality.

The incantation bowls provide material controls but introduce other problems: many were excavated before modern archaeological standards, while many more entered collections through the antiquities market with no secure context.

### Discredited or unsupported popular theories

- Metatron appears in the canonical Bible under that name — **false**.
- All ancient references to the Angel of the Lord secretly mean Metatron — **unprovable harmonization**.
- Metatron was worshipped in Solomon’s Temple — **no evidence**.
- “Metatron’s Cube” is an ancient Jewish emblem — **no evidence**.
- Metatron is explicitly a Gnostic archon in Nag Hammadi texts — **false**.
- The Qurʾan names Metatron or says Idrīs became Metatron — **false**.
- Metatron was “removed from the Bible” by a church council — **modern invention**.
- Metatron is straightforwardly Satan, Lucifer, or a demon — **unsupported conflation**.
- The title “lesser YHWH” proves a stable Jewish cult of two equal gods — **overstatement contradicted by the punishment and servant traditions**.

## 11. Sources and assessment

### Primary editions and corpora

The core documentary base consists of the Babylonian Talmud; late-antique Aramaic incantation bowls; Hekhalot manuscripts in Schäfer’s *Synopse*; Odeberg’s and Alexander’s editions/translations of *3 Enoch*; medieval midrashic and magical compilations; and the Zoharic corpus.

For historical reconstruction, the most important caution is that modern English editions can make unstable manuscript collections look like single authored books. Any claim dependent on a name-list, angelic hierarchy, or appendix should be checked against the manuscript apparatus.

### Principal modern scholarship

- Hugo Odeberg, *3 Enoch or the Hebrew Book of Enoch* (1928).
- Gershom Scholem, *Major Trends in Jewish Mysticism*; *Jewish Gnosticism, Merkabah Mysticism, and Talmudic Tradition*; encyclopedia studies on Metatron and Kabbalah.
- Peter Schäfer, *Synopse zur Hekhalot-Literatur* and later studies of Jewish mysticism.
- Philip S. Alexander, “3 (Hebrew Apocalypse of) Enoch” in *The Old Testament Pseudepigrapha*, vol. 1.
- Andrei A. Orlov, *The Enoch-Metatron Tradition*.
- Yakir Paz, “Metatron Is Not Enoch: Reevaluating the Evolution of an Archangel.”
- Alan F. Segal, *Two Powers in Heaven*.
- Daniel Boyarin, *Border Lines* and *The Jewish Gospels*, used cautiously for broader mediatorial debates.
- James Davila, *Hekhalot Literature in Translation*.
- Rebecca Lesses, *Ritual Practices to Gain Power*.
- Michael D. Swartz, *Scholastic Magic*.
- Joseph Dan and Moshe Idel on Jewish mysticism and Kabbalah.
- James A. Montgomery and subsequent editors of Jewish Babylonian Aramaic bowls.

Davidson’s *Dictionary of Angels*, Bamberger’s *Fallen Angels*, Jeffrey Burton Russell’s histories of the Devil, and Henry Ansgar Kelly’s *Satan: A Biography* are useful for reception and comparative demonology. They should not be used to turn Metatron into a fallen angel: the primary Metatron traditions do not do so.

### Final evidentiary judgment

The fullest defensible reconstruction is not “Enoch was always Metatron.” It is a sequence:

**translated Enoch and other exalted mediators → late-antique named Metatron in Babylonia → Palestinian and Hekhalot combinations of angelic offices → explicit Enoch–Metatron synthesis → medieval Kabbalistic incorporation → Western occult correspondence → modern fictional and sacred-geometric reinvention.**

At every stage, Metatron is a test case in how traditions authorize a mediator without surrendering divine uniqueness. His exaltation and humiliation are not contradictory debris to be harmonized away; together they constitute the figure’s central documented history.
