# Lilith: Research Dossier

> **Class:** In the earliest Hebrew evidence, a wilderness being or class of nocturnal demon; in late-antique Jewish texts, a winged, long-haired female demon and, often, a plural class of *liliths*; in medieval Jewish legend, Adam’s rebellious first wife and a child-killing demoness; in thirteenth-century Kabbalah, a cosmic demonic “matron,” counterpart or consort of Samael; in modern occult and feminist traditions, variously goddess, archetype, patron, or symbol. She is not securely attested as an ancient goddess with temples or an organized cult.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation of Hebrew לילית (*lîlît*):** Isaiah 34:14, in a prophetic oracle conventionally assigned to the exilic or early post-exilic period, approximately the sixth–fifth century BCE. The verse supplies no genealogy, Adam narrative, personal history, or cult: *lîlît* merely finds repose among the creatures haunting devastated Edom. Earlier Akkadian *lilû*, *lilītu*, and *ardat lilî* are plausible linguistic and demonological antecedents, but they are categories of spirits rather than securely documented appearances of the later Jewish individual Lilith.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early attested]** Secure pre-rabbinic evidence.
- **[Later canonical]** A normative or highly influential text within a tradition, without implying universal acceptance.
- **[Regional variant]** A geographically or communally bounded form.
- **[Learned systematization]** Rabbinic, philosophical, or kabbalistic organization of older motifs.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A historical equation with another culture’s being.
- **[Medieval/early-modern accretion]** Material first securely documented in those periods.
- **[Folklore]** Custom or oral-derived narrative not necessarily institutionally canonical.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** A scholarly hypothesis joining incomplete evidence.
- **[Modern invention/reinvention]** A demonstrably recent doctrine, image, ritual, or identity.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Hebrew and Aramaic

The Masoretic form is **לִילִית**, *lîlît*. Isaiah 34:14 is its only occurrence in the Hebrew Bible. The common explanation as “night creature” connects it with Hebrew **לַיְלָה**, *laylāh*, “night,” but the form is more plausibly related historically to Akkadian *lilītu*. Association with Hebrew “night” nevertheless affected Jewish interpretation and made “night demon” an intelligible folk etymology.

Late Hebrew and Aramaic sources use:

- **לילית**, *Lilith/Lilit*: either an individual demoness or a generic feminine demon.
- **לילין**, *lilin*: plural spirits or demons.
- **ליליתא**, *lilitha*: Aramaic form.
- **ליליות**, *liliyyot*: feminine plural “liliths.”
- **לילית רבתי**, “Great/Greater Lilith,” and a **Lesser Lilith** in medieval Kabbalah.
- **חוה ראשונה**, *Ḥavvah Rishonah*, “First Eve,” in later interpretation.
- **הצפונית**, “the Northerner,” in Isaac ha-Kohen’s demonic cosmology.
- “Matron Lilith,” “evil Lilith,” and “queen of demons” are medieval or later titles, not biblical epithets.

### Mesopotamian comparanda

Akkadian texts distinguish, though not invariably with complete consistency:

- **lilû**: usually masculine.
- **lilītu**: feminine.
- **ardat lilî**, approximately “maiden of the *lilû*”: an unmarried female ghost or demon, sexually dangerous to men.
- **idlu lilî**, a corresponding young male spirit in some classifications.

These beings have often been understood as ghosts whose human life cycles—especially marriage and reproduction—were left incomplete. They therefore menace the living through frustrated sexuality, illness, nightmare, or reproductive harm. Judith Scurlock summarizes *lilû*, *lilītu*, and *ardat lilî* as resentful dead who had failed to complete expected life stages. This is a stronger historically controlled comparison than the popular claim that “Lilith was a Sumerian goddess.” [Scurlock’s reference entry](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781444338386.wbeah24129)

The derivation from Sumerian **líl**, often glossed “wind,” “spirit,” or “phantom,” has been influential, but exact pathways remain debated. A direct identification of every *lilītu* or *ardat lilî* with the later personal Lilith exceeds the evidence.

### Greek and Latin translation

The Septuagint does not transliterate Lilith in Isaiah 34:14. Its Greek reads of *daimonia* and **ὀνοκένταυροι**, “donkey-centaurs/onocentaurs,” translating the passage into a Hellenistic bestiary. [Greek Isaiah 34](https://www.biblindex.org/en/bible/septuaginta/is-34)

Aquila reportedly transliterated the name; Symmachus used **Λάμια**, *Lamia*. Jerome’s Vulgate likewise has **lamia**. These are [syncretistic identifications]: translators mapped an obscure Hebrew desert being onto familiar Greek and Roman monsters. They do not prove that Lilith and the Greek Lamia had always been the same figure. [Text and reception of Isaiah 34:14](https://scribe.bibletraditions.org/bible/Is34%2C14-17)

### Classification through time

1. Mesopotamian comparanda: restless ghost/demon classes.
2. Isaiah: wilderness or nocturnal being; whether a proper name or common noun remains disputed.
3. Qumran: explicitly one of a company of dangerous spirits.
4. Talmud and incantation bowls: winged, long-haired sexual and reproductive demon; also a plural species.
5. *Alphabet of Ben Sira*: individual demoness and Adam’s first wife.
6. Kabbalah: cosmic female power of the “other side,” consort of Samael.
7. Modern feminism and occultism: reclaimed rebel, goddess, archetype, or devotional being—classifications created through reception, not recovered intact from antiquity.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Mesopotamian background

Second- and first-millennium BCE Akkadian lexical, medical, and incantation traditions attest *lilû*, *lilītu*, and *ardat lilî*. They are not supplied with the biography familiar from medieval Lilith narratives. Their relevance is linguistic and typological: nocturnal or intrusive spirits, sexual danger, frustrated marriage, and reproductive harm.

A Sumerian figure in the *Gilgamesh, Enkidu and the Netherworld* episode was once read **ki-sikil-líl-lá-ke₄** and freely identified as “Lilith,” living in Inanna’s *huluppu* tree alongside a serpent and the Anzu bird. The reading and equation are disputed. Even if the phrase corresponds to Akkadian *ardat lilî*, it does not name the medieval Lilith or establish her as Inanna’s handmaid, priestess, or goddess. [Modern reconstruction/disputed]

### Isaiah 34:14

The Hebrew verse places *lîlît* amid jackals, desert creatures, goat beings, and birds in ruined Edom:

> **אַךְ־שָׁם הִרְגִּיעָה לִּילִית וּמָצְאָה לָהּ מָנוֹחַ**  
> “There Lilith/the lilith rests and finds for herself a place of repose.”

The passage establishes wilderness residence and perhaps supernatural classification. It does **not** establish wings, long hair, seduction, child killing, Adam, Samael, Eden, or worship. [Hebrew text](https://www.sefaria.org/Isaiah.34.14)

The Great Isaiah Scroll’s orthography has sometimes been read as plural *liliyyot*. Interpretation is difficult, but the manuscript evidence is important because it shows that plurality was possible early in transmission.

### Dead Sea Scrolls

**4Q510–511, Songs of the Sage/Maskil**, late Second Temple period, invokes divine praise to terrify “spirits of the ravaging angels,” bastard spirits, demons, Lilith, howlers, and wilderness beings. Unlike Isaiah’s poetic ruin landscape, this is an apotropaic or exorcistic setting. It confirms that at least one Jewish community understood Lilith demonologically. The text does not identify her as Adam’s wife.

Lilith is not a named character in **1 Enoch, Jubilees, Tobit**, or the surviving **New Testament**. Those works possess rich demonologies—Watchers, evil spirits, Mastema, Asmodeus, Satan—but later comparison must not be mistaken for direct attestation. Likewise, no named Lilith occurs in the surviving **Nag Hammadi codices**, Irenaeus, Hippolytus, or Epiphanius. Claims that Lilith was an ancient Gnostic aeon, Sophia’s daughter, or the serpent’s canonical Gnostic identity are modern syncretisms unless tied to a specifically modern text.

### Babylonian Talmud

The Babylonian Talmud, redacted chiefly in the fifth–sixth centuries CE, assumes rather than narrates Lilith:

- **Eruvin 100b:** a woman’s long hair is compared with Lilith’s.  
- **Niddah 24b:** a malformed fetus resembling Lilith is said to have wings.
- **Shabbat 151b:** a man sleeping alone in a house is warned lest Lilith seize him.
- **Bava Batra 73a:** manuscripts variously call the prodigious Hormin a son of Lilith or of a class of night demons.

These notices establish long hair, wings, night attack, and sexual or bodily danger. They do not contain the first-wife story. Jastrow’s lexicon usefully assembles the passages and their manuscript variants. [Talmudic dossier](https://www.sefaria.org/Jastrow%2C_%D7%9C%D6%B4%D7%99%D7%9C%D6%B4%D7%99%D7%AA.1?with=Shabbat)

### Incantation bowls and amulets

From approximately 500–700 CE, Jewish Babylonian Aramaic, Syriac, and Mandaic bowls from Sasanian and early Islamic Mesopotamia bind, divorce, repel, or chain male and female *lilin*. They protect houses, spouses, pregnancy, childbirth, and infants. Some texts give individual liliths names; others treat them as a swarm or class. Drawn figures—frequently bound, with disordered hair—may represent captured demons, but not every bowl figure is securely Lilith.

A British Museum Mandaic bowl from southern Iraq, dated sixth–eighth century, explicitly lists several liliths. This is material evidence of practiced apotropaic religion, not merely literary mythology. [British Museum bowl 117881](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_1980-0415-28?selectedImageId=738040001)

Another bowl asks protection from an evil Lilith who leads human hearts astray. Such objects were deposited in houses, often upside down beneath floors or thresholds. They document fear and ritual counteraction, not a cult worshipping Lilith. [British Museum exhibition documentation](https://www.britishmuseum.org/sites/default/files/2022-05/feminine_power_exhibition_large_print_guide.pdf)

### Alphabet of Ben Sira

The anonymous **Alphabet of Ben Sira**, generally dated between the eighth and tenth centuries CE, is the earliest surviving source for Lilith as Adam’s first wife. It is a comic, irreverent, and often obscene medieval work, not the biblical book of Sirach and not rabbinic scripture.

God creates Lilith from earth as he created Adam. Adam demands the superior sexual position; Lilith argues that equal creation entails equality. She pronounces the divine Name and flies away to the Red Sea. At Adam’s request, God sends three angels—Senoy, Sansenoy, and Semangelof—to retrieve her. She refuses. The angels threaten that one hundred of her demonic children will die daily; she accepts this penalty and swears not to harm an infant protected by an amulet bearing the angels’ names or forms.

This story combines a gender conflict, explanation of infant-protection amulets, and demon genealogy. Calling it “the suppressed original Genesis” is [modern invention]. Its medieval date is decisive.

### Kabbalistic corpus

Isaac ben Jacob ha-Kohen’s **Treatise on the Left Emanation**, written in thirteenth-century Castile, supplies the earliest comprehensive kabbalistic demonic cosmology centered on Lilith. Samael and the Greater Lilith emerge as an intertwined pair mirroring Adam and Eve; a Lesser Lilith is paired with Asmodeus. This is [learned systematization], not ancient Gnosticism. [Treatise overview and publication history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Treatise_on_the_Left_Emanation)

The **Zohar**, largely composed in late thirteenth-century Castile and conventionally associated with Moses de León, integrates several Lilith traditions. She becomes a power of the *sitra aḥra*, “other side,” associated with illicit sexuality, nocturnal emissions, infant danger, and demonic offspring. She appears with Samael, Naamah, and other female demons. In one Zoharic passage, Samael’s breath acts after he has been separated from his female counterpart Lilith. [Zohar, Mishpatim](https://www.sefaria.org/Zohar%2C_Mishpatim.3.16-18)

Later printed **Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh** material transmits childbirth protections and magical names, but the work is layered and composite. Its received form should not be projected wholesale into antiquity. Modern research treats it as a conduit of diverse medieval Ashkenazi and Byzantine magical traditions. [Study of *Sefer Raziel*’s layers](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/asch-2024-2017/html)

### Corpora in which Lilith is absent

No secure named Lilith appears in:

- Genesis;
- 1 Enoch, Jubilees, or Tobit;
- the New Testament or standard early Christian apocrypha;
- Pseudo-Dionysius or Aquinas;
- the Qur’an or canonical hadith;
- al-Ṭabarī’s standard Adam traditions;
- the Nag Hammadi codices;
- the *Lemegeton*’s catalogues or the core *Abramelin* material;
- Dante’s *Commedia* or Milton’s *Paradise Lost*.

These works may contain Eve, Lamia, succubi, Asmodeus, Satan, Iblis, fallen angels, or female demons, but later writers have created connections that their texts do not make. Absence is especially important here: “Lilith is in the Qur’an,” “Lilith is a Goetic spirit,” and “Milton names Lilith” are false as textual claims.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

- **Isaiah:** devastated wilderness and nocturnal repose.
- **Qumran:** harmful spirits subject to apotropaic praise.
- **Talmud and bowls:** night attack, sexuality, pregnancy, infancy, bodily abnormality, and domestic danger.
- **Ben Sira:** sexual equality and rebellion within a satirical narrative; infant killing; demonic maternity.
- **Kabbalah:** illicit sexuality, nocturnal emission, demonic generation, the left or impure side of creation.
- **Modernity:** female autonomy, sexual freedom, rage, exile, queer and feminist resistance, occult initiation, and astrological “shadow”—all reception-historical developments.

### Form

Long hair and wings are Talmudically attested. Bowl figures may be naked or disheveled and bound by chains or inscriptions. The bird feet, horned crown, owls, and lions familiar from modern Lilith art come principally from the Old Babylonian “Queen of the Night” plaque, not from an identifying inscription.

The plaque, about 1800–1750 BCE, depicts a winged nude female with talons, flanked by owls and lions. Suggestions have included Ishtar, Ereshkigal, and Lilith; the British Museum prefers an underworld goddess, probably Ereshkigal. There is no inscription naming Lilith. Treating it as “the earliest portrait of Lilith” is therefore [discredited modern reconstruction]. [British Museum object record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/W_2003-0718-1)

No ancient standard colors, sacred number, weekday, planet, herb, metal, altar form, or feast is securely attested for Lilith. Red, black, the Moon, owls, serpents, roses, and the astrological glyph ⚸ belong largely to modern occult correspondence systems.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

Lilith has no biblical genealogy. Mesopotamian *lilītu* is a type, not a daughter in a stable divine family. Genealogy begins to crystallize only in late-antique and medieval sources.

### Competing genealogical systems

- **Incantation tradition:** multiple male and female *lilin*, sometimes spouses or household invaders; individual parents vary by text.
- **Ben Sira:** Lilith is created by God from earth, parallel to Adam; her demonic children are already assumed.
- **Treatise on the Left Emanation:** Greater Lilith and Samael form a cosmic pair; Lesser Lilith is the daughter of Qafsefoni and mate of Asmodeus.
- **Zohar:** Lilith belongs among female powers of the “other side”; relations with Samael, Adam, Naamah, and demonic offspring vary across passages.
- **Later folklore:** Lilith is sometimes merged with the “First Eve,” the Queen of Sheba, Lamia, Gello/Gyllou, or Abyzou. These equations are historically secondary and never completely stable.

### Documented divine or supernatural interactions

- **God — creation and judgment:** God creates Lilith from earth and sends angels after her; *Alphabet of Ben Sira*, eighth–tenth century. **[Medieval accretion]**
- **Adam — marriage and quarrel:** Lilith refuses Adam’s demanded sexual subordination, pronounces the divine Name, and departs; *Alphabet of Ben Sira*. **[Medieval accretion]**
- **Senoy — attempted retrieval and amuletic pact:** one of three angels sent after Lilith; *Alphabet of Ben Sira*. **[Medieval accretion]**
- **Sansenoy — same episode:** *Alphabet of Ben Sira*. **[Medieval accretion]**
- **Semangelof — same episode:** *Alphabet of Ben Sira*. **[Medieval accretion]**
- **Samael — consort and cosmic counterpart:** first securely systematized in Isaac ha-Kohen’s *Treatise on the Left Emanation*, thirteenth century; developed in the Zohar. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Asmodeus — mate of Lesser Lilith:** *Treatise on the Left Emanation*, thirteenth century. This is not necessarily the same Lilith as Samael’s matron. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Naamah — associated demonic female:** the Zohar describes Naamah’s intercourse with men and Lilith recognizing and rearing some resulting children rather than killing them. [Zohar, Achrei Mot](https://www.sefaria.org/Zohar%2C_Achrei_Mot.59.362) **[Later canonical kabbalistic]**
- **Iggeret daughter of Mahalath — joint survival among unclean spirits:** named with Lilith and Naamah in the Zohar. [Zohar, Bereshit](https://www.sefaria.org/Zohar%2C_Bereshit.94.365?with=all) **[Later canonical kabbalistic]**
- **Eve — no interaction in Ben Sira:** Eve succeeds Lilith, but the two do not meet in the surviving story. Later feminist narratives that make them friends or lovers are modern rewritings.
- **Inanna/Ishtar — no secure personal interaction:** the *huluppu*-tree equation depends on disputed philology; no text securely calls Lilith Inanna’s handmaid.
- **Ereshkigal — no documented interaction:** their connection derives from competing identifications of the Queen of the Night plaque.
- **Satan/Iblis — no early direct interaction:** Samael may be satanic in kabbalistic systems, but equating him without qualification with every scriptural Satan or Qur’anic Iblis collapses distinct traditions.
- **Jesus, Sophia, Yaldabaoth, Barbelo, and other named Gnostic beings — no ancient attested interaction.**
- **Lamaštu — no surviving narrative meeting:** similarities in reproductive danger encouraged comparison and partial functional fusion, but they are not originally one named being.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The wilderness rest

Isaiah’s oracle imagines Edom reduced to an anti-city inhabited by liminal animals and beings. Lilith’s role is a single action: she settles and finds rest. The text’s force is political and poetic—the reversal of inhabited land into chaos—not a biography of the creature. **[Early attested]**

### The threatened household

Late-antique bowls preserve a practical narrative world: liliths enter homes, attach themselves to clients, injure spouses or children, and are expelled through divorce formulae, angelic authority, divine names, adjurations, and binding images. The demon may be given a writ of divorce as though an illicit spouse. **[Regional late-antique practice]**

### Adam’s first wife

Ben Sira’s tale is the first full narrative. Its enduring core is:

1. Equal creation from earth.
2. Dispute over hierarchy during intercourse.
3. Lilith’s assertion of equality.
4. Her use of the divine Name and flight.
5. Three angels’ pursuit to the Red Sea.
6. Refusal to return.
7. Death of her demonic offspring.
8. Covenant respecting protective amulets.

The satire explains an already existing amulet practice through a mythic charter. It is neither in Genesis nor evidence of an excised biblical chapter. Later versions often soften or omit the killing of children, transform the sexual quarrel into generalized political liberty, or portray Eve and Lilith as allies. **[Medieval accretion; modern retelling]**

### The demonic couple

Isaac ha-Kohen’s system mirrors sacred order with an evil emanatory order. Samael and Lilith form a male-female pair parallel to Adam and Eve. The Zohar disperses this conception through commentaries on creation, sexuality, impurity, and redemption. Later Lurianic and popular kabbalistic traditions expand the pair, sometimes assigning Lilith temporary union with Adam or human men and generating demons from wasted semen. **[Learned systematization]**

### Elijah and the child-killing demon

Medieval and early-modern amuletic narratives often stage a prophet or angel meeting a female child-killing demon, forcing her to disclose names and swear an oath. Lilith traditions overlap here with Gello, Gyllou, Abyzou, and related Mediterranean demons. Individual manuscript names and details differ. “Elijah defeats Lilith” is therefore a family of amuletic stories, not one universally canonical myth. **[Folklore and magical-text accretion]**

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

There is no secure evidence for a temple dedicated to Lilith, a Lilith priesthood, public festival, sacrificial calendar, oracle, pilgrimage center, sacred prostitution, or human sacrifice.

The documented practices are overwhelmingly **apotropaic**:

- incantation bowls buried in domestic contexts;
- metal or paper amulets near mothers and infants;
- inscriptions naming protective angels;
- divorce writs expelling attached demons;
- divine names and scriptural verses;
- warnings against sleeping alone;
- later printed childbirth sheets and bedside customs.

These practices feared, constrained, or expelled Lilith; they were not a cult offered to her. Claims of an ancient Lilith temple or priestesshood ordinarily result from combining *lilītu*, Inanna/Ishtar, the disputed *huluppu*-tree reading, and obsolete theories of “sacred prostitution.” Modern scholarship has shown that much purported evidence for widespread ancient temple prostitution rests on tendentious translations or circular reconstruction. [Review of Budin’s source critique](https://www.querelles-net.de/index.php/qn/en/article/view/805)

There is likewise no documentary basis for Lilith receiving human sacrifice. Infant deaths attributed to her belong to explanations of misfortune and rituals of protection, not evidence that infants were sacrificed to her.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Second–first millennia BCE: antecedent demon classes

Akkadian *lilû/lilītu/ardat lilî* supply the strongest linguistic and functional prehistory. They are not a single goddess with a continuous biography.

### Sixth–fifth century BCE: Isaiah

Hebrew *lîlît* enters the record as a wilderness being. The name’s singularity in scripture leaves classification uncertain.

### Third century BCE–first century CE: translation and demonological interpretation

The Septuagint replaces the obscure term with Hellenistic monsters. Qumran places Lilith among demons repelled through liturgical power.

### Third–seventh centuries CE: rabbinic and magical consolidation

The Babylonian Talmud supplies wings, long hair, and nocturnal danger. Mesopotamian bowls provide the richest material evidence: multiple liliths, named clients, household ritual, binding, divorce, and protection.

### Eighth–tenth centuries: first-wife narrative

The *Alphabet of Ben Sira* makes Lilith Adam’s first wife and connects her with the three protective angels. This is the decisive narrative transformation.

### Thirteenth century: kabbalistic cosmologization

Isaac ha-Kohen makes Lilith the mate of Samael within a structured evil emanation. The Zohar incorporates and multiplies her roles. A household demon becomes simultaneously a cosmic principle.

### Early modern period

Printed Kabbalah, amulet books, and Christian Hebraism circulate Lilith more widely. Johannes Buxtorf’s seventeenth-century lexicographical work helped introduce the first-wife legend to Christian European scholarship. Goethe’s *Faust I* (1808) presents Lilith at the witches’ sabbath as Adam’s first wife, marked by dangerous beauty and hair.

### Nineteenth century

Dante Gabriel Rossetti’s poem “Lilith”/“Body’s Beauty” and *Lady Lilith* paintings transformed her into the self-absorbed, erotic femme fatale. The Metropolitan Museum’s watercolor derives from Rossetti’s 1866 oil and uses Fanny Cornforth as sitter. [Metropolitan Museum record](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/337500)

### Twentieth century to present

Lilith became a feminist counter-hero, occult divinity, literary character, astrological point, and commercial image. This is not simple survival of an ancient cult: it is active reinvention using selected medieval motifs.

No continuous institutional worship from ancient Mesopotamia to contemporary neopaganism can be demonstrated. Present devotional Lilith traditions are revivals or new religious movements.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Feminism

Judith Plaskow’s 1972 “The Coming of Lilith” rewrites Lilith as returning to Eden and forming solidarity with Eve. Its friendship narrative is intentionally new midrash, not a recovered ancient source. The later essay collection explicitly covers her work from 1972 onward. [Beacon Press](https://www.beacon.org/The-Coming-of-Lilith-P517.aspx)

**Lilith magazine**, founded in 1976, adopted the name for a Jewish feminist publication and describes itself as a feminist change agent within Jewish communities. [Magazine history](https://lilith.org/about/)

**Lilith Fair**, founded by Sarah McLachlan and collaborators after precursor concerts in 1996 and launched as a touring festival in 1997, turned the name into a symbol of women musicians’ collective commercial and artistic power. Its use is feminist cultural reception, not ritual devotion.

### Occultism and neopaganism

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century ceremonial magicians, Satanists, Luciferians, demonolaters, witches, and neopagans variously invoke Lilith as queen of demons, dark goddess, initiator, sexual liberator, or mother of monsters. These systems selectively combine Ben Sira, Kabbalah, romantic art, Jungian psychology, goddess spirituality, and modern left-hand-path writing.

Lilith is not a named spirit in the classical *Lemegeton* hierarchy. Modern “sigils of Lilith” frequently presented as ancient are mostly recent designs whose provenance is poorly documented. Modern ritual titles such as “Queen of Hell,” “Mother of Vampires,” and fixed elemental or planetary correspondences require source-by-source dating.

### Astrology

“Black Moon Lilith” is not a planet, moon, or ancient Jewish celestial doctrine. It is normally the lunar apogee or the empty focus of the Moon’s orbit. Its Lilith interpretation developed in twentieth-century astrology; Dom Néroman’s French work in the 1930s was formative. A different “Dark Moon Lilith” was imagined as a hypothetical second satellite. Their conflation is modern. [History and distinction](https://www.divinityatlas.com/religious-symbols/black-moon-lilith)

### Literature, visual culture, and popular media

Goethe and Rossetti made Lilith a beautiful fatal seductress. George MacDonald’s novel *Lilith* (1895) spiritualized her through fantasy. Later fiction, comics, television, games, and music use the name for vampires, demon queens, witches, aliens, and rebellious women. These representations have often influenced public knowledge more strongly than Isaiah or the bowls.

### Political appropriation

Lilith’s rebellion has served feminist and queer liberation; conversely, some contemporary spiritual-warfare movements identify “Lilith” as a territorial demon blamed for feminism, abortion, sexuality, or LGBTQ life. Such claims represent modern charismatic demonology, not continuity with ancient Jewish ritual. No general claim that a political opponent is “controlled by Lilith” has historical evidentiary standing.

## 9. Historiography and disputed material

### Is Isaiah’s *lîlît* a demon, bird, or night creature?

The demon reading is supported by Akkadian cognates, Qumran’s demonic list, and later Jewish interpretation. Zoological translations—“screech owl,” “nightjar,” “night creature”—reflect uncertainty or rationalization. Because *lîlît* is a hapax in the Hebrew Bible, absolute certainty is unavailable.

### Was Lilith Sumerian?

Only with qualification. Mesopotamia supplies cognate demon classes and possibly a disputed Sumerian precursor. It does not supply the complete Jewish Lilith or Adam narrative. “Sumerian goddess Lilith” is misleading.

### Is the *huluppu*-tree figure Lilith?

Older scholars often said yes; later philological criticism is more cautious. The phrase is not simply the Hebrew personal name, and the episode gives no Adam, infants, Samael, or Lilith cult. The equation remains a scholarly reconstruction, not secure identification.

### Is the Queen of the Night Lilith?

No inscription says so. Ishtar and Ereshkigal are stronger candidates in current museum treatment. The famous image should not be captioned unconditionally as Lilith.

### Arslan Tash

The first-millennium BCE amulets from Arslan Tash have been read as containing a Lilith-like name and a protection against a flying intruder. Authenticity, segmentation, language, and reading have all been debated. They are significant evidence for West Semitic household protection only if those textual judgments are accepted; they cannot bear the popular claim of a fully formed ancient Lilith biography.

### Was Lilith an ancient goddess suppressed by patriarchy?

No extant temple, priesthood, offering list, hymn, festival, or theophoric cult record establishes this. The claim commonly depends upon treating every *lilītu*, the *huluppu* figure, Lamaštu, and the Queen of the Night as one goddess. That composite is modern.

### Is the first-wife legend ancient or biblical?

Its earliest surviving narrative is medieval. Genesis contains two creation accounts whose differences helped later interpretation, but it never names Lilith as Adam’s wife.

### Is Lilith Gnostic?

Ancient Gnostic sources do not name her. Modern Gnostic, Luciferian, and occult systems may legitimately include her as modern religious creation, but they should not be presented as translations of Nag Hammadi evidence.

### Human sacrifice, sacred prostitution, and cult violence

No secure evidence connects Lilith with human sacrifice, a temple-sex institution, cult armies, or historical persecution of Lilith worshippers. Ancient and medieval sources attribute attacks on infants and sexual assault to demons; these are beliefs about supernatural harm. They are not records of sacrificial practice.

### Key modern scholarship

Important modern points of reference include Raphael Patai’s synthetic but sometimes overly harmonizing treatment; Lowell K. Handy and the *Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible*; Manfred Hutter and Walter Farber on Mesopotamian and Jewish demonology; Joseph Dan on Samael and Lilith in early Kabbalah; Gershom Scholem on Jewish mysticism; Dan Levene, Siam Bhayro, James Nathan Ford, and later bowl specialists; and modern critiques of the “ancient goddess” genealogy.

Davidson’s *Dictionary of Angels*, Bamberger’s *Fallen Angels*, Jeffrey Burton Russell’s history of the Devil, and Henry Ansgar Kelly’s *Satan: A Biography* are useful for reception and adjacent demonology, but Lilith must not be inserted into sources those works discuss when she is not named there. Pearson and Rudolph illuminate ancient Gnosticism primarily by establishing the actual Gnostic corpora against which modern Lilith claims can be tested.

## 10. Chronology

- **Second millennium BCE:** Akkadian *lilû*, *lilītu*, and *ardat lilî* appear in Mesopotamian demonological and magical traditions. **[Antecedents]**
- **Approximately sixth–fifth century BCE:** Isaiah 34:14 supplies the earliest secure Hebrew *lîlît*. **[Early attested]**
- **Hellenistic period:** Septuagint Isaiah renders the verse through *daimonia* and *onocentaurs*. **[Syncretistic translation]**
- **Late Second Temple period:** 4Q510–511 lists Lilith among dangerous spirits. **[Early attested]**
- **Approximately third–sixth centuries CE:** Babylonian rabbinic traditions describe Lilith’s hair, wings, and nocturnal danger. **[Rabbinic elaboration]**
- **Approximately 500–700:** Jewish, Mandaic, and Syriac incantation bowls name and bind liliths in Mesopotamia. **[Material and regional attestation]**
- **Eighth–tenth centuries:** *Alphabet of Ben Sira* first makes Lilith Adam’s first wife and names the three pursuing angels. **[Medieval accretion]**
- **Eleventh century:** Rashi glosses Isaiah’s Lilith as the name of a female demon. [Rashi on Isaiah](https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Isaiah.34.14)
- **Approximately 1260–1280:** Isaac ha-Kohen composes the *Treatise on the Left Emanation*; Samael and Lilith become a cosmic pair.
- **Late thirteenth century:** the Zohar develops Lilith within the *sitra aḥra*.
- **Medieval–early modern period:** layered *Sefer Raziel* and childbirth amulet traditions circulate protections against Lilith.
- **Seventeenth century:** Christian Hebraist lexicography helps transmit the first-wife legend in Europe.
- **1808:** Goethe’s *Faust I* introduces a highly visible Romantic Lilith.
- **1860s–1870s:** Rossetti paints and writes *Lady Lilith*, establishing the Pre-Raphaelite femme-fatale image.
- **1895:** George MacDonald publishes *Lilith*.
- **Early twentieth century:** occultists and astrologers attach Lilith to a hypothetical dark moon and, later, the lunar apogee.
- **1972:** Judith Plaskow writes “The Coming of Lilith.”
- **1976:** *Lilith* magazine is founded.
- **1997:** the first full Lilith Fair tour takes place.
- **Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries:** feminist spirituality, neopaganism, Satanism, Luciferianism, demonolatry, astrology, fiction, games, and online culture generate divergent devotional and symbolic Liliths.
- **Present:** Lilith remains both a feared demon in some religious discourse and a chosen emblem of autonomy in feminist and occult communities. Neither use is a transparent survival of one ancient cult.

- Judith Plaskow, “The Coming of Lilith” (1972).

### Source-critical conclusions

The record supports a historical sequence from Mesopotamian demon classes, to Isaiah’s isolated wilderness being, to Second Temple and rabbinic demonology, to late-antique domestic magic, to Ben Sira’s first-wife legend, to kabbalistic cosmology, and finally to modern feminist, artistic, occult, and astrological reinvention. It does not support a single unchanged goddess, a continuous temple cult, an ancient Gnostic Lilith, an original biblical first wife, or secure identification of the Queen of the Night plaque.
