# Abaddon: Research Dossier

**Class:** **place/abstraction** in the Hebrew Bible and early Jewish texts; **named angel and king of abyssal locusts** in Revelation; **angel of death/archangel** as *Abbaton* in a regional Coptic Christian tradition; **underworld regions in the plural** in Mandaean literature; **demon or infernal prince** in later Christian demonology and modern occultism. No early source calls Abaddon a god.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The word Hebrew אֲבַדּוֹן (*ʾăḇaddôn*, “destruction, ruin; place of destruction”) belongs to the Hebrew Wisdom corpus, but those books cannot be dated precisely enough to identify which occurrence was written first. The earliest securely datable surviving physical witness containing the term is the Hebrew *Hodayot* or Thanksgiving Hymns manuscript 1QHᵃ from Qumran, copied approximately 30 BCE–68 CE, with radiocarbon ranges extending approximately 21 BCE–61 CE. There Abaddon remains a place: “the torrents of Belial break into Abaddon.” The earliest securely datable personification as an individual is Revelation 9:11, normally dated about 90–100 CE, where Ἀβαδδών is “the angel of the abyss” and king of the locusts.

**Evidentiary labels used below:** **[Early attested]**, **[Later canonical]**, **[Regional variant]**, **[Learned systematization]**, **[Syncretistic identification]**, **[Medieval/early-modern accretion]**, **[Folklore]**, **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**, and **[Modern invention]**.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Hebrew

**[Early attested]** אֲבַדּוֹן, vocalized *ʾăḇaddôn* in the Masoretic tradition, derives from the Semitic root אבד, *ʾ-b-d*, “to perish, become lost,” and in causative stems “destroy.” The nominal formation is commonly understood as an abstract or intensive noun: “destruction,” “ruin,” or concretely “the place of destruction.” The Hebrew Bible uses it six times: Job 26:6; 28:22; 31:12; Psalm 88:12 in the Masoretic numbering (88:11 in many English Bibles); Proverbs 15:11; and 27:20. The consonantal reading in Proverbs 27:20 has a textual peculiarity: the written form is commonly represented as אֲבַדּוֹה/*ʾăbaddōh*, while the marginal Masoretic reading directs *ʾăbaddôn*. None of these passages unambiguously names an angel.

The meanings vary by context:

- Job 26:6: Abaddon parallels Sheol and is uncovered before God.
- Job 28:22: “Abaddon and Death” speak metaphorically about Wisdom.
- Job 31:12: adulterous desire is compared to a fire consuming “as far as Abaddon.”
- Psalm 88:12: Abaddon parallels the grave as a place where God’s faithfulness is not proclaimed.
- Proverbs 15:11: Sheol and Abaddon lie open before YHWH.
- Proverbs 27:20: Sheol and Abaddon are insatiable.

The *Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible* therefore judges “place of destruction” suitable throughout the Hebrew Bible. It allows that Job 28:22 and Proverbs 27:20 use poetic personification, but warns that this is not yet evidence for an independently conceived demon.

### Greek

**[Early translation]** The Septuagint usually renders the Hebrew with ἀπώλεια, *apōleia*, “destruction, perdition,” confirming that its translators understood a noun or realm rather than an angelic proper name.

**[Later canonical]** Revelation 9:11 gives both:

- Ἀβαδδών, *Abaddōn*: a Greek transcription of the Hebrew;
- Ἀπολλύων, *Apollyōn*: the substantivized present participle of ἀπόλλυμι, “destroy,” hence “the destroying one” or “Destroyer.”

*Apollyōn* is not the ordinary noun corresponding to Hebrew *ʾăḇaddôn*—that would be *apōleia*. The author deliberately chose an agent noun. The proximity of *Apollyōn* to Ἀπόλλων, *Apollōn*, “Apollo,” has produced a substantial interpretive debate.

### Latin and vernacular forms

**[Later canonical transmission]** The Vulgate transcribes *Abaddon* and *Apollyon* and adds the explanatory Latin *Exterminans*, “Destroyer.” This is a gloss, not a third name in the earliest Greek text. Medieval and modern forms include *Abadon*, *Abbadon*, *Abaddón*, *Abadón*, *Apollyon*, *Apollion*, and French *Apollyon* or *Apollin*.

### Coptic

**[Regional variant]** Sahidic Coptic Ⲁⲃⲃⲁⲧⲱⲛ, *Abbaton*, appears in the *Investiture/Enthronement of Abbaton*. There he is not the locust-king of Revelation in narrative function but the angel of death, originally named Muriel. The phonetic and semantic connection to Abaddon is explicit in scholarship, but the Coptic story is a major independent elaboration rather than a recoverable part of Revelation’s original narrative.

Documented Coptic titles include:

- “Abbaton, the angel of death”;
- “king of all humanity” or ruler over living and dead;
- “archangel Abbaton”;
- in a Tebtunis inscription, “Lord Abbaton, the angel of death, who respects not persons.”

### Mandaic

**[Regional variant]** Classical Mandaic *abdunia/ʿbdunia* denotes “places of desolation” or “Abaddons,” in the plural, within the World of Darkness. This is not securely the personal angel of Revelation. It preserves the older spatial sense and pluralizes it into infernal regions. Mandaean texts are composite: elements of their liturgical and mythological traditions may reach the third century CE, but extant *Ginza* manuscripts are much later and individual passages cannot automatically be dated to the earliest layer.

### Claimed equations

- **Abaddon = Satan:** **[Later interpretation]**, not stated in Revelation 9:11. Revelation names Satan explicitly elsewhere as the dragon and ancient serpent, making automatic identity unsafe.
- **Abaddon = Jesus or Michael:** **[Modern or regional theological interpretation]**. Some interpreters reason that the locusts act only within divinely imposed limits and that Revelation 20 gives a loyal angel the key to the abyss. The text never names that angel Abaddon. The Coptic tradition sometimes assimilated Abbaton’s function to Michael, but this is regional and late.
- **Abaddon = Asmodeus:** **[Discredited identification]**. Older reference works report the equation but acknowledge that it was never demonstrated.
- **Apollyon = Apollo:** **[Possible literary wordplay]**, not an ontological identification. The names sound alike; Apollo could bring plague, and Roman emperors cultivated Apollo imagery. Whether Revelation intends a specific anti-imperial pun remains disputed.
- **Abaddon = Azrael:** **[Late comparative conflation]**. Islamic scripture calls the relevant figure simply *malak al-mawt*, “angel of death.” Neither the Qur’an nor sound hadith names Abaddon; even “Azrael” is extra-Qur’anic.
- **Abaddon = Hades, Pluto, Thanatos, or a pre-Christian “destroyer god”:** **[Modern syncretistic invention unless explicitly attributed to a modern practitioner]**. Similar functions do not establish historical identity.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Hebrew Bible

**[Early attested]** The six biblical occurrences form the oldest literary stratum. Their composition dates are debated. Proverbs contains older collections but reached its present form through extended editing, generally completed in the first millennium BCE; Job is commonly placed somewhere between the sixth and fourth centuries BCE, though proposals are wider; Psalm 88 is likewise difficult to date. Consequently, “Job is the first appearance” is a canonical-order statement, not a secure chronological conclusion.

The texts’ shared semantic field is underworld, death, loss, and insatiability. They do not supply a body, genealogy, throne, cult, servants, or proper-name formula. Job 28:22 lets “Death and Abaddon” speak, but this is poetic prosopopoeia in a wisdom poem.

The Septuagint’s ἀπώλεια is important negative evidence: its Jewish translators did not preserve *Abaddon* as the name of an angel in these verses.

### Dead Sea Scrolls

**[Early attested]** In 1QHᵃ 11:32 and parallel *Hodayot* fragments, the “torrents of Belial break into Abaddon,” while the deeps roar. The passage describes eschatological cosmic destruction. Abaddon is a destination or abyssal region penetrated by Belial’s torrents, not Belial’s personal ally. Elsewhere in the *Hodayot*, Sheol, the Pit, death, and Abaddon overlap as spatial metaphors for mortal peril and the underworld.

The principal scroll is leather, discovered in Qumran Cave 1 in 1947. Paleographic estimates place its script broadly in the late first century BCE or early first century CE; published radiocarbon results are compatible with that range. This distinction—date of manuscript versus date of poetic composition—is essential.

### Second Temple Jewish corpus audit

**[Absence finding]**

- **1 Enoch:** no named Abaddon angel in the principal Ethiopic, Aramaic, or Greek textual tradition. Its fallen Watchers, destroying angels, pits, and abyssal prisons furnish a relevant apocalyptic environment, but equating any of them with Abaddon is reconstruction.
- **Jubilees:** no named Abaddon. Mastema and subordinate spirits are separate figures.
- **Tobit:** no Abaddon. Asmodeus and Raphael belong to another narrative.
- **Dead Sea sectarian texts:** *Abaddon* occurs as a destructive place; no secure personal angel Abaddon has been established.
- **Pseudo-Philo’s Biblical Antiquities:** uses destruction/Abaddon spatially, not as Revelation’s named locust-king.

### Revelation 9:1–12

**[Later canonical elaboration; earliest secure personal being]** After the fifth trumpet, a star fallen from heaven receives the key to the shaft of the abyss. Smoke rises like that of a furnace; from it emerge locust-like beings empowered like scorpions. They may not damage vegetation, contrary to normal locust behavior, but may torment for five months those without God’s seal. They cannot kill. Their appearance combines war horses, crown-like objects, human faces, women’s hair, lions’ teeth, iron breastplates, thunderous wings, and scorpion tails. Verse 11 says:

> They have as king over them the angel of the abyss; his name in Hebrew is Abaddon, and in Greek he is called Apollyon.

This verse does **not** say that Abaddon is the fallen star of verse 1, that he opens the pit, that he is Satan, or that he rebels against God. Those are interpretations.

The majority dating places Revelation’s final form near the end of Domitian’s reign, about 95 CE, largely on early reception and the book’s Roman imperial setting; a Neronian date before 70 CE remains a minority position. Surviving early textual witnesses include papyrus fragments from the third century; the earliest substantially complete manuscripts are fourth-century codices such as Sinaiticus. Codex Vaticanus lacks Revelation.

### Jewish rabbinic literature

**[Learned systematization]** Babylonian Talmud, *Eruvin* 19a, transmits a saying attributed to Rabbi Joshua ben Levi, a third-century Palestinian amora: Gehenna has seven names—Sheol, Abaddon, Pit of Destruction, Tumultuous Pit, Miry Clay, Shadow of Death, and Netherworld. In the Talmudic wording these are seven **names**, not necessarily seven physically separate chambers.

**[Medieval accretion]** *Midrash Konen* and related cosmographies turn such names into differentiated departments of hell; Abaddon becomes the second compartment. Later summaries often back-project this architecture into “the Talmud,” but that overstates *Eruvin* 19a.

**[Absence finding]** The Hekhalot corpus, *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh*, and the Zohar do not supply a stable, narratively developed angel Abaddon comparable to Revelation or Coptic Abbaton. They contain underworld terminology, angels of punishment, and citations of biblical verses using *avadon*. Modern angel dictionaries frequently combine these separate data into one biography.

### Early and medieval Christian theology

**[Absence finding]** Pseudo-Dionysius’ *Celestial Hierarchy* and Aquinas’ angelology do not assign Abaddon a place in the standard nine orders. Likewise, Dante’s *Comedy* does not contain an Abaddon character. Claims that “Dante’s Abaddon” is the jailer of Tartarus derive from modern derivative fiction or fan wikis.

**[Later interpretation]** Christian commentators variously treated Revelation’s figure as:

- Satan or a satanic prince;
- Antichrist;
- a subordinate demon;
- a morally neutral or loyal executor of divine judgment;
- occasionally Christ or Michael.

None is forced by the Greek text. Its strongest explicit facts are office, kingship over the locusts, abyssal association, and destructive name.

### Coptic apocrypha

**[Regional variant]** The *Investiture of Abbaton*, also called *Enthronement* or *Encomium on Abbaton*, survives in one Sahidic manuscript, British Library Or. 7025, copied at or for the Monastery of Saint Mercurius at Edfu. Its colophon supports a copying date around 983 CE, though cataloguing ranges can extend into the eleventh century. The work is pseudonymously attributed to Timothy I, archbishop of Alexandria in 381–384. Modern work treats it as a late-antique or early-medieval Coptic composition, often placed around the seventh–eighth centuries, not an authentic fourth-century homily.

Its frame claims that Timothy discovered an apostolic book in Jerusalem. That “discovery” is part of the work’s pseudepigraphic authentication, not an independently verified manuscript history.

A related Coptic *Book/Apocalypse of the Resurrection of Jesus Christ by Bartholomew* places Abbaton at Christ’s tomb. It should not be confused with the Greek *Questions of Bartholomew* without qualification.

### Gnostic and Mandaean audit

**[Absence finding]** Abaddon is not a named figure in the surviving Nag Hammadi tractates indexed in the modern Coptic Gnostic corpus, nor in the principal systems reported by Irenaeus, Hippolytus, or Epiphanius. Their abysses, archons, angels, and personified powers are not automatically Abaddon.

**[Regional Gnostic-related tradition]** The Mandaean *Ginza Rba* uses plural “Abaddons” for regions of desolation in the World of Darkness. Mandaeism is a living Gnostic religion, but this usage is spatial and collective, not evidence that Sethian or Valentinian Christians worshipped a god Abaddon.

### Islamic corpus audit

**[Absence finding]** Abaddon is absent from the Qur’an, canonical hadith, and al-Ṭabarī’s standard exegetical angelology. Qur’an 32:11 calls the taker of souls *malak al-mawt*. Later tradition calls him ʿIzrāʾīl/Azrael, although that personal name is itself absent from the Qur’an and sound prophetic hadith. Similarities between Azrael legends and Coptic Abbaton stories are subjects of comparative transmission study, not proof of identity.

### Grimoires

**[Absence finding]** Abaddon is not one of the seventy-two spirits of the *Ars Goetia/Lemegeton* and does not have a classical Goetic seal, enn, office, or appearance. He is likewise not a principal demon of the medieval Abramelin operation. Modern books that advertise a “Goetic Abaddon,” ancient sigil, enn, planet, metal, tarot card, or summoning rite are creating new ritual systems.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Hebrew and Qumran layers

**[Early attested]** Abaddon’s “domain” is initially semantic and topographic: destruction, mortality, the grave, and the deepest region of the dead. Its defining relations are parallelism with Sheol, Death, grave, Pit, and Abyss. It is exposed to God and never independent of divine knowledge.

No securely ancient color, number, weekday, planet, animal, weapon, sex, or cult symbol belongs to this layer.

### Revelation

**[Later canonical]** Abaddon is king of the abyssal locusts. His powers are not separately itemized; the locusts’ mandate defines his sphere. Their power is constrained:

- they torment but do not kill;
- their term is five months;
- they cannot harm those bearing God’s seal;
- their authority is “given,” a recurring Revelation formula for delegated and limited power.

The text never describes Abaddon’s own anatomy. Illustrations that make him a giant locust, dragon, armored angel, or humanoid demon visualize his army or combine him with later imagery.

Possible symbols, confined to Revelation’s episode, are the abyss, smoke/furnace, locusts, scorpions, crowns, armor, war horses, and the number five. None is evidence for a cultic color or planetary correspondence.

### Coptic Abbaton

**[Regional variant]** The *Investiture* describes a terrifying angel of death whose form the righteous fear, although his function is ultimately subordinated to God and protective toward devotees. The strongest material depiction comes from a now-fragmentary wall painting at Tebtunis in the Fayum: a gigantic armored winged being with claw-like feet, identified by inscription as Lord Abbaton, angel of death. The archaeological documentation survives in A. S. Hunt’s photographs and Crum Notebook 67 at the Griffith Institute; modern publication dates the Christian paintings to the medieval Coptic occupation of the site, not apostolic times.

### Bunyan

**[Early-modern literary reinvention]** In *The Pilgrim’s Progress* (1678), Apollyon is a composite monster covered with fish scales, with dragon wings, bear’s feet, a lion’s mouth, and fire and smoke issuing from him. He throws flaming darts and claims to be prince and god of the City of Destruction. This enormously influential image is Bunyan’s allegorical synthesis of biblical monster imagery, not an ancient portrait of Revelation’s angel.

### Modern occult correspondences

**[Modern invention unless otherwise dated]** Black or red coloring, the Sun or “Black Sun,” Mars, Venus, north/south/east, Sunday, January, July, tarot Judgment, a “sacred enn,” and newly drawn sigils vary from author to author. Their contradictions demonstrate that they are new ritual correspondences, not recovered ancient tradition.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

**[Early attested]** Hebrew and Revelation sources provide no parentage, birth, consort, siblings, children, or genealogy.

**[Regional Coptic variant]** Abbaton begins as the angel Muriel. God appoints him after his service in Adam’s creation; this is transformation of office and name, not biological birth. He has no consort or offspring.

**[Modern invention]** Claims that Abaddon is Apollo’s form, Satan’s son, an infernal spouse, a “twin” of Apollyon, or a primordial god lack ancient support. Abaddon and Apollyon are two language labels for one figure in Revelation, not siblings.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **YHWH/God — Abaddon lies open before God; nothing conceals it.** Job 26:6 and Proverbs 15:11; first-millennium BCE Wisdom texts. **[Early attested; place, not person]**
- **Death (*Māwet*) — Death and Abaddon jointly report only hearsay of Wisdom.** Job 28:22. This is poetic personification, not evidence of kinship. **[Early attested]**
- **Belial — Belial’s torrents break into Abaddon during cosmic judgment.** 1QHᵃ 11:29–32 and parallels, manuscript late first century BCE/early first century CE. Abaddon is a place. **[Early attested, Second Temple]**
- **God — Abaddon’s locusts are subject to imposed limits protecting the sealed and restricting torment to five months.** Revelation 9:4–5, 10–11, c. 90–100 CE. God is not named in verse 11 but is the issuing authority in the vision. **[Later canonical]**
- **The abyssal locusts — Abaddon is their king.** Revelation 9:11. They are a collective supernatural army, not individual named beings. **[Later canonical]**
- **The fifth trumpet angel — Abaddon appears within the woe initiated by this angel’s trumpet.** Revelation 9:1–12. The text does not say they meet or are identical. **[Later canonical; indirect co-occurrence]**
- **The fallen star/key-holder — the star opens the abyss from which Abaddon’s subjects emerge.** Revelation 9:1–3. Identity with Abaddon is unestablished. **[Later canonical; indirect interaction]**
- **God — God commands Muriel to obtain clay for Adam, later appoints and enthrones him as Abbaton, angel of death.** *Investiture of Abbaton*, probably seventh–eighth-century composition, extant manuscript c. 983. **[Regional Coptic variant]**
- **Jesus Christ — Jesus narrates Abbaton’s origin to John and explains how the righteous may face him.** *Investiture of Abbaton*. **[Regional Coptic variant]**
- **Holy Spirit — participates with God and Christ in the Coptic work’s creation account in which Muriel serves.** *Investiture of Abbaton*. No separate combat or kinship is narrated. **[Regional Coptic variant]**
- **Michael — casts down the First-Creature/Lucifer after other angels cannot approach him; Abbaton belongs to the same narrative and is later functionally compared with Michael in Coptic angelology.** *Investiture of Abbaton* and later Coptic interpretation. A direct Abbaton–Michael combat is not reported. **[Regional variant; later syncretistic identification]**
- **Gabriel — appears among the angels in the *Investiture* narrative; no securely distinct Abbaton–Gabriel episode is preserved.** **[Regional variant; co-occurrence only]**
- **First-Creature/Satan/Lucifer — Satan’s rebellion, temptation of Adam and Eve, and later threat to humanity occasion Muriel’s appointment as Abbaton to protect the righteous and punish the wicked.** *Investiture of Abbaton*. **[Regional Coptic variant]**
- **Jesus Christ — Abbaton is present at Christ’s tomb or Resurrection in the Coptic Bartholomew tradition.** Medieval Coptic *Book of the Resurrection*. **[Regional variant]**
- **Christian — Apollyon claims Christian as his escaped subject, duels him, wounds him, is wounded by Christian’s sword, and flees.** Bunyan, *The Pilgrim’s Progress*, 1678. Christian is a human allegorical protagonist, not a deity. **[Early-modern literary accretion]**
- **Adonai/Holy Guardian Angel — Crowley’s *Liber LXV* contrasts an aspirant’s union with Adonai against a tentacled horror “more hideous than the shells of Abaddon.”** Written 1907, first published 1909. Abaddon does not appear personally. **[Modern Thelemic reception]**

**Absence finding:** No securely documented interaction joins Abaddon personally to Enoch, the Watchers, Mastema, Raphael, Asmodeus, Sophia, Yaldabaoth, Barbelo, Azrael, Muhammad, Apollo, Dante’s Lucifer, or the seventy-two Goetic spirits.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The place of destruction

**[Early attested]** This is not one continuous myth but a poetic complex. Abaddon is where creation’s visible order fails: the dead are hidden there, yet it is visible to God; Wisdom is not possessed there; destructive fire reaches it; praise is absent there; and it is never satisfied. The Hebrew texts do not explain its creation or ruler.

### The fifth trumpet and locust king

**[Later canonical]** Revelation’s episode transforms the place-name into its angelic sovereign. The locusts invert Exodus and Joel imagery: they do not consume crops but attack humans; unlike Proverbs 30:27’s ordinary locusts, which have no king, these have one. Their grotesque warlike form makes them an apocalyptic army rather than zoological insects.

Interpretive families include:

- **Demonic reading:** the abyss is a prison of hostile spirits; their hideous forms and torment make their king evil.
- **Divine-agent reading:** the restrictions, passive “was given,” and immunity of God’s sealed people suggest an executor of judgment under divine command.
- **Satan identification:** possible but unstated; Revelation elsewhere distinguishes Satan by other names.
- **Imperial parody:** *Apollyon* may pun on Apollo and indirectly attack Nero, Domitian, or imperial Apollo ideology.
- **Historical armies:** Parthian or Roman military imagery has been proposed, especially for the cavalry of the sixth trumpet, but the locusts transcend any literal army.
- **Futurist technological readings:** identifications with helicopters, drones, biological weapons, or modern nations are modern inventions unsupported by the first-century text.

### Muriel’s investiture as Abbaton

**[Regional Coptic variant]** The fullest independent Abaddon myth is Coptic:

1. God decides to make Adam and asks angels to bring clay.
2. Six refuse because the clay foresees the sin and suffering that humanity will cause.
3. Muriel obeys and obtains the material.
4. Adam is created; the First-Creature refuses the command to bow and is cast down.
5. Satan tempts Adam and Eve and threatens humanity.
6. In response to Muriel’s obedience and the needs of divine judgment, God transforms or appoints him as Abbaton, angel of death.
7. His appearance is terrifying, but he protects or treats mercifully those who honor him.
8. At the last judgment he brings souls to the Valley of Jehoshaphat.
9. The work closes by encouraging commemoration of Abbaton on his feast.

This cycle combines Genesis exegesis, the noncanonical “angels bow to Adam” story, angel-of-death lore, saintly patronage, and institutional promotion of a feast. Its claim to Timothy’s authorship is pseudepigraphic.

A medieval Arabic Coptic polemic preserved in *Kitāb al-Īḍāḥ* attacks the story: it argues that God would not need an angel to fetch clay, that prophetic clay would appear wiser than God, and that the narrative compromises divine omniscience. This is rare direct evidence that the cult and legend were contested inside Coptic Christianity rather than universally accepted.

### Bunyan’s combat

**[Early-modern accretion]** In the Valley of Humiliation, Apollyon confronts Christian, asserts sovereignty over the City of Destruction, accuses him of desertion, and demands his return. Christian refuses because he has pledged himself to the King of Princes. Apollyon blocks the entire road, launches a flaming dart, and wounds Christian’s head, hand, and foot. After losing his sword, Christian recovers it and cites Micah 7:8 and Romans 8:37. His thrust forces Apollyon to spread his dragon wings and flee. The episode turned a one-verse apocalyptic ruler into a personal tempter and martial Devil for generations of English-speaking readers.

### Thelemic imagery

**[Modern occult reception]** Crowley’s *Liber LXV* does not enthrone Abaddon as a Thelemic god. “The shells of Abaddon” appear as an image of dispersion and horror opposed to the aspirant’s angelic union. In Crowley’s larger system, Choronzon—not Abaddon—is normally the named dweller or demon of the Abyss. Modern occult writers often merge the two because both are connected with “the Abyss,” but that is secondary syncretism.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Ancient Israel and Second Temple Judaism

**[Absence finding]** There is no inscription, temple, priesthood, sacrifice, theophoric name, festival, votive deposit, or image showing worship of Abaddon in ancient Israel or Judah. The Hebrew term belongs to mortuary and wisdom vocabulary. Qumran usage likewise gives no cult.

Consequently there is no documented human sacrifice, temple prostitution, cult violence, or political priesthood of Abaddon. Claims of an ancient “Abaddon cult” are modern invention.

### Revelation communities

**[Absence finding]** Revelation addresses seven Christian assemblies in Roman Asia; it does not tell them to invoke, propitiate, or worship Abaddon. The figure belongs to visionary judgment imagery. No first-century shrine or liturgy is known.

### Coptic Egypt

**[Regional cult]** Here the evidence changes. The *Investiture* presupposes a feast on 13 Hathor, conventionally corresponding to 9 or 10 November depending on calendar conversion, and urges hearers to honor Abbaton. The manuscript donor hoped Abbaton would show him kindness before God, indicating devotional rather than purely literary use.

The Tebtunis mural provides material evidence of veneration or at least church representation. The inscribed, armored, winged figure stood in a punishment-of-sinners program. This is the only securely identified monumental image presently central to the discussion; it does not prove a large empire-wide cult.

The *Kitāb al-Īḍāḥ* refutation indicates active ecclesiastical opposition. Scholarship concludes that Abbaton devotion was geographically limited within Egypt and eventually disappeared, rather than remaining a normal element of universal Coptic liturgy.

### Medieval and early-modern magic

**[Learned systematization]** Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia* III.18, published in its complete form in 1533, arranged nine orders of evil spirits. The seventh “mansion” contains Furies—powers of discord, war, devastation, and wasting—whose prince is Apollyon/Abaddon. This is a Renaissance Christian-Qabalistic classification built from Revelation, not a transmitted ancient cult.

**[Absence finding]** Neither the *Lemegeton* nor Abramelin provides a traditional Abaddon evocation. The absence matters because contemporary commercial demonolatry often advertises “ancient” Abaddon rites without manuscript provenance.

### Modern worship

**[Modern invention and revival]** Small demonolatry, Left-Hand Path, and eclectic occult communities now invoke Abaddon as destroyer, warrior, initiator, purifier, or power of transformative dissolution. Published “enns,” sigils, offerings, planetary correspondences, and personal revelations are usually traceable only to late twentieth- or twenty-first-century authors and web communities. Their scale cannot presently be established reliably, and they have no demonstrated institutional continuity with Coptic Abbaton devotion.

## 7. Historical evolution

### First millennium BCE: destruction and underworld

**[Early attested]** *ʾĂḇaddôn* develops from “perishing/destruction” into a specialized poetic term for the realm of death. Parallelism, rather than theology of a named spirit, defines it.

### Late Second Temple period

**[Early attested]** Qumran hymns retain the spatial meaning but place Abaddon within an intensified eschatological landscape of Belial, fiery torrents, abyssal deeps, and cosmic war. This environment helps explain how later apocalyptic literature could personify underworld structures without proving a direct textual chain.

### Late first century CE

**[Later canonical transformation]** Revelation converts the Hebrew term into a proper angelic name and pairs it with the Greek agent noun *Apollyon*. The being rules the fifth-trumpet locusts but remains textually ambiguous as to moral allegiance.

### Third–sixth centuries

**[Learned Jewish systematization]** Rabbinic tradition counts Abaddon among the names of Gehenna. Christian commentary increasingly treats Apollyon as a devil, Satan, or destroying angel. Standard systematic angelologies, however, do not assign him a fixed choir.

### Seventh–eleventh centuries: Coptic expansion

**[Regional variant]** Egyptian Christians produce an elaborate angel-of-death biography: Muriel becomes Abbaton, receives an office, terrifying form, feast, and protective devotional role. A mural and manuscript donation show that the tradition entered material and practical religion. Arabic Coptic polemic subsequently contests it.

### Medieval Western Europe

**[Medieval accretion]** Old French epic uses *Apollin* among imaginary Muslim idols. The *Song of Roland*, composed in surviving form around the late eleventh century, makes its fictional Muslims invoke Mahound, Apollin, and Termagant. This documents Latin-Christian fantasy about Islam, not Islamic belief or an Abaddon cult.

Dante does not give Abaddon a character. Milton later uses “Abaddon” as a place: in *Paradise Regained* IV.264 the term denotes the abyss, closer to Hebrew usage than to a demon biography.

### Renaissance demonology

**[Learned systematization]** Agrippa makes Abaddon prince of the seventh infernal order, the Furies. This is the decisive step toward later ranked demonology. It is absent from the biblical text and from the medieval Goetic catalogues.

### Seventeenth century

**[Early-modern literary reinvention]** Bunyan’s Apollyon becomes an individualized, speaking, dragon-like adversary. The popularity of *The Pilgrim’s Progress* makes this image more influential in Anglophone culture than most theological commentaries.

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

**[Modern reception]** Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* labels Abaddon “the destroyer” and chief of demons of the seventh hierarchy, largely perpetuating the Agrippan scheme. Gustav Davidson’s *Dictionary of Angels* gathers biblical, rabbinic, Coptic, and occult notices but often presents heterogeneous materials as a single angel’s dossier; it is useful as an index, not a substitute for primary sources.

Crowley’s *Liber LXV*, received in 1907 and published in 1909, uses “shells of Abaddon” symbolically. The principal Thelemic abyss-demon remains Choronzon. Later occult synthesis increasingly detaches Abaddon from Revelation and treats him as an invocable autonomous power.

### Late twentieth century to present

**[Modern reinvention]** Fiction routinely uses “Abaddon” for a world-destroyer, demon general, fallen angel, spacecraft, planet, monster, corporation, or apocalyptic project. These uses reinforce “destroyer” while abandoning the locust episode’s constraints.

No evidence establishes continuous worship from antiquity to modern demonolatry.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature

- **John Bunyan, *The Pilgrim’s Progress* (1678):** foundational monster-duel image.
- **John Milton, *Paradise Regained* (1671):** Abaddon as abyss/place, not a speaking fallen angel.
- **Friedrich Gottlieb Klopstock, *Der Messias* (1748–1773):** creates *Abbadona*, a remorseful angel drawn into Satan’s revolt. This is a new literary character.
- **Robert Browning, “Childe Roland to the Dark Tower Came” (1855):** invokes Apollyon proverbially as a formidable adversary.
- **Louisa May Alcott, *Little Women* (1868–1869):** “Apollyon” becomes a metaphor for Jo’s anger through Bunyan.
- **Modern novels and horror:** often use the name as shorthand for annihilation without biblical characterization.

### Occult and esoteric traditions

- **Agrippa, 1533:** prince of infernal Furies.
- **Francis Barrett, *The Magus* (1801):** reproduces and expands Renaissance correspondences.
- **Collin de Plancy, 1818 onward:** dictionary demon.
- **Crowley, 1907/1909:** shells or dispersive horrors of Abaddon; not a central Thelemic deity.
- **Late modern Qliphothic systems:** assign Abaddon to inconsistent shells, planets, tunnels, or gates. These are system-specific innovations.
- **Contemporary demonolatry:** offers channeled sigils, enns, mantras, feast days, gender presentations, and rites. These are explicitly modern revelations when honestly labeled; calling them Sumerian, Goetic, or ancient Hebrew rites is unsupported.

### Film, television, comics, and games

Materially influential examples include:

- **Warhammer 40,000:** Abaddon the Despoiler, a human-derived Chaos warlord and leader of the Black Legion. The borrowing is nominal and thematic, not an adaptation of Revelation.
- **Darksiders (2010):** Abaddon is an angel whose fall drives the game’s apocalyptic plot; scholarly reception analysis notes its deliberate moral ambiguity.
- **Supernatural:** Abaddon is reimagined as a female Knight of Hell. The gender, rank, and biography are modern invention.
- **Shin Megami Tensei and related games:** often represent Abaddon as a devouring demon.
- **The Binding of Isaac:** uses Apollyon as a playable apocalyptic figure.
- **SCP terminology:** “Apollyon” becomes a classification for effectively uncontainable world-ending anomalies.
- **Games Workshop and other franchises:** emphasize military command, aligning loosely with Revelation’s locust kingship and Bunyan’s martial monster.

These works have done more than modern churches to standardize the visual image of Abaddon as a horned or armored demon, although Revelation never describes his body.

### Political and extremist appropriation

**[Modern invention]** “Abaddon” occasionally appears in extremist aliases, metal aesthetics, apocalyptic propaganda, and conspiracy theories. The popular claim that Revelation 9:11 cryptically predicts or names the attacks of 11 September 2001 is retrospective numerology: chapter-and-verse numbering was added many centuries after Revelation’s composition. No documentary evidence connects the perpetrators’ date selection to Abaddon.

No stable nationalist movement or state cult centered on Abaddon is documented.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Place versus person

The central scholarly consensus is a historical sequence:

1. Hebrew common/poetic noun;
2. specialized underworld place;
3. occasional poetic personification;
4. proper angelic name in Revelation;
5. divergent Jewish, Coptic, demonological, and literary elaborations.

The principal error in popular accounts is to read Revelation’s angel backward into Job and then construct a timeless biography.

### Abaddon and Death

Job 28:22 grammatically personifies both Death and Abaddon as speakers. Scholars differ on how much mythic residue lies behind such poetry. A cautious formulation is that biblical poets could animate cosmic abstractions without requiring an organized cult or independently worshipped deity.

### Apollyon and Apollo

Arguments favoring a pun cite:

- the unusual choice *Apollyōn* rather than *apōleia*;
- phonetic similarity to Apollo;
- Apollo’s plague-bearing aspect;
- imperial Apollo imagery;
- ancient wordplay connecting Apollo’s name with destruction.

Objections include:

- *Apollyōn* is a natural Greek “destroyer” formation;
- proposed locust symbolism for Apollo is not sufficient by itself;
- connecting the pun specifically to Domitian depends partly on the debated date and reconstruction of imperial claims.

The strongest conclusion is “plausible secondary wordplay,” not “Abaddon is Apollo.”

### Satan, loyal angel, or neither?

Revelation does not supply an adjective such as holy, fallen, evil, or satanic. The locusts’ emergence from the abyss suggests demonic forces; their obedience to limits suggests divine sovereignty. Calling Abaddon “Satan” or “a good angel” goes beyond the verse. Literary ambiguity may be intentional.

### Coptic date and origin

The *Investiture*’s attribution to Timothy I was once taken more literally. Manuscript archaeology and comparison with Coptic pseudo-apostolic memoirs favor a later composition. Proposed dependencies include Jewish Adam legends, Christian apocrypha, and Islamicate angel-of-death traditions. Direction of borrowing is unsettled: similar Islamic and Coptic stories may share earlier materials rather than one copying the other directly.

### Mandaean “Abaddons”

The Mandaic plural supports the semantic survival of “places of destruction,” but dating individual *Ginza* strata is difficult. It should not be treated as proof of a single pan-Gnostic demon. Nag Hammadi “Gnosticism,” Mandaeism, and medieval Coptic Christianity are distinct corpora.

### Reference works

- The *Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible* is strongest on Hebrew semantics and warns against premature demonization.
- Bamberger, Kelly, and Jeffrey Burton Russell are useful for the broader formation of fallen-angel and Devil traditions, but Abaddon has only a small independent footprint.
- Gustav Davidson’s *Dictionary of Angels* is influential but compiles sources of very different dates and authority, sometimes without enough separation.
- Pearson and Rudolph illuminate Gnostic classifications but do not establish Abaddon as a Nag Hammadi archon.
- The *Encyclopaedia of Islam* and Islamic angelology support the negative finding: Abaddon is not an Islamic scriptural angel.

### Primary evidence and repositories

- 1QHᵃ: Shrine of the Book/Israel Antiquities Authority; critical editions by Sukenik, García Martínez and Tigchelaar, Schuller and Newsom.
- Revelation manuscripts: major biblical collections and critical editions such as Nestle-Aland and ECM materials.
- British Library Or. 7025: unique Sahidic witness to the *Investiture of Abbaton*.
- Tebtunis mural documentation: Griffith Institute, Crum Notebook 67 and expedition photographs.
- Mandaean manuscripts: collections in Paris, Leiden, Oxford, the British Library, and living Mandaean priestly collections; editions by Petermann, Lidzbarski, and modern translators.

### Discredited or unsupported claims

- Abaddon was an ancient Hebrew god: unsupported.
- Abaddon received human sacrifice: unsupported.
- Abaddon is named in 1 Enoch, Jubilees, Tobit, Qur’an, or canonical hadith: false.
- Abaddon is one of the seventy-two Goetic spirits: false.
- Dante created Abaddon as Lucifer’s jailer: false.
- Crowley made Abaddon the guardian of the Thelemic Abyss: misleading; that role belongs chiefly to Choronzon.
- Ancient sources give an Abaddon sigil, enn, tarot card, planet, or ritual incense: unsupported.
- Abaddon and Apollyon are separate twins or male/female counterparts: modern invention.
- Revelation predicts helicopters or drones under Abaddon: modern speculative interpretation.
