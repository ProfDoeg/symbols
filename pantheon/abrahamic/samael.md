# Samael: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the earliest secure sources, Samael is a named evil angel or angelic adversary; in rabbinic and medieval Jewish literature he becomes an accuser, seducer, chief of accusing angels, angel of death, patron of Rome/Edom, and eventually a demonic prince. In several early Christian and Gnostic texts, *Samael* is a name of Satan/Belial or of the ignorant creator-archon Yaldabaoth. In medieval Kabbalah he is the principal masculine power of the “other side,” paired with Lilith. Western ceremonial magic variously classifies Samael, Zamael, or Camael as a martial angel, spirit, or demonic counterpart.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Probably the composite *Ascension of Isaiah*, especially its *Martyrdom of Isaiah* stratum, conventionally dated between the late first and early second centuries CE, where **Sammael** is associated or identified with **Beliar/Belial** and the murder of Isaiah. A claim that Samael already occurs in *1 Enoch* 6 is text-critically insecure: the earliest Aramaic and principal Ethiopic witnesses have other Watcher names, while some Greek variants resembling *Sammane* or *Semiel* generated the disputed identification. The approximately contemporary *Greek Apocalypse of Baruch* (*3 Baruch* 4) securely names **Samael/Sammael** as the angel who planted the forbidden vine. The figure is absent by name from the Hebrew Bible, Septuagint, New Testament, Qumran manuscripts presently published, Jubilees, Tobit, and the Qur’an.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Principal forms

- **Hebrew:** סַמָּאֵל or סמאל, usually vocalized *Sammāʾēl* or *Samael*. Medieval manuscripts often omit vowels, so the exact historical pronunciation cannot always be recovered.
- **Aramaic:** סמאל and related spellings.
- **Greek:** Σαμμαήλ and variants such as *Sammaēl*, *Sammane*, and *Semiel*. Some forms in angel lists may represent different names corrupted during transmission.
- **Coptic:** forms transliterating Greek *Samaēl* in the Nag Hammadi codices.
- **Ethiopic:** *Sammael* in the transmitted *Ascension of Isaiah*; the name attributed to *1 Enoch* depends on disputed readings.
- **Latin and European vernaculars:** *Samael, Sammael, Samaël, Samiel, Samil,* and *Smil*.
- **Occult variants:** *Zamael* and sometimes *Camael/Khamael*. These must not automatically be treated as orthographic forms of the same being: Renaissance and Golden Dawn tables sometimes distinguish the good planetary angel, evil planetary spirit, and Kabbalistic demon.

### Etymology

Two old explanations compete:

1. **“Blind god” or “god of the blind.”** The Gnostic texts explicitly gloss *Samael* this way, drawing on Aramaic/Hebrew סמי or סומא, “blind,” plus אל, “god.” In the *Hypostasis of the Archons*, a voice answers the creator’s arrogant claim to sole divinity: “You are mistaken, Samael”—then explains the name as “god of the blind.” In the *Apocryphon of John*, Samael is the third name of the ignorant archon Yaldabaoth. Birger Pearson argues that this Gnostic etymology probably adapted an already Jewish characterization of the devil or angel of death as blind. **[securely attested late-antique interpretation; proposed Jewish prehistory is modern scholarly reconstruction]** [Pearson’s discussion](https://gnosis.study/library/%D0%93%D0%BD%D0%BE%D0%B7%D0%B8%D1%81/%2B%20%D0%9D%D0%B5%20%D1%82%D0%BE%D0%BB%D1%8C%D0%BA%D0%BE%20%D0%BE%20%D0%B3%D0%BD%D0%BE%D0%B7%D0%B8%D1%81%D0%B5/ENG/Pearson%20B.A.%20-%20Gnosticism%2C%20Judaism%2C%20and%20Egyptian%20Christianity.pdf)

2. **“Poison/venom of God.”** Jewish interpreters related *sam* or *samma*, “poison/drug,” to *El*, “God,” especially because the angel of death kills with a drop of poison. This is a traditional wordplay, not a demonstrated historical derivation. **[later rabbinic/medieval etymology]** The early *Jewish Encyclopedia* records both it and the much less accepted suggestion of derivation from a Syrian deity called Shemal. [Jewish Encyclopedia](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/13055-samael)

No etymology securely proves the name’s historical origin. “Severity of God,” “left hand of God,” and “he who blinds God” are common modern occult expansions but lack early linguistic support. **[modern invention or speculative reinterpretation]**

### Titles and classifications

- **Sammael-Malkira/Malchira:** In the *Ascension of Isaiah*, connected with Beliar and the agents who accuse and kill Isaiah. *Malkira* has been explained as Hebrew *melek raʿ*, “evil king,” or *malʾakh raʿ*, “evil angel,” but the transmitted personal names are unstable. **[early Jewish-Christian apocalypse]**
- **Chief of the accusing angels / chief of the satans:** Especially *Deuteronomy Rabbah* 11 and *Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer*. **[late-antique or early-medieval rabbinic elaboration]**
- **Angel of death:** Explicit in Targum Pseudo-Jonathan Genesis 3:6 and medieval Jewish sources; his sword or poisonous drop reflects broader traditions about the angel of death. **[early-medieval elaboration]**
- **Satan, the evil inclination, and the angel of death:** Later Jewish texts frequently merge these functions, although earlier rabbinic statements about “Satan, the evil inclination, and the angel of death” do not invariably name that being Samael. Identification must therefore be established passage by passage.
- **Prince of Rome/Edom/Esau:** Rabbinic and Kabbalistic historical allegory made Samael the celestial patron of Israel’s imperial enemy. **[late-antique/medieval political theology]**
- **Yaldabaoth/Saklas/Samael:** Three names of the chief archon in the *Apocryphon of John*. Here Samael is not merely a subordinate demon but the ignorant creator and ruler of the material cosmos. **[second-century Gnostic systematization]**
- **Male power of the left side / husband of Lilith:** First systematically attested in Isaac ben Jacob ha-Kohen’s thirteenth-century *Treatise on the Left Emanation*, then developed in the Zohar and later Kabbalah. **[medieval learned systematization]**
- **Planetary angel or spirit of Mars:** Medieval and Renaissance magical tables variously assign Samael/Zamael to Mars, Tuesday, iron, warfare, severity, and sometimes the west wind. The Golden Dawn normally uses **Zamael** for the angel of Mars while also retaining **Samael** in Qliphothic demonology. **[medieval–modern occult systematization]**

“Samael” is therefore not a timeless alternative name for “the Devil.” It is a shifting name attached to several related but nonidentical constructions.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The disputed Enochic evidence

The *Book of Watchers* in *1 Enoch* 1–36 was composed in stages, probably from the third or early second century BCE; Aramaic fragments survive at Qumran. Chapter 6 lists leaders of the Watchers. Standard modern reconstructions include **Shemihazah/Semyaza** and **Samsapeel**, not Samael. Some later Greek witnesses contain forms such as Σαμμανή and Σεμιέλ, and older editions occasionally reconstructed a bracketed Samael. Matthew Black’s critical apparatus demonstrates that the witnesses vary substantially.

Accordingly:

- “Samael is named in the original Book of Watchers” is **not secure**.
- The similar Watcher names may have contributed to later association, but identity with Samael is **modern scholarly conjecture**, not a demonstrable fact.
- Neither the published Aramaic Qumran fragments nor the principal Ethiopic text makes Samael a securely identifiable Watcher.

The Online Critical Pseudepigrapha provides the appropriate manuscript-oriented framework for examining Enochic witnesses. [Online Critical Pseudepigrapha](https://pseudepigrapha.org/)

### *Ascension of Isaiah*

The work is composite: the martyrdom narrative, visionary ascent, and Christian apocalypse probably reached their surviving form between roughly 70 and 175 CE. The complete text survives principally in Ethiopic, with Greek, Latin, Slavonic, and Coptic fragments or versions.

In chapters 1–5, **Sammael Malkira** is associated with **Beliar**, who controls the apostate king Manasseh and inspires Isaiah’s accusation and execution by sawing. The textual relationship among Sammael, Malkira, Belkira, and Beliar is difficult: in some readings Sammael is a supernatural ruler while Malkira/Belkira is the human false prophet; in others the names blur. The secure conclusion is that the narrative places Sammael within Beliar’s persecuting power, not that every manuscript establishes a clean one-to-one identity. **[earliest secure named appearance; early Jewish-Christian apocalypse]** [Older translation and discussion](https://www.ccel.org/ccel/deane/pseudepig.vii.ii.html)

### *Greek Apocalypse of Baruch* (*3 Baruch*)

Probably composed after 70 CE and before the third century; it survives in divergent Greek and Slavonic recensions. In Greek 4:8, the forbidden tree is the vine planted by the angel **Samael**; God cursed him and his planting, and the devil used the vine to deceive Adam. The longer Slavonic account distributes the planting of Paradise among Michael, Gabriel, Uriel, Raphael, and **Satanael**, showing active textual variation between Samael and Satanael. **[secure early attestation; Greek/Slavonic regional-textual variants]** [Text](https://www.pseudepigrapha.com/pseudepigrapha/3Baruch.html) [Critical discussion](https://www.marquette.edu/maqom/apoc123.pdf)

### Gnostic corpus

The original Greek compositions are normally dated to the second or third centuries; surviving Nag Hammadi copies are fourth-century Coptic codices discovered near Nag Hammadi in 1945.

- **Apocryphon of John:** preserved in four copies—Nag Hammadi Codices II, III, IV and Berlin Codex 8502—in long and short recensions. Yaldabaoth is born from Sophia’s unauthorized activity, generates archons, boasts that he alone is God, and bears three names: **Yaldabaoth, Saklas, and Samael**. Samael expresses his ignorance or blindness. **[second-century Gnostic learned systematization]** [Waldstein–Wisse-based long recension](https://www.gnosis.org/naghamm/apocjn-long.html)
- **Hypostasis of the Archons** (*Reality/Nature of the Rulers*), NHC II,4: the arrogant chief ruler claims sole godhood; an incorruptible voice rebukes him as Samael, “god of the blind.” Pistis Sophia precipitates him into chaos; later his son Sabaoth repents and is exalted. **[late-second/third-century Gnostic narrative]** [Layton translation](https://www.gnosis.org/naghamm/hypostas.html) [Claremont Coptic Encyclopedia](https://ccdl.claremont.edu/digital/collection/cce/id/1001/)
- **On the Origin of the World**, NHC II,5 and parallel witnesses: develops the same archontic creation cycle and glosses Samael through blindness. **[late-antique Gnostic variant]**

Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 1.30, and later heresiologists report related “Ophite” systems involving Ialdabaoth, Sophia, and planetary rulers. They are independent evidence that such systems circulated by the later second century, but their polemical taxonomies cannot simply be treated as the names by which the groups described themselves. Samael’s explicit equation with Yaldabaoth is clearest in the Nag Hammadi texts, not in every heresiological report.

### Rabbinic, midrashic, and targumic corpus

- **Genesis Rabbah 56:11**—compiled in Roman Palestine, conventionally fifth century: Samael tries first to dissuade Abraham and then Isaac from the binding. **[rabbinic elaboration]** [Text](https://www.sefaria.org/Bereshit_Rabbah.56.11?lang=en)
- **Babylonian Talmud, Sotah 10b**—redacted roughly fifth–sixth centuries: Samael removes Tamar’s identifying pledges to prevent Judah’s confession and the ancestry of David; Gabriel restores them. **[rabbinic elaboration]** [Text](https://www.sefaria.org/Sotah.10b?with=Genesis)
- **Deuteronomy Rabbah 11**—a composite medieval midrash, often dated later than the classical Palestinian rabbah collections: Samael is head of the accusing angels and seeks Moses’ soul but cannot take it; God ultimately receives Moses’ soul. **[medieval midrashic elaboration]** [Text](https://www.sefaria.org/Devarim_Rabbah.11?lang=en)
- **Targum Pseudo-Jonathan Genesis 3:6:** Eve sees “Sammael, the angel of death,” and fears him. Its final form is probably post-Islamic, with proposed dates ranging from late antiquity to the twelfth century; it is not a Second Temple witness. **[early-medieval targumic elaboration]** [Text](https://www.sefaria.org/Targum_Jonathan_on_Genesis.3?lang=en)
- **Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer 13–14**, probably eighth–ninth century: Samael, a great heavenly prince with twelve wings, envies Adam, recruits his host, selects the serpent as the most cunning animal, and rides or possesses it to tempt Eve. God casts Samael and his company from heaven and punishes the serpent. **[early-medieval narrative systematization]** [Text](https://www.sefaria.org/Pirkei_DeRabbi_Eliezer.13.7)
- Later chapters and cognate midrash associate him with Abraham, Sarah, Jacob, Moses, and Israel’s accusers.

### Hekhalot, magical, and Kabbalistic texts

The Hekhalot corpus is manuscript-fluid and was assembled from late antiquity through the early Middle Ages. Samael appears in portions of the later magical and cosmological tradition, but there is no single “Hekhalot doctrine” of him. He is not a central throne-attendant comparable to Metatron.

**Sefer ha-Razim**, *Harba de-Moshe*, amulets, and later **Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh** transmit angelic lists and adjurations. *Sefer Raziel* in its medieval form associates Samael with calendrical and planetary powers, including Tuesday/Mars in some recensions. These books combine older magical materials with medieval compilation; a printed *Sefer Raziel* must not be backdated wholesale to late antiquity.

**Treatise on the Left Emanation**, Isaac ben Jacob ha-Kohen, Castile, approximately 1260s: first surviving systematic account of Samael and Lilith as a paired demonic power, generated beneath the Throne and structurally opposed to Adam and Eve. It also distinguishes Lilith the Matron from a lesser Lilith and embeds the pair in a hierarchy of evil emanations. **[medieval Kabbalistic systematization]** [Joseph Dan’s study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0364009400000052/type/journal_article)

**Zohar**, principally late-thirteenth-century Castile, with later strata: expands Samael’s place in the *sitra aḥra*, the “other side.” He rides the serpent; he and Lilith function as a male-female pair; he is linked with idolatry, impurity, murder, sexual transgression, Esau, and the left side. Some passages distinguish several entities called Samael rather than one perfectly unified personality. **[medieval learned systematization]** [Zohar on Samael and the serpent](https://www.sefaria.org/Zohar%2C_Bereshit.44.454?with=Commentary+ConnectionsList) [Zohar on separation from Lilith](https://www.sefaria.org/Zohar%2C_Mishpatim.3.16-18)

### Negative findings in the named corpora

- **Hebrew Bible and Septuagint:** no Samael. The adversary in Job 1–2 and Zechariah 3 is *ha-satan*, “the accuser/adversary”; the destroyer in Exodus and Samuel is unnamed. Later identification with Samael is reception history.
- **Qumran/Dead Sea Scrolls:** published texts name Belial, Mastema, Melchiresha, fallen Watchers, destroying angels, and spirits, but no securely identified Samael.
- **Jubilees:** its principal adversarial angel is Mastema, not Samael.
- **Tobit:** its demon is Asmodeus; Raphael binds him. Samael is absent.
- **New Testament:** Satan, the devil, Beelzebul, Belial/Beliar, the tempter, and the ruler of this world appear, but Samael is not named.
- **Pseudo-Dionysius and Aquinas:** their systematic angelologies do not give Samael a canonical place. Aquinas discusses Satan and fallen angels, not a distinct Samael doctrine.
- **Qur’an, canonical hadith, and al-Tabari’s principal creation narratives:** Iblis, al-Shaytan, jinn, and the angel of death occur, but Samael is not a Qur’anic or canonical Islamic name. Later Arabic occult manuscripts may transliterate Jewish angel names; this does not make Samael an Islamic scriptural figure.
- **Lemegeton:** Samael is not one of the seventy-two Goetic spirits. Editions may contain adjacent planetary material, but claims that he is a Goetic king are modern conflation.
- **Book of Abramelin:** Mathers-derived English occult literature sometimes assimilates its demon-princes to broader Kabbalistic lists; Samael is not a stable principal across the older German manuscript tradition.

## 3. Domains, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and actions

Samael’s domains accrued rather than emerging simultaneously:

- accusation before the heavenly court;
- execution of divine death sentences;
- temptation and sexual seduction;
- obstruction of Israel’s patriarchs and messianic ancestry;
- hostility to Moses and Michael;
- guardianship of Rome/Edom/Esau;
- command of destructive or accusing angels;
- the left side, impurity, and demonic masculinity in Kabbalah;
- ignorant cosmic rule in Gnostic creation myths;
- Mars, iron, severity, bloodshed, and Tuesday in magical correspondences.

The dual role of adversary and divine servant is important. In many Jewish narratives Samael is wicked, jealous, or rebellious, yet he still operates within a cosmos ruled by God and may require divine authorization. The Gnostic Samael differs radically: as Yaldabaoth, he falsely believes himself supreme and creates or rules the deficient material order.

### Form

No stable ancient iconography identifies Samael. There are no securely excavated temples, cult statues, coins, or inscribed portraits.

Textual forms include:

- a winged heavenly prince with twelve wings in *Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer*;
- rider or animating intelligence of the serpent;
- sword-bearing angel of death in later Jewish imagination;
- lion-faced or leonine-serpentine chief archon in Gnostic cosmology, although particular physical descriptions more often attach explicitly to Yaldabaoth;
- paired androgynous or sexually conjoined power with Lilith in Kabbalistic speculation.

Medieval Jewish manuscripts generally avoid producing a fixed portrait of Samael. Later Christian occult diagrams supplied sigils and planetary characters, but these encode names and correspondences rather than preserving ancient likenesses. Modern horned, black-winged, red-armored, or goat-headed images derive mainly from generic Devil iconography, fantasy art, games, and demonolatry. **[modern invention]**

### Symbols and correspondences

- **Serpent:** secure in *Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer* and the Zohar, but Samael is variously the serpent’s rider, possessing spirit, ideal form, or the serpent itself.
- **Sword and poisonous drop:** inherited from angel-of-death traditions; the precise equipment varies.
- **Vine/wine:** *3 Baruch* identifies him as planter of the forbidden vine.
- **Mars/Tuesday/iron/red:** medieval and Renaissance astrological magic; not biblical or early rabbinic attributes.
- **Left/north:** Kabbalistic symbolic geography.
- **Dragon or lion-serpent:** chiefly Gnostic and modern composite iconography.
- Claims that Samael has a universally sacred number, flower, incense, seal, holiday, or color are generally products of modern correspondence tables.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Origin and hierarchy

No early source supplies Samael with biological parents. Jewish sources normally assume that God created him as an angel. The Gnostic version gives a specific emanational genealogy: **Sophia** produces Yaldabaoth without her consort; Yaldabaoth/Samael then generates subordinate archons. Kabbalistic texts describe Samael and Lilith as emanated powers beneath the Throne or from the left side, not as children born in an ordinary divine family.

### Consort and offspring

The earliest systematic pairing of Samael with **Lilith** is Isaac ha-Kohen’s thirteenth-century *Treatise on the Left Emanation*, not the Bible, Talmud, or *Alphabet of Ben Sira*. Isaac depicts them as a demonic counterpart to Adam and Eve. The Zohar and later Kabbalah elaborate the pairing and sometimes distinguish the “Great Lilith,” wife of Samael, from a lesser Lilith associated with Asmodeus. Stories making Cain the literal son of Samael and Lilith combine separate traditions: some targumic/Kabbalistic texts say Samael impregnated **Eve**, whereas Lilith’s marriage to Samael belongs to a different medieval construction.

### Every documented named divine interaction

The following are discrete textual relationships, not assertions that all sources describe one identical being.

- **God / the Holy One:** Samael’s planting is cursed; God casts him down or sends him as death’s agent. *3 Baruch* 4; *Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer* 13; *Deuteronomy Rabbah* 11. **[early apocalypse; later rabbinic elaboration]**
- **Beliar/Belial:** Samael-Malkira operates within or is partially identified with Beliar’s power in Isaiah’s martyrdom. *Ascension of Isaiah* 1–5, late first–second century. **[secure early but textually unstable identification]**
- **Yaldabaoth:** Samael is one of Yaldabaoth’s names, not a separate interlocutor. *Apocryphon of John*, second century. **[Gnostic identification]**
- **Saklas:** another name of the same ignorant chief archon alongside Yaldabaoth and Samael. *Apocryphon of John*. **[Gnostic identification]**
- **Sophia:** Sophia produces Yaldabaoth/Samael; in related texts Pistis Sophia exposes or casts down the blind ruler. *Apocryphon of John*; *Hypostasis of the Archons*. **[Gnostic genealogy and punishment]**
- **Pistis Sophia:** instigates the chief archon’s descent into chaos after his boast. *Hypostasis of the Archons*. **[Gnostic interaction]**
- **Sabaoth:** son or subordinate archon of Yaldabaoth/Samael who repents and is exalted; the father envies him. *Hypostasis of the Archons* and *On the Origin of the World*. **[Gnostic narrative]**
- **Norea:** resists the archons of the chief ruler and appeals to the higher realm. *Hypostasis of the Archons*. The encounter is with the archontic collective; a face-to-face meeting explicitly naming Samael is not always present. **[Gnostic interaction, qualified]**
- **Michael:** opposed champion of Israel; disputes with Samael concerning Moses and later martyr traditions. *Deuteronomy Rabbah* 11 and medieval midrash. **[medieval rabbinic elaboration]**
- **Gabriel:** restores Tamar’s pledges after Samael removes them. Babylonian Talmud, *Sotah* 10b. **[rabbinic canonical elaboration]**
- **The serpent:** Samael chooses, rides, or inhabits it to tempt Eve. *Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer* 13; Zoharic passages. **[early-medieval and Kabbalistic elaboration]**
- **Lilith:** spouse, sexual counterpart, and co-ruler of the demonic left. *Treatise on the Left Emanation* and Zohar, thirteenth century. **[medieval Kabbalistic systematization]**
- **Asmodeus:** later Kabbalistic schemes sometimes pair a lesser Lilith with Asmodeus while the elder Lilith belongs to Samael. This is a structural relation, not a well-developed meeting or combat. **[late medieval systematization]**
- **Metatron:** later Kabbalistic and magical diagrams contrast the holy and demonic hierarchies headed by Metatron and Samael. A single early narrative encounter is not securely attested. **[medieval schematic opposition]**
- **Mastema:** modern reference works sometimes equate him with Samael because both accuse and destroy, but *Jubilees* does not name Samael or stage an interaction. **[modern comparative identification; no documented interaction]**
- **Azazel and Shemihazah:** later demonologies place them near Samael, but *1 Enoch* does not securely make Samael their fellow Watcher. **[disputed/late association]**
- **Satan:** many medieval texts treat Samael as Satan’s proper name or as chief of the satans; earlier sources can distinguish title and individual. **[progressive syncretistic identification]**
- **Angel of Death:** frequently identified with Samael, especially Targum Pseudo-Jonathan and medieval texts. Where “the angel of death” is unnamed, identity cannot be presumed. **[later identification]**
- **Camael/Khamael/Zamael:** occult tables conflate or distinguish these spellings. They are documentary variants within Western magic, not a securely attested ancient meeting between separate angels. **[Renaissance/modern systematization]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Isaiah’s martyrdom

Under Manasseh, Isaiah withdraws from Jerusalem. Beliar’s influence controls the apostate regime; a hostile accuser denounces Isaiah, who is executed with a wooden saw. Sammael-Malkira is named within this persecuting complex. The episode adapts traditions of prophetic persecution and perhaps underlies Hebrews 11:37, though Hebrews does not name Isaiah or Samael.

The textual identities are unstable: “Sammael,” “Malkira,” and “Belkira” may denote the supernatural instigator, the human accuser, or names corrupted into one another. **Early core:** Isaiah’s martyrdom under Manasseh. **Later elaboration:** the surviving Christian ascent apocalypse. **Unestablished:** a precise original genealogy linking all named antagonists.

### The forbidden vine

In Greek *3 Baruch*, Samael planted the vine in Paradise; God cursed both angel and planting, and the devil used it to deceive Adam. The Flood displaced but did not annihilate the vine, and Noah deliberated whether to replant it. The Greek text then contains overtly Christian interpretation of wine as capable of becoming Christ’s blood; this may be secondary.

The Slavonic recension names **Satanael** and assigns different trees to Michael, Gabriel, Uriel, and Raphael. Thus “Samael planted the Tree of Knowledge” is too broad: the source identifies the tree as a vine, and its recension history varies. **[secure early apocalypse with Christian and Slavonic elaborations]**

### Samael and the serpent

In *Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer*, Samael envies Adam’s honor, descends with his host, and finds the serpent uniquely suited for evil. He mounts or animates it “like a rider on a horse,” and through it engineers Eve’s temptation. God casts Samael down and curses the serpent.

This is not stated in Genesis. Genesis speaks only of the serpent; Revelation’s “ancient serpent” is linked with the Devil and Satan, but not with Samael by name. The explicit rider story is early medieval. Zoharic Kabbalah transforms it into metaphysics: Samael is the serpent’s ideal or spiritual power and Lilith its female counterpart.

### The Binding of Isaac

In *Genesis Rabbah* 56:11, Samael attempts to stop Abraham by accusing him of folly and future bloodguilt. When Abraham refuses, Samael approaches Isaac, telling him that Sarah’s possessions will pass to Ishmael. Isaac remains willing but is emotionally disturbed. Later retellings add that Samael tells Sarah that Isaac has been or nearly has been killed, causing her death. The temptation scene is classical midrash; Samael’s direct responsibility for Sarah’s death is more elaborated in later collections.

### Tamar and Judah

In *Sotah* 10b, Samael removes the pledges with which Tamar can prove Judah’s paternity. His objective is to prevent Judah’s confession and thereby destroy Tamar and obstruct the Davidic lineage. Gabriel restores the objects. The story makes cosmic opposition part of the genealogy of David. **[rabbinic canonical elaboration]**

### Jacob’s adversary

Later midrash identifies Jacob’s nocturnal wrestling opponent or Esau’s guardian angel with Samael. Genesis 32 does not name the being. Because Rome was identified with Edom and Edom with Esau, Samael became Rome’s celestial prince. The myth therefore acquired a historical-political function: Jacob/Israel’s struggle with Samael symbolized Jewish endurance under Rome and Christian imperial power. **[late-antique/medieval political exegesis]**

### Death of Moses

In *Deuteronomy Rabbah* 11, Samael, head of the accusing angels, eagerly awaits Moses’ death and confronts Michael. God orders him to take Moses’ soul, but Moses’ radiance and knowledge of the divine name terrify him. Moses strikes or drives him away in expanded versions. God finally takes Moses’ soul with a kiss. Samael is powerful over ordinary mortality but incapable of seizing Moses without divine authorization. **[medieval midrashic cycle]**

### Gnostic creation and fall

Sophia generates an incomplete being without her consort. The offspring, Yaldabaoth, is ignorant of the higher divine world. He creates subordinate rulers and boasts, echoing biblical declarations of divine uniqueness, that no god exists besides him. A higher voice reveals his error and calls him **Samael**, “blind god.” The archons form Adam but cannot animate him fully until power taken from the higher realm enters him. Eve, Norea, the serpent, and the revealer participate differently across recensions in humanity’s acquisition of knowledge and escape from archontic domination.

Here Samael is not Judaism’s loyal but severe angel of death. He is the deluded demiurge. The shared name enabled polemical reuse of Jewish traditions, but it does not establish that Jewish communities worshipped or taught a Gnostic demiurge called Samael.

### Samael and Lilith

Isaac ha-Kohen’s *Treatise on the Left Emanation* presents Samael and Lilith as an androgynous or paired birth from the left/demonic emanation, corresponding negatively to Adam and Eve. Their union produces or sustains demonic powers. Later Kabbalah discusses restraining their sexual conjunction, since their unity intensifies the “other side.” This myth is thirteenth-century, although it combines older materials concerning Lilith, Samael, the serpent, and demonic sexuality.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

There is no evidence for an ancient public **cult of Samael**:

- no securely identified temple;
- no priesthood;
- no festival calendar;
- no civic sacrifices;
- no theophoric naming tradition demonstrably invoking him as a patron;
- no securely identified ancient cult image.

The documentary record instead belongs to scripture interpretation, storytelling, mystical speculation, and magic.

### Apotropaic and magical use

Jewish amulets and magical texts may name Samael to repel, bind, redirect, or acknowledge destructive power. Naming a demon on an amulet is not evidence of worship; it is normally coercive or prophylactic. Medieval planetary magic assigns him times, winds, metals, or planets, allowing practitioners to address, avert, or manipulate a named power.

Kabbalistic prayer and ritual generally seek protection from Samael and Lilith or separation of their powers. Some Yom Kippur interpretations connect the scapegoat sent to Azazel with appeasing or silencing the accusing power identified as Samael, but the Torah names Azazel, not Samael. Calling the biblical rite “worship of Samael” is therefore inaccurate.

Claims of ancient human sacrifice, temple prostitution, or institutional “Samael worship” have no archaeological or early textual support. Modern ritual sacrifice allegations attached to Satanism belong largely to polemic and the twentieth-century “Satanic panic,” not to the ancient record.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the name: biblical and Second Temple antecedents

The Hebrew Bible supplied several roles later gathered under Samael: prosecutor (*ha-satan*), destroyer, hostile national angel, serpent, and angelic opponent. Second Temple works developed Watchers, Azazel, Mastema, Belial, and elaborate angelic hierarchies. None can simply be renamed Samael, but they furnished the conceptual environment from which his later profile emerged.

### Late first to third centuries

The *Ascension of Isaiah* securely places Sammael in a Beliaric complex of persecution. *3 Baruch* makes him planter of the forbidden vine. Gnostic writers appropriate the name for the blind demiurge. These uses already differ: persecuting Satanic power, fallen or offending angel, and cosmic creator-archon.

### Rabbinic late antiquity

Palestinian and Babylonian rabbis inserted Samael into biblical narrative gaps: the Akedah, Tamar, Jacob, and heavenly accusation. He increasingly gathers the functions of Satan and the angel of death, but no single rabbinic creed defines him.

### Early Middle Ages

Targum Pseudo-Jonathan and *Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer* create the most influential Edenic image: Samael as angel of death, commander of a host, and rider of the serpent. Midrashic Moses cycles make him head of accusing angels. Political exegesis connects him with Edom/Rome.

### Thirteenth-century Kabbalah

Isaac ha-Kohen gives evil an emanational hierarchy and pairs Samael with Lilith. The Zohar embeds them in the *sitra aḥra*, integrating biblical exegesis, sexuality, cosmic duality, and ritual symbolism. This is the decisive transformation from mobile legendary adversary to metaphysical prince of the demonic left.

### Renaissance and early-modern Christian Cabala

Christian Hebraists and magicians extracted angelic names from Jewish sources and reorganized them into planetary and hierarchical tables. Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia* appeared in complete form in 1533. Its manuscript and printed traditions contain **Zamael/Samael** among planetary correspondences, especially Mars, but distinguish an intelligence “for good” from an evil spirit in some tables. [Agrippa text](https://www2.esotericarchives.com/agrippa/op2.htm)

Knorr von Rosenroth’s *Kabbala Denudata* (1677–1684) transmitted Zoharic and Lurianic ideas into Christian esotericism. This process detached Samael from Jewish narrative contexts and helped create the correspondence-table being familiar in later occultism.

### Nineteenth-century occult revival and Golden Dawn

Éliphas Lévi, Papus, and other occultists popularized a synthetic “Kabbalah” that combined Christian Cabala, Tarot, ceremonial magic, and romanticized demonology. The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, founded in Britain in 1888, codified planetary and Qliphothic hierarchies.

Golden Dawn material commonly gives:

- **Elohim Gibor** as divine name of Mars/Geburah;
- **Khamael/Camael or Zamael** as archangel or angel;
- **Graphiel** as intelligence;
- **Bartzabel** as spirit;
- **Samael** in the adverse or Qliphothic hierarchy.

Israel Regardie’s published compilation states: “the hierarchy for Mars is … Elohim Gibor,” with **Zamael**, Graphiel, and Bartzabel. This is a modern initiatory table, not an ancient Jewish angelic canon. [Regardie compilation](https://scriptaetveritas.com.br/wp-content/uploads/2016/04/ISRAEL_REGARDIE_COMPLETE_GOLDEN_DAWN_SYSTEM_OF_MAGIC.pdf)

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Samael entered occult lodges, Luciferianism, some forms of demonolatry, Left-Hand-Path religion, chaos magic, fantasy literature, heavy metal, comics, film, and games. There is no reliable global membership count for devotees specifically worshipping Samael. Online devotional systems often combine the Gnostic demiurge, Kabbalistic husband of Lilith, Mars angel, biblical serpent, and modern rebel-deity into one figure. That composite is **modern syncretism**, not a recovered ancient cult.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature

Dante does not make Samael a character in the *Commedia*. Milton’s *Paradise Lost* names Satan, Lucifer, Beelzebub, Belial, Moloch, and other fallen spirits, but not Samael as Satan’s personal name. Claims that Dante or Milton canonized Samael are therefore false. Their immense influence on the sympathetic, tragic, or politically rebellious Satan nevertheless shaped the visual and emotional vocabulary later attached to Samael.

Nineteenth-century occultists and twentieth-century fantasy writers increasingly treated Samael as a personal proper name for Satan. Gustav Davidson’s *Dictionary of Angels* assembled many such references but often juxtaposed sources from widely different centuries without adequate transmission history; it remains useful as an index, not as a critical edition.

### Modern occultism

Golden Dawn descendants, Thelemites, and ceremonial magicians use Samael/Zamael in inconsistent ways:

- angel of Mars;
- adverse spirit of Mars;
- archdemon of Geburah;
- Qliphothic ruler;
- Satanic or Luciferian mask;
- consort of Lilith;
- blind demiurge.

No single usage is normative. The common diagram “Samael, Qliphah opposite Hod” belongs to modern Qliphothic tables derived through Christian Kabbalah and occult orders, not directly to the Zohar.

Modern demonolatry sometimes presents invocations, enn-syllables, sigils, sacred colors, and feast days for Samael as though inherited from antiquity. Where no manuscript lineage is supplied, these should be labeled **modern ritual inventions**.

### Religious Satanism and political appropriation

Public organized Satanism is chiefly a modern phenomenon. Anton LaVey founded the Church of Satan in 1966; later organizations developed theistic, atheistic, esoteric, and activist forms. Samael appears in some independent theistic Satanist and Luciferian currents but is not central to all of them. Scholarly histories caution against projecting modern Satanist institutions back into antiquity. [Oxford history of modern Satanism](https://academic.oup.com/book/11834/chapter-abstract/160931441)

Some extremist occult milieus combine Satanic names, including Samael, with racialist or neo-Nazi ideologies. This is documented as a modern appropriation, not an implication of rabbinic, Gnostic, or Kabbalistic sources. Conversely, sensational claims of a continuous murderous “cult of Samael” are unsupported.

### Popular culture

Materially influential examples include:

- the name **Sammael** for the multiplying hell-hound creature in Guillermo del Toro’s *Hellboy* (2004);
- Samael as a demon lord in the *Darksiders* games;
- characters or entities named Samael in *Silent Hill*, *Shin Megami Tensei*, *Supernatural*, comics, manga, and role-playing games;
- the Swiss metal band **Samael**, formed in 1987.

These works popularized Samael as a visually embodied infernal sovereign. Their horns, armor, monstrous anatomy, sigils, and personal histories are modern fictional iconography.

## 9. Historiography and contested material

### Origin dispute

Older reference works sometimes call Samael a Watcher from *1 Enoch*. Modern textual criticism makes that claim unsafe. It depends on unstable Greek spellings and confusion among Semyaza, Samsapeel, and related names. The safest first secure attestations remain the *Ascension of Isaiah* and *3 Baruch*.

### Jewish–Gnostic relationship

Pearson argues that the Gnostic demiurge draws substantially on the Jewish devil and angel-of-death figure called Samael. Other scholars emphasize the creative inversion of biblical creation theology and warn against positing a single Jewish myth behind all Sethian texts. The name and “blind” gloss demonstrate contact with Semitic traditions; they do not by themselves prove direct descent from a fully developed rabbinic Samael.

### Satan, Samael, and the angel of death

Popular summaries say Judaism teaches that Satan’s “real name” is Samael. The evidence is diachronic:

- Hebrew Bible: no Samael;
- early apocalyptic sources: Sammael is associated with Beliar or a fallen angel;
- rabbinic narratives: named adversary;
- early-medieval texts: chief of satans and angel of death;
- Kabbalah: cosmic demonic prince.

Identity is therefore a historical convergence, not an original scriptural fact.

### Lilith

The statement “Lilith has always been Samael’s wife” is demonstrably false. Earlier Lilith traditions concern a night demon, child-killing demon, or Adam’s rebellious first wife. The surviving systematic marriage begins with Isaac ha-Kohen in thirteenth-century Castile. Joseph Dan’s study remains fundamental for locating that innovation historically. [Dan, “Samael, Lilith, and the Concept of Evil in Early Kabbalah”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/product/identifier/S0364009400000052/type/journal_article)

### Islamic identification

Internet sources sometimes supply Arabic spellings such as سمائل or سمسمائيل and call Samael an Islamic angel of death. The Qur’an calls the death-agent *malak al-mawt* and names Iblis as Adam’s tempter; neither is called Samael. Arabic occult manuscripts can borrow Jewish angelic names, but canonical Islamic identification is unestablished. Equating Samael with Iblis, Azrael, or an angel in al-Tabari without a specific manuscript citation is **modern syncretism**.

### Human sacrifice, violence, and suppression

No ancient cult of Samael is documented, so there is no evidence of a Samaelite institution conducting human sacrifice or temple prostitution. The violence in the sources is literary and theological: Isaiah’s execution, attempts against patriarchs, the taking of souls, accusations against Israel, and cosmic punishment. Jewish communities did not suppress a competing “Samael religion”; they transmitted him as an adversarial figure. Christian censorship and persecution affected Kabbalistic and Jewish books generally, but no documented campaign uniquely targeted a Samael cult.

### Primary evidence and preservation

The evidence survives principally in:

- Qumran fragments and later Ethiopic/Greek witnesses for Enochic comparison;
- Ethiopic and fragmentary versions of the *Ascension of Isaiah*;
- Greek and Slavonic manuscripts of *3 Baruch*;
- fourth-century Coptic Nag Hammadi codices, now held in Cairo;
- rabbinic manuscripts and medieval printed editions;
- Kabbalistic manuscripts, especially Castilian materials;
- European magical manuscripts and printed occult compilations.

The 1945 Nag Hammadi discovery transformed study by replacing dependence on hostile heresiologists with substantial Coptic primary texts. Critical work by Robinson, Layton, Waldstein and Wisse, Meyer, Pearson, Rudolph, and the Coptic Gnostic Library project established the manuscript basis for the Gnostic Samael.
