# Abraxas: Research Dossier

**Class:** not one stable class. In the surviving evidence Abrasax/Abraxas is variously (1) the **great archon** ruling 365 heavens in heresiological accounts of Basilidian Christianity; (2) an **angelic or aeonic minister** in Sethian texts; (3) a **divine or daimonic ritual name** in Graeco-Egyptian magic; (4) by uncertain association, the name of the **rooster-headed, serpent-legged anguipede** on magical gems; and, much later, (5) an occult god, demon, literary symbol, or psychological image.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Irenaeus of Lyons, *Against Heresies* 1.24.7, composed in Greek about **180 CE** but preserved principally through a later Latin translation, reports that Basilidians called the chief of their 365 heavens **Abraxas**. Some magical gems may be contemporary or earlier within the second century, but most lack excavated contexts and cannot furnish a more securely dated first occurrence. The spelling most characteristic of Greek evidence is **Ἀβρασάξ / ΑΒΡΑΣΑΞ, Abrasax**; “Abraxas” reflects a transposition that became standard in Latin and modern reception.

## Evidentiary key

- **[Early attested]** second- to fourth-century textual or material evidence.
- **[Later canonical elaboration]** development within a recognizable religious textual tradition, without implying universal acceptance.
- **[Regional variant]** geographically or textually localized form.
- **[Learned systematization]** intellectual classification imposed by theologians, antiquarians, or occultists.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** explicit or contextual equation with another divine name.
- **[Medieval/early-modern accretion]** post-antique magical or antiquarian development.
- **[Folklore]** popular transmission without a secure institutional lineage.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** inference from incomplete evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** a datable new interpretation presented without ancient continuity.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### The ancient figures

“Abraxas” is not a single continuously worshipped god with one coherent biography. The surviving record preserves several overlapping uses of a powerful name.

1. **Basilidian great archon.** **[Early attested through hostile testimony]** Irenaeus says that the Basilidians posit 365 heavens and “hold that their chief is Abraxas,” because the name’s letters total 365. Hippolytus, in *Refutation of All Heresies* 7.14, calls **Abrasax** the great archon of the 365 heavens. This ruler belongs below Basilides’ ultimate, transcendent “nonexistent God” in Hippolytus’ substantially different account; therefore “the supreme God of Basilides” is an oversimplification. [Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 1.24](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0103124.htm); [Hippolytus, *Refutation* VII](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/050107.htm).

2. **Sethian angel or aeonic minister.** **[Early attested, separate textual tradition]** In the *Holy Book of the Great Invisible Spirit*, Abrasax is one of the ministers associated with the four luminaries. In the *Apocalypse of Adam*, Abrasax, Sablo, and Gamaliel descend in luminous clouds to rescue an imperishable people from fire. In *Zostrianos*, the name belongs to the wider Sethian baptismal and celestial nomenclature. This figure is not described as ruler of 365 heavens.

3. **Magical name or invoked power.** **[Early attested]** The Greek magical papyri use **Abrasax** in voces magicae, divine-name strings, protective operations, bindings, invocations, and cosmogonies. Its referent changes with the spell: a creator, an identifying name of the operator or spirit, a name written beside Helios, or one term in sequences containing Iaō, Sabaōth, Adōnai, Michael, and Egyptian gods. The corpus therefore does not support a single PGM theology.

4. **Gem-associated being.** **[Early attested image; identification disputed]** Numerous second- to fourth-century gems show an armored anthropomorphic torso, rooster’s head, and two serpents in place of legs, usually carrying a whip or scourge and shield. “Abrasax” occurs on many—but not all—such gems, while the same name appears beside other images. “Abrasax” may identify the anguipede in some specimens, but “every rooster-headed anguipede is Abraxas” and “every Abrasax gem is Gnostic” are obsolete generalizations. The Campbell Bonner material describes this as the gem type most responsible for the category “Gnostic gems,” while warning that its religious significance was overestimated. [Campbell Bonner database essay](https://cbd.mfab.hu/pandecta/1568); [Coptic Magical Papyri discussion](https://www.coptic-magic.phil.uni-wuerzburg.de/index.php/2019/01/11/religion-in-the-coptic-magical-papyri-iv-sethian-gnosticism-and-magical-texts/).

### Spellings and scripts

- **Ἀβρασάξ / ΑΒΡΑΣΑΞ — Abrasax:** the normal Greek form; seven letters.
- **Ἀβράξας / ΑΒΡΑΞΑΣ — Abraxas:** transposed Greek/Latinized variant, dominant in modern European usage.
- **Abraxae:** Latin genitive in Pseudo-Tertullian.
- **Habrasax:** variant or aspirated rendering in some transmissions of Hippolytus.
- **Abracax / Abracas:** early-modern French variants.
- Coptic manuscripts transliterate the Greek name; later Syriac magical material includes forms such as **ܐܒܪܐܟܣ**. Medieval Jewish magical texts employ Hebrew-script adaptations, but these are borrowings into magical nomenclature, not evidence that Abraxas originated in the Hebrew Bible or rabbinic angelology. [Shandruk’s survey and etymological discussion](https://knowledge.uchicago.edu/record/468/files/Shandruk_uchicago_0330D_13327.pdf).

### The number 365

**[Early attested]** In Greek isopsephy:

| Letter | Value |
|---|---:|
| Α | 1 |
| Β | 2 |
| Ρ | 100 |
| Α | 1 |
| Σ | 200 |
| Α | 1 |
| Ξ | 60 |
| **Total** | **365** |

This requires **ΑΒΡΑΣΑΞ**, not the rearranged ΑΒΡΑΞΑΣ if read in sequence, although both contain the same letters and total. Irenaeus relates 365 to the heavens; Hippolytus also connects the number to the days of the year. Epiphanius later adds comparisons with the year and purported 365 bodily members. These are learned numerical constructions, not an etymology.

### Etymology

The etymology remains **unestablished**.

- **[Early attested fact]** The name functions as a meaningful numerical name in Greek because its letters total 365.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Proposals connect it with Semitic roots, especially Hebrew/Aramaic words for “four,” a divine tetrad or the tetragrammaton; with forms such as *Abrahsiya* in later Jewish magic; or with Egyptian sacred-language traditions.
- **[Discredited or unproved older theory]** Coptic “holy name,” Hebrew *ha-berakhah* (“the blessing”), and *ab rāzā* (“father of the secret”) are ingenious but unverified.
- **[Unproved popular claim]** “Abracadabra” derives from Abraxas. The resemblance was asserted by early-modern antiquarians and Collin de Plancy, but modern etymological reference works find no documentary proof for any proposed origin of *abracadabra*. Its secure ancient appearance is in the medical poem attributed to Quintus Serenus Sammonicus, where it is written in a diminishing triangle against fever. [Etymology summary](https://www.etymonline.com/word/Abraxas).

### Epithets and titles

Secure titles are sparse:

- **Chief/ruler of the 365 heavens** — Irenaeus, *Against Heresies* 1.24.7.
- **Great archon** — Hippolytus, *Refutation* 7.14.
- **Supreme deity** — Pseudo-Tertullian’s divergent report, not safely attributable to Basilides himself.
- **Minister** associated with a luminary — *Holy Book of the Great Invisible Spirit*.
- **Eternal one / soul of darkness / Michael / Thōouth** — a deliberately syncretistic magical self-identification in PGM LXXIX; not a universal theology.
- Modern epithets such as “god above God and Devil,” “lord of the world,” or “god of opposites” derive principally from Jung and Hesse, not from Basilidian evidence.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Irenaeus

**[Earliest secure textual attestation, c. 180 CE]** *Against Heresies* 1.24 describes teachings attributed to Basilides and his followers. Its surviving formulation says that lower angels made the visible world; their chief made the Jews his people; Christ came from the unbegotten Father; and the Basilidians’ 365 heavens have Abraxas as their chief because the name totals 365.

Important limits:

- Basilides’ own *Exegetica* and gospel are lost.
- Irenaeus wrote polemic, not neutral ethnography.
- His report may combine Basilides with later Basilidians.
- He does not connect Abraxas to a rooster-headed image, temple, sacrifice, or public cult.

### Hippolytus

**[Early attested/alternative heresiological account, early third century]** *Refutation of All Heresies* 7 presents a markedly different Basilidian cosmogony: a nonexistent God, world-seed, triple sonship, great archon of the Ogdoad, and lesser archon of the Hebdomad. In this scheme Abrasax is described amid the 365 heavens and their rulers. The account may preserve material nearer Basilides’ *Exegetica*, but that dependence remains debated. It proves that the 365/Abrasax complex circulated; it does not resolve whether Basilides himself originated it.

### Pseudo-Tertullian

**[Later heresiological systematization, probably third century]** *Against All Heresies* 4 says that a supreme deity named Abraxas produces Mind (*Nous*), then Word, Providence, Power, and Wisdom; from these proceed powers, principalities, angels, 365 heavens, and the world. The Jewish God is reduced to a turbulent subordinate angel, and Christ is sent by Abraxas rather than by the maker of the world. This is a distinct recension and should not be silently combined with Hippolytus. [English text](https://ccel.org/ccel/tertullian/against_all_heresies/anf03.v.xi.i.html); [Latin text](https://www.tertullian.org/latin/adversus_omnes_haereses.htm).

### Epiphanius

**[Late-antique elaboration, c. 374–377 CE]** *Panarion* 24 largely depends on earlier anti-heretical traditions but expands the numerology and polemic. Its evidence is valuable for fourth-century reception, not independent access to every second-century practice. [Complete *Panarion* translation](https://orcuttchristian.org/Panarion%20Epiphanius%20COMPLETE.pdf).

### Nag Hammadi corpus

The codices were copied in Coptic during the fourth century and found in Upper Egypt in 1945. Their underlying Greek compositions are earlier, but exact dates remain disputed. They now belong principally to the Coptic Museum, Cairo. [Coptic Museum](https://egymonuments.gov.eg/en/subportals-group/coptic-museum/coptic-museum/manuscripts); [modern codicological overview](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/333084781B98EFDB225C0C6EC60D1F84/9781009441469int_1-22.pdf/introduction.pdf).

- **The *Apocalypse/Revelation of Adam*, NHC V,5.** Usually assigned broadly to the first through third centuries, with disagreement over Christian and Jewish layers. Abrasax, Sablo, and Gamaliel rescue the elect from eschatological fire. [Dating and textual discussion](https://www.earlyjewishwritings.com/apocadam.html).
- **The *Holy Book of the Great Invisible Spirit*, NHC III,2 and IV,2.** Often called the “Coptic Gospel of the Egyptians,” though that title can mislead. A Sethian cosmogonic and baptismal work, perhaps second or third century, preserved in two Coptic witnesses. Abrasax appears in a ministerial celestial series, not as Basilidian ruler of 365 heavens. [Textual introduction](https://www.earlychristianwritings.com/greatspirit.html).
- ** *Zostrianos*, NHC VIII,1.** A difficult Sethian ascent apocalypse, normally placed in the late second or third century; Porphyry reports that Plotinus’ circle knew a revelation of “Zostrianos.” Abrasax belongs to its dense celestial/baptismal nomenclature. [Translation](https://gnosis.org/naghamm/zostr.html).

### Greek magical papyri and related ritual texts

**[Early attested, predominantly third–fourth-century manuscripts preserving earlier material]** The PGM are handbooks from Roman Egypt, written in Greek, Demotic, and Old Coptic. Abrasax appears repeatedly, but not consistently as a narrated personality.

Notable cases include:

- **PGM V:** Abrasax occurs in voces magicae within a coercive or protective formula.
- **PGM VII:** an operator or invoked being declares “I, Abrasax,” illustrating ritual identification.
- **PGM XIII, “Eighth Book of Moses”/“Monad”:** a creator’s successive laughter produces light, waters, mind, generation, fate, time, and soul; Abrasax is identified as the creator’s name “in the language of the baboons.” From further sounds emerge Python, primal man/fear, and Iaō. This elaborate magical cosmogony is not a Basilidian scripture.
- **PGM LXXI:** instructs the engraving of an ouroboros, Selene, and Helios, placing Abrasax beside Helios and Iaō Sabaōth around the stone. [PGM LXXI translation](https://serpentarium.org/3_coins/2_creatures/abrasax/3_2_abr-002.html).
- **PGM LXXIX:** the speaker identifies the “soul of darkness” successively as Abrasax, “the eternal,” Michael, and Thōouth. This is ritual syncretism rather than a stable genealogy.

A searchable English translation is available in Hans Dieter Betz’s edited collection, although the edition must be used with Preisendanz’s Greek apparatus for philological work. [*Greek Magical Papyri in Translation*](https://mahabodhi.saranagamana.org/studies/texts/other/The_Greek_Magical_Papyri_in_Translation.pdf).

### Corpora in which Abraxas is absent

**[Negative finding]** Abraxas/Abrasax does **not** appear as a named being in:

- the Hebrew Bible or Septuagint;
- 1 Enoch, Jubilees, or Tobit;
- the biblical Dead Sea Scrolls or recognized sectarian compositions;
- the Mishnah, Babylonian or Jerusalem Talmud as an established angel;
- the New Testament;
- Pseudo-Dionysius’ celestial hierarchy;
- Aquinas’ angelology;
- the Qur’an, canonical hadith, or classical Qur’anic angelology;
- al-Ṭabarī’s standard prophetic and Qur’anic narratives;
- Dante or Milton as a named character;
- the standard spirit catalogues of the *Lemegeton* or *Book of Abramelin*.

Later Jewish, Syriac, Coptic, and Arabic magical texts may borrow cognate forms. That does not retroactively place Abraxas in biblical, rabbinic, Christian-canonical, or Islamic angelology. Davidson’s *Dictionary of Angels* and popular demon dictionaries conflate source periods more freely than current source criticism permits.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

No single domain survives across the sources.

- **Cosmic administration:** ruler or numerical summation of 365 heavens in the Basilidian reports.
- **Rescue and ascent:** Abrasax helps remove the elect from destructive fire in the *Apocalypse of Adam*.
- **Baptismal/celestial mediation:** Sethian liturgical texts.
- **Protection, healing, coercion, revelation, and ritual authority:** magical papyri and gems.
- **Solar or calendrical association:** inferred from 365, Helios contexts, radiate or rooster imagery, but not universally present.
- **Union of opposites:** Jung and Hesse; a modern reconstruction, not an ancient defining domain.

### The rooster-headed anguipede

The standard image has:

- rooster’s head;
- human armored torso;
- two serpents for legs;
- round shield;
- whip or scourge;
- sometimes the inscriptions **ΙΑΩ**, **ΑΒΡΑΣΑΞ**, **ΣΑΒΑΩΘ**, or palindromes.

A British Museum third-century dark-green jasper shows the armored rooster-headed anguipede with whip and shield. [British Museum 1986,0501.43](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/H_1986-0501-43). A second- to third-century gem reset in a fourth-century Romano-British gold ring from the Thetford Hoard bears **IAŌ** on the shield and **ABRASAX SABAŌTH** on the reverse, demonstrating the image’s geographic travel but not a British temple cult. [Thetford ring](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/H_1981-0201-13).

Interpretations remain contested:

- the rooster may evoke solar dawn, vigilance, or military aggression;
- serpents may signify chthonic power, eternity, protection, or cosmic motion;
- shield and whip make the being an armed apotropaic ruler;
- Egyptian, Greek, Roman military, and Jewish divine-name elements coexist.

No ancient text supplies an iconographic key saying “the cock means X and the snake legs mean Y.” Modern explanations should therefore be labeled hypotheses.

### Sacred animals, colors, days, and substances

- **Rooster and serpent:** securely present in the gem image, but their theological meanings are reconstructed.
- **Number 365:** securely associated with the name in heresiology.
- **Solar year:** explicitly inferred by Hippolytus; reinforced by magical associations with Helios.
- **Seven planets:** the assertion that the seven letters each represent a classical planet is a later esoteric systematization, not securely attested Basilidian doctrine.
- **Jasper/chalcedony:** frequently used for gems; green, red, brown, and yellow stones survive. Material choice can be ritual but was not exclusive.
- No securely ancient sacred plant, exclusive weekday, festival color, temple animal, or sacrificial victim is documented.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

There is no common genealogy. In Irenaeus and Hippolytus, Abrasax is an archon inside a layered cosmos. Pseudo-Tertullian makes Abraxas the progenitor of a chain of hypostases. Sethian texts instead place Abrasax among ministers and angels. Magical texts equate or coordinate the name with multiple gods.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The 365 heavens

**[Early attested through Irenaeus, c. 180]** The fullest recoverable outline is cosmological rather than narrative. Successive ranks of powers and angels produce heavens, until the total reaches 365. Their ruler or summative great archon is Abrasax, whose name numerically embodies that total. Lower angels fashion the visible world. Salvation comes from beyond the lower cosmic rulers.

Variants:

- Irenaeus presents Abraxas as chief of the heavens.
- Hippolytus embeds the name within a more elaborate nonexistent-God/world-seed cosmology and does not simply make Abrasax the absolute God.
- Pseudo-Tertullian makes Abraxas the head of an emanational genealogy.
- Epiphanius expands the numerology.

There is no surviving Basilidian manuscript against which these hostile accounts can be checked.

### Rescue from the conflagration

**[Early Sethian narrative]** In the *Apocalypse of Adam*, earthly rulers attack the imperishable people with fire, sulfur, asphalt, and darkening mist. Great clouds of light descend; Abrasax, Sablo, and Gamaliel lift the people above the aeons and rulers into the dwelling of the holy angels, where they become angel-like. Abrasax is a salvific celestial agent, not the persecuting demiurge. [Text and manuscript discussion](https://gnosis.study/library/%D0%93%D0%BD%D0%BE%D0%B7%D0%B8%D1%81/%D0%98%D1%81%D1%81%D0%BB%D0%B5%D0%B4%D0%BE%D0%B2%D0%B0%D0%BD%D0%B8%D1%8F/ENG/Linder%20P.-A.%20-%20The%20Apocalypse%20of%20Adam.%20Nag%20Hammadi%20Codex%20V%2C5%20Considered%20from%20its%20Egyptian%20Background.pdf).

### The seven laughs of creation

**[Early magical cosmogony, not Basilidian canon]** PGM XIII describes seven creative laughs producing successive constituents of the cosmos: light, division of primordial waters, mind, generative power, fate, time/celestial measures, and soul. Abrasax is a secret linguistic name of the creator. Further vocalizations generate Python, primal man/fear, and Iaō. The myth exemplifies Egyptian-Greek ritual speculation in which sound itself creates.

### No combat myth for the anguipede

Despite modern art depicting Abraxas as a warrior, no ancient narrative tells of the rooster-headed god fighting a named opponent. Shield and whip establish martial/apotropaic iconography, not a recoverable epic.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No demonstrated public cult

No temple securely dedicated to Abraxas, civic priesthood, festival calendar, public altar, state sacrifice, pilgrimage center, or theophoric naming tradition has been identified. Consequently, “cult of Abraxas” should be used cautiously. The evidence concerns minority cosmologies, household or specialist ritual, portable amulets, and textual invocation.

### Gems and amulets

Thousands of late-antique engraved magical gems survive; hundreds bear the anguipede type. Many entered early-modern collections without findspots, which limits conclusions about users. Securely contextualized examples such as the Thetford gem demonstrate possession and reuse, not the owner’s doctrinal identity.

Likely functions include:

- bodily protection and healing;
- defense against demons or illness;
- authority over hostile powers;
- erotic or coercive magic;
- solar or cosmic empowerment;
- ritual identification with a superior power.

Specific functions must be read from each inscription. The name alone does not establish purpose.

### Ritual handbooks

The PGM direct practitioners to speak, write, engrave, wear, bury, or manipulate divine names. “Abrasax” participates in this technology of efficacious naming. Jewish names such as Iaō and Sabaōth appear beside Greek and Egyptian gods without implying conversion to Judaism or membership in a Gnostic church.

### Human sacrifice, sexual rites, and cult violence

**[Negative finding]** No credible primary evidence connects Abraxas worship with human sacrifice, temple prostitution, institutional sexual rites, or organized cult violence. Heresiologists accuse various “Gnostics” of sexual and magical misconduct, but these generalized polemics are not evidence for an Abraxas cult. Pseudo-Tertullian’s statement that the subordinate Jewish angel “sheds human blood” is theological invective inside his account, not documentation of devotees sacrificing humans to Abraxas.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### c. 120–160: Basilides and early Basilidians

Basilides taught in Alexandria during the reigns conventionally associated with Hadrian and Antoninus Pius. His writings are lost. Whether he personally taught the precise Abrasax numerology or whether it crystallized among successors cannot be demonstrated.

### c. 150–250: formation of overlapping traditions

The name circulated in Christian cosmological speculation, Sethian ritual mythology, and Graeco-Egyptian magic. These streams shared the multilingual religious environment of Roman Egypt but cannot be assumed to represent one organization.

### c. 180–400: documentation and material peak

Irenaeus, Hippolytus, Pseudo-Tertullian, and Epiphanius preserve heresiological versions. Magical gems and papyri show the widest material dissemination during approximately the second through fourth centuries. Coptic copies of Sethian texts preserve a distinct celestial Abrasax.

### Fourth–sixth centuries: Christianization and transformation

Imperial Christianization reduced the institutional space for traditional sacrifice and stigmatized unauthorized magic and “heresy.” No edict specifically suppressing a temple or priesthood of Abraxas is known. The name nevertheless survived in Coptic, Syriac, Jewish, and other magical transmission. This is transformation and textual survival, not evidence for an uninterrupted Abraxas church.

### Medieval period

Abraxas is absent from mainstream Byzantine, Latin scholastic, rabbinic, and Islamic angelological canons. Occasional magical forms survive in manuscript traditions. The *Lemegeton*, *Abramelin*, Hekhalot literature, *Sefer Raziel*, and *Zohar* do not establish Abraxas as one of their core named beings. Claims that Abraxas was a medieval Kabbalistic archangel generally retroject late-antique or modern occult material.

### Renaissance to Enlightenment antiquarianism

Collectors grouped engraved stones as “Basilidian” or “Gnostic gems.” Because Irenaeus mentioned Abraxas and gems bore Abrasax, scholars constructed a single Basilidian iconographic system. Modern gem scholarship has dismantled that automatic equation.

### Nineteenth century: demonology and occult recoding

Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* calls Abracax/Abraxas a god of certain “Asian theogonies” and repeats the derivation of *abracadabra*. Later popular demonology turned this into a “demon,” sometimes with a king’s head and serpent feet. The entry is antiquarian compilation, not evidence for ancient demonic classification. [1863 entry](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.%2C_1863/Abracax).

### Twentieth century to present

Jung, Hesse, Crowley, modern Gnostic churches, eclectic occultists, artists, musicians, game designers, and neopagans reinvented the figure. Contemporary devotion exists on an individual and small-group basis, but no reliable census or evidence of institutional continuity from antiquity exists.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Aleister Crowley

**[Modern occult adaptation]** Crowley wrote *Liber XV: The Gnostic Mass* in 1913 and published it from 1918 onward. Its saints and invocations appropriate late-antique Gnostic names, including Abrasax, for Thelemic liturgy. This is not a reconstruction of a Basilidian rite. [Modern ritual edition](https://www.gnosticmass.org/Mass-Missal.pdf).

No evidence establishes a distinctive Abraxas doctrine in the original Golden Dawn curriculum. Assertions that “the Golden Dawn worshipped Abraxas” generally infer backward from Crowley or later ceremonial magic.

### Carl Gustav Jung

**[Modern invention, 1916]** Jung privately printed *Septem Sermones ad Mortuos*, attributing it fictionally to “Basilides of Alexandria.” His Abraxas is “effectiveness” transcending God/Helios and Devil, joining fullness and emptiness, generation and destruction. Jung’s pseudonymous attribution is literary and visionary, not a discovered Basilidian text. [Complete text](https://gnosis.org/library/7Sermons.htm).

Jung later incorporated the work into the autobiographical materials surrounding *Memories, Dreams, Reflections*; *The Red Book* materials clarify that this Abraxas belongs to Jung’s personal cosmology. The common statement that ancient Gnostics worshipped Abraxas as “the union of good and evil” is principally a back-projection of Jung.

### Hermann Hesse, *Demian*

**[Modern literary reinvention]** Written in 1917 and published in 1919 under “Emil Sinclair,” *Demian* makes Abraxas a god encompassing divine and demonic poles. The famous note reads: “The bird fights its way out of the egg … The bird flies to God. That God’s name is Abraxas.” Hesse uses the figure to dramatize psychological rebirth and escape from conventional moral dualism. [Project Gutenberg text](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/74222/74222-h/74222-h.htm); [Hesse’s official literary site](https://www.hhesse.de/werk/demian/).

### Santana, *Abraxas*

**[Modern commercial and artistic reception]** Santana’s second studio album, released in 1970, took its name through Hesse’s *Demian*, not through direct reconstruction of Basilidian ritual. The album’s Mati Klarwein artwork further associated the name with psychedelic, erotic, multicultural, and visionary imagery. The Library of Congress essay dates the US release to September 23, 1970 and explicitly traces the title to Hesse. [Library of Congress essay](https://www.loc.gov/static/programs/national-recording-preservation-board/documents/Abraxas.pdf); [Santana discography](https://santana.com/discography/abraxas/).

### Contemporary occultism and neopaganism

Current practitioners variously treat Abraxas as:

- a deity of totality or nonduality;
- a solar power;
- an archon or demiurge;
- a demon;
- a protective magical name;
- an archetype of integration;
- a chaos-magical egregore.

These positions are mutually inconsistent and overwhelmingly derive from Jung, Hesse, nineteenth-century gem literature, modern demon dictionaries, and freely syncretistic readings. They are living religious interpretations, but they are not evidence for ancient doctrine. No demonstrable continuous priesthood, initiatory lineage, or inherited temple ritual connects them with Basilides.

### Political and extremist appropriation

The name appears sporadically in transgressive and countercultural occult networks, including groups interested in “left-hand path” symbolism. Evidence does not support treating Abraxas as a general extremist emblem. Particular organizations must be examined individually; the ancient record supplies no nationalist or racial ideology.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Abraxas Basilides’ supreme God?

- **For:** Irenaeus calls him chief; Pseudo-Tertullian explicitly says “supreme Deity.”
- **Against:** Hippolytus places an ineffable/nonexistent God above all generated reality and calls Abrasax the great archon of 365 heavens. The witnesses disagree and may describe different historical stages.
- **Judgment:** “Great cosmic ruler in reported Basilidian systems” is secure; “Basilides’ absolute God” is not.

### Are the gems Gnostic?

Older scholarship called them “Gnostic gems” or “Basilidian stones.” Bonner and later specialists showed that the iconography and inscriptions belong to a broader Graeco-Egyptian magical koine. A gem bearing Abrasax may use a name known to Gnostics without having been made by a Gnostic congregation. The strongest modern position is case-by-case analysis.

### Is the anguipede Abraxas?

Some gems explicitly join image and name, making the identification plausible for those objects. Yet:

- the name occurs without the image;
- the image occurs with Iaō, Sabaōth, or other inscriptions;
- ancient texts never give an identifying description.

Thus “Abrasax-anguipede” is a valid catalogue shorthand, not proof of one universal iconographic identity.

### Egyptian, Jewish, Greek, or “Oriental” origin?

The evidence is hybrid. Greek isopsephy gives the preserved numerical mechanism; Jewish divine names permeate the magical material; Egyptian ritual and iconographic contexts are fundamental; Roman military imagery shapes the anguipede. Nineteenth-century searches for a single national origin are methodologically inadequate.

### Abraxas and abracadabra

The relationship is possible but unproved. Similarity, magical use, and early-modern assertions do not demonstrate derivation. The ancient evidence never says that *abracadabra* is Abraxas’ name or formula.

### Primary evidence and its repositories

- Heresiological texts survive through medieval manuscript transmission and modern critical editions.
- The Nag Hammadi codices are principally in Cairo’s Coptic Museum.
- PGM manuscripts are dispersed among collections in Berlin, Paris, Leiden, London, and elsewhere.
- Gems are dispersed through the British Museum, Fitzwilliam Museum, Bibliothèque nationale de France, Staatliche Museen zu Berlin, private collections, and the Campbell Bonner database.
- Many gems lack archaeological provenance, one of the field’s central limitations.

The Brill *Encyclopedia of Early Christianity* appropriately treats Abrasax across heresiology, three Nag Hammadi texts, papyri, gems, and curse tablets rather than as one simple god. [Maria Fallica, “Abrasax”](https://iris.uniroma1.it/handle/11573/1649966).

## 10. Chronology

| Date | Historical event or attestation |
|---|---|
| c. 120–150 CE | Basilides teaches in Alexandria; his own surviving fragments do not securely mention Abrasax. |
| Second century | Earliest possible production of some Abrasax/anguipede gems; most dates are stylistic rather than archaeological. |
| c. 180 | Irenaeus provides the earliest securely datable textual notice: Abraxas is chief of 365 heavens. |
| Late second–third century | Composition or development of Sethian works containing Abrasax, including *Apocalypse of Adam*, *Holy Book of the Great Invisible Spirit*, and *Zostrianos*. Exact dating disputed. |
| Early third century | Hippolytus records Abrasax as great archon amid a distinct Basilidian cosmology. |
| Third century | Pseudo-Tertullian presents Abraxas as head of an emanational chain. |
| Third–fourth centuries | Principal surviving magical papyri and many Abrasax/anguipede gems are produced. |
| Fourth century | Nag Hammadi Coptic codices copied; Thetford’s older Abrasax gem reset in a Romano-British ring. |
| c. 374–377 | Epiphanius composes *Panarion*, expanding anti-Basilidian numerology. |
| Late antiquity–medieval period | Name persists intermittently in Coptic, Syriac, and Jewish magic; no public cult continuity demonstrated. |
| Renaissance–eighteenth century | European collectors classify “Abraxas stones”; antiquarians increasingly associate them with Basilides. |
| 1818–1863 | Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* popularizes Abracax/Abraxas in demonological antiquarianism. |
| 1888 onward | Golden Dawn and successor occultism provide the environment for later Gnostic-name revival, though no original Golden Dawn Abraxas cult is established. |
| 1913 | Crowley writes *The Gnostic Mass*. |
| 1916 | Jung privately prints *Seven Sermons to the Dead*, creating Abraxas as a power beyond God and Devil. |
| 1917–1919 | Hesse writes and publishes *Demian*, making Abraxas a symbol of psychological and moral totality. |
| 1945 | Nag Hammadi codices discovered, making independent Sethian references directly available to scholarship. |
| 1950 | Campbell Bonner publishes *Studies in Magical Amulets*, helping detach magical gems from automatic Basilidian attribution. |
| 1970 | Santana releases *Abraxas*, transmitting Hesse’s reinvented figure into global popular culture. |
| 1970s–present | Critical editions, gem databases, and Nag Hammadi scholarship increasingly distinguish Basilidian, Sethian, magical, and modern uses. |
| Present | Small-scale occult and devotional appropriation continues; its scale is unquantified and no continuous ancient lineage is established. |

## 11. Conclusions

The fullest defensible reconstruction is not a biography but a history of a name moving between systems.

The earliest secure Abraxas is the numerically defined ruler of 365 heavens reported by Irenaeus. Soon afterward—or contemporaneously—the form **Abrasax** appears in ritual magic and in Sethian celestial lists, where it performs different functions. The iconic rooster-headed anguipede is materially real and widely attested, but its universal identification as “the Gnostic god Abraxas” is a scholarly inheritance rather than an ancient certainty. No temple cult, priesthood, festival, public sacrifice, continuous church, or standard Abrahamic angelological office can be reconstructed.

Modern Abraxas is largely a second creation. Jung supplied the god beyond God and Devil; Hesse transformed that figure into a symbol of self-birth and moral totality; Santana globalized Hesse’s name. Those reinventions are historically important, but they should not be projected backward onto Basilides, the Nag Hammadi texts, or the magical gems.
