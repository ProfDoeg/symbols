# Raziel: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Angel (*malʾakh*), revelatory angel, and in later Jewish and Christian occult systems an archangel or sephirotic intelligence. Raziel is not a god, demon, fallen angel, or Gnostic aeon in the surviving premodern sources examined here.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The name רזיאל (*Rāzīʾēl/Raziʾel*) occurs in the textual tradition of *Sefer ha-Razim* (“Book of Secrets”), whose materials are commonly assigned to late antiquity, perhaps the third–fourth centuries CE, but whose surviving Hebrew, Judeo-Arabic, and Latin witnesses are medieval. Consequently, “late antique Raziel” is a defensible textual reconstruction, not an extant late-antique inscription or manuscript. Raziel is unambiguously and abundantly documented in medieval Jewish esoteric literature and in the Latin *Liber Razielis* tradition by the thirteenth century.
>
> **Evidentiary summary:** Raziel is absent by name from the Hebrew Bible, Septuagint, New Testament, Qur’an, canonical hadith, 1 Enoch, Jubilees, Tobit, the presently published Dead Sea Scrolls, classical rabbinic literature, Pseudo-Dionysius, Aquinas, Dante, and Milton. The familiar “angel of secrets who gave a book to Adam” is principally a medieval Jewish and Jewish-derived Christian tradition, albeit one incorporating older magical and cosmological materials. The precise Amsterdam *Sefer Raziʾel ha-Malʾakh* is a composite anthology first printed in 1701, not an intact primordial or late-antique book.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Name and etymology

The standard Hebrew form is **רזיאל**, conventionally vocalized **רָזִיאֵל**, *Rāzīʾēl* or *Raziʾel*. Common European spellings include **Raziel, Razi’el, Razielis, Rezial, Reziel, Rasiel**, and **Raxhael** in particular manuscript environments.

The name divides naturally into:

- **רז** (*rāz*), “secret, mystery”; and
- **אל** (*ʾēl*), “God” or “divine.”

Accordingly, it can be rendered “secret of God,” “mystery of God,” or, if construed as a sentence-name, “God is my mystery.” The most historically appropriate gloss is probably **“Mystery/Secret of God.”**

The noun *rāz* is not native Common Semitic vocabulary. It is usually regarded as an Iranian loanword, cognate with an Old Iranian term for a secret or mystery; it entered Jewish Aramaic and Hebrew and appears prominently in the Aramaic portions of Daniel. That Iranian history belongs to the word, not to evidence for a Persian god or angel named Raziel. No secure pre-Jewish Iranian prototype of the being has been identified.

### Titles

Documented titles and descriptions include:

- **רזיאל המלאך — Raziʾel ha-malʾakh**, “Raziel the angel”: the standard medieval and early-modern Hebrew designation.
- **ממונה על רזין עילאין קדישין**, approximately “appointed over the holy supernal mysteries”: the Zoharic description in *Zohar*, Bereshit 94.
- **מארי דרזין**, “master/lord of mysteries”: associated Zoharic language.
- **Angelus Raziel / Razielis Archangelus**, “the angel/archangel Raziel”: Latin manuscript tradition.
- **Angel of secrets**, **angel of mysteries**, **keeper of divine secrets**: accurate paraphrases of the medieval function, though their capitalization as fixed official titles is mostly modern.
- **Archangel of Chokmah:** an early-modern Christian-kabbalistic and especially Golden Dawn systematization, not a title found in the Hebrew Bible, Talmud, or earliest Raziel materials.

### Proposed identifications

Older compilations sometimes equate Raziel with **Akraziel** or **Gallizur/Galitzur**, angels connected with proclamation or revelation. These equations rest principally on similarity of office and exegetical wordplay, not on an early text stating that the beings are identical. They should be classified as **learned medieval or later harmonization**.

Raziel is sometimes confused with:

- **Raguel**, one of the angels in some Enochic lists;
- **Azrael**, the angel of death in later Jewish and Islamic usage;
- **Sariel**, an angel named in Enochic and Qumran traditions;
- **Rasiel/Rathiel**, similarly spelled names in magical lists.

No textual basis establishes these as the same being.

Raziel has no secure identification with a Gnostic aeon, archon, or luminary in the Nag Hammadi codices. Placing him in a “Gnostic pantheon” is therefore a modern comparative taxonomy, not an ancient Gnostic classification.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The late-antique problem

*Sefer ha-Razim* survives through a difficult collection of Hebrew and Judeo-Arabic fragments, medieval manuscripts, and a Latin descendant. Mordecai Margalioth reconstructed and published it in 1966; Bill Rebiger and Peter Schäfer produced a substantially more critical edition in 2009. The work describes seven heavens populated by many named angels and supplies rituals for healing, divination, coercion, favor, victory, and other purposes.

Its preface identifies the transmitted book as a book of secrets given to **Noah “from the mouth of Raziel the angel.”** Noah passes it through a pseudobiblical chain that eventually reaches Solomon. The preface thus supplies the earliest reconstructable Raziel narrative.

Scholars commonly place important materials in the work in late antiquity, in part because of its Greek magical vocabulary, cosmology, and relationship to late-antique ritual culture. Yet the documentary limitation is decisive: no third- or fourth-century codex naming Raziel survives. Rebiger and Schäfer describe two related works reaching back into late antiquity, but the textual and redactional history is too complicated to treat the reconstructed form as a pristine ancient original. The earliest secure conclusion is therefore:

- **Secure:** medieval witnesses transmit a text in which Raziel gives secrets to Noah.
- **Probable scholarly reconstruction:** elements, perhaps including the revelatory framework, descend from late antiquity.
- **Not established:** that the entire surviving prologue, in its present wording, existed in the fourth century.

The work is distinct from the later printed *Sefer Raziʾel ha-Malʾakh*. Confusing their similar titles erases an important textual history.

### Corpora in which Raziel is absent

A source-driven dossier must record negative findings:

- **Hebrew Bible and Septuagint:** no Raziel. Biblical *rāz* occurs as “secret,” especially in Daniel, but is not an angel’s name there.
- **1 Enoch:** no Raziel in the principal Ethiopic text. Raguel, Sariel, Raphael, Michael, Gabriel, Uriel, and other angels must not be silently renamed Raziel.
- **Jubilees, Tobit, and the Dead Sea Scrolls:** no securely identified Raziel.
- **Mishnah, Babylonian and Jerusalem Talmuds, and classical named midrashim:** no secure named Raziel. Later writers sometimes identify other secret-bearing or proclaiming angels with him; the identification is retrospective.
- **New Testament and early Christian apocrypha:** no secure Raziel.
- **Pseudo-Dionysius’ *Celestial Hierarchy*:** no Raziel. Its ninefold hierarchy later provided a framework into which occultists placed him.
- **Aquinas:** no developed Raziel figure comparable to the medieval Jewish and magical traditions.
- **Qur’an, canonical hadith, and early tafsir including al-Tabari:** no established angel Raziel. Assertions that Raziel is a canonical Islamic archangel are modern misinformation or confusion with Riḍwān, ʿAzrāʾīl, Isrāfīl, or Rūḥ.
- **Nag Hammadi codices and the major heresiologists Irenaeus, Hippolytus, and Epiphanius:** no securely identifiable Raziel.
- **Dante and Milton:** no Raziel. Their importance belongs to the broader reception of Christian angelology, not to Raziel’s documentary corpus.

### *Sefer Raziʾel ha-Malʾakh*

The work now sold under this title is a **medieval and early-modern anthology**, principally Hebrew and Aramaic, of cosmology, divine names, angelology, letter mysticism, astrology, prayers, seals, amulets, and magical procedures.

No known manuscript corresponds exactly to the Amsterdam printed edition. Recent scholarship identifies several strata:

1. **Late-antique and early-medieval materials:** passages related to *Sefer ha-Razim*, *Shiʿur Qomah*, *Seder Rabba de-Bereshit*, *Otiyyot de-Rabbi Aqiva*, and *Sefer Yetzirah*.
2. **Thirteenth-century Ashkenazi pietist material:** much associated with **Eleazar of Worms** (c. 1176–1238).
3. **Material associated with Nehemiah ben Solomon, the “prophet of Erfurt.”**
4. **Later kabbalistic, magical, and redactional additions.**

Older scholarship debated whether Eleazar authored “the Book of Raziel.” Leopold Zunz inferred his authorship from linguistic and literary evidence; Adolf Jellinek objected on manuscript grounds. The better formulation is that Eleazar or his literary school supplied a large portion of the anthology, while neither the complete work nor the Amsterdam redaction is the product of one author.

The anthology itself preserves incompatible origin accounts:

- In its opening, Raziel gives a book to **Adam**.
- Elsewhere, **Raphael** communicates material to **Noah** before the Flood.
- Related *Sefer ha-Razim* tradition makes Raziel the revealer to **Noah**.
- The wisdom passes through patriarchal succession and ultimately into **Solomon’s** possession.

These are variant strata, not one internally consistent narrative.

### The Zohar

The *Zohar*, written chiefly in artificial Aramaic and emerging in late-thirteenth-century Castile, gives the most influential literary formulation of Raziel’s office. In *Zohar*, Bereshit 94, God sends Adam a book through Raziel, described as the holy angel appointed over the supernal mysteries. The book contains sacred wisdom, “seventy-two branches” and “six hundred and seventy inscriptions” of higher mysteries. When Adam sins, the book departs; after Adam prays and weeps, it is restored in the Zoharic narrative complex.

The Zohar also knows books associated with Enoch and Solomon. These literary books should not automatically be identified with extant works bearing those names. The *Zohar’s* “book of Adam” is a mythic object inside the narrative, not bibliographic proof that the Amsterdam 1701 volume existed in antiquity.

### The Latin *Liber Razielis*

A large Latin magical corpus circulated as *Liber Razielis Archangeli*, *Liber Salomonis*, or related titles. One major form is organized into seven books and combines translated Jewish material with Arabic, Hermetic, astrological, and image-magic traditions.

Its prologue associates its production with the court of **Alfonso X of Castile** and a date around 1259. Some scholars accept a substantial Alfonsine setting; others argue that this origin story is partly fictive and that the received compilation took shape in the mid- to late fourteenth century. The distinction between source translations and the final compilation remains unsettled.

A *Liber institutionis Razielis* was already named among suspect or condemned magical books in the thirteenth-century *Speculum astronomiae*. In 1434 Lope de Barrientos reported that a copy belonging to Enrique de Villena was burned on the order of Juan II of Castile. This is documented suppression of a book attributed to Raziel, not persecution of a Raziel cult.

British Library Sloane MS 3846 preserves an English *Book of the Angel Raziel*, copied from Latin materials and conventionally called *Liber Salomonis*. Paris, BnF lat. 3666, copied in northern Italy in the third quarter of the fifteenth century, preserves another Raziel-related angel-invocation text.

### Amsterdam 1701

The editio princeps was printed in Amsterdam in **5461/1701** by Moses ben Abraham Mendes Coutinho. Its extended title begins, in effect, “This is the book of Adam the First, which Raziel the angel gave him.” The editor assembled manuscript materials; he did not print a single ancient codex.

Its title-page commendation promises blessing, success, wise descendants, protection from harmful spirits, and protection so that destructive fire will not rule the owner’s house. This material history explains why owners came to keep the physical book unopened as an amulet.

The correct distinction is:

- **Traditional attribution:** revelation by Raziel to Adam.
- **Historical compilation:** medieval Ashkenazi and older magical materials subsequently combined.
- **Exact printed artifact:** Amsterdam, 1701.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Core domain

Raziel’s stable medieval domain is **the transmission and guardianship of divine secrets**: divine names, cosmology, astrology, angelic hierarchies, sacred alphabets, ritual knowledge, and knowledge of events hidden from ordinary humanity.

He does not rule a temple, city, weather phenomenon, animal species, or national pantheon. His identity is literary and revelatory: he mediates a heavenly book.

### Powers and characteristic actions

Depending on the textual stratum, Raziel:

- communicates a book to Adam or Noah;
- explains hidden structures of creation;
- reveals names and offices of angels;
- supplies knowledge for divination and ritual operations;
- teaches procedures for protection, healing, favor, and other magical aims;
- functions as the heavenly warrant for a book’s authority.

A recurrent limitation is that secret knowledge remains governed by God. Raziel is a messenger, not an independent creator or rival deity.

### Form and iconography

No securely identified ancient or medieval cult image of Raziel is known. The Amsterdam volume contains diagrams, magical characters, seals, tables, and angelic names, but these are not securely portraits of Raziel. Early art did not develop a stable Raziel iconography comparable to Michael’s armor and dragon, Gabriel’s annunciation lily, or Raphael’s companionship of Tobias.

Modern art commonly gives Raziel:

- wings and luminous robes;
- a book, scroll, key, or glowing sigil;
- rainbow or indigo/violet coloring;
- stars, galaxies, prisms, or crystalline light.

These are modern illustrative conventions. Rainbow colors, crystals, “third-eye” imagery, and fixed aura colors are not securely attested in the medieval Raziel corpus.

### Sephirotic and planetary correspondences

Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia* (printed in its authorized form in 1533) participates in the Christian-kabbalistic mapping of Hebrew divine and angelic names onto the ten sefirot. Raziel came to be assigned to **Chokmah**, the second sefirah, with the divine name *Yah* and the angelic order *Ophanim*. The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn adopted and standardized such tables from 1888 onward.

These are **learned Christian-kabbalistic and modern occult systematizations**. Assignments to Uranus, a specific weekday, crystal, color, chakra, or zodiacal office vary among recent manuals and should not be projected into medieval Judaism.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

Raziel has no documented parents, birth story, consort, offspring, siblings, sacred animal, or mount. Jewish and Christian angelologies normally treat angels as created heavenly beings rather than genealogical families. Statements that Raziel is the “brother” of named archangels are devotional or fictional idiom, not textual genealogy.

His rank varies:

- *Sefer ha-Razim*: revelatory angel, without a universally fixed place among seven canonical archangels.
- Medieval Hebrew esotericism: angel of mysteries and authority behind transmitted esoteric books.
- Latin tradition: often **archangelus**, an elevation encouraged by Christian hierarchical vocabulary.
- Christian Kabbalah/Golden Dawn: archangel or intelligence of Chokmah.
- Modern New Age literature: one of an expanded roster of personally accessible archangels.

Adam, Noah, Enoch, and Solomon are human or mythic-human recipients/holders rather than divine beings, but their narrative relations are central:

- **Adam:** receives the book; studies it; loses it after sin in some versions; regains it through prayer.
- **Noah:** receives a Raziel book in *Sefer ha-Razim*; in another stratum receives knowledge from Raphael.
- **Enoch:** possesses a different heavenly book in the Zoharic account.
- **Solomon:** becomes the eventual owner of the transmitted secret literature.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Raziel gives the book to Adam

**Medieval canonical form:** After Adam’s sin or expulsion, he grieves and prays. God sends Raziel with a sacred book. The angel finds Adam by the river flowing from Eden, reassures him, and teaches him how to approach the book in purity. It contains heavenly wisdom, knowledge of future calamities and prosperity, cosmic arrangements, divine names, and methods for calling angels.

The Zoharic form places the gift while Adam is still in Eden, then makes the book depart after his transgression and return after repentance. Other forms place the gift explicitly after expulsion as consolation and instruction.

The legend’s fullest accessible English retelling appears in Louis Ginzberg’s *Legends of the Jews* (1909–1938), but Ginzberg is a modern collector synthesizing earlier Jewish sources, not an ancient witness.

**Developmental assessment:**

- Heavenly books given to primordial sages are an old Jewish apocalyptic and wisdom motif.
- Raziel’s specific role is securely medieval and may preserve older magical tradition.
- The identification of the extant Amsterdam book with Adam’s original volume is traditional pseudepigraphy, not historical bibliography.

### The jealous angels and the sea

In a later narrative expansion, other angels resent Adam’s access to divine secrets, steal the book, and cast it into the sea. Adam prays; God orders **Rahab** to recover it. The episode dramatizes the contested transfer of heavenly knowledge to humanity.

The story is frequently repeated from Gustav Davidson and modern angel encyclopedias. It is less securely anchored than the Zoharic gift narrative and should be labeled **late medieval or modernly amplified legend**, not a biblical episode.

### Raziel gives the book to Noah

The *Sefer ha-Razim* prologue states that the book of mysteries was given to Noah from the mouth of Raziel before the Flood. Its knowledge passes through Shem, Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Levi, Moses, Joshua, the elders, prophets, and ultimately Solomon.

This chain performs several functions:

- it grants ritual knowledge antediluvian antiquity;
- integrates magic into sacred history;
- links heavenly revelation to Solomon’s proverbial wisdom and magical reputation;
- authorizes practices that otherwise stood uneasily beside rabbinic prohibitions.

It is distinct from the Adam narrative, although later anthologists combined them.

### The sapphire book

The book is said in some forms to have been engraved on or embodied in a **sapphire stone** and transmitted through the patriarchs. The sapphire evokes the divine pavement of Exodus 24:10, heavenly tablets, and durable celestial writing. It is a literary sacred object; no material sapphire “Book of Raziel” is known.

### The book as universal knowledge

Later tradition expands the book’s contents until it becomes nearly encyclopedic: creation, astronomy, astrology, calendars, human physiology, divine names, angelic offices, prophecy, future events, healing, protection, and ritual techniques. This growth mirrors the anthology’s actual redaction: heterogeneous works were gathered under the authority of a single heavenly revealer.

### Protection from fire

The 1701 title-page paratext says that possession of the book is a *segullah*, an efficacious protective object, including protection against fire. Owners consequently kept copies in houses as amulets—sometimes without reading them.

This is not a story in which Raziel personally extinguishes fires. It is the ritual biography of a printed book. A later skeptical tradition attributed to Rabbi Nachman of Bratslav observes that copies themselves had burned, rejecting both the book’s Adamic authorship and automatic fireproofing. Thus the fire claim was never universally accepted even within Jewish tradition.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No ancient or institutional cult

There is no evidence for:

- a temple dedicated to Raziel;
- a priesthood of Raziel;
- civic festivals or sacrificial calendars;
- animal or human sacrifice to Raziel;
- temple prostitution;
- royal patronage of a Raziel cult;
- a continuous ancient worshipping community.

Raziel’s historical locus is manuscripts, books, amulets, learned circles, and ritual specialists—not temples.

### Textual ritual practice

The Raziel corpora contain or transmit:

- angel invocations;
- divine and angelic names;
- astrological timing;
- talismanic images;
- seals and magical characters;
- prayers and adjurations;
- protective and healing recipes;
- operations for favor, knowledge, prophecy, divination, and defense;
- instructions for writing amulets.

The classification “magic” is historically useful but requires care. Some operations invoke angels directly; others are prayers framed as divine service; others employ astral correspondences that Christian authorities classified as illicit. Medieval users did not necessarily recognize a modern boundary between religion, science, medicine, and magic.

### The printed book as amulet

The Amsterdam edition’s most distinctive ritual use was passive possession. The title page advertised the book itself as protective. This changed the work from a repository of ritual instructions into a manufactured talismanic object. Its economic history therefore includes printing, collecting, domestic display, and the commerce of *segullot*.

### Sacrifice and violence

The dedicated contested-material search found no evidence of human sacrifice, animal sacrifice, cult violence, or extremist political appropriation centered on Raziel. *Sefer ha-Razim* belongs to a magical environment containing coercive and sometimes aggressive operations, but that fact cannot be converted into evidence for a sacrificial Raziel cult.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Late antiquity: reconstructed background

Jewish apocalyptic literature, angelology, magical handbooks, amulets, and incantation traditions developed extensive bodies of named angels and heavenly books. The materials underlying *Sefer ha-Razim* plausibly belong partly to this environment. Raziel’s late-antique existence is a textual-critical reconstruction rather than an epigraphic certainty.

### Early Middle Ages: transmission and recombination

Geniza fragments and later manuscript families show that Jewish magical cosmology continued in Hebrew, Aramaic, and Judeo-Arabic transmission. Secret-book traditions were copied, excerpted, translated, and attributed to Adam, Noah, Moses, or Solomon. Raziel emerged as the speaking authority of some such material.

### Twelfth–thirteenth centuries: Ashkenazi consolidation

The **Ḥasidei Ashkenaz**, the Jewish pietists of the Rhineland, preserved and reworked older mystical and magical literature. Eleazar of Worms and his school contributed divine-name theology, angelology, cosmology, and ritual materials later incorporated into *Sefer Raziʾel ha-Malʾakh*. This is the decisive period for the recognizable Hebrew Raziel corpus.

### Thirteenth–fifteenth centuries: Castilian and Latin expansion

Jewish, Arabic, and astral-magic material entered the Latin *Liber Razielis* complex. The Alfonsine court supplied either a real translation setting, a legitimating fiction, or both. Latin readers treated Raziel as an archangelic authority over licit or illicit celestial knowledge.

Ecclesiastical and royal anxieties focused on the book’s magic. Copies circulated in monasteries and learned libraries even as texts bearing Raziel’s name appeared in condemnatory lists. The reported 1434 burning of Enrique de Villena’s copy is the clearest suppression episode.

### Late thirteenth century onward: the Zohar

The *Zohar* transformed Raziel’s book into an element of a large kabbalistic sacred history. Raziel became the angel appointed over supernal mysteries, and Adam’s book became a precursor of kabbalistic revelation.

### Renaissance and early modern Europe

Christian Hebraists, magicians, and kabbalists—including traditions associated with Trithemius, Postel, Agrippa, and later compilers—used Raziel literature as evidence for an ancient Adamic wisdom. The textual tradition acquired Hermetic and prisca-theologia interpretations: Hebrew secret knowledge, astral magic, and primordial revelation were treated as remnants of one ancient theology.

### 1701: print transformation

The Amsterdam printing stabilized one influential composite form. Its title page explicitly marketed supernatural benefits. Reprinting expanded access, while the artifact’s talismanic reputation sometimes mattered more than its contents.

### Nineteenth–twentieth centuries: scholarship and occult tables

Modern Jewish scholarship separated the anthology’s strata and rejected literal Adamic authorship as a historical claim. Western occult orders incorporated Raziel into sephirotic tables, especially as archangel of Chokmah. Angel dictionaries then detached individual claims from their textual settings, producing the composite modern personality “Raziel, archangel of mysteries and magic.”

### Late twentieth century to the present

Raziel appears in ceremonial magic, angel devotion, New Age healing, oracle cards, online “angel numbers,” meditation systems, and commercial spiritual services. These movements are real modern religious phenomena, but their detailed color, crystal, chakra, personality, and therapeutic claims are usually modern inventions.

No reliable evidence establishes the scale of exclusive Raziel worship. Practice is dispersed among occultists, New Age practitioners, independent angel devotees, and some Jewish readers who value the book as a *segullah*. This is reception and revival, not demonstrable institutional continuity from antiquity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Modern occultism

Golden Dawn correspondence tables helped standardize:

- Raziel = Chokmah;
- divine name = *Yah*;
- angelic order = Ophanim;
- Raziel as a very high archangel of primordial wisdom.

Later ceremonial-magical writers expanded those tables. Assertions that Raziel is straightforwardly present in the *Lemegeton* or *Book of Abramelin* are generally misleading: those grimoires contain many angelic names and overlapping traditions, but Raziel is not a central dramatic being in their principal textual forms.

### New Age angel spirituality

Late-twentieth- and early-twenty-first-century angel literature recast Raziel as an approachable personal guide who assists with:

- psychic development;
- dream interpretation;
- manifestation;
- memory of past lives;
- alchemy and “quantum” spirituality;
- rainbow light or aura healing;
- esoteric study.

These claims are **modern devotional inventions**. They extrapolate from the authentic medieval association with secrets but are not themselves medieval doctrine. Doreen Virtue’s former angel-card system was especially influential in popularizing friendly therapeutic archangels; she publicly repudiated her New Age work after her 2017 evangelical conversion.

### Literature and popular media

Notable modern transformations include:

- **Legacy of Kain: Soul Reaver** and its sequels: Raziel is a vampire-turned-wraith protagonist. The name evokes a fallen or secret-revealing angel, but the character’s biography is fictional.
- **Cassandra Clare’s *The Mortal Instruments* / *Shadowhunters*:** the angel Raziel creates or empowers the Nephilim by giving blood in a cup. This is a modern literary mythology without basis in medieval Raziel sources.
- **DC and other comics, games, anime, and role-playing systems:** the name is frequently used for secretive angels, weapons, mecha, or magical entities.
- Online fiction often invents rivalries between Raziel and Lucifer, Satan, Paimon, or Astaroth. No premodern source supports those pairings.

### Political appropriation

No substantial nationalist, racist, terrorist, or extremist Raziel cult was found. Occult names may appear incidentally in fringe subcultures, but there is no evidence sufficient to describe Raziel as a significant political emblem.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Dating *Sefer ha-Razim*

Margalioth’s reconstruction presented the work as a magical book of the Talmudic period, perhaps late third or early fourth century. Rebiger and Schäfer’s edition demonstrated a more complicated history involving two related compositions and extensive manuscript variation. Current responsible usage distinguishes:

- the date of underlying materials;
- the date of a literary redaction;
- the dates of surviving witnesses;
- the medieval form of the Raziel prologue.

### Authorship of *Sefer Raziʾel*

The anthology’s attribution to Adam or Raziel is pseudepigraphic. Eleazar of Worms is crucial but cannot simply be called author of the complete Amsterdam book. The text contains heterogeneous theological positions and sources of different periods. No manuscript exactly matches the 1701 print.

### Relationship between *Sefer ha-Razim* and *Sefer Raziʾel*

They are neither identical nor unrelated. The later anthology incorporated *Sefer ha-Razim*-related material, while both use the authority of an angel of mysteries. Similar titles and overlapping Noah/Adam traditions encouraged bibliographic confusion.

### The Alfonsine controversy

One account makes the Latin *Liber Razielis* a translation project commissioned at Alfonso X’s court around 1259. Damaris Gehr and others have argued that the attribution may be fictive and that the compilation took form later in the fourteenth century. The safest position is that the corpus contains genuine cross-cultural translation material connected with medieval Iberia, while the precise date and unity of the received Latin work remain disputed.

### “Kabbalistic archangel” versus magical book-authority

Modern dictionaries often describe a fully developed celestial person: an archangel with rank, colors, planets, symbols, personality, and relationships. The primary sources offer much less biography. Raziel is principally the authoritative revealer named in books. His elaborate personality is largely the product of systematic occult tables and modern devotional writing.

### Major discoveries and editions

- **1963:** Margalioth recognized related Geniza and manuscript fragments as remains of *Sefer ha-Razim*.
- **1966:** he published his reconstructed Hebrew edition.
- **1983:** Michael A. Morgan published an English translation.
- **2009:** Rebiger and Schäfer published a critical edition of *Sefer ha-Razim I–II*, with German translation and commentary.
- Recent work by scholars including Bill Rebiger, Peter Schäfer, Yosef Dan, Sophie Page, Damaris Gehr, Alejandro García Avilés, and others has emphasized textual plurality, medieval translation, and ritual-book culture.

Gustav Davidson’s *Dictionary of Angels* remains useful as an index to modern angel lore, but it frequently compresses sources of radically different dates. It should not substitute for checking the cited text. Likewise, popular statements that Raziel appears in Enoch, the Talmud, Islam, Gnosticism, or ancient temple cult require evidence that is presently lacking.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Documentary event |
|---|---|
| Biblical period | No Raziel by name in the Hebrew Bible or Septuagint. The Iranian loanword *rāz*, “secret,” appears in Daniel. |
| c. 3rd–4th centuries CE, disputed reconstruction | Materials underlying *Sefer ha-Razim* may be composed in Roman/Byzantine Palestine; its prologue’s Raziel-to-Noah revelation may preserve this stratum, but no contemporary manuscript survives. |
| Late antiquity–early Middle Ages | Hekhalot, *Shiʿur Qomah*, magical, cosmological, and divine-name materials later incorporated into Raziel anthologies circulate. |
| Medieval period | Hebrew and Judeo-Arabic manuscript witnesses transmit *Sefer ha-Razim*-related material. |
| c. 1176–1238 | Lifetime of Eleazar of Worms; substantial Ashkenazi pietist material later included in *Sefer Raziʾel* is associated with him and his school. |
| Early–mid 13th century | Hebrew Raziel books and related works become clearly visible in citations and manuscript culture. |
| Mid-13th century | A *Liber institutionis Razielis* appears in the *Speculum astronomiae*’s discussion of suspect magical works. |
| c. 1259, traditional/contested | Claimed Alfonsine production or translation setting of the seven-book Latin *Liber Razielis*. |
| Late 13th century | The Castilian *Zohar* describes Raziel as guardian of holy supernal mysteries who conveys a book to Adam. |
| 14th century, alternative dating | Some scholarship places the effective formation of the Latin *Liber Razielis* compilation here rather than at Alfonso X’s court. |
| 1434 | Lope de Barrientos reports the burning of Enrique de Villena’s copy of *Liber Razielis* by royal order. |
| 15th century | Latin and vernacular Raziel manuscripts circulate; BnF lat. 3666 is copied in northern Italy in the third quarter of the century. |
| 16th century | Christian Kabbalah and works surrounding Agrippa integrate Raziel into systematic occult correspondences. |
| 1701 | First printed Hebrew *Sefer Raziʾel ha-Malʾakh*, Amsterdam; the title page advertises the book as protection, including against fire. |
| 18th–19th centuries | Reprints and domestic amuletic use spread; Jewish scholars debate authorship and textual layers. |
| 1888 onward | The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn standardizes Raziel as archangel of Chokmah in ceremonial-occult tables. |
| 1909–1938 | Ginzberg’s *Legends of the Jews* popularizes an English narrative synthesis of Raziel’s gift to Adam. |
| 1966 | Margalioth publishes reconstructed *Sefer ha-Razim*. |
| 1967 | Davidson’s *Dictionary of Angels* gives Raziel wide popular reference-book visibility. |
| 1983 | Morgan publishes an English translation of *Sefer ha-Razim*. |
| 1990s onward | Raziel becomes prominent in games, fantasy fiction, angel cards, and New Age spirituality. |
| 1999 | *Legacy of Kain: Soul Reaver* makes a fictional Raziel a major game protagonist. |
| 2009 | Rebiger and Schäfer publish the modern critical edition of *Sefer ha-Razim I–II*. |
| 21st century | Continued reprinting, online ritual use, commercial angel devotion, and scholarly redaction-history research. |

### Evidentiary verdict

The historically recoverable Raziel is not an ancient biblical archangel with a stable biography. He is a **name and revelatory office that emerges from Jewish secret-book and magical transmission**, possibly with late-antique roots and securely flourishing in the Middle Ages. Thirteenth-century Ashkenazi writers, the Castilian Zohar, and Latin translators made him the heavenly guarantor of primordial wisdom. The Amsterdam printers of 1701 converted that accumulated manuscript tradition into a standardized book that was itself treated as an amulet. Christian Kabbalah, the Golden Dawn, modern angel spirituality, and popular fiction then supplied most of the colors, ranks, personality traits, and relationships now presented online as timeless tradition.
