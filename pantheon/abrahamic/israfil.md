# Israfil: Research Dossier

**Class:** Angel (*malak*; commonly “archangel” in modern comparative language), not a god, demon, jinn, or Gnostic archon. In mainstream Islamic tradition Isrāfīl is a high celestial servant of God, especially the angel assigned to the eschatological trumpet.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The name **Isrāfīl** is absent from the Qur’an. Its earliest securely datable, widely accepted textual attestation belongs to the formative Islamic hadith tradition, preserved most decisively in the ninth-century *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* prayer report naming “Jibrīl, Mīkāʾīl, and Isrāfīl.” The familiar identification of Isrāfīl as the trumpet angel is later than the Qur’anic trumpet passages and rests on hadith, exegesis, and narrative angelology of unequal authority. No pre-Islamic occurrence of an angel demonstrably identical to Islamic Isrāfīl has been securely established.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Qur’anic]** Explicit in the seventh-century Qur’an.
- **[Early canonical]** In major ninth-century Sunni hadith collections or comparably early transmitted material.
- **[Later canonical elaboration]** Accepted or influential in later theology, tafsir, or devotional teaching but not explicit in the Qur’an.
- **[Narrative tradition]** *Qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ*, cosmography, universal history, sermon, or eschatological storytelling.
- **[Regional/sectarian variant]** Confined principally to a particular school, region, or textual family.
- **[Learned systematization]** A theologian’s, philosopher’s, mystic’s, or encyclopedist’s ordering of inherited material.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A comparison or equation made across religious traditions.
- **[Medieval/early-modern accretion]** Material first securely visible in medieval or early-modern sources.
- **[Folklore]** Popular narrative insufficiently tied to an early authoritative text.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A historical or philological proposal, not a traditional identification.
- **[Modern invention/reception]** Modern literature, occultism, art, entertainment, or unsupported retrospective attribution.
- **[Unestablished]** Repeated in secondary literature but not demonstrable from the cited primary evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

**[Early canonical]** *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* 770 transmits from ʿĀʾisha that Muḥammad began his night prayer by addressing God as:

> “Lord of Jibrīl, Mīkāʾīl, and Isrāfīl, Creator of the heavens and the earth…”

This establishes Isrāfīl as a named, eminent angel placed beside Gabriel and Michael. It does **not** describe his appearance or call him the trumpeter. The compilation is that of Muslim ibn al-Ḥajjāj (d. 875), though the isnād presents the prayer as prophetic. This is the safest terminus for the name’s securely documented circulation.

**[Later canonical elaboration]** Islamic exegetical and angelological tradition made Isrāfīl the *ṣāḥib al-ṣūr* or *ṣāḥib al-qarn*, “possessor of the Trumpet/Horn,” who awaits God’s command to inaugurate the resurrection. Modern descriptions routinely call him one of Islam’s “four archangels,” beside Jibrīl, Mīkāʾīl, and the Angel of Death, often called ʿAzrāʾīl. That fourfold table is a useful later systematization, not a list supplied by the Qur’an.

### Forms of the name

- Arabic: **إِسْرَافِيل**, *Isrāfīl*.
- Common transliterations: Israfil, Isrāfīl, Israfeel.
- Persian and Ottoman Turkish: **اسرافیل**, normally *Esrāfīl/İsrâfil* according to the language’s transcription conventions.
- European literary form: **Israfel**, established especially by Edgar Allan Poe.
- Occasionally encountered comparative forms: Sarafiel, Seraphiel, Israfel. These are not interchangeable without qualification.

No securely established feminine form, cult title, or independent divine name is documented.

### Etymology

**[Unestablished/modern scholarly reconstruction]** No consensus etymology exists.

1. **Seraphiel hypothesis.** The name has been related to Hebrew **Śĕrāfīʾēl/Seraphiel**, approximately “seraph of God” or “burning one of God,” from *śārāf* and *ʾēl*. A. J. Wensinck connected Isrāfīl with “seraph/seraphim,” while Stephen Burge treats some connection with Seraphiel as possible but emphasizes that Islamic Isrāfīl’s developed role is substantially different.

2. **Raphael equation.** European comparative tables have often treated Isrāfīl as the Islamic counterpart of Raphael. This is functional harmonization: Jewish and Christian lists often contain Gabriel, Michael, Raphael, and Uriel, whereas popular Islamic lists contain Gabriel, Michael, Isrāfīl, and the Angel of Death. Raphael’s biblical role is healing and guidance in *Tobit*, not sounding the final trumpet. The equation is therefore **[syncretistic identification]**, not demonstrated name identity.

3. **Uriel or Sariel comparisons.** These also arise from attempts to align varying lists of chief angels. No early Islamic text identifies Isrāfīl as Uriel or Sariel.

4. **Arabic lexical explanations.** Medieval lexicographical speculation sometimes divided the name into supposed components interpreted as “servant” and *īl*, “God.” Such analysis is uncertain and does not establish an Arabic derivation.

The prudent conclusion is that the name belongs to the wider late-antique West Asian stock of theophoric angel names ending in *-īl/-el*, but its exact path into Arabic cannot presently be reconstructed.

### Titles and classifications

- **Isrāfīl** — personal name; securely attested in hadith.
- **Ṣāḥib al-ṣūr** (“possessor/master of the Trumpet”) — the Qur’an never applies this title to him by name.
- **Ṣāḥib al-qarn** (“possessor of the horn”) — early hadith wording for the unnamed trumpet angel.
- **Al-malak al-muwakkal bi-l-ṣūr** (“the angel entrusted with the Trumpet”) — exegetical description.
- **Angel of Resurrection** — later descriptive title, especially in translations and museum catalogues.
- **Archangel** — conventional modern translation; Islam has no universally binding angelic hierarchy identical to Pseudo-Dionysius’s nine Christian orders.
- **Bearer of the Throne** — attributed to Isrāfīl in some later transmitted angelological reports; not Qur’anic and not uniformly accepted.
- **Master of the Horn** — a later narrative and devotional epithet.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Pre-Islamic background: what is and is not Israfil

**[Background, not direct attestation]** Trumpets and horns already marked divine manifestation, war, enthronement, and eschatological assembly in Jewish and Christian texts:

- Hebrew Bible: Exodus 19 describes the trumpet at Sinai; Isaiah 27:13 announces a “great trumpet”; Zechariah 9:14 makes God sound a trumpet.
- Septuagint: translates these traditions into Greek but supplies no Isrāfīl.
- New Testament: Matthew 24:31 has angels and a great trumpet; 1 Corinthians 15:52 and 1 Thessalonians 4:16 associate resurrection with God’s trumpet; Revelation 8–11 gives trumpets to seven angels. None is named Israfil.
- *1 Enoch*, *Jubilees*, *Tobit*, and the Dead Sea Scrolls supply extensive named-angel and eschatological environments. Raphael appears in *Tobit* and *1 Enoch*; Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, and other archangels appear in Enochic lists. Israfil does not.
- Rabbinic, midrashic, and Hekhalot literature develops angelic princes, heavenly liturgy, and throne attendants, but no securely early Isrāfīl has been found.
- Pseudo-Dionysius, probably around 500 CE, systematized nine Christian celestial orders. Isrāfīl does not appear.
- Neither Aquinas nor Dante provides a historical attestation of Isrāfīl.

These are antecedent environments, not proof that Islamic Isrāfīl was borrowed from one identifiable earlier trumpeter.

### Qur’an

**[Qur’anic]** The Qur’an repeatedly describes the blowing of **al-ṣūr**, but never names its blower. Relevant passages include 6:73; 18:99; 20:102; 23:101; 27:87; 36:51; 39:68; 50:20; and 69:13.

Qur’an 39:68 gives the clearest two-stage sequence:

> “The Trumpet will be blown, and whoever is in the heavens and whoever is on the earth will fall stricken, except whom God wills. Then it will be blown another time, and behold, they will be standing, looking on.”

The Arabic uses passives—**وَنُفِخَ فِي ٱلصُّورِ** and **ثُمَّ نُفِخَ فِيهِ أُخْرَىٰ**—and does not identify the agent. The verse supports two explicit blowings: one causing the cosmic collapse or death, the other resurrection. Whether a separate “blast of terror” makes three became an exegetical dispute.

Qur’an 27:87 describes a blowing at which inhabitants of heaven and earth are terrified; 36:51 describes people emerging from graves when the trumpet is blown. Some interpreters combine terror and death as one blowing; others separate them.

### Hadith

**[Early canonical]** *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* 770 explicitly names Isrāfīl beside Gabriel and Michael in a prayer. This is the strongest early personal-name attestation.

**[Early canonical, identity of angel unstated]** A report transmitted through Abū Saʿīd al-Khudrī and preserved by al-Tirmidhī and Aḥmad says:

> “How can I be at ease when the possessor of the horn has placed the horn to his mouth, bent his forehead, and inclined his hearing, waiting to be commanded to blow?”

Al-Tirmidhī called a form of it *ḥasan*. Crucially, the matn calls the figure **ṣāḥib al-qarn**, not Isrāfīl. The popular translation inserting “Israfil” supplies the later traditional identification. “His lips are already at the trumpet” is therefore a substantially faithful paraphrase of the early report’s image, but naming those lips as Isrāfīl’s is an exegetical gloss.

**[Early canonical]** The report answering “What is the *ṣūr*?” with “a horn which is blown” is preserved in Abū Dāwūd and al-Tirmidhī. Again, it does not necessarily name Isrāfīl.

**[Contested hadith]** The long “Hadith of the Trumpet” names Isrāfīl, describes God creating the trumpet and giving it to him, and narrates three blowings. Its principal route turns on Ismāʿīl ibn Rāfiʿ and contains instability and weakness. Ibn Kathīr and later hadith critics discuss these defects. It is highly important evidence for medieval narrative belief, but not equivalent in authority to *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*.

### Al-Ṭabarī

**[Learned compilation, late ninth/early tenth century]** Muḥammad ibn Jarīr al-Ṭabarī (839–923) preserves Isrāfīl material in both his Qur’anic commentary and universal history. His method frequently records alternative reports with isnāds rather than endorsing every narrative as fact.

In his tafsir on trumpet passages, al-Ṭabarī adduces reports describing the “possessor of the trumpet” with the horn at his mouth, watching and listening for the command. Some recensions or explanatory framing identify him as Isrāfīl.

In his historical treatment of Muḥammad’s prophetic beginnings, al-Ṭabarī records a report from al-Shaʿbī that Isrāfīl was attached to Muḥammad for three years before revelation came through Gabriel. This is **[narrative tradition/contested report]**, not a Qur’anic teaching and not the dominant canonical account of revelation.

Al-Ṭabarī’s corpus thus documents the growth of Isrāfīl from a named high angel into a participant in prophetic biography and eschatology.

### Qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ

**[Narrative tradition]** Al-Thaʿlabī’s early eleventh-century *ʿArāʾis al-majālis fī qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ* belongs to the genre of expansive prophetic tales. Such works draw on Qur’an, hadith, exegetical reports, storytellers, biblical material, and *isrāʾīliyyāt*. Isrāfīl participates in expanded scenes of creation, prophetic commissioning, heavenly deliberation, and the Last Day. The genre is evidence for medieval Muslim religious imagination, not automatically for an early prophetic report.

Later works attributed generically to *Qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ*, and Ibn Kathīr’s *al-Bidāya wa-l-nihāya*, repeat and rationalize many such descriptions, sometimes preserving explicit criticism of their isnāds.

### Al-Suyūṭī

**[Learned systematization]** Jalāl al-Dīn al-Suyūṭī (1445–1505), in *al-Ḥabāʾik fī akhbār al-malāʾik*, collected roughly 750 reports about angels. His Isrāfīl dossier helped consolidate material concerning the trumpet, proximity to the Throne, dimensions, wings, weeping, cosmic duties, and relations with other principal angels. Collection by al-Suyūṭī proves medieval circulation, not authenticity of every report.

### Jewish Seraphiel

**[Possible parallel, not identity]** **Seraphiel** appears most famously in *3 Enoch* 26 as prince over the seraphim. The text describes his fiery radiance, eagle-like body, immense crown, command over four seraphim, and direction of their heavenly song. *3 Enoch* is a late-antique/early-medieval Hekhalot work whose final redaction is commonly placed around the fifth or sixth century, with a complicated transmission history.

Seraphiel does not blow the resurrection trumpet in this chapter. His relationship to song, burning beings, throne service, and an *-el* name makes comparison with Isrāfīl suggestive. It does not establish that they are the same angel.

### Gnostic corpora and heresiologists

**[Negative finding]** Searches of the Nag Hammadi corpus and standard angel/archon lists in Irenaeus, Hippolytus, and Epiphanius yield no securely attested Israfil. Israfil does not belong to an ancient “Gnostic pantheon” on present evidence.

Names such as Iao, Sabaoth, Saklas, Yaldabaoth, Michael, Gabriel, and numerous planetary or archontic powers occur in Gnostic and heresiological sources. None supplies a documented Isrāfīl episode. Assigning Israfil to a Gnostic pantheon is therefore **[modern classificatory invention]** unless it means only that a modern reference project places Abrahamic supernatural beings and Gnostic figures in one umbrella dataset.

### Grimoires

**[Negative finding]** Isrāfīl is not a demonstrable spirit of the core *Lemegeton* or the standard medieval/early-modern *Abramelin* textual tradition. Those works name many Judeo-Christian angels, demons, and divine names, but a claim that Isrāfīl belongs to their original spirit catalogues requires a manuscript citation that has not been produced.

Modern occult handbooks sometimes incorporate Israfel as an angel of music, resurrection, the trumpet, or the element of air. This is **[modern occult reception]**, commonly influenced by Poe and general angel dictionaries rather than by the *Lemegeton*.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Core domains

1. **The final trumpet.** **[Later canonical elaboration]** Isrāfīl’s defining function is to sound the trumpet at God’s command. The event is Qur’anic; the personal attribution is post-Qur’anic.

2. **Resurrection.** **[Later canonical elaboration]** He does not independently resurrect the dead. God causes death and resurrection; Isrāfīl executes the commanded signal.

3. **Readiness and vigilance.** **[Early hadith image]** The trumpet bearer has the horn at his mouth, forehead inclined, listening for permission.

4. **Heavenly liturgy or music.** **[Medieval development, amplified by modern reception]** Some Muslim narratives associate his voice with celestial praise. Poe transformed this into the central image of an angel whose heartstrings form a lute. “Angel of music” is not his Qur’anic office.

5. **Transmission of divine commands.** **[Narrative/learned systematization]** Some reports place Isrāfīl nearest the Throne, receiving commands and passing them to Michael, Gabriel, or the Angel of Death. This is not uniform dogma.

6. **Throne bearing.** **[Regional or later report]** Some traditions count him among the Bearers of the Throne. The Qur’an mentions throne bearers but does not name him.

### Number of trumpet blasts

- **Two-blast model:** Grounded most directly in Qur’an 39:68—death/collapse, then resurrection. This became the preferred model of many theologians and hadith critics.
- **Three-blast model:** Terror, death, and resurrection, constructed by distinguishing Qur’an 27:87 from 39:68 and elaborated in the long trumpet narrative.
- **Assessment:** The Qur’an unequivocally describes multiple eschatological trumpet events but does not provide a numbered doctrinal scheme or name Isrāfīl. The three-blast account is textually later and often rests on weak narration.

### Appearance

**[Narrative tradition]** Reports give Isrāfīl enormous cosmic dimensions: feet beneath or upon the lowest earth, head near or beneath the Throne, and multiple wings extending across the heavens. Four-wing descriptions are widespread, although reports vary.

**[Narrative tradition]** Some accounts distinguish wings used for covering the body, wings extending east and west, and a wing used as a veil before God. Exact configurations are not stable.

**[Narrative tradition]** Tears or weeping appear in descriptions of Isrāfīl contemplating hell or divine majesty. Claims that his tears would inundate the earth if God did not restrain them belong to expansive angelological storytelling.

No Qur’anic physical description of Isrāfīl exists.

### Trumpet

The Arabic **ṣūr** was understood by dominant hadith interpretation as a horn, reinforced by the response **qarn yunfakhu fīhi**, “a horn into which one blows.” Some early lexical speculation read *ṣūr* in relation to “forms/images,” but classical exegesis overwhelmingly adopted the horn.

**[Weak narrative]** The long trumpet report describes apertures corresponding to created souls. Such mechanics are not Qur’anic and depend on contested material.

### Visual art

Islamic aniconism was never historically uniform. Manuscript painting, especially in Persianate and Ottoman settings, produced identifiable images of Isrāfīl:

- **Circa 1370–80:** Iraqi folios from al-Qazwīnī’s *ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt* depict a turbaned, winged Israfil blowing a trumpet.
- **Later medieval Persianate manuscript traditions:** Cosmographies depict him frontally or in profile, often with bright multicolored wings and an elongated horn.
- **Ottoman, 1717:** Walters manuscript W.659 includes a folio explicitly catalogued as “The Angel Israfil.”
- **Sixteenth-century Ottoman materials:** Images attributed to the *Siyer-i Nebi* tradition circulate as “Israfil blowing the seven-fold trumpet,” but individual online attributions must be checked against the actual manuscript folio; some reproductions have weak provenance.

The trumpet—not face, color, costume, or wing count—is the most dependable iconographic identifier. “Seven-fold trumpet” is not a stable scriptural specification.

### Colors, planets, days, and elements

**[Learned systematization]** The Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ linked Isrāfīl with the sphere of the Sun in their philosophical angelology. Other occult tables assign different planets, colors, directions, or elements.

There is no universally canonical Islamic color, weekday, number, plant, animal, incense, or zodiacal sign belonging to Isrāfīl. Tables giving him Sunday, the Sun, yellow or gold, air, music, or resurrection are philosophical, magical, or modern correspondences and must not be retrojected into the Qur’an.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Origin and hierarchy

Islamic angels normally have functions, ranks, and assignments rather than biological genealogies. No canonical source gives Isrāfīl parents, spouse, siblings, or offspring.

**[Later systematization]** Calling Gabriel, Michael, Isrāfīl, and the Angel of Death “four brothers” is metaphorical or folkloric unless a particular report is cited. They are more properly co-servants in a celestial administration.

Isrāfīl remains wholly subordinate to God. He neither decides when the world ends nor possesses an autonomous power of resurrection.

### Every documented named interaction or joint appearance

- **God — command and subordination.** God assigns or is expected to assign Isrāfīl the trumpet; Isrāfīl watches the Throne and awaits permission. Qur’an 39:68 supplies the divine eschatological action without naming the angel; later hadith and tafsir name him. **[Qur’anic event plus later identification]**

- **God — invoked as Isrāfīl’s Lord.** Muḥammad’s night prayer calls upon “the Lord of Gabriel, Michael, and Israfil.” *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* 770, compiled ninth century. **[Early canonical]**

- **Jibrīl/Gabriel — joint invocation.** Isrāfīl and Jibrīl are named together in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* 770. No action between them is narrated there. **[Early canonical joint appearance]**

- **Mīkāʾīl/Michael — joint invocation.** Isrāfīl and Mīkāʾīl are named together in the same prayer. No interpersonal episode occurs. **[Early canonical joint appearance]**

- **Jibrīl/Gabriel — succession in prophetic instruction.** A report preserved by al-Ṭabarī says Isrāfīl attended Muḥammad for three years before Gabriel brought revelation. This is not the dominant canonical account. **[Narrative variant, late ninth/early tenth-century attestation]**

- **Jibrīl, Mīkāʾīl, Angel of Death — transmitted commands.** Some later angelological reports place Isrāfīl beneath the Throne receiving divine commands and distributing them to these angels. **[Later learned/narrative systematization]**

- **Gabriel and Michael — death before the resurrection.** Expansive eschatological narratives have the principal angels die in a sequence, often followed by Isrāfīl and then the Angel of Death; accounts disagree about the order. **[Weak narrative tradition]**

- **Angel of Death/ʿAzrāʾīl — Isrāfīl’s soul taken.** In some narratives the Angel of Death takes Isrāfīl’s soul after the first blast; God later restores Isrāfīl so that he may sound the resurrection blast. Neither the name ʿAzrāʾīl nor this sequence is Qur’anic. **[Medieval accretion]**

- **Muḥammad — named in prophetic prayer.** Isrāfīl is an object of reference, not invocation as a deity, in the prayer narrated by ʿĀʾisha. **[Early canonical]**

- **Muḥammad — alleged pre-revelatory instruction.** The al-Shaʿbī report in al-Ṭabarī makes Isrāfīl Muḥammad’s attendant before Gabriel. **[Narrative variant]**

- **Raphael — no narrative interaction.** The two are equated by later comparative writers; no ancient text stages a meeting between them. **[Syncretistic identification; absence of interaction]**

- **Seraphiel — no narrative interaction.** They occur in distinct traditions and texts. Proposed identity is etymological or comparative, not an episode. **[Modern scholarly comparison]**

- **Metatron — Seraphiel, not Isrāfīl, interacts with Metatron.** In *3 Enoch*, Metatron describes Seraphiel to Rabbi Ishmael. Equating that Seraphiel with Isrāfīl would be an additional hypothesis. **[Distinct Jewish tradition]**

- **Satan/Iblis — no secure direct encounter.** *3 Enoch* associates Seraphiel’s seraphim with destroying accusations written by Satan, Sammael, and Dubiel, but this cannot simply be transferred to Isrāfīl. **[No documented Isrāfīl interaction]**

- **Sammael and Dubiel — no Isrāfīl interaction.** Their episode belongs to *3 Enoch*’s Seraphiel complex. **[Negative finding]**

- **Gnostic archons — no documented interaction.** No Nag Hammadi or heresiological episode joins Isrāfīl to Yaldabaoth, Saklas, Sabaoth, Sophia, Barbelo, or another named Gnostic power. **[Negative finding]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. The waiting trumpeter

**Earliest core:** The Qur’an establishes the trumpet and world-ending/resurrection sequence but not its angel. The hadith of the “possessor of the horn” supplies the image of an angel already holding the instrument to his mouth, forehead bent and hearing inclined.

**Later development:** Tafsir identifies this angel as Isrāfīl. Sermons intensify the image: he has not blinked since receiving the trumpet, fearing that the command may arrive before his gaze returns; his eyes resemble brilliant stars; one foot advances while another remains behind in readiness.

**Function:** The narrative makes the Last Judgment immediate rather than remote. In the hadith, the companions respond with the formula “God is sufficient for us and the best disposer of affairs; in God we trust.”

### B. The two or three blasts

**Two-blast sequence:** At the first blowing, inhabitants of heaven and earth fall dead or senseless except those God wills; at the second, they stand resurrected. This is closest to Qur’an 39:68.

**Three-blast sequence:** A blast of terror throws creation into panic; a second causes death; a third raises the dead. The long trumpet report integrates numerous Qur’anic eschatological passages into a continuous narrative.

**Textual judgment:** Three blasts are an exegetical harmonization, not an explicit Qur’anic enumeration. The long report naming Isrāfīl is isnād-weak according to significant hadith criticism, though culturally influential.

### C. The deaths of the angels

**[Medieval narrative]** After earthly and heavenly creatures perish, God orders the deaths of principal angels. Versions differ over Gabriel, Michael, Isrāfīl, and the Angel of Death and over who dies last. Isrāfīl must then be restored before the resurrection blast in versions where he has died.

The story dramatizes divine uniqueness—every created power, including the greatest angels, is mortal before God. It is not stated in this form by the Qur’an.

### D. Isrāfīl and Muḥammad before revelation

Al-Ṭabarī preserves a report attributed to al-Shaʿbī that Isrāfīl accompanied or instructed Muḥammad for three years before Gabriel was assigned to him. The report never displaced Gabriel’s dominant office as bearer of Qur’anic revelation. It should be retained as an early Abbasid-era attestation of a variant prophetic narrative, not harmonized into the standard sīra.

### E. Isrāfīl’s cosmic body and tears

Cosmographies and angel collections describe an immense body spanning cosmic levels. In some accounts he looks repeatedly toward hell and weeps so abundantly that his tears would flood the earth. These motifs express angelic awe, compassion, and cosmic scale. Precise anatomy varies and none is Qur’anic.

### F. Heavenly song

Muslim material sometimes praises Isrāfīl’s voice in the celestial liturgy. Poe’s nineteenth-century poem magnified this strand until song became his defining characteristic. The modern “angel of music” is therefore a reception history produced from a limited premodern motif, not an adequate summary of early Islamic evidence.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### No independent cult

There are no documented ancient temples, priesthoods, sacrificial economies, public festivals, temple estates, or pilgrimage centers devoted specifically to Isrāfīl. Islamic worship is directed to God, not to angels.

No securely documented human or animal sacrifice to Isrāfīl exists. No evidence connects him with temple prostitution, cult violence, or an independent political priesthood.

### Prayer and devotion

The principal early ritual evidence is the night prayer in *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* 770. It addresses God as the Lord of Isrāfīl, not Isrāfīl himself.

Later eschatological preaching uses the trumpet hadith to encourage repentance, vigilance, and trust in God. Formulae such as **ḥasbunā Allāhu wa-niʿma al-wakīl** are prescribed in response to the terror of the waiting trumpeter.

Angel names, including Isrāfīl, occur in later devotional manuals, talismanic diagrams, prayers, and amulets across Arabic, Persianate, Ottoman, South Asian, and African manuscript cultures. Each object requires separate dating; the presence of his name in an amulet does not demonstrate an independent cult.

### Image use

Paintings of Isrāfīl occur chiefly as manuscript illustration—cosmography, wonders-of-creation literature, prophetic history, and eschatology—not as temple idols. Their function was literary, didactic, courtly, contemplative, or decorative.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the seventh century

Trumpet eschatology and named archangels existed in Jewish and Christian traditions, but no securely identifiable Isrāfīl is attested. Seraphiel in Hekhalot literature is a possible name-parallel, not a demonstrated precursor in function.

### Seventh century: Qur’anic foundation

The Qur’an establishes repeated trumpet imagery, cosmic terror, death, assembly, and resurrection. It names Gabriel and Michael elsewhere but leaves the trumpet blower anonymous. This silence is historically important.

### Eighth–ninth centuries: hadith formation

Reports circulate concerning the unnamed “possessor of the horn.” The name Isrāfīl is preserved in prayer tradition and becomes associated with the trumpet bearer. By the compilation of *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim*, *Jāmiʿ al-Tirmidhī*, and related collections, both elements are documented, although not always joined in the same matn.

### Ninth–tenth centuries: tafsir and universal history

Al-Ṭabarī records trumpet traditions and the unusual report of Isrāfīl’s pre-Gabrielic attendance on Muḥammad. Exegetes increasingly treat the trumpeter’s name as known, even when the underlying Qur’anic or hadith wording remains anonymous.

### Eleventh–thirteenth centuries: narrative expansion

*Qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ*, eschatological works, cosmography, and philosophical encyclopedias expand Isrāfīl’s body, wings, trumpet, throne proximity, song, tears, and administrative relations. Al-Qazwīnī’s *ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt* helps establish his visual type.

### Fourteenth–sixteenth centuries: manuscript iconography and compilation

Persianate, Iraqi, and Ottoman illustrators represent him as a winged angel with a horn. Al-Suyūṭī compiles accumulated angel traditions into a major late-medieval collection. The figure is fully stabilized in popular Sunni cosmology as the eschatological trumpeter.

### Early-modern period

Isrāfīl remains prominent in sermons, cosmologies, illustrated manuscripts, and eschatological writing. Western grimoires do not show a correspondingly secure, continuous Isrāfīl tradition. Claims of his presence in the *Lemegeton* or original *Abramelin* catalogues are unsubstantiated.

### Nineteenth century: Poe’s reinvention

Edgar Allan Poe published “Israfel” in *Poems* (1831). His prefatory claim—derived from George Sale’s orientalist presentation of Islam—that Israfel’s heart was a lute and that he possessed the sweetest voice of God’s creatures, converted the eschatological angel into a Romantic emblem of heavenly artistic perfection.

Poe contrasts Israfel’s celestial freedom with the earthly poet’s constrained life:

> “If I could dwell / Where Israfel / Hath dwelt…”

This is **[modern literary reinvention]**. Poe’s Israfel became more influential in English-language literature than most medieval Islamic details.

Arthur Edward Waite’s *Israfel: Letters, Visions and Poems* (1886) further used the name in a fin-de-siècle literary and esoteric atmosphere. Hervey Allen titled his 1934 Poe biography *Israfel*, fusing the angel with Poe himself.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Israfel entered angel dictionaries, occult correspondences, fantasy literature, music, comics, animation, and games. The “angel of music” characterization often derives primarily from Poe.

The Japanese *Neon Genesis Evangelion* franchise uses Israfel as the name of an “Angel” capable of division into paired bodies. This is a modern fictional borrowing, not Islamic angelology.

The *Megami Tensei* game franchise depicts Israfel among recruitable or summonable supernatural beings and blends Islamic, Jewish, Christian, and occult reference material. Its statistics, alignment, and appearance are modern game design.

Modern Muslim teaching continues to present Isrāfīl as the trumpet angel, frequently as one of four principal angels. Some scholars and preachers note the evidentiary qualification: his name is not in the Qur’an, and the strongest “horn at his mouth” hadith does not itself name him.

No measurable organized “neopagan worship” of Isrāfīl has been documented. Individual occult invocation may occur, but it is decentralized modern esotericism, not a demonstrated survival or revival of an ancient cult.

No substantial modern extremist movement centered on Isrāfīl has been documented. Apocalyptic movements may invoke the trumpet generally, but that is not evidence of an Israfil cult.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Orientalism and literary transmission

George Sale’s eighteenth-century English presentation of Islamic lore supplied European readers with numerous details detached from their isnād and genre distinctions. Poe’s epigraph treated one such account as “the Koran,” even though neither Isrāfīl’s name nor the heart-lute motif occurs in the Qur’an. This misattribution materially shaped Western reception.

Poe’s spelling **Israfel** became an English literary name associated with music, poetic genius, and unattainable celestial beauty. It is a reception form, not a more ancient spelling.

### Occultism

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century occult compilers increasingly treated all named angels as available for correspondence tables or ritual invocation. Israfel was assigned such domains as music, resurrection, announcement, air, or the trumpet.

Davidson’s *A Dictionary of Angels* is useful as an index to this reception but frequently mixes scripture, apocrypha, grimoires, poetry, folklore, and modern secondary assertions. Its entries require verification at the cited source.

The Golden Dawn’s core rituals prominently use Raphael, Gabriel, Michael, and Uriel, not a historically Islamic Isrāfīl as a standard quarter archangel. Later eclectic practitioners may substitute or add Israfel. Such substitution is **[modern syncretism]**.

### Modern commercial and popular culture

Israfel appears in band names, song titles, novels, role-playing systems, anime, and video games. Recurring modern traits include:

- angel of music;
- celestial singer;
- herald of apocalypse;
- resurrection magic;
- sound- or vibration-based powers;
- paired identity or duplication;
- conflation with Raphael or Seraphiel.

Only the herald/trumpet theme has a strong basis in Islamic tradition. The rest ranges from selective adaptation to outright invention.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is the trumpeter named in sound hadith?

Two claims must be separated:

1. A sound hadith names Isrāfīl: *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* 770 does.
2. A sound hadith explicitly says, “Isrāfīl is the trumpet blower”: this is much harder to demonstrate.

Al-Ḥalīmī reportedly treated the identification as agreed upon; Ibn Ḥajar notes explicit naming in several reports but also discusses the weak route of the long trumpet narrative. Modern traditionalist writers divide between accepting the accumulated reports and consensus-like reception, and insisting that no individually sound marfūʿ report securely joins the name to the office.

The historically precise formulation is: by classical Islam, Isrāfīl was overwhelmingly recognized as the trumpeter; the Qur’an and strongest concise trumpet reports do not themselves name him.

### Two blasts or three?

Two rests most directly on Qur’an 39:68. Three results from treating the terror of 27:87 as a separate event and is elaborated by the weak long trumpet report. Both are historically attested interpretations; they should not be silently merged.

### Seraphiel, Raphael, or neither?

- **Seraphiel:** best phonological comparison, especially given throne and song associations.
- **Raphael:** common functional/table-based Western equation but poor linguistic and functional match.
- **Independent Islamic development:** Burge stresses that Isrāfīl is so distinctive in Islamic tradition that simple derivation from a Jewish or Christian counterpart is inadequate.

No surviving text documents a historical moment when Muslim transmitters explicitly renamed Seraphiel or Raphael as Isrāfīl. All origin theories remain reconstructions.

### “Angel of music”

Islamic tradition contains heavenly voice and praise motifs, but the modern defining label largely reflects Poe. Poe’s attribution of the heart-lute saying to the Qur’an is false as a textual claim.

### Gnostic Israfil

No primary evidence has been established. Neither Nag Hammadi texts nor the chief patristic catalogues of Gnostic beings document him. Modern databases that group him with Gnostic figures are taxonomic projects, not evidence of ancient Gnostic belief.

### Grimoire Israfil

Neither the *Lemegeton* nor the standard *Abramelin* corpus supplies a secure historic Isrāfīl entry. Later angel manuals and internet lists frequently back-project modern syncretic material into “ancient grimoires.”

### State of the evidence

The central sources survive principally as manuscripts and later critical editions rather than contemporary seventh-century documents. Hadith evidence must be assessed both as a dated compilation and as a claimed chain of earlier transmission. Islamic paintings are preserved in institutions including the British Museum, Walters Art Museum, and Smithsonian’s National Museum of Asian Art.

Stephen Burge’s research is particularly important because it treats Islamic angelology as a developed field rather than a derivative appendix to biblical studies. His work also demonstrates how al-Suyūṭī’s collecting preserves incompatible traditions side by side.

## 10. Chronology

- **First millennium BCE:** Hebrew scriptures attest divine and eschatological trumpet imagery, but no Israfil.
- **Third–first centuries BCE:** Jewish apocalyptic literature develops named archangels; *1 Enoch* knows Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, and others, but not Isrāfīl.
- **Late first century CE:** New Testament texts connect trumpets with resurrection, assembly, and angelic action; no Israfil.
- **Approximately fifth–sixth century CE:** *3 Enoch*’s final literary development includes Seraphiel, prince of the seraphim. Proposed connection with Israfil remains comparative.
- **Early seventh century:** Qur’an describes the blowing of *al-ṣūr*, explicitly including the two blowings of 39:68; the blower is unnamed.
- **Eighth–ninth centuries:** Hadith tradition circulates the “possessor of the horn” report and traditions naming Isrāfīl.
- **Before 875:** *Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim* preserves the prayer naming Jibrīl, Mīkāʾīl, and Isrāfīl.
- **Late ninth century:** Al-Tirmidhī records the hadith of the horn already placed to the angel’s mouth; the angel remains unnamed in the matn.
- **Late ninth–early tenth century:** Al-Ṭabarī compiles trumpet exegesis and the report that Isrāfīl attended Muḥammad before Gabriel.
- **Early eleventh century:** Al-Thaʿlabī’s *ʿArāʾis al-majālis* exemplifies expanded *qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ* angelology.
- **Tenth century:** Ikhwān al-Ṣafāʾ philosophical cosmology associates Isrāfīl with the solar sphere.
- **Thirteenth century:** Al-Qazwīnī’s *ʿAjāʾib al-makhlūqāt* provides an influential cosmographic setting for Isrāfīl descriptions and later illustrations.
- **Circa 1370–80:** Surviving Iraqi manuscript paintings show Isrāfīl blowing a trumpet.
- **Fifteenth century:** Ibn Kathīr’s eschatological compilation preserves the long trumpet account while exposing weaknesses in its transmission.
- **Late fifteenth century:** Al-Suyūṭī’s *al-Ḥabāʾik* systematizes a vast corpus of angel reports.
- **Sixteenth–eighteenth centuries:** Persianate and Ottoman manuscript art continues the winged trumpeter iconography.
- **1717:** Walters manuscript W.659 depicts “The Angel Israfil.”
- **1734:** George Sale’s English Qur’an and commentary become an important conduit of Islamic lore to anglophone writers.
- **1831:** Poe publishes “Israfel,” transforming him into the paradigmatic celestial musician.
- **1886:** Arthur Edward Waite publishes *Israfel: Letters, Visions and Poems*.
- **1934:** Hervey Allen publishes *Israfel: The Life and Times of Edgar Allan Poe*.
- **1967:** Gustav Davidson’s *A Dictionary of Angels* popularizes a composite angelological Israfel for anglophone readers.
- **Late twentieth century:** Israfel enters occult reference works, role-playing games, Japanese games, and animation.
- **1995–96:** *Neon Genesis Evangelion* uses Israfel as the name of a fictional Angel.
- **2010:** Stephen Burge completes a major study and translation project on al-Suyūṭī’s angelology.
- **2024–26:** Museum display, digitization, and online hadith and manuscript catalogues continue to expand public access; popular conflation with Raphael, Seraphiel, and “angel of music” remains common.
- **Present:** Isrāfīl remains a living figure of Muslim eschatological belief, overwhelmingly understood as the trumpet angel, while scholarship preserves the distinction between Qur’anic trumpet imagery and its later personal identification.
