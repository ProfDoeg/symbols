# Leviathan: Research Dossier

**Class:** Primordial sea monster or chaos-dragon in the Hebrew Bible; a created marine creature in some biblical passages; an eschatological monster in Second Temple and rabbinic Judaism; allegorically Satan, the Devil, or Hell in much Christian exegesis and art; a high-ranking demon only in medieval and early-modern magical classifications and their modern descendants. Leviathan is not securely attested as an independently worshipped god.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest securely datable cognate and narrative antecedent is Ugaritic **ltn** (𐎍𐎚𐎐, conventionally *Lôtānu/Litānu*, “Lotan”) in the Baal Cycle tablets from Late Bronze Age Ugarit, conventionally dated to the thirteenth century BCE, though the traditions may be older. The earliest Hebrew attestations of **לִוְיָתָן** (*liwyātān*) are biblical poems whose individual dates remain disputed: Psalm 74, Isaiah 27:1, Job 3:8 and 40:25–41:26 in Hebrew numbering, and Psalm 104:26. The oldest surviving physical witnesses are Second Temple-period biblical manuscripts and translations, not autographs of those poems.

**Evidentiary caution:** Lotan is the strongest linguistic and mythological antecedent of Hebrew Leviathan, but “Lotan = Leviathan” is a historical-philological reconstruction, not an ancient bilingual equation preserved on a tablet. Likewise, the dragons of Revelation, Gnostic ouroboroi, Islamic cosmic fish, whales, crocodiles, Satan, and Hell-mouth are not automatically Leviathan: each association arose in a particular interpretive setting and must be dated separately.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Hebrew and cognate names

The Masoretic Hebrew name is **לִוְיָתָן**, *liwyātān*. Six occurrences appear under five biblical passages because Isaiah 27:1 names it twice:

- Job 3:8
- Job 40:25 in Hebrew numbering, normally Job 41:1 in English versions
- Psalm 74:14
- Psalm 104:26
- Isaiah 27:1, twice

The Ugaritic spelling is **ltn**, 𐎍𐎚𐎐. Because the consonantal Ugaritic script does not normally write vowels, proposed vocalizations—*Lôtānu*, *Lītānu*, *Lātānu*—are scholarly reconstructions. J. A. Emerton specifically questioned how Ugaritic *ltn* should be vocalized and whether its relation to Hebrew *liwyātān* can be expressed through a simple native Hebrew derivation.

A common Hebrew etymology connects the word with the root **לוה**, *l-w-h*, “to twist, wind, or join,” hence “the twisting/coiled one.” This fits the serpentine epithets but is not certain: the internal consonants and ending are difficult to derive regularly. A foreign loan or inherited Northwest Semitic proper name is therefore plausible. “Wreath-animal” and similarly exact translations are conjectures, not ancient glosses.

### Attested epithets

The closest Ugaritic and Hebrew formulas are strikingly parallel:

- Ugaritic **ltn bṯn brḥ**, “Lotan, the fleeing serpent”
- Ugaritic **bṯn ʿqltn**, “the twisting/crooked serpent”
- Ugaritic **šlyṭ d šbʿt rašm**, “the powerful one with seven heads,” though damaged contexts leave debate over whether every phrase names the same monster
- Hebrew **לִוְיָתָן נָחָשׁ בָּרִחַ**, *liwyātān nāḥāš bārîaḥ*, “Leviathan the fleeing serpent”
- Hebrew **לִוְיָתָן נָחָשׁ עֲקַלָּתוֹן**, *liwyātān nāḥāš ʿaqallātôn*, “Leviathan the twisting serpent”
- Hebrew **הַתַּנִּין אֲשֶׁר בַּיָּם**, *hattannîn ʾăšer bayyām*, “the dragon/sea monster that is in the sea” (Isaiah 27:1)

Psalm 74:14 gives Leviathan plural “heads,” recalling the seven-headed Ugaritic monster without explicitly saying seven. Job calls it “king over all the sons of pride” or “all proud beasts” (Job 41:34 in most English numbering), but this is a poetic description, not a cult title.

### Greek, Latin, and later forms

The Septuagint frequently translates rather than transliterates:

- Greek **δράκων**, *drakōn*, “dragon/serpent,” in Job and Isaiah
- In Psalm 104:26 the Greek textual tradition uses **δράκων**
- Psalm 74:14 similarly speaks of the “heads of the dragon”

The Vulgate generally preserves **Leviathan**, while medieval vernaculars produced *Leviatan*, *Léviathan*, *Leviatano*, *Leviatán*, and related forms. English *leviathan* eventually became a common noun for an immense creature, especially a whale, and—after Hobbes—for an overwhelmingly powerful state or institution.

### Shifting classification

- **Ugaritic antecedent:** A serpentine divine or quasi-divine adversary associated with Sea/Yamm and defeated in divine combat.
- **Biblical poetry:** Depending on passage, a many-headed chaos monster, an eschatological enemy, a formidable creature beyond human mastery, or a creature made by God to play in the sea.
- **Second Temple apocalyptic literature:** A female primordial monster paired with male Behemoth and reserved for eschatological judgment or food.
- **Rabbinic Judaism:** A created primordial animal, sometimes a surviving male whose female mate was killed and salted for the righteous.
- **Christian exegesis:** Frequently an allegory or manifestation of Satan, the Devil, sinful pride, or the swallowing power of Hell.
- **Grimoires and demonology:** A prince or ruler among demons; this is a late classification, not biblical angelology.
- **Modern zoological interpretation:** Crocodile, whale, large marine animal, or a poetic composite; none resolves every passage.
- **Modern occultism and popular culture:** Elemental ruler of water, prince of Hell, dragon, kaiju, spaceship, corporation, or political metaphor.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Ugarit and Lotan

The Baal Cycle was found at Ras Shamra, ancient Ugarit, on clay tablets written in alphabetic cuneiform. The principal tablets belong to the Late Bronze Age and are associated with the scribe Ilimilku. In the combat complex, Baal and, in another passage, Anat boast of defeating serpentine opponents associated with Yamm/Sea, including Lotan. The formulas “fleeing serpent” and “twisting serpent” are so close to Isaiah 27:1 that literary continuity or reuse is highly probable.

What this evidence establishes is a Northwest Semitic combat tradition antedating the surviving Hebrew manuscripts. It does not prove that Israelites copied a particular extant Ugaritic tablet, that Lotan had a temple, or that Leviathan was ever worshipped in Israel.

### Hebrew Bible

**Psalm 74:13–14 — securely attested biblical mythic combat.** God breaks Sea, crushes the heads of the *tannînîm*, then crushes Leviathan’s heads and gives it as food “to the people/creatures of the wilderness.” The poem combines creation-combat imagery with national lament, possibly interpreting creation and Exodus through the same divine-war vocabulary. “People of the wilderness” and the Septuagint’s “Ethiopians” reflect an old textual difficulty.

**Psalm 104:25–26 — securely attested domestication.** Leviathan is not here an independent enemy: God made it “to play” in the sea, or possibly “to play with.” This is a significant theological transformation. The chaos-monster has become part of the ordered creation.

**Isaiah 27:1 — securely attested eschatological reuse.** On “that day” YHWH will punish Leviathan the fleeing and twisting serpent and kill the dragon in the sea. The verse closely reproduces Ugaritic serpent formulas but places the combat in the future. Proposed historical referents include imperial enemies, cosmic evil, and mythic chaos; the verse itself does not name Assyria, Babylon, Egypt, or Satan.

**Job 3:8 — securely attested magical allusion.** Job calls for professional day-cursers, “those ready to rouse Leviathan,” to curse the day of his birth. The verse presupposes specialists believed capable of disturbing cosmic order. It does not narrate such a ritual.

**Job 40:25–41:26 Hebrew/41:1–34 English — securely attested wisdom poem.** God asks whether Job can hook, leash, tame, sell, or domesticate Leviathan. Its armored scales, terrifying teeth, luminous eyes, smoking nostrils, fiery mouth, stone-hard heart, weapon resistance, jagged underside, and wake in the sea exceed ordinary zoology. Yet fishing, harpooning, merchant division, and aquatic behavior also invite animal comparison. Job is often dated to the Persian or early Hellenistic period; its extensive Aramaisms make a Persian-period setting likely, though its layers and final form remain disputed. [Yale University Press’s discussion](https://yalebooks.yale.edu/2020/08/05/the-historical-context-of-the-book-of-job/) summarizes the linguistic case, while Michael V. Fox’s study argues for a whale-based image rather than a purely demonic dragon. [Fox’s article](https://www.bsw.org/biblica/vol-93-2012/behemoth-and-leviathan/497/article-p264.html)

The Greek Job calls the creature **δράκων**, “dragon,” rather than preserving the Hebrew proper name. Its surviving major codices are fourth- and fifth-century CE, centuries later than the translation itself. [Swete’s Greek text](https://biblehub.com/sepd/job/41.htm)

### Second Temple Jewish literature

**1 Enoch 60:7–10, 24 — later canonical elaboration in Ethiopian Christianity; Jewish apocalyptic source.** Leviathan is explicitly female; Behemoth is male. They were separated, Leviathan dwelling above the ocean’s springs and Behemoth in the wilderness Dundayin east of Eden. They are reserved for the great day and, in the chapter’s later description, become food associated with judgment. The Book of Parables’ date is disputed, most commonly placed around the late first century BCE or first century CE; no Aramaic fragments of this particular section were found at Qumran. The complete text survives in Geʿez manuscripts. [Text of 1 Enoch 60](https://www.pseudepigrapha.com/pseudepigrapha/1enoch_all.html)

**4 Ezra/2 Esdras 6:49–52 — later apocalyptic elaboration, late first century CE.** God preserves Behemoth on dry land and Leviathan in the watery part of creation so that they may become food for those appointed for the end-time.

**2 Baruch 29:4 — later apocalyptic elaboration, late first or early second century CE.** Behemoth and Leviathan emerge and become food for survivors in the messianic age.

**Dead Sea Scrolls.** Leviathan is not a major named figure in the extant Qumran corpus. The scrolls preserve manuscripts of Job and wider combat-monster imagery, but popular claims of an elaborate Qumran “Leviathan cult” are unsupported. Fragmentary banquet and eschatological texts are sometimes compared with the Leviathan feast, but the comparison should not be turned into a named attestation unless the damaged text actually preserves the name.

**Jubilees, Tobit.** Neither supplies a developed named Leviathan narrative comparable to 1 Enoch 60. Their inclusion in bibliographic lists often reflects their relevance to Second Temple demonology generally, not direct Leviathan evidence.

### Rabbinic and medieval Jewish corpus

**Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 74b–75a — later canonical rabbinic elaboration, redacted approximately fifth–sixth centuries CE.** Genesis’s great sea monsters are interpreted as the male “slant serpent” and female “twisting serpent.” God castrates the male, kills and salts the female, preventing them from destroying the world. Gabriel will hunt the survivor with divine aid. Its flesh becomes the righteous’s feast; its skin becomes booths, coverings, necklaces, and radiant material spread over Jerusalem’s walls. The passage also describes its breath boiling the deep and its drinking furrowing the sea. [Bava Batra 74b](https://www.sefaria.org/Bava_Batra.74b)

This differs from 1 Enoch: Enoch makes Leviathan female and Behemoth male, while the Talmud makes “Leviathan” a sexually differentiated pair and preserves the male. These traditions should not be harmonized.

**Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer 10–11 — early-medieval Jewish elaboration.** Jonah’s fish fears being eaten by Leviathan. Jonah confronts Leviathan, displays Abraham’s covenantal seal, and makes it flee; Jonah announces its future capture for the righteous’s feast. [Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer](https://www.sefaria.org/Pirkei_DeRabbi_Eliezer.11/)

**Midrash and piyyut.** Genesis Rabbah, Leviticus Rabbah, later midrashim, and liturgical poetry elaborate the primordial pair, divine play, and eschatological banquet. The *Akdamut* poem recited in Ashkenazi communities at Shavuot famously stages a future contest between Leviathan and Behemoth: Behemoth’s horn gores Leviathan, whose fins slaughter Behemoth, after which both feed the righteous. This is liturgical-poetic elaboration, not biblical narrative.

**Zohar — medieval mystical systematization, chiefly late thirteenth-century Castile.** Zoharic passages use Leviathan as a cosmic, encircling, or unifying serpent and develop erotic and cosmological symbolism from the male-female traditions. Attributions of these passages to the second-century sage Simeon bar Yohai belong to the text’s traditional pseudepigraphy; historical scholarship associates the core Zohar with Moses de León’s milieu.

**Hekhalot literature and Sefer Raziel.** These corpora concern palaces, angels, divine names, and magic, but Leviathan is not consistently a principal angel or demon in their oldest recoverable strata. Modern lists that place it in a fixed “Abrahamic angelic hierarchy” usually synthesize later demonology.

### Christian corpus

The **New Testament never names Leviathan**. Revelation 12:3–9 and 20:2 identifies a great dragon and “ancient serpent” with Devil and Satan; Revelation 13 has a many-headed beast from the sea. Christian readers connected these figures retrospectively with Psalm 74, Isaiah 27, and Job. That is a canonical intertextual identification, not a lexical New Testament attestation.

**Gregory the Great, *Moralia in Job*, c. 578–595 — late-antique Christian systematization.** Gregory interprets Leviathan repeatedly as the ancient enemy, Devil, or Satan, whose members are wicked persons. Christ catches the monster by a hook: his visible humanity is bait concealing divinity. This enormously influential commentary helped make the satanic reading standard in the Latin Middle Ages. [Gregory, *Moralia* XXXIII](https://www.lectionarycentral.com/GregoryMoralia/Book33.html)

**Thomas Aquinas, *Expositio super Iob*, thirteenth century — scholastic interpretation.** Aquinas also treats Job’s description in a literal-natural register and is among premodern interpreters associated with the whale identification. This demonstrates that medieval exegesis was not uniformly “Leviathan = Satan.”

**Pseudo-Dionysius.** The Dionysian celestial hierarchy does not give Leviathan a place among the nine angelic orders. Claims that it does are modern conflations.

**Dante.** Dante’s *Commedia* profoundly shaped Western Hell but does not make a named Leviathan a major character. Treating every devouring infernal monster in Dante as Leviathan is interpretive overreach.

**Milton, *Paradise Lost* (1667; revised 1674) — literary reception.** Milton compares Satan’s gigantic prostrate body to Leviathan, the immense sea beast mistaken by sailors for an island. This is a simile, not a claim that Satan and Leviathan are literally one being.

### Islamic corpus

The Qurʾan does not name Leviathan, and no canonical hadith securely identifies an entity by the Hebrew name. Later Islamic cosmography knows enormous fish or whales—most famously traditions eventually grouped under **Bahamut**—supporting the earth or inhabiting the cosmic ocean. These have sometimes been equated with Leviathan by European comparativists and modern popular writers, but that is a syncretistic comparison, not a Qurʾanic identification.

Al-Ṭabarī and other exegetes transmit cosmic-ocean, great-fish, and primordial-creature traditions, but a claim that “Leviathan is an Islamic demon” requires a specific Arabic text and terminology. Neither **لوياثان**, a modern Arabic transliteration, nor biblical borrowings in modern Arabic Bibles establish an indigenous Qurʾanic figure. The same caution applies to conflating Leviathan with the Qurʾanic sea creature that swallowed Jonah.

### Gnostic and Manichaean material

No principal Nag Hammadi tractate supplies a straightforward biography of a named Hebrew Leviathan. Late antique Gnostic and related cosmologies do, however, employ world-encircling dragons or ouroboroi, and later texts such as the *Pistis Sophia* tradition use **Leviathan** for a dragon-like cosmic power associated with the outer darkness and punishment.

Thus the historically responsible label is **late-antique syncretistic identification**: biblical Leviathan was applied to a widespread ouroboros cosmogram. It is not evidence for a single “Gnostic Leviathan pantheon” shared by all Valentinians, Sethians, Basilideans, Ophites, or Manichaeans. A recent study discusses the world-enclosing ouroboros called Leviathan in this cosmological tradition. [“The Corrupted Wheel of Life”](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/opth-2024-0030/html?lang=en)

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and powers

Leviathan’s oldest stable domain is the **sea as dangerous, untamable, or residual chaos**. Its role changes substantially:

- In Psalm 74 it is conquered chaos.
- In Psalm 104 it is a playful creature within creation.
- In Isaiah 27 it is an enemy still awaiting final destruction.
- In Job it is the exemplary being humans cannot dominate.
- In apocalyptic Judaism it is stored food for the righteous.
- In rabbinic interpretation it threatens reproduction on a world-destroying scale.
- In Christianity it becomes the Devil, pride, death, or Hell.
- In political theory it becomes the sovereign commonwealth.
- In modern English it becomes any enormous animal, machine, state, corporation, or system.

### Form

The Hebrew sources give no single consistent body:

- Serpent in Isaiah 27
- Multiple heads in Psalm 74
- Armored aquatic monster in Job
- Female ocean monster in 1 Enoch
- Sexed pair in the Talmud
- Whale or giant fish in medieval and modern naturalization
- Dragon or enormous jaws in Christian art
- Ouroboros in some late-antique cosmologies

Job’s fire-breathing language is poetic but decisive for later dragon iconography. Its eyes resemble dawn; smoke comes from its nostrils; flame from its mouth; its scales admit no air; weapons fail; it churns the deep. Crocodilian armor, serpentine movement, whale-like surfacing and wake, and mythic fire coexist.

### Material images

No surviving Ugaritic image is securely inscribed “Lotan.” Multiheaded serpents on ancient Near Eastern seals and plaques offer comparative iconography, but their identification remains inferential.

The medieval **Hell-mouth**—a gigantic animal’s jaws receiving the damned—crystallized in Western art around the tenth century. Job’s fiery, toothed Leviathan contributed to the motif alongside biblical images of the pit, the devouring lion, the dragon, and Satan. Calling every Hell-mouth “a portrait of Leviathan” is too simple, but medieval texts and later art-historical terminology repeatedly associate the jaws with it. [García Arranz’s iconographic study](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/DMAE/en/article/view/66814)

Important examples include Anglo-Saxon and Romanesque Last Judgment and Harrowing of Hell images, later illuminated psalters, mystery-play stage machinery, and monumental church sculpture. The monster ceases to be a sea body and becomes architectural space: its mouth is Hell’s entrance.

### Animals and substances

No ancient source assigns Leviathan a fixed sacred animal, plant, color, number, weekday, or planet. Claims that its color is blue, its direction west, and its element water are primarily modern occult systematizations. Seven heads have ancient Ugaritic support; “seven” is therefore not wholly modern, but the Hebrew Bible only says “heads.”

Rabbinic sources make its **flesh, skin, and radiance** eschatological materials. The female’s flesh is salted; the survivor’s hide roofs booths and illuminates Jerusalem.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

Leviathan has no securely attested parents, consort, offspring, or temple genealogy.

The Ugaritic antecedent Lotan belongs to Yamm/Sea’s combat complex and is opposed by Baal and Anat. Biblical authors transfer victory over sea-monsters to YHWH without preserving a genealogy. Second Temple apocalypse pairs Leviathan with Behemoth by complementary habitat and sex, not by parentage. The Talmud creates male and female Leviathans but prevents offspring.

### Documented being-to-being interactions

- **Lotan — Yamm/Sea:** Lotan appears within Yamm’s hostile sea-monster complex; the precise relation “servant,” “ally,” or manifestation varies by reconstruction. *Baal Cycle*, KTU 1.3 and 1.5, thirteenth century BCE tablets. **Secure antecedent; disputed internal identification.**
- **Lotan — Baal/Hadad:** Baal is said to have smitten the fleeing serpent/Lotan and destroyed the twisting serpent. *Baal Cycle*, KTU 1.5 I. **Secure Ugaritic attestation.**
- **Lotan — Anat:** Anat boasts that she destroyed Yamm, River, the twisting serpent, and the seven-headed monster. *Baal Cycle*, KTU 1.3 III. Whether every epithet here denotes Lotan is debated. **Secure text; identification partly reconstructed.**
- **Leviathan — YHWH:** YHWH crushes Leviathan’s heads. Psalm 74:14, likely first millennium BCE. **Secure biblical attestation.**
- **Leviathan — YHWH:** YHWH creates Leviathan to play in the sea. Psalm 104:26. **Secure biblical attestation; theological domestication.**
- **Leviathan — YHWH:** YHWH will punish and kill Leviathan with a great sword. Isaiah 27:1. **Secure biblical attestation; eschatological form.**
- **Leviathan — YHWH:** YHWH demonstrates that Job cannot capture what is under divine sovereignty. Job 40:25–41:26 Hebrew. **Secure biblical attestation.**
- **Leviathan — Behemoth:** Female Leviathan and male Behemoth are separated into sea and desert and reserved for the end. 1 Enoch 60; 4 Ezra 6; 2 Baruch 29. **Second Temple/later canonical apocalyptic elaboration.**
- **Male Leviathan — female Leviathan:** God castrates the male and kills and salts the female to prevent reproduction. Bava Batra 74b. **Rabbinic elaboration.**
- **Leviathan — Gabriel:** Gabriel will hunt Leviathan, but only with God’s aid. Bava Batra 74b. **Rabbinic elaboration.**
- **Leviathan — Jonah:** Jonah confronts Leviathan, shows Abraham’s covenantal seal, and makes it flee. Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer 10. **Early-medieval Jewish accretion.**
- **Leviathan — Satan/Devil:** Gregory the Great and other Christian exegetes identify Job’s Leviathan allegorically with the ancient enemy. *Moralia in Job*, c. 578–595. **Late-antique Christian systematization, not explicit biblical identity.**
- **Leviathan — Christ:** In patristic and medieval “divine fishhook” exegesis, Christ’s humanity baits and his divinity catches Leviathan/Satan. Gregory’s *Moralia* gives a mature Latin form. **Christian allegorical elaboration.**
- **Leviathan — Behemoth in combat:** Later Jewish poetry has them kill one another before the righteous’s banquet. Most famously *Akdamut*, eleventh century. **Medieval liturgical accretion.**
- **Leviathan — a Gnostic cosmic ruler or outer darkness:** Late-antique cosmological sources place a Leviathan-like dragon around the lower cosmos. **Syncretistic identification; not uniform across Gnosticism.**

No securely attested interaction exists between Leviathan and Michael, Raphael, Azazel, Samael, Lilith, Metatron, the Qurʾanic Iblis, or the Nag Hammadi demiurge Yaldabaoth in the earliest sources. Modern charts linking them generally combine unrelated corpora.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The chaos battle

**Early core:** A storm-god defeats Sea and serpentine monsters. Ugaritic poems preserve Baal’s and Anat’s victories; biblical poems recast the victory as YHWH’s. The repeated “fleeing serpent/twisting serpent” formula makes continuity unusually strong.

**Variants:**

- Psalm 74 places the killing in primordial or foundational time.
- Isaiah 27 postpones it to the eschatological future.
- Psalm 104 makes Leviathan a harmless created animal.
- Job refuses to narrate an actual battle and instead emphasizes divine ownership and human incapacity.

These are theological alternatives, not stages that can be securely placed in a single linear order.

Comparison with Mesopotamian Tiamat is illuminating at the level of chaos-combat structure, but no direct equation **Leviathan = Tiamat** occurs in an ancient source. The Ugaritic evidence is linguistically much closer.

### The two primordial monsters

In 1 Enoch, God separates female Leviathan and male Behemoth into sea and wilderness. 4 Ezra places their creation on the fifth and sixth days and reserves them as food. 2 Baruch makes them nourishment in the messianic age.

The cycle answers several questions: why sea and land have distinct monsters, why the monsters survive after creation, and how primordial danger will be converted into eschatological abundance.

### The rabbinic feast

Bava Batra 74b–75a radically expands the banquet:

1. God creates male and female Leviathans.
2. Their reproduction would destroy the world.
3. God castrates the male and kills and salts the female.
4. Gabriel’s future hunt requires divine assistance.
5. The righteous eat the flesh.
6. Remaining flesh is sold in Jerusalem.
7. The skin becomes booths or canopies proportionate to merit.
8. The leftover hide illuminates Jerusalem.

Later poetry adds the mutual slaughter of Leviathan and Behemoth. The famous “Leviathan banquet” is therefore a composite history: biblical hints about feeding wilderness creatures, Second Temple apocalyptic pairing, talmudic exegesis, and medieval liturgy.

### Leviathan as Satan and Hell

The Hebrew Bible does not say Leviathan is Satan. Christian identification developed by reading Job and Isaiah alongside Revelation’s dragon, “the ancient serpent, Devil and Satan.” Patristic exegesis then interpreted the hook, jaws, armor, and defeat christologically.

Medieval art externalized that reading as Hell-mouth. Leviathan became simultaneously:

- the Devil caught by Christ;
- the infernal power swallowing sinners;
- the entrance to Hell;
- and, occasionally, Hell’s whole monstrous body.

This is a real and influential tradition, but it is **Christian allegorical reception**, not the original sense of every biblical occurrence.

### Hobbes’s commonwealth

Thomas Hobbes’s *Leviathan* (1651) calls the commonwealth an artificial human and “great Leviathan,” even a “Mortal God” under the immortal God. The frontispiece represents a crowned sovereign whose body consists of many subjects, holding sword and crozier over civil and ecclesiastical landscapes. Its biblical motto, from Job, declares that no earthly power compares with it.

Hobbes did not merely use “big monster” as ornament: the creature’s unmatched power provided a figure for the artificial sovereign formed by covenant. [Full public-domain text](https://pages.uoregon.edu/rbear/hobbes/leviathan.html)

### The whale

The whale identification has several histories:

- Ancient Greek **κῆτος**, “sea monster/great fish,” could cover whales without modern taxonomy.
- Medieval bestiaries frequently conflated whales, islands, sea monsters, and enormous fish.
- Aquinas was among premodern interpreters favoring a whale-like literal referent.
- The King James Version translates Hebrew *tannînîm* in Genesis 1:21 as “great whales,” helping merge biblical sea monsters and cetaceans in English.
- Milton’s island-like Leviathan and Melville’s *Moby-Dick* made the whale association culturally dominant.
- Modern scholarship remains divided. Crocodile explains scales, belly, riverine capture, and teeth; whale explains great size, marine habitat, wake, surfacing, and sailors’ reports; dragon explains fire, invulnerability, and biblical/Ugaritic parallels. Fox argues that Job’s image is based on a whale but admits composite and fantastic features.

“Leviathan was simply a whale” is therefore not a recovered ancient zoological fact. It is one interpretive solution to a deliberately excessive poetic creature.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

No temple, priesthood, altar, festival, votive inscription, theophoric personal name, or sacrificial calendar is securely attributable to Leviathan.

At Ugarit, Yamm and Baal belong to documented religious literature, but Lotan is known as a defeated monster, not as the recipient of an independently demonstrated cult. In Israel and Judah, Leviathan occurs in poetry rather than cult inscriptions. Psalmic performance may have ritual settings, but that does not establish worship of the monster.

Job 3:8’s “those ready to rouse Leviathan” is the best evidence for an imagined ritual relationship. It refers to curse specialists, perhaps mythic or actual professionals, who could awaken a cosmic monster to darken a day. No ritual manual for doing so survives.

Rabbinic feast traditions are eschatological teachings, not evidence that Jewish communities sacrificed animals to Leviathan. Sukkot associations arise through wordplay on Job and the skin-booth promise; they are not a Leviathan cult festival.

### Contested-material findings

- **Human sacrifice:** No evidence connects human sacrifice to Leviathan worship.
- **Temple prostitution:** No evidence.
- **Cult violence:** No documented Leviathan cult existed to conduct it.
- **Suppression or forced conversion:** No identifiable historical worshipping community was suppressed.
- **Theophoric names:** No securely recognized Northwest Semitic personal names invoke Leviathan/Lotan as patron.
- **Modern extremist appropriation:** “Leviathan” is used rhetorically for states, global institutions, naval power, and alleged conspiratorial systems, but no single extremist cult forms a continuous ancient tradition.
- **Neopagan reconstruction:** Individual practitioners may venerate Lotan or Leviathan, but this is modern reconstruction or demonolatry, not demonstrated cult continuity.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Late Bronze Age: Lotan

Ugaritic tradition preserves a multiheaded serpent defeated by Baal or Anat. This is the earliest secure member of the name-and-motif complex.

### First millennium BCE: Israelite and Judahite transformation

Hebrew poets appropriate the old combat vocabulary for YHWH. Leviathan can be slain, awaiting slaughter, or peacefully created. This plurality resists the theory that biblical monotheism simply “demoted a foreign god into a demon”; the sources also naturalize and domesticate it.

### Hellenistic and Roman periods

The Septuagint translates Leviathan as a dragon. Jewish apocalypses pair it with Behemoth, assign sex and habitats, and reserve the monsters for the end-time feast. This is the decisive shift from scattered poetic allusions to connected narrative.

### Rabbinic period

The Babylonian Talmud integrates Genesis, Psalms, Isaiah, and Job through midrash. Leviathan becomes a primordial breeding threat, divine plaything, quarry of Gabriel, and food and architecture for the righteous.

### Late antiquity and the Middle Ages

Christian exegetes identify it with Satan through Revelation and develop Christ’s fishhook victory. Jewish mystics develop cosmic and unitive symbolism. Christian artists transform it into Hell-mouth. Bestiaries and natural philosophers oscillate among dragon, crocodile, whale, and allegory.

### Early modern demonology and political philosophy

The *Book of Abramelin* tradition lists Leviathan among major demonic rulers alongside Lucifer, Satan, and Belial. Seventeenth-century compilations such as the *Lemegeton* belong to the same broad grimoire environment, although Leviathan is not one of the Ars Goetia’s standard seventy-two spirits with an individual seal.

Peter Binsfeld’s 1589 demonological classification is popularly credited with assigning Leviathan to **envy** among seven infernal princes. This should be treated as early-modern learned demonology, not patristic consensus.

Hobbes then secularizes and monumentalizes the name as the sovereign state.

### Nineteenth century to present

Philology after the discovery of Ugarit in 1928–1929 transformed the discussion. Before Ugarit, interpreters could compare biblical monsters with Babylonian or classical dragons; after decipherment, *ltn* and Isaiah’s parallel epithets provided direct Northwest Semitic evidence.

Modern translations and commentaries alternate among retaining “Leviathan,” footnoting “crocodile,” and emphasizing the mythic dragon. Occult and popular media increasingly treat it as a named demon, elemental water prince, or kaiju.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Esotericism and Satanism

The *Abramelin* tradition’s high-ranking demon is an early source for occult Leviathan. Later demonological handbooks, including Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal*, consolidated the infernal reading.

Anton LaVey’s *The Satanic Bible* (1969) names Leviathan one of the Four Crown Princes of Hell, corresponding to **water** and **west**. The Church of Satan’s Sigil of Baphomet places the Hebrew letters spelling Leviathan around the pentagram. These elemental and directional correspondences are **modern inventions or codifications**, not biblical attributes.

Contemporary demonolatry and ceremonial magic may regard Leviathan as a personal spirit of water, emotion, the unconscious, chaos, or envy. These practices have documented modern textual lineages but no demonstrated continuity with Ugaritic or Israelite worship.

### Literature

- **Milton, *Paradise Lost*:** Leviathan as island-like whale in a simile for Satan.
- **Hobbes, *Leviathan*:** sovereign commonwealth.
- **Melville, *Moby-Dick* (1851):** “Leviathan” repeatedly denotes the whale and the whaling subject itself.
- **Julien Green, Paul Auster, and numerous later writers:** use the name for overwhelming persons, institutions, cities, or violence.
- **Scott Westerfeld’s *Leviathan*:** an engineered flying ecosystem, demonstrating the word’s separation from its sea-monster origin.

### Film, games, comics, and commercial imagery

Modern franchises use Leviathan for gigantic sea creatures, dragons, demons, alien vessels, war machines, and corporations. Prominent examples occur in *Final Fantasy*, *Dungeons & Dragons*, *Warhammer*, *Mass Effect*, *Supernatural*, *Hellraiser*, DC and Marvel properties, *Subnautica*, and *Destiny*. These versions often combine Job’s invulnerability, the Ugaritic serpent, the infernal prince, and the modern whale.

The recurrent “Leviathan is the demon prince of envy” in games and animation descends chiefly from early-modern demon classifications and modern occult handbooks. It is not an ancient Jewish doctrine.

### Political appropriation

After Hobbes, “Leviathan” became a contested political metaphor:

- defenders may use it for the state powerful enough to prevent civil war;
- libertarians and anarchists use it for coercive centralized government;
- nationalist and conspiratorial writers use it for empires, financial systems, global organizations, or an alleged “deep state.”

These mutually incompatible uses share Hobbes’s political vocabulary, not an ancient prophecy about a specific modern government.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Lotan and linguistic continuity

The dominant view connects Hebrew *liwyātān* with Ugaritic *ltn*. The near-verbatim serpent epithets in Isaiah 27:1 are stronger evidence than general resemblance. Debate remains over vocalization, exact etymology, whether Lotan is distinct from Yamm and Tunannu, and whether Psalm 74’s plural heads directly presuppose the Ugaritic seven-headed form.

### Dragon, crocodile, or whale?

Three principal approaches persist:

1. **Mythic dragon:** Best explains Ugaritic parallels, plural heads, fire, cosmic conflict, and eschatological death.
2. **Crocodile:** Best explains scales, teeth, belly, harpoons, and Egyptian or riverine associations.
3. **Whale or composite cetacean:** Best explains immense size, seafaring wonder, wake, and Psalm 104’s open sea.

A literary-composite view is methodologically strongest across the whole corpus: different poems adapt a traditional name to different theological and zoological purposes. It is unnecessary to make Psalm 74’s many-headed defeated being anatomically identical to Psalm 104’s playful creature or Job’s uncatchable animal.

### Creation combat

Older “myth-and-ritual” scholarship sometimes reconstructed an annual Israelite enthronement festival in which YHWH ritually reenacted victory over Sea. The poems certainly preserve combat imagery, but a specific annual Leviathan drama is not directly documented. Likewise, claims that Genesis 1 deliberately suppresses a full Leviathan battle are plausible literary models, not preserved narrative evidence.

### Satanic identity

The Hebrew Bible never calls Leviathan Satan. The identity emerges through Christian canonical synthesis, especially Revelation’s dragon plus patristic reading of Job. Gregory the Great is a central transmitter. Treating this as the “original biblical meaning” erases the history of interpretation.

### Gnostic identity

The evidence supports late-antique use of world-encircling dragons and occasional naming of such powers as Leviathan. It does not support a single pan-Gnostic god with a standardized genealogy. Neither Robinson’s nor Meyer’s collections of Nag Hammadi texts justify inserting Leviathan into every Gnostic system.

### Islamic identity

Leviathan is not Qurʾanic. Comparisons with Bahamut or the cosmic fish belong to comparative religion and the history of folklore. They may reflect shared late-antique cosmological environments, but derivation in either direction is difficult to prove.

### State of the evidence

- Ugaritic tablets are fragmentary but provide the oldest close name and formulas.
- Biblical composition dates are ranges; surviving manuscripts are later than composition.
- 1 Enoch 60 survives completely in Geʿez, not in an extant Qumran Aramaic copy.
- Rabbinic evidence is preserved in manuscripts later than the rabbis quoted.
- Medieval iconography is abundant, but identifying a monster as “Leviathan” requires inscriptions, textual context, or a defensible iconographic chain.
- No archaeological cult installation is known.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Leviathan was a dinosaur witnessed by Job.
- Leviathan was a literal fire-breathing marine reptile destroyed in the nineteenth or twentieth century.
- The Levites were originally Leviathan worshippers.
- Leviathan is explicitly one of Pseudo-Dionysius’s angelic orders.
- Leviathan appears by name in Revelation.
- Leviathan is a Qurʾanic demon.
- Every ouroboros is Leviathan.
- A continuous secret Leviathan cult survived from Ugarit to modern Satanism.
- Modern elemental correspondences are ancient Hebrew teaching.

All are unsupported, overgeneralized, or demonstrably late.

## 10. Chronology

- **c. thirteenth century BCE:** Ugaritic Baal Cycle tablets attest *ltn*, the fleeing/twisting and probably seven-headed serpent defeated in divine combat.
- **First millennium BCE, dates disputed:** Psalm 74 attributes Leviathan’s defeat to YHWH.
- **First millennium BCE, dates disputed:** Psalm 104 describes Leviathan as God’s created plaything in the sea.
- **Late monarchic to Persian/Hellenistic period, disputed:** Isaiah 27:1 reuses the Lotan formulas for a future divine victory.
- **Probably Persian or early Hellenistic final form:** Job 3 and 40–41 use Leviathan in curse language and the divine speeches.
- **Third–first centuries BCE:** Septuagint translators render Leviathan principally as *drakōn*.
- **Late first century BCE–first century CE, disputed:** 1 Enoch 60 makes Leviathan female and pairs her with Behemoth.
- **Late first century CE:** 4 Ezra 6 preserves the pair for eschatological food.
- **Late first–early second century CE:** 2 Baruch 29 includes them in the messianic banquet.
- **First century CE:** Revelation identifies its own dragon with Satan but does not name Leviathan.
- **Fifth–sixth centuries CE:** Babylonian Talmud records the male/female pair, prevention of reproduction, Gabriel’s hunt, banquet, and skin-pavilions.
- **c. 578–595:** Gregory the Great’s *Moralia in Job* systematizes Leviathan as the Devil.
- **c. eighth–ninth centuries:** Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer connects Jonah with Leviathan.
- **Tenth century onward:** Western Hell-mouth iconography crystallizes.
- **Eleventh century:** *Akdamut* gives poetic form to the Leviathan-Behemoth combat and feast.
- **Thirteenth century:** Aquinas comments on Leviathan naturalistically; Zoharic literature develops mystical cosmology.
- **Fourteenth–fifteenth-century textual nucleus; later manuscripts and editions:** *Abramelin* tradition lists Leviathan among major demonic princes.
- **1589:** Peter Binsfeld’s demonological scheme associates major demons with deadly sins; later reception makes Leviathan the prince of envy.
- **1651:** Hobbes publishes *Leviathan*, converting the monster into the artificial sovereign commonwealth.
- **1667/1674:** Milton’s *Paradise Lost* uses Leviathan as an island-like sea-monster simile.
- **1851:** Melville’s *Moby-Dick* helps cement “leviathan” as whale.
- **1928–1929:** Discovery of Ugarit and its texts transforms the historical reconstruction of Leviathan’s Northwest Semitic background.
- **1969:** Anton LaVey assigns Leviathan water and west in *The Satanic Bible*.
- **Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries:** Biblical scholarship increasingly treats the figure as passage-specific and historically layered; occultism and popular culture proliferate demon, dragon, whale, and kaiju versions.
- **Present:** No continuous ancient cult is documented. Jewish study and liturgy preserve the feast traditions; Christian preaching preserves satanic allegory; occult practitioners construct new devotional relationships; “Leviathan” remains a productive political and popular metaphor.
