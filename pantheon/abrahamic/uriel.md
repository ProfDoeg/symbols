# Uriel: Research Dossier

**Class:** A named heavenly messenger or “holy angel” in Second Temple Jewish apocalyptic literature; subsequently an archangel in Jewish, Eastern Christian, Ethiopian Orthodox, and some Anglican traditions; an angel of cosmological revelation, judgment, resurrection, and divine light; in one Gnostic anatomical catalogue, a ruler over bodily demons; and, under the same name but probably as a different entity, an evil wandering prince in the *Lemegeton*.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Uriel first enters the recoverable literary record in the composite Aramaic traditions behind *1 Enoch*, especially the *Book of the Watchers* and *Astronomical Book*, whose relevant strata are conventionally dated to the third or second century BCE. Cave 4 at Qumran yielded Aramaic copies of both works from the late Second Temple period, but the surviving fragments do not preserve every occurrence of the name. The oldest extant physical witnesses that unmistakably spell Uriel’s name are therefore later Greek and Ethiopic manuscripts, while the composition containing him is demonstrably pre-Christian. In the Aramaic fragment corresponding to *1 Enoch* 9, moreover, the relevant angel may be **Sariel**, where the later Greek and Ethiopic textual tradition has **Uriel**. The earliest claim must consequently be phrased as: **Uriel is securely present in the textual tradition of a work composed by the second century BCE, but the name at some early textual loci was unstable between Uriel and Sariel/Suriel.**

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** Second Temple or early Christian primary evidence, normally before c. 300 CE.
- **[CE] Later canonical elaboration:** Canonical within a particular receiving community, though not necessarily within Judaism, Roman Catholicism, or Protestantism.
- **[RV] Regional or recension variant.**
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** Rabbinic, patristic, scholastic, kabbalistic, or comparable ordering of inherited material.
- **[SY] Syncretistic identification.**
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[FO] Folklore or popular devotion.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported modern attribution.**

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

**[EA]** In *1 Enoch*, Uriel is one of four angels who see the violence produced by the Watchers and giants; the angel commissioned to warn Noah; Enoch’s guide through places of cosmic punishment; one of seven “holy angels who watch”; the angel “over the world and over Tartarus”; and the instructor who reveals the courses of the heavenly luminaries. These are distinct literary offices accumulated in the Enochic collection, not necessarily a single original job-description.

**[EA]** In *4 Ezra* 3–14, composed in the aftermath of the Roman destruction of Jerusalem, approximately 90–120 CE, Uriel is God’s principal interpreting angel, sent to debate Ezra about theodicy, the limits of human knowledge, the end of the age, and Ezra’s visions. Calling him the “fourth archangel of *4 Ezra*” is misleading: the work does not present the same four-member list found in *1 Enoch*. Uriel is its main *angelus interpres*; Jeremiel appears separately at 4:36 in the Latin textual tradition.

**[EA/RV]** The *Prayer of Joseph*, probably Jewish and commonly dated around the first century CE but surviving only in quotations by Origen, makes Uriel the angel who wrestles with Jacob/Israel and disputes precedence with him.

**[EA]** Christian apocalypses then extend Uriel’s eschatological office. The *Apocalypse of Peter* makes him the angel who restores soul and spirit to the dead; *Sibylline Oracles* II makes him break Hades’ gates and marshal the resurrected dead for judgment.

### Names and scripts

- Hebrew: **אוּרִיאֵל**, *ʾŪrīʾēl* or *ʾÛrîʾēl*.
- Unpointed Hebrew/Aramaic: **אוריאל**.
- Greek: **Οὐριήλ**, *Ouriēl*.
- Latin: **Uriel**, sometimes **Oriel**, **Urihel**, or **Vriel**.
- Coptic: conventionally transliterated **Ouriel** from forms representing Greek Οὐριήλ.
- Geʿez/Amharic: **ዑራኤል** or **ዑርኤል**, normally transliterated *ʿUraʾel/Ura’el*.
- English occult spelling: **Auriel**, especially in Hermetic-Qabalistic and Golden Dawn literature.

### Etymology

**[EA/MSR]** The name is an ordinary Northwest Semitic theophoric formation ending in **ʾēl**, “God/El.” The first element belongs to the Hebrew root **אור**, associated with light or flame. Plausible renderings include “God is my light,” “my light is God,” “light of God,” or “flame/fire of God.” Strict grammatical equivalence between all four English renderings should not be assumed. The common titles “Light of God” and “Fire of God” are interpretive translations, not separately attested ancient epithets.

**[EA]** The Hebrew Bible uses Uriel as a **human** personal name: a Kohathite Levite in 1 Chronicles 6:24/6:9 and 15:5, 11, and the father or ancestor of Maacah/Micaiah in 2 Chronicles 13:2. The Septuagint transliterates the human name as Οὐριήλ. These passages prove the antiquity of the name but do not attest an angel called Uriel.

### Variant identities

**[RV] Sariel/Suriel/Uryan.** In the textual history of *1 Enoch* 9 and 10, Uriel alternates with Sariel, Suriel, or related forms. This may reflect scribal substitution between established angelic names rather than proof that all were originally one being.

**[RV/LS] Phanuel.** *1 Enoch* 40 names the fourth presence as Phanuel, “set over the repentance unto hope of those who inherit eternal life,” whereas other Enochic passages use Uriel in a four-angel group. Later angelologies sometimes identify them. The Ethiopic Enochic tradition can preserve them as distinct figures; equivalence is therefore a harmonization, not an early textual fact.

**[LS] Jeremiel.** Because *4 Ezra* normally employs Uriel as Ezra’s interpreter but names Jeremiel at 4:36, some commentators have proposed a textual substitution or identification. The manuscript evidence does not establish that Uriel and Jeremiel were universally regarded as one angel.

**[LS] Nuriel.** Jewish mystical sources sometimes treat **Nuriel**—from *nūr*, “fire”—as Uriel’s fiery or judgmental manifestation. The *Zohar*, *Pekudei*, explicitly says that a western light called Nuriel “is Uriel.” This is medieval kabbalistic interpretation, not the original Enochic etymology.

**[SY, disputed] Israfil.** Popular comparative angelology sometimes equates Uriel with the Islamic Isrāfīl because both receive eschatological functions. Neither the Qur’an nor securely authenticated early hadith names Uriel, and Islamic sources usually develop Isrāfīl independently as the trumpet angel. Raphael, Seraphiel, and Uriel have all been suggested as analogues; none is a demonstrated identity.

**[MI] Ariel.** Uriel and Ariel are frequently confused in modern occult lists. They are differently spelled names with separate textual careers. John Dee’s composite or interpretive angelology and later ceremonial magic encouraged conflation; philology does not.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Hebrew Bible, Septuagint, and New Testament

**[EA: negative finding]** No angel named Uriel occurs in the Masoretic Hebrew Bible, the canonical books of the Septuagint, or the New Testament. The biblical Uriel is human. Genesis 3:24 does not name the cherub guarding Eden; 2 Kings 19:35 does not name the destroying angel; Revelation 8:2 does not name its seven angels. Assigning these actions to Uriel is later exegesis.

**[EA]** Daniel names Michael and Gabriel; Tobit names Raphael; these texts provide the immediate literary environment in which individual angelic names proliferated. Tobit does not name Uriel. Revelation’s “seven angels who stand before God” furnished Christian readers with a canonical framework into which names from *1 Enoch* and other apocrypha could be placed, but Revelation itself supplies none of those seven names.

### 1 Enoch

**Language and transmission.** The component works were composed principally in Aramaic from approximately the third century BCE through the first century CE. Aramaic fragments survive among the Dead Sea Scrolls; Greek fragments preserve parts of chapters 1–32; the complete collection survives in Geʿez. The principal modern editions are J. T. Milik’s Aramaic fragments, Michael Knibb’s Ethiopic edition, and the Hermeneia translation and commentaries of George W. E. Nickelsburg and James C. VanderKam.

**Principal Uriel passages:**

- **1 Enoch 9:1–11 [EA/RV]:** Michael, Uriel/Sariel, Raphael, and Gabriel see bloodshed and lawlessness on earth and appeal to God.
- **10:1–3 [EA/RV]:** God sends Uriel—Sariel in some witnesses—to Lamech’s son, conventionally Noah, warning him of the flood and instructing him to escape.
- **19:1–3 [EA]:** Uriel explains to Enoch the fate of angels who mingled with women and led humanity into error.
- **20:1–8 [EA]:** among seven holy watchers, Uriel is “over the world and over Tartarus.”
- **21:5–10 [EA]:** Uriel explains the prison of transgressing stars or angels.
- **27:1–4 [EA]:** he explains the accursed valley prepared for those who blaspheme God.
- **33:3–4 [EA]:** Uriel shows Enoch the gates through which stars emerge.
- **72:1–82:20 [EA]:** in the *Astronomical Book*, Uriel is the holy angel and leader who reveals the ordered courses of sun, moon, winds, portals, seasons, and the 364-day calendar.
- **40:9 [EA/RV]:** the fourth presence is Phanuel rather than Uriel, an important resistance to later harmonization.

The Qumran copies prove that the Enochic books containing this angelology were Jewish and pre-Christian. They do not prove that every later Ethiopic reading is identical to the earliest Aramaic recension. An Aramaic fragment of *1 Enoch* 9 preserves **Sariel**, illustrating precisely why Uriel/Sariel variants must be reported.

### Jubilees and the Dead Sea Scrolls

**[EA: negative finding]** *Jubilees* shares the 364-day calendar, Watcher traditions, heavenly tablets, and unnamed angels of the presence with Enochic literature, but it does not securely name Uriel. It is contextual evidence for the cosmology in which Uriel’s Enochic office developed, not a Uriel text.

**[EA: negative finding]** No independent Qumran composition has securely yielded a developed Uriel cult or biography. The Dead Sea Scrolls matter principally because their Enoch manuscripts establish the antiquity and Aramaic transmission of his literary setting.

### Prayer of Joseph

**[EA/RV]** Probably composed in Greek or translated from a Semitic original around the first century CE; now lost except for fragments quoted by Origen in his *Commentary on John* 2.31 and related witnesses. Jacob declares that his heavenly name is Israel, that he is an archangel and chief captain, and that Uriel came against him, wrestled with him, and contested angelic rank. This rewrites Genesis 32:24–30: the unnamed wrestler becomes Uriel, while Jacob is himself a superior angel temporarily embodied as a human. Origen calls the work “not to be despised” and says it circulated among Jews, but it never became broadly canonical.

### 4 Ezra / 2 Esdras 3–14

**[EA/CE]** A Jewish apocalypse probably composed in Hebrew around 90–120 CE after the destruction of the Second Temple; the Semitic original is lost. It survives through Latin, Syriac, Ethiopic, Arabic, Armenian, Georgian, and other versions. “2 Esdras” in the Latin and English Apocrypha contains later Christian additions: chapters 1–2 (*5 Ezra*) and 15–16 (*6 Ezra*). Uriel belongs to the Jewish core, chapters 3–14.

Uriel is “the angel who was sent” to Ezra at 4:1. He answers Ezra’s protest over Israel’s suffering by exposing the limits of human knowledge—asking him to weigh fire, measure wind, recall the past, or understand the sea’s storehouses. He then explains signs of the end, the narrowness of salvation, the fate of souls, and portions of the visions. His answers do not simply settle Ezra’s objections: the literary tension between Ezra’s moral protest and Uriel’s insistence on cosmic order is central to the book.

**[RV]** Jeremiel appears at 4:36 answering the souls in their chambers. Some textual and harmonizing traditions assimilate him to Uriel, but the safer reading treats the name as a genuine variant or another angel.

The text is scriptural in the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo tradition and was transmitted in Slavonic and other Eastern Bibles. In Western Latin Christianity it circulated widely and influenced liturgy and theology without acquiring full canonical status.

### Apocalypse of Peter

**[EA/RV]** This early Christian apocalypse was probably composed in the first half of the second century and was known by about 150 CE. Its Greek Akhmim fragment and Ethiopic version differ. In the fuller Ethiopic eschatological sequence, Uriel restores soul and spirit to the dead at God’s command. He is consequently an officer of bodily resurrection and judgment. Descriptions of him as a demon are misleading: his punishments are severe, but the text presents him as God’s commissioned angel.

### Sibylline Oracles II

**[EA/RV]** Book II is a Jewish-Christian composite, usually assigned in its present form to the second or third century CE. Lines 214–294 name Barakiel, Ramiel, Uriel, Samiel, and Azael as angels who bring human souls to judgment. Uriel then breaks the adamantine bolts and bronze gates of Hades and summons Titans, giants, flood victims, the drowned, burned, and devoured to God’s judgment seat. The “angel of the Sibylline Oracles” is thus an eschatological jail-breaker and musterer of the dead, not primarily a solar angel.

### Life of Adam and Eve

**[EA/RV]** This is a multilingual textual family—Greek, Latin, Armenian, Georgian, Slavonic, and Coptic—not a single stable book. Its Jewish core is often placed between the first century BCE and first century CE, although surviving recensions contain Christian development. In the Greek *Apocalypse of Moses* 40–43 and related versions, God orders Michael and Uriel to wrap Adam and Abel in linen and bury them in Paradise. This is the earliest source for Uriel as an attendant at Adam’s burial. Identifying him as the unnamed cherub of Genesis 3 is secondary.

### Gnostic: Apocryphon of John

**[EA/RV]** The *Apocryphon* or *Secret Book of John* is a second-century Sethian or Sethian-related Christian work surviving in four Coptic witnesses, including Nag Hammadi Codices II, III, and IV and the Berlin Codex. In its long recension’s anatomical catalogue, seven powers govern the demons responsible for Adam’s bodily members: Michael, **Ouriel/Uriel**, Asmenedas, Saphasatoel, Aarmouriam, Richram, and Amiorps.

This Uriel is embedded in the lower creators’ organization of Adam’s material body. The text does **not** make the familiar Enochic archangel a supreme Gnostic aeon, nor does it provide a narrative proving that the benevolent Uriel has “fallen.” The reuse of Jewish angelic names for lower cosmic administrators is characteristic of Gnostic mythmaking. Identity with the Enochic angel is therefore **onomastic and polemical, not demonstrably personal continuity**.

**[EA: negative finding]** Uriel does not play a significant named role in the *Hypostasis of the Archons*, *Gospel of Truth*, *Gospel of Thomas*, or most other Nag Hammadi tractates. Irenaeus, Hippolytus, and Epiphanius remain essential for reconstructing movements called Gnostic, but they do not preserve a separate major “Uriel cult.” Claims that Uriel was universally a Gnostic archon overstate one catalogue in one textual family.

### Rabbinic and midrashic literature

**[LS]** Uriel is absent as an angel from the Mishnah and does not possess a sustained narrative in the Babylonian or Jerusalem Talmud. Later midrash supplies the durable fourfold arrangement. *Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer* 4, probably redacted in the eighth or ninth century, places Michael to God’s right, Gabriel to the left, Uriel before him, Raphael behind, with the Shekhinah in the center.

**[LS]** Numbers Rabbah 2:10 and related midrashic passages likewise arrange four angels around God or Israel and explain Uriel through divine illumination. These sources are late antique or medieval compilations and cannot be cited as evidence for a biblical-era four-direction cult.

### Hekhalot, Sefer Raziel, and Zohar

**[LS/negative finding]** The Hekhalot corpus, approximately fifth–ninth centuries in surviving literary form, elaborates dense angelic hierarchies. Its central guide is normally Metatron, not Uriel. Uriel occurs in the broader magical-mystical angelic vocabulary, but no extant Hekhalot narrative rivals his roles in *1 Enoch* or *4 Ezra*. Claims that *3 Enoch* is fundamentally “Urielian” are unsupported.

**[MA]** *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh*, a medieval compilation with earlier magical materials, uses Uriel in angelic names, amulets, illumination, and adjurations. Its printed form is not an ancient book delivered intact to Adam, despite its pseudepigraphic frame.

**[MA]** The *Zohar*, composed principally in thirteenth-century Castile, identifies Uriel and Nuriel in some passages, assigns them different manifestations of fire or light, and inserts Uriel into directional and sefirotic symbolism. These are kabbalistic reinterpretations, not evidence that the Second Temple author of *1 Enoch* knew the later sefirotic system.

### Pseudo-Dionysius and Aquinas

**[LS: negative finding]** Pseudo-Dionysius’ *Celestial Hierarchy*, around the late fifth or early sixth century, constructed the influential nine orders—seraphim, cherubim, thrones; dominions, virtues, powers; principalities, archangels, angels—but did not assign Uriel a distinctive place in it.

**[LS: negative finding]** Thomas Aquinas’ *Summa theologiae* systematized angelic intellect, will, rank, and incorporeality, drawing on Scripture, Augustine, and Pseudo-Dionysius. Aquinas did not canonize Uriel, make him a seraph, or establish his modern elemental correspondences. Later charts that place Uriel confidently in one Dionysian choir are post-medieval syntheses.

### Qur’an, hadith, tafsir, and al-Ṭabarī

**[EA/LS: negative finding]** The Qur’an names Jibrīl and Mīkāl but not Uriel, Raphael, Azrael, or Isrāfīl. It repeatedly mentions the trumpet of resurrection without naming its blower. Later hadith and exegetical traditions assign that office to Isrāfīl.

No securely sourced passage in al-Ṭabarī’s tafsir or universal history gives the Enochic Uriel a stable Islamic office. Comparisons between Uriel and Isrāfīl are modern comparative reconstructions based on overlapping eschatological functions, not a Qur’anic equation. The Arabic angel-name Artiyāʾīl has been compared philologically with Uriel or Ariel, but the resemblance is inconclusive.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Historically layered domains

- **Cosmic surveillance [EA]:** he sees earthly bloodshed with Michael, Raphael, and Gabriel in *1 Enoch* 9.
- **Flood warning [EA/RV]:** he warns Noah in *1 Enoch* 10; some witnesses name Sariel.
- **Cosmological interpretation [EA]:** he guides Enoch through the world’s extremities, fiery prisons, star-gates, and cursed valleys.
- **Astronomy and calendar [EA]:** he reveals the sun’s and moon’s portals and the 364-day year in *1 Enoch* 72–82.
- **Tartarus and punishment [EA]:** *1 Enoch* 20 places him over the world and Tartarus.
- **Theodicy and apocalypse [EA]:** he debates and instructs Ezra in *4 Ezra*.
- **Resurrection [EA]:** he restores or marshals the dead in the *Apocalypse of Peter* and *Sibylline Oracles*.
- **Burial [EA/RV]:** he assists Michael in burying Adam and Abel in the *Life of Adam and Eve*.
- **Divine illumination [LS]:** rabbinic, Christian, and later devotional sources draw “enlightener” from the name’s light etymology and his explanatory roles.
- **Sun [MA]:** Renaissance and early-modern cosmological systems, followed spectacularly by Milton, make him regent of the sun. This is not his fixed office in *1 Enoch*.
- **Earth and north [MA/MI]:** the Golden Dawn makes Auriel the archangel of Earth and the northern quarter. This is a modern ceremonial arrangement, not an ancient tradition.
- **Confirmation, arts, sciences, poetry, prosperity, vegetation [FO/MI]:** these circulate in modern Anglican, occult, and New Age sources but lack a single ancient foundation.

### Form and emblems

**[EA]** Early texts do not provide Uriel with a stable physiognomy, color, sexed body, clothing, wings, halo, or individual emblem. He is grammatically and narratively masculine in Greek, Latin, Hebrew, and Ethiopic reception, but angelic incorporeality became a theological commonplace.

**[MA/CE]** Eastern Orthodox iconography commonly shows Uriel holding a naked sword and a flame. Modern Orthodox descriptions interpret these as divine zeal, truth, and enlightenment. He normally appears as a youthful winged courtier among the archangels.

**[CE/RV]** Ethiopian iconography can show Uriel bearing a chalice. This refers to the Ethiopian *Dǝrsanä ʿUraʾel*: Uriel catches blood and water from Christ’s pierced side and carries or sprinkles it over Ethiopia, sanctifying future church sites. This attribute is Ethiopian and late medieval; it is not derived from *1 Enoch*.

**[MA]** Western seven-archangel art associated with Palermo shows Uriel with sword and flame and sometimes the title **Fortis Socius**, “strong companion.” A purported old mural of seven named angels was uncovered at Palermo in 1516 and inspired Antonio Lo Duca’s campaign for their veneration. The original mural is lost or heavily mediated through later copies, making its claimed antiquity difficult to verify.

**[MA, disputed identification]** The angel in Leonardo’s *Virgin of the Rocks* is often called Uriel because later legend made Uriel the protector of the infant John the Baptist. The National Gallery officially describes the figure simply as “an angel”; the painting itself contains no identifying inscription or unique Uriel attribute. “Leonardo painted Uriel” is therefore a traditional identification, not an epigraphically secure one.

**[MI]** Citrine, olive, russet, and black robes, wheat, pentacle, and the northern quarter come from Golden Dawn color scales and successors. They should never be projected into Second Temple Judaism.

### Sacred numbers, times, and celestial bodies

**[EA/LS]** Uriel belongs alternately to groups of four and seven. Four is structurally tied to quarters around God or the earth; seven is tied to the seven angels before God and later planetary schemes. No single ancient text gives him a universally fixed number.

**[MA]** Medieval magical calendars variously assign Uriel to Sunday, Wednesday, the sun, or another planet. The contradictions arise because different tables align planets, days, directions, elements, and angelic names differently.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

Uriel has no securely attested parents, consort, offspring, siblings, mount, or biological genealogy. Angels are created servants in Jewish and Christian monotheistic frameworks, and later references to fellow angels as “brothers” indicate order or fraternity, not descent.

### Hierarchical positions

- **[EA]** One of four principal angels in *1 Enoch* 9.
- **[EA]** First-listed among seven holy watchers in *1 Enoch* 20.
- **[EA]** “Over the world and Tartarus” in *1 Enoch* 20.
- **[EA]** Leader of the luminaries’ revelation in *1 Enoch* 72.
- **[EA]** God’s commissioned interpreter in *4 Ezra*.
- **[LS]** One of four directional angels around the Shekhinah in *Pirkei de-Rabbi Eliezer*.
- **[CE]** One of seven or eight named archangels in Eastern Christian lists.
- **[MA]** Solar regent in Milton.
- **[MI]** Northern archangel of Earth in Golden Dawn ritual.
- **[RV]** One of seven lower anatomical rulers in the long *Apocryphon of John*.
- **[MA, probably distinct homonym]** An evil wandering prince in the *Ars Theurgia-Goetia*.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **God —** God hears the four angels’ appeal and commissions Uriel to warn Noah. *1 Enoch* 9–10, third–second century BCE. **[EA/RV]**
- **Michael —** jointly observes human bloodshed and petitions God. *1 Enoch* 9. **[EA/RV]**
- **Raphael —** jointly observes bloodshed and petitions God. *1 Enoch* 9. **[EA/RV]**
- **Gabriel —** jointly observes bloodshed and petitions God. *1 Enoch* 9. **[EA/RV]**
- **Michael —** appears with Uriel in the angelic burial of Adam and Abel. Greek *Life of Adam and Eve/Apocalypse of Moses* 40–43. **[EA/RV]**
- **Jacob-Israel, explicitly an archangel in that text —** wrestles with Uriel and disputes heavenly precedence; Israel claims superior rank. *Prayer of Joseph*, first century CE, preserved by Origen. **[EA/RV]**
- **Jeremiel —** appears in the same apocalypse as Uriel and answers the waiting souls; whether a second angel, textual replacement, or alternate name is disputed. *4 Ezra* 4:36, c. 90–120 CE. **[EA/RV]**
- **Barakiel —** jointly brings souls from darkness to judgment. *Sibylline Oracles* II.214–294, second–third century CE. **[EA]**
- **Ramiel —** jointly brings souls to judgment. Same source and date. **[EA]**
- **Samiel —** jointly brings souls to judgment. Same source and date. **[EA]**
- **Azael —** jointly brings souls to judgment. Same source and date. **[EA]**
- **Yaldabaoth —** Uriel belongs to the subordinate administration of the material Adam’s body within Yaldabaoth’s created order; no direct dialogue survives. Long *Apocryphon of John*, second-century composition, fourth-century Coptic manuscripts. **[EA/RV]**
- **Michael, Asmenedas, Saphasatoel, Aarmouriam, Richram, and Amiorps —** the seven are named together as rulers over bodily demons in the long *Apocryphon of John*. **[EA/RV]**
- **Nuriel —** explicitly treated as Uriel under another aspect in the *Zohar*. **[MA/LS]**
- **Raphael, Gabriel, Michael, Selaphiel, Jehudiel, Barachiel, and sometimes Jeremiel —** joint liturgical appearance in Eastern Orthodox synaxes and icons; no single shared myth is implied. **[CE/RV]**
- **Christ —** in the Ethiopian homily, Uriel receives blood and water from Christ’s wounded side and carries it to Ethiopia. *Dǝrsanä ʿUraʾel*, attested from the fifteenth century onward. **[CE/RV]**
- **Michael —** in later Ethiopian versions, accompanies Uriel in dispersing Christ’s blood over Ethiopia. **[CE/RV]**
- **Satan —** disguised as a cherub, deceives Uriel into revealing the location of Paradise; Uriel later recognizes the deception and warns Gabriel. Milton, *Paradise Lost* III–IV, 1667/1674. **[MA, literary reinvention]**
- **Gabriel —** Uriel warns Gabriel that an evil spirit has entered Eden. Milton, *Paradise Lost* IV. **[MA]**

**Absence findings:** No ancient source records Uriel fighting Lucifer in the primordial angelic revolt; no canonical biblical text makes Uriel Michael’s biological brother; no early source gives him a romance, consort, offspring, or a quarrel with Raphael; and Dante’s *Divine Comedy* does not name Uriel.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The Watchers, flood, and cosmic prisons

**[EA]** In *1 Enoch* 6–16, rebellious Watchers descend, take women, father giants, and transmit destructive arts. Uriel, Michael, Raphael, and Gabriel survey the resulting violence and ask why heaven has not acted. God apportions tasks: Uriel warns Noah; Raphael binds Asael/Azazel; Gabriel moves against the giants; Michael binds Shemihazah and his associates. Later, Uriel leads Enoch through fiery prisons holding stars or angels and explains their judgment.

The text thus makes Uriel simultaneously observer, mediator, flood herald, and interpreter. He does not personally cause the Watchers’ rebellion or single-handedly defeat them.

**[RV]** Sariel replaces Uriel at key points in Aramaic or other witnesses. Modern retellings that make “Uriel warned Noah” without qualification suppress a real textual variant.

### Uriel’s astronomical revelation to Enoch

**[EA]** *1 Enoch* 72–82 presents the cosmos as a regulated system of portals. Uriel shows Enoch the sun’s annual circuit, variations in day length, lunar phases, winds, seasons, intercalation problems, and a 364-day year. Enoch records the teaching for Methuselah.

Its historical function was calendrical and priestly: correct knowledge of sacred time determined festivals and ritual order. Whether this calendar directly belonged to the Qumran community remains debated, but the shared 364-day structure made Enochic calendrology important in Dead Sea Scrolls research.

### Uriel and Ezra

**[EA]** Ezra laments the destruction of Zion and asks why sinful Rome prospers while Israel suffers. Uriel responds with riddles demonstrating that a mortal who cannot weigh fire, measure wind, or recover a past day cannot comprehend God’s judgment. Ezra persists. Their dialogue moves from philosophical protest toward apocalyptic disclosure: the present age is narrow and corrupt; the coming age belongs to the righteous; signs will precede the end.

The narrative does not present Uriel as omniscient or emotionally triumphant. His function is to police the boundary between divine and human knowledge while gradually interpreting visions. Modern summaries that reduce the exchange to “Uriel answers Ezra’s questions” miss that many answers defer, reframe, or deny comprehension.

### Uriel and Jacob-Israel

**[EA/RV]** In the fragmentary *Prayer of Joseph*, Jacob says his true heavenly identity is Israel, the first minister before God. Uriel descends, takes or contests the earthly name Jacob, and wrestles him. Israel identifies Uriel as “my eighth” or in another difficult reading assigns him a subordinate position. The text transforms Genesis’s mysterious assailant into an angelic rivalry and elevates Jacob above Uriel.

Because the work survives only through hostile or selective quotation, its complete plot and sectarian setting cannot be recovered.

### Resurrection and the breaking of Hades

**[EA]** The *Apocalypse of Peter* gives Uriel responsibility for restoring the dead’s soul and spirit so judgment can occur. *Sibylline Oracles* II expands the spectacle: Uriel breaks Hades’ adamantine bars, casts down its bronze gates, and gathers every class of dead person, including those whose bodies were destroyed.

These sources likely develop Uriel’s Enochic association with Tartarus and forbidden cosmic regions. They do not make him ruler of hell in the later demonic sense; he is an agent who opens and empties it under divine command.

### Burial of Adam and Abel

**[EA/RV]** At Adam’s death, God orders Michael and Uriel to obtain linen and bury Adam and Abel in Paradise. Uriel therefore becomes a funerary attendant at the first human burial. Later art and folklore expanded this into claims that he guarded Eden, escorted Adam, or carried divine fire; those details require separate sourcing.

### Ethiopian blood-of-Christ cycle

**[CE/RV]** In the Ethiopian *Homily on Uriel*, Uriel receives blood and water flowing from Christ’s side, carries it to Ethiopia, and sprinkles it upon sites destined to become churches and monasteries. The story integrates universal salvation history with Ethiopian sacred geography.

The earliest presently identified homily witness is manuscript EMML 1835 from the reign of Zärʾa Yaʿǝqob, 1434–1468; a longer witness, EMML 1841, is seventeenth century. Later redactions continued into the modern imperial period. The myth is therefore a documented Ethiopian Christian tradition, but not an early apostolic account.

### Milton’s solar Uriel

**[MA]** In *Paradise Lost* III, Satan reaches the sun, disguises himself as a lesser cherub, and deceives Uriel—“Regent of the Sun” and the sharpest-sighted spirit in heaven—into pointing out Earth and Paradise. In Book IV Uriel sees the stranger’s distorted passions, descends on a sunbeam, and warns Gabriel.

Milton makes Uriel good but fallible: wisdom is vulnerable because innocence does not instinctively suspect hypocrisy. The solar office is a learned literary synthesis drawing on the name’s light associations and Renaissance angelology; it is not an episode from Scripture or *1 Enoch*.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Antiquity

**[EA: negative finding]** No securely identified Second Temple sanctuary, priesthood, sacrificial cult, treasury, festival, pilgrimage center, or dedicatory inscription devoted specifically to Uriel is known. His early habitat is literary, visionary, calendrical, and magical rather than temple-institutional.

**[EA/MA]** Greek, Coptic, Aramaic, and Jewish magical texts invoke Uriel alongside Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, divine names, and other angels for healing, protection, revelation, and adjuration. These documents attest practical invocation, but not a centralized “cult of Uriel.”

There is no documentary connection between Uriel and human sacrifice, temple prostitution, or an independent priesthood. Searches coupling Uriel with those subjects yield no credible ancient evidence.

### The Roman synod of 745

**[MA, contested-material correction]** The often repeated statement that “the Council of 745 removed Uriel from the Roman calendar” is historically inaccurate.

At a Roman synod under Pope Zachary on 25 October 745, the missionary Boniface presented the case against the heterodox preacher Aldebert. Aldebert’s prayer invoked Uriel, Raguel, Tubuel, Michael, Inias/Adinus, Tubuas, Sabaoc, and Simiel. Bishops objected that, apart from Michael, the invoked figures were regarded as demons and that Scripture authorized the names Michael, Gabriel, and Raphael.

What the evidence establishes:

1. The synod condemned Aldebert and his angelic prayer.
2. It restricted acceptable named angels in the Latin ecclesiastical setting to Michael, Gabriel, and Raphael.
3. Uriel was included among the disallowed names in that prayer.

What it does **not** establish:

1. That Uriel had previously possessed a universal feast in the Roman calendar.
2. That the synod formally “deleted” such a feast.
3. That it issued a philosophical judgment that every being called Uriel was both angel and demon.
4. That Eastern churches were bound by this local Roman action.

The 789 *Admonitio generalis* renewed resistance to unauthorized angel names, invoking the anti-angelolatry tradition associated with Laodicea. Western suppression was therefore a regulatory process, not one neat calendar deletion.

### Eastern Orthodox devotion

**[CE]** Byzantine and Slavic Orthodox practice commemorates Uriel among the bodiless powers at the Synaxis of the Archangel Michael and the Other Bodiless Powers on 8 November. Lists vary between seven and eight named chief angels. Modern Orthodox iconographic instructions give Uriel a sword and flame.

This is collective rather than normally temple-specific worship: hymns and icons honor the heavenly hosts under Michael’s synaxis. Claims that the fourth-century Council of Laodicea itself established the complete modern named list are ecclesiastical tradition rather than a conclusion compelled by the surviving canons.

### Ethiopian Orthodox devotion

**[CE/RV]** Ethiopia preserves the strongest distinct Uriel devotion because both *1 Enoch* and *4 Ezra* are scriptural within the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo canon. The fifteenth-century Uriel homily transformed the interpreting angel into a patron of Ethiopian sacred geography. Emperor Nāʾod, reigning 1494–1508, is credited with establishing an early church dedicated to Uriel. Churches, monthly commemorations, homilies, icons, and contemporary feasts attest continuing devotion.

Uriel’s chalice of Christ’s blood is specifically Ethiopian and should not be universalized across Orthodoxy.

### Western medieval survival

**[MA]** Despite Roman restriction, Uriel continued in Irish, Anglo-Saxon, Carolingian, and other protective prayers and angel lists. The early twelfth-century *Textus Roffensis* lists Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, Urihel, Barachiel, Raguel, and Pantasaron. A Carolingian litany names Orihel; the Bobbio Missal contains a protective list with Oriel/Uriel. Regulation therefore reduced official Latin authorization without erasing manuscript or popular practice.

### Palermo and early-modern Catholic negotiation

**[MA]** The reported discovery in 1516 of a mural depicting seven named angelic princes at Palermo inspired Antonio Lo Duca. His campaign contributed to the foundation of Santa Maria degli Angeli e dei Martiri in Rome under Pius IV, with Michelangelo adapting the Baths of Diocletian. Roman authorities later removed or censored the four non-scriptural names from some images. This episode shows revival followed by renewed control, not uninterrupted official acceptance.

### Anglican devotion

**[RV/FO]** Anglican formularies universally commemorate **Saint Michael and All Angels** on 29 September, but Uriel’s individual official status is inconsistent among provinces and communities. Some Anglican and Episcopal churches, stained-glass programs, devotional books, and local calendars name him; the Church of St Uriel the Archangel at Sea Girt, New Jersey, is a concrete institutional example.

Claims that “the Anglican Communion officially recognizes Uriel as patron of confirmation” are too broad. This is a local devotional attribution, not a doctrine uniformly enacted by every Anglican province. Anglican survival is nevertheless real at parish, artistic, and devotional levels.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Third–second centuries BCE: emergence

**[EA]** The Uriel tradition emerges within Jewish apocalyptic angelology. Named angels become specialized members of the divine court. Enochic sources connect Uriel with cosmic order, the flood, punishment, and revelation. No independent cult is visible.

### First century BCE–first century CE: diversification

**[EA]** The *Prayer of Joseph* turns Uriel into Jacob’s rival. The *Life of Adam and Eve* makes him Adam’s burial attendant. Enochic books continue to circulate in Aramaic. His identity is already unstable alongside Sariel and, in the four-presence scheme, Phanuel.

### c. 90–120 CE: interpreter of Israel’s catastrophe

**[EA]** *4 Ezra* makes Uriel the voice confronting post-70 Jewish theodicy. This becomes his most influential role after the Enochic cosmological guide.

### Second–third centuries: Christian eschatology and Gnostic reuse

**[EA/RV]** The *Apocalypse of Peter* and *Sibylline Oracles* assign Uriel bodily resurrection and the opening of Hades. The *Apocryphon of John* reuses his name within a lower cosmic anatomy. These are parallel receptions, not a unified doctrine.

### Fourth–seventh centuries: canon boundaries

**[CE/RV]** Enochic literature diminished in most Greek and Latin churches but remained in Ethiopic transmission. Latin and Eastern churches inherited different scriptural and liturgical environments. Pseudo-Dionysian hierarchy supplied ranks without fixing Uriel in one.

### Eighth–ninth centuries: restriction and rabbinic systematization

**[MA]** Rome’s 745 condemnation of Aldebert’s prayer delegitimized Uriel’s public invocation in the Latin West. Carolingian rules reinforced the preference for the three names found in Western canonical Scripture.

**[LS]** Jewish midrash arranged Uriel with Michael, Gabriel, and Raphael around the Shekhinah. His directional and luminous identity became more explicit.

### Medieval period: magic, liturgy, and Kabbalah

**[MA]** Uriel persisted in amulets, angelic litanies, magical texts, Jewish mysticism, and Eastern iconography. Kabbalah connected him with Nuriel, directions, fire, and divine emanations. There was no single medieval Uriel system.

### Fifteenth–seventeenth centuries: Ethiopian expansion and Western revival

**[CE/RV]** Ethiopian homilies produced a distinctive national sacred-geography cycle.

**[MA]** Palermo’s seven-angel imagery and Antonio Lo Duca prompted renewed Western interest. Grimoires made Uriel an instructor, conjured spirit, divine-name component, or occasionally a dangerous prince. Milton’s *Paradise Lost* fixed the memorable solar image.

### Nineteenth–twentieth centuries: occult standardization

**[MI/MA]** The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, founded in 1888, stabilized the now-familiar ritual quartet: Raphael/east/air, Michael/south/fire, Gabriel/west/water, Auriel/north/earth. Israel Regardie’s publications disseminated the arrangement internationally.

This system reversed or replaced several older correspondences. Uriel’s modern association with Earth, wheat, vegetation, brown or russet robes, prosperity, and “grounding” largely follows from it.

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries: recovery and reinvention

**[MSR]** Discovery and publication of the Dead Sea Scrolls established the Jewish antiquity of the Enochic corpus. The Nag Hammadi codices made the Gnostic anatomical Uriel directly accessible. Critical editions exposed the Uriel/Sariel and Uriel/Phanuel variations obscured by harmonized popular accounts.

**[FO/MI]** Contemporary angel spirituality presents Uriel as a provider of practical wisdom, money, creativity, emotional healing, environmental concern, or flashes of insight. These practices are genuine modern religious phenomena, but the detailed portfolios and color correspondences are mostly modern constructions.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Grimoires

**[MA]** The *Key of Solomon* manuscript tradition places “Vriel/Uriel” on a sword or its pommel in some recensions. The name functions protectively within a ritual apparatus.

**[MA]** In the *Grimoire of Armadel*, Uriel is explicitly connected with Ezra and is invoked for knowledge of divine mysteries, preserving his apocalyptic reputation through Christian ritual magic.

**[MA]** Mathers’s English *Book of the Sacred Magic of Abramelin* contains the name Uriel in magical squares or editorial identifications. The work’s German manuscript tradition is early modern; the celebrated English translation dates to 1898. Calling the printed system an unchanged fifteenth-century Jewish Kabbalah is untenable.

**[MA, distinct homonym]** The *Ars Theurgia-Goetia*, second book of the seventeenth-century *Lemegeton*, has a wandering prince Uriel with ten chief dukes and numerous servants. He is “evil,” unwilling, false, and appears as a serpent with a virgin’s head and face. This is not safely identifiable with the archangel. The shared name demonstrates how grimoire catalogues recycle angelic names across moral categories.

**[MA]** The *Grimorium Verum* uses Uriel in a divinatory conjuration involving a child and a vessel or mirror. Again, this is operative magic, not evidence of ancient public worship.

### Golden Dawn and successors

**[MI]** The Golden Dawn’s ritual documents invoke Auriel as “the Great Archangel of Earth” in the north. Its pentagram ritual surrounds the operator with Raphael, Gabriel, Michael, and Uriel. Aleister Crowley, Israel Regardie, Dion Fortune, modern Wiccan-derived ceremonialists, and eclectic occultists transmitted variants.

The popular “Uriel = Earth = North” formula is therefore datable to the modern Western esoteric synthesis, even though its ingredients—four angels, four quarters, elemental cosmology—are older.

### Literature and art

**[MA]** Milton’s *Paradise Lost* is the single most influential literary reinvention: Uriel is solar, exceptionally perceptive, deceived by Satan, and then vigilant.

**[Negative finding]** Dante does not name Uriel in the *Divine Comedy*. Lists claiming “Dante’s Uriel” generally confuse unnamed gatekeepers, later commentary, or modern works titled *Divine Comedy*.

**[MI]** Modern fiction, comics, television, anime, and games frequently make Uriel female, rebellious, apocalyptic, militaristic, or fallen. These are creative retellings. There is no ancient consensus on Uriel’s gendered visual body or fall.

### New Age and psychological readings

**[MI]** Twentieth- and twenty-first-century angel manuals associate Uriel with sudden ideas, intellectual clarity, red or amber light, earthquakes, environmental disaster, money, healing resentment, or “root-chakra grounding.” These attributions combine the etymological “light,” Enochic instruction, Golden Dawn Earth symbolism, and modern therapeutic spirituality.

A specifically Jungian Uriel archetype is not a doctrine formulated by Jung; it is a later application of archetypal psychology.

### Politics and extremism

**[Negative finding]** No well-documented major nationalist or extremist movement has made Uriel its central divine patron in a manner comparable to some appropriations of Michael. “Uriel” does occur as a personal name, organizational name, and fictional military designation. Without specific documentary context these uses should not be classified as religious appropriation.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Uriel present in the earliest Enoch text?

The principal problem is textual variation. Greek and Ethiopic *1 Enoch* contain Uriel, but an Aramaic witness at *1 Enoch* 9 supports Sariel. Scholars must distinguish:

1. the antiquity of the four-angel narrative;
2. the antiquity of Uriel elsewhere in Enochic tradition;
3. the precise name in each original passage.

The safest conclusion is that Uriel belongs to early Enochic angelology, but some loci were transmitted with Sariel.

### Is Uriel identical with Phanuel?

The argument for identity is structural: both can occupy the fourth position in a quartet and both have judgment or repentance functions. The argument against is explicit naming: *1 Enoch* 20 includes Uriel, while *1 Enoch* 40 explicitly names Phanuel; Ethiopian lists may include both. Identity is a later harmonization, not established authorial intent.

### Is Uriel “the fourth archangel”?

Only relative to particular fourfold lists. In *1 Enoch* 9 the order itself varies by witness. In *1 Enoch* 20 he is first among seven. In *4 Ezra* he is the dominant interpreter, not member four of an enumerated college. The apparently fixed title is a modern simplification.

### Iranian origin theories

Older scholars, including Kohut and some comparativists, explained Jewish angelology extensively through Zoroastrian influence and linked Uriel’s glory or fire to Iranian concepts. Persian-period contact remains historically relevant, but one-to-one equations between Uriel and an Iranian yazata or *xᵛarənah* are not demonstrable. Contemporary scholarship more often studies multiple Persian, Babylonian, biblical, courtly, and priestly contexts without assuming direct identity.

### Uriel as a fallen angel or demon

Three bodies of evidence must not be collapsed:

- In *1 Enoch*, *4 Ezra*, and Christian apocalypse, Uriel serves God.
- In the *Apocryphon of John*, Ouriel is part of the lower creators’ bodily administration.
- In the *Lemegeton*, an evil wandering prince named Uriel is probably a homonym.

The Roman synod of 745 treated the unauthorized figures in Aldebert’s prayer as demonic; it did not rewrite all Uriel literature into a fall narrative.

### The “removal from the Roman calendar”

This is a popular historical compression. The synodal dossier concerns Aldebert’s condemned prayer, not a surviving decree deleting a universal feast of Uriel from a calendar. Later Latin policy restricted angel names; that is the documented claim.

### Primary evidence and discoveries

- Qumran Cave 4’s Aramaic Enoch fragments demonstrated the pre-Christian Jewish antiquity of the corpus.
- The Greek Akhmim/Panopolitan fragments preserve portions of the angel lists and journeys.
- Complete Geʿez manuscripts preserve *1 Enoch* and Ethiopian Uriel traditions.
- Origen alone preserves most of the *Prayer of Joseph* fragments.
- The Nag Hammadi discovery supplied Coptic witnesses to the *Apocryphon of John*.
- EMML 1835 and EMML 1841 preserve major Ethiopian Uriel homily recensions.
- Western magical manuscripts are dispersed among the British Library, Bibliothèque nationale de France, and other collections.

Davidson’s *Dictionary of Angels* is an extraordinarily useful index to names but frequently aggregates sources of radically different date and authority. It must be checked against the cited primary texts. Bamberger, Kelly, and Jeffrey Burton Russell illuminate fallen-angel and Devil traditions, but Uriel is normally peripheral because the dominant Uriel is not a fallen angel. The *Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible* is valuable for the broader emergence of named angels and divine intermediaries; Pearson, Rudolph, and modern Nag Hammadi scholarship are essential safeguards against treating “Gnosticism” as one uniform pantheon.
