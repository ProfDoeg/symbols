# Beelzebub: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the earliest source, a foreign city-god and oracular deity, **Baal-zebub, god of Ekron**; in the Greek New Testament, **Beelzebul**, a demonic ruler or “ruler of the demons”; in the *Testament of Solomon*, a fallen celestial being and chief demon; in medieval and early-modern Christian demonology, a prince or monarch of hell. He is not securely attested as a named figure in Gnostic, Islamic, or prebiblical Canaanite texts.

**Earliest secure attestation:** **בַּעַל־זְבוּב, Baʿal-zəḇūḇ**, four times in **2 Kings 1:2–16**, a Hebrew narrative probably incorporated into Kings during the first millennium BCE and describing the ninth-century-BCE Israelite king Ahaziah consulting “Baal-zebub, the god of Ekron.” No independent inscription from Ekron presently names this deity. The earliest surviving physical witnesses to this particular passage are much later than the episode narrated.

## Evidence labels used

- **[Early attested]** Earliest surviving textual or material evidence.
- **[Later canonical]** Scriptural elaboration accepted within a later canon.
- **[Regional variant]** A locally or linguistically limited form.
- **[Learned systematization]** A theological hierarchy constructed from inherited material.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** An equation with another divine or demonic figure.
- **[Late accretion]** Medieval or early-modern legend, grimoire lore, possession literature, or demonography.
- **[Folklore]** Popular tradition without secure ancient documentation.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** A scholarly inference rather than an attested ancient statement.
- **[Modern invention]** A demonstrably modern literary, occult, artistic, or religious creation.
- **[Uncertain]** Evidence is fragmentary or the identification disputed.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic historical identity

Beelzebub is not one continuously documented being with a stable biography. The dossier begins with an Ekronite god known only through an Israelite polemical narrative; resumes centuries later with a similarly named ruler of demons in the Synoptic Gospels; then develops through Christian pseudepigrapha, exegesis, demonological taxonomies, possession trials, grimoires, literature, and modern popular culture.

The continuity between the Ekronite **Baal-zebub** and New Testament **Beelzebul** is historically plausible and became standard in Christian transmission, but it is not demonstrated by an unbroken chain of inscriptions or texts. The different spellings are themselves evidence of reinterpretation.

### Documented forms

| Form | Script and transliteration | Meaning or proposed meaning | Attestation |
|---|---|---|---|
| Baal-zebub | בַּעַל־זְבוּב, *Baʿal-zəḇūḇ* | Literally “Baal/Lord of a fly” or “of flies” in biblical Hebrew | 2 Kings 1:2, 3, 6, 16 |
| Baal-muia | Βααλ μυῖαν / Βααλ μυιαν, *Baal myian* | “Baal-Fly” or “Baal of the fly” | Septuagint 4 Kingdoms/2 Kings 1 |
| Beelzebul | Βεελζεβούλ, *Beelzeboul* | Disputed: “Baal the prince,” “lord of the lofty dwelling,” “lord of the house,” or a deliberately altered divine name | Greek Synoptic tradition |
| Beezebul | Βεεζεβούλ | Alexandrian manuscript variant lacking the first lambda | Some major Greek Gospel manuscripts |
| Beelzebub | *Beelzebub* | Form assimilated to Hebrew *Baal-zebub* | Syriac and Latin transmission; older English Bibles |
| Belzebuth / Belzébuth | Latin/French forms | Western vernacular development | Medieval and modern French demonology |
| Belzebud / Beelzebuth | Grimoire spellings | Orthographic variants | *Abramelin* and later occult literature |

### The etymological dispute

**1. “Lord of the flies.” [Early attested linguistic reading]**  
The Hebrew noun *zəḇūḇ* means “fly.” The Septuagint renders the title with Greek *myia*, “fly,” and Josephus, writing in the late first century CE, says that Ahaziah sent to “the Fly, which was the god of Ekron.” These witnesses prove that the fly interpretation was ancient, although they do not prove that it was the god’s original indigenous title. The Septuagint passage reads “Baal-Fly, god of Akkaron/Ekron”; Josephus likewise treats “Fly” as the god’s name. [Septuagint text](https://thekoinebible.org/text/rahlfs/2-kings/01.html); [Josephus, *Antiquities* 9.2.1](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/josephus/ant-9.html).

**2. A mocking Israelite alteration of *Baal-zebul*. [Modern scholarly reconstruction]**  
Many scholars propose that *zebub* is a polemical distortion of **zbl**, an honorific Northwest Semitic element variously rendered “prince,” “exalted one,” or “lofty dwelling.” On this reconstruction, an original title such as *Baal-zebul*—“Baal the Prince” or “Lord of the [lofty] dwelling”—was converted into “Baal of flies.” The proposal explains why the Gospel form is *Beelzebul*, but no Ekronite inscription preserving *Baal-zebul* has been found. Therefore “the biblical writers certainly changed the name” overstates the evidence. A scholarly survey notes that no otherwise-attested “fly Baal” settles the question and that the corruption theory remains plausible rather than proven. [Discussion and bibliography](https://etheses.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/5979/1/245561.pdf).

**3. “Lord of dung” or “lord of the dunghill.” [Late polemical interpretation]**  
This explanation depends on Jewish and Christian wordplay linking idolatry, filth, manure, and the flies gathering over it. It is not the lexical sense of biblical *zəḇūḇ* and is not an independently attested Ekronite title.

**4. A genuine fly-protecting or healing god. [Modern reconstruction]**  
Because Ahaziah consults the god about recovery, interpreters have proposed a deity who sends, controls, or averts disease-bearing flies. Greek Zeus Apomyios (“Averter of Flies”) and related fly-expelling cult titles provide analogies, not proof. Nothing in 2 Kings explicitly says that Baal-zebub governs flies, plague, or healing; it identifies him only as Ekron’s god and an oracle consulted about illness.

### Classification shifts

- **2 Kings:** *ʾĕlōhê ʿEqron*, “god of Ekron.” **[Early attested]**
- **Septuagint and Josephus:** foreign god called “Baal-Fly” or “the Fly.” **[Ancient interpretation]**
- **Synoptic Gospels:** possessor or patron of demons, called their ruler. **[Later canonical]**
- **Jesus’ rebuttal in the Synoptic controversy:** treated in parallel with Satan, though the saying does not formally define whether Beelzebul is a second name for Satan or a subordinate demonic ruler. **[Later canonical; identification disputed]**
- ***Testament of Solomon*:** chief of the demons, a former heavenly angel, sometimes distinct from Satan. **[Late-antique pseudepigraphic elaboration]**
- **Medieval and early-modern demonology:** prince, king, lieutenant, grand officer, fallen seraph, or embodiment of a capital sin. These rankings conflict. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Modern demonolatry and occultism:** sometimes restored as a pre-Christian god or treated as an autonomous spirit. **[Modern reconstruction/reinvention]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 2 Kings 1

**[Early attested]** Ahaziah of Israel falls through the lattice of his upper room in Samaria and sends messengers to ask Baal-zebub of Ekron whether he will recover. The angel of YHWH directs Elijah to intercept them: “Is it because there is no God in Israel that you are going to inquire of Baal-zebub, the god of Ekron?” Elijah predicts Ahaziah’s death. After confrontations in which heavenly fire consumes two military detachments, Elijah repeats the sentence to the king, who dies.

The chapter establishes only that:

1. its author knew or represented Ekron as possessing an oracular god called Baal-zebub;
2. an Israelite king could be accused of consulting that oracle;
3. the narrator opposed such consultation to loyalty to YHWH.

It does **not** provide Baal-zebub’s image, genealogy, temple plan, priests, myths, sacrifices, sacred animal, or indigenous theology. Nor is Ahaziah’s consultation independently corroborated. The narrative is theological polemic, although it may preserve knowledge of a real Ekronite oracle. [Hebrew and translation](https://classic.net.bible.org/passage.php?passage=2Ki+1%3A2&theme=wiki).

The episode is set shortly after Ahab’s death, conventionally in the ninth century BCE. The final literary formation of Kings is normally dated much later, commonly to the late monarchic and exilic periods. Consequently, “ninth-century attestation” should not be confused with the date of the surviving account.

### Septuagint

**[Ancient translation]** The Greek version renders the title rather than simply transliterating it: **Βααλ μυῖαν θεὸν Ακκαρων**, “Baal-Fly, god of Akkaron.” This confirms that Jewish translators several centuries before or around the turn of the era understood *zebub* as “fly.” It does not establish whether that sense was original to Ekron. [Greek text and parallel translations](https://openlxx.com/2-kings/1/).

### Josephus

**[Ancient reception]** In *Jewish Antiquities* 9.2.1, composed about 93/94 CE, Josephus retells the episode and says Ahaziah sent “to the Fly, which was the god of Ekron.” He supplies no independent cult history and appears dependent on biblical tradition. [Primary text](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/josephus/ant-9.html).

### Synoptic Gospels

**[Later canonical]** The earliest extant occurrence of the precise demonic form **Beelzebul** is probably Mark 3:22, ordinarily dated around 65–75 CE. Scribes from Jerusalem accuse Jesus: “He has Beelzebul,” and “by the ruler of the demons he casts out demons.” Jesus answers with the divided-kingdom and strong-man sayings: if Satan casts out Satan, Satan’s kingdom cannot stand.

Matthew and Luke preserve expanded parallels:

- **Matthew 10:25:** opponents may call the “master of the house” Beelzebul, probably inviting wordplay with Greek *oikodespotēs*.
- **Matthew 12:24–29:** Pharisees attribute an exorcism to “Beelzebul, ruler of the demons.”
- **Matthew 12:27:** Jesus asks by whom the accusers’ own sons cast demons out.
- **Luke 11:15–22:** the same controversy, followed by the stronger-man saying.
- **Luke 11:18–19:** Jesus substitutes “Satan” when reformulating the accusation.

The full set is conveniently displayed in parallel translations [here](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Matthew+10%3A25%2CMark+3%3A22%2CLuke+11%3A15%2CMatthew+12%3A24%2CMatthew+9%3A34&version=NKJV%3BNASB%3BNIV%3BESV%3BKJV).

The Greek manuscript tradition overwhelmingly reads **Beelzebul/ Beezebul**, not *Beelzebub*. Latin and Syriac forms helped align the Gospel name with 2 Kings, and older English translations inherited that alignment. [Textual commentary](https://greeknewtestament.net/mt10-25); [manuscript-form summary](https://strongerbible.com/lemma/G0954/beelzeboul).

The passages leave an interpretive ambiguity. The accusers might identify Beelzebul as the chief demon through whose delegated authority exorcisms occur; Jesus answers in terms of “Satan.” Many readers therefore equated them. Yet later demonologies frequently separated Beelzebub, Satan, and Lucifer into different persons and ranks. A recent study emphasizes that the form *Beelzebul* is not securely attested before the New Testament and that Mark’s rhetorical use cannot simply be back-projected into 2 Kings. [Scholarly study](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/14/8/1070).

### 4Q560, an Aramaic magical text from Qumran

**[Uncertain]** 4Q560 is a fragmentary Aramaic apotropaic/incantation text, generally dated paleographically to the late Second Temple period. Douglas Penney and Michael Wise proposed reading an adjuration “by the power of Beelzebub,” which, if correct, would be extraordinarily important evidence for the name in Jewish magic near the time of the Gospels. The fragments are damaged, however, and the reading and reconstruction are disputed. It must not be presented as a secure pre-Gospel occurrence. The proposal appeared as “By the Power of Beelzebub: An Aramaic Incantation Formula from Qumran (4Q560),” *Journal of Biblical Literature* 113 (1994), 627–650; the study and alternate readings are indexed in the [Comprehensive Aramaic Lexicon bibliography](https://cal.huc.edu/getbibsigla.php?myauthor=4Q560), while the standard scroll edition lists the debate [here](https://ancientmodern.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/02/the_dead_sea_scrolls_study_edition_brill_1999.pdf).

### *Testament of Solomon*

**[Late-antique pseudepigraphic elaboration]** This Greek demonological work is composite and notoriously difficult to date. Proposals range across the early centuries CE, while the surviving manuscripts are medieval. It should not be assigned unqualifiedly to Solomon, the first century, or a single author.

In chapters 3–6, Solomon uses a divine ring to compel **Beelzeboul**, “ruler of the demons.” Beelzeboul promises to summon the demons under him. He says that he was formerly “the highest-ranking angel in heaven,” is associated with the evening star, brings destruction through tyrants, excites demon worship, jealousy, murder, and war, and can be thwarted through a divine or angelic name. The text also predicts defeat by “Emmanuel,” a transparent Christian layer. The work thus transforms the Gospel title into a biography, cosmic office, magical jurisdiction, and catalogue of evils. An older English translation is available [here](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3ATestament_of_Solomon.djvu/3); a modern study should consult D. C. Duling’s critical introduction and translation in *The Old Testament Pseudepigrapha*.

### Gospel of Nicodemus / *Acts of Pilate*, Descent into Hell

**[Late-antique accretion]** In parts of the textual tradition of the *Descent into Hell*, Hades addresses Satan or the Devil with a Beelzebub-form. Other recensions distinguish the names. The work’s core is usually placed in late antiquity, but its recensions and medieval translations vary substantially. It cannot establish first-century demonology.

### Texts named in the tradition-specific brief but yielding no Beelzebub dossier

Absence is material evidence:

- **1 Enoch:** no securely identified Beelzebub. Its chief hostile beings include Asael/Azazel, Semihazah, and the spirits of the giants.
- **Jubilees:** no Beelzebub; Mastema fills the role of chief accuser or ruler of harmful spirits.
- **Tobit:** no Beelzebub; Asmodeus is the named demon.
- **Most Dead Sea Scrolls:** Belial, Mastema, spirits of the lot of darkness, and other destroyers appear, but Beelzebub is absent apart from the disputed 4Q560 reconstruction.
- **Talmud and midrash:** no stable Beelzebub demonology. A passage concerning Baal-berith and a portable fly-shaped idol is sometimes imported into the dossier, but Baal-berith is not thereby proved identical with Baal-zebub.
- **Hekhalot literature, *Sefer Raziel*, and the *Zohar*:** no securely established central Beelzebub tradition.
- **Nag Hammadi codices:** no securely identified Beelzebub. Their named archons—Yaldabaoth, Saklas, Samael, Sabaoth, and others—must not be silently equated with him. The searchable corpus is available through the [Nag Hammadi Library index](https://www.gnosis.org/naghamm/nhsearch.html).
- **Irenaeus, Hippolytus, and Epiphanius on “Gnostic” systems:** they preserve many archon names but do not establish Beelzebub as a Gnostic aeon, demiurge, or archon.
- **Pseudo-Dionysius:** supplies the ninefold angelic hierarchy later demonologists inverted, but no developed Beelzebub biography.
- **Aquinas:** discusses whether demons possess ranks and whether one demon can operate through another; he does not promulgate the later popular table in which Beelzebub governs gluttony.
- **Qur’an, canonical hadith, early tafsir, and al-Tabari:** no securely attested being named Beelzebub/Baʿl-zabūb. Iblis, al-Shaytan, and named jinn belong to different textual histories. Claims that Beelzebub is a Qur’anic demon are modern conflations.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains by period

- **Oracle concerning illness and survival:** the only activity ascribed to Baal-zebub in 2 Kings. **[Early attested]**
- **Flies or averting flies:** inferred from the Hebrew name and Greek translation, never described as an action in 2 Kings. **[Ancient interpretation/modern reconstruction]**
- **Rulership over demons and authority behind exorcism:** Synoptic accusation. **[Later canonical]**
- **War, murder, jealousy, tyranny, and demon worship:** *Testament of Solomon*. **[Late-antique elaboration]**
- **Pride or gluttony:** conflicting early-modern taxonomies. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Sorcery, possession, and infernal monarchy:** grimoires and possession literature. **[Late accretion]**
- **Internal human savagery:** Golding’s literary symbolism. **[Modern invention]**

### Form and iconography

There is **no securely identified ancient image of Baal-zebub from Ekron** and no biblical physical description. Consequently, winged humanoid, horned demon, gigantic fly, and crowned insect images are all later inventions.

The best-known visual type comes from the sixth edition of Jacques Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* (1863), illustrated by Louis Le Breton. One plate depicts **Belzébuth as a grotesque fly-like creature**, consolidating the literalized fly imagery for modern occult books, tattoos, games, and online demon catalogues. The image is documented by the [Library of Congress edition](https://www.loc.gov/item/63056292/) and [Wikimedia’s source record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AIll_dict_infernal_p0103-89_belzebuth.jpg).

Another plate presents a bizarre biped with a huge eye-like head, hat, enormous hands, and thin legs, captioned “Une des figures de Belzébuth.” This demonstrates that even Collin de Plancy’s own visual tradition was not singular. [Image record](https://www.oldbookillustrations.com/illustrations/beelzebub-1/).

### Attributes not anciently established

No ancient source securely assigns Beelzebub:

- a sigil;
- a color;
- a month, weekday, planet, zodiacal sign, or magical number;
- a weapon;
- a consort or offspring;
- a specific sacrificial animal;
- a fly-shaped cult statue;
- a rank among the seventy-two Goetic spirits.

Modern occult charts assigning such correspondences are synthetic systems, not recoveries of Ekronite religion.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No source gives Baal-zebub parents, siblings, spouse, or children. Calling him a “form,” “son,” or “epithet” of the Ugaritic storm-god Baal/Hadad is a modern comparative hypothesis. Since *baʿal* means “lord” and can function as title or divine name, the biblical wording alone cannot determine whether Ekron’s god was Hadad, a local manifestation of a wider Baal, or an otherwise unknown deity.

The New Testament likewise supplies no origin story. The *Testament of Solomon* first makes Beelzeboul a former high angel, but gives no parentage. Early-modern writers place him among fallen Seraphim, a conclusion created by combining Christian fall-of-angels traditions with Pseudo-Dionysian ranks.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Ahaziah and the oracle of Ekron

**[Early attested]** This is the only surviving narrative about the deity as god of Ekron. Its plot is not a myth about Baal-zebub’s deeds but a prophetic contest of authority. Ahaziah seeks knowledge from Ekron; Elijah insists that seeking a foreign oracle denies the presence of Israel’s God; the king dies in accordance with Elijah’s pronouncement.

The likely function within Kings is theological and political: royal apostasy validates prophetic authority and explains dynastic disaster. It may also preserve a real pattern of cross-border pilgrimage to prestigious healing or divinatory shrines. Nothing permits reconstruction of what Ekron’s priests would have answered.

### Jesus and the divided demonic kingdom

**[Later canonical]** In Mark, scribes from Jerusalem attribute Jesus’ exorcisms to Beelzebul and call him the ruler of demons. Jesus does not narrate Beelzebul’s origin. Instead he argues that a kingdom divided against itself falls and that a strong man’s house can be plundered only after the strong man is bound.

Matthew and Luke expand the controversy. Matthew adds “If I cast out demons by the Spirit of God, then the kingdom of God has come upon you”; Luke has “the finger of God.” Thus Beelzebul functions as the hostile explanation against which Jesus’ exorcistic authority and the arrival of God’s kingdom are defined.

### Solomon’s binding of the demon-prince

**[Late-antique elaboration]** The *Testament of Solomon* expands a title into a character. Solomon’s ring bears divine authority. Beelzeboul acknowledges his rule over demons, describes his former celestial status and harmful works, and becomes the means by which subordinate spirits are brought before the king. This narrative supplies nearly everything absent from the Gospels: fall, rank, residence, powers, subordinates, and an eschatological enemy.

The work is not one stable ancient edition. Jewish magical traditions, Christian names and prophecies, astrological demon lore, and Solomonic legend accumulated in transmission. Individual claims must therefore be cited to chapters or recensions rather than attributed simply to “ancient Judaism.”

### The infernal court

**[Medieval/early-modern accretion]** European demonologists increasingly treated hell as an inverted monarchy or bureaucracy. Beelzebub could be Satan’s lieutenant, emperor, chief minister, second fallen seraph, or one of several sovereigns. These models do not agree because they were assembled for different purposes: theological explanation, anti-witchcraft polemic, exorcistic narration, satire, or ritual magic.

### Loudun

**[Early-modern judicial legend]** Beginning in 1632, Ursuline nuns at Loudun claimed possession by named demons and accused the priest Urbain Grandier of causing it. A purported infernal pact presented during the prosecution bore the names or marks of Satan, Beelzebub, Lucifer, Elimi, Leviathan, and Astaroth. Grandier was tortured, condemned, and burned alive on 18 August 1634.

The document is evidence that officials and polemicists invoked Beelzebub in a capital prosecution; it is not evidence that Grandier made such a pact. Contemporary and later critics regarded the proceedings as fraudulent or politically manipulated. A translated early history preserves the accusation that Grandier gave himself to Beelzebub [here](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/pdf/fdb95b2297804.pdf), while the sentence itself ordered Grandier burned with the supposed pacts and his anti-celibacy manuscript [here](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Le_Diable_au_XIXe_si%C3%A8cle/XXIV).

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Ekron

**[Early attested, but externally reported]** 2 Kings represents Baal-zebub as “god of Ekron” with an oracle sufficiently prestigious for an Israelite king to consult. It implies messengers, a shrine or recognized oracular institution, and personnel capable of giving an answer. It does not explicitly mention a temple or priesthood.

Ekron—identified with Tel Miqne in modern scholarship—was a major Philistine city. Archaeology has documented substantial occupation, monumental buildings, cultic objects, and a seventh-century royal dedicatory inscription naming Ekron’s king Ikausu and a goddess commonly read Ptgyh. None of this currently identifies Baal-zebub. Statements that archaeologists have found “Beelzebub’s temple” are therefore unsupported.

### Rituals

The only early ritual action securely connected to the name is **inquiry**, probably divination or oracle consultation. The method is unknown. Suggestions involving flies’ movement or buzzing, incubation, lots, sacrifice, or ecstatic prophecy are speculative.

### Sacrifice and contested claims

- **Human sacrifice:** no text or excavation securely associates human sacrifice with Baal-zebub.
- **Child sacrifice:** no secure Beelzebub-specific evidence.
- **Temple prostitution:** no secure evidence.
- **Fly idols:** later descriptions of fly-shaped idols or portable Baal-berith images cannot establish the appearance of Ekron’s god.
- **Cult violence:** 2 Kings narrates Elijah’s supernatural destruction of royal troops, not violence committed by Baal-zebub’s cult.
- **Suppression:** no document records the historical closure, destruction, or conversion of a Baal-zebub temple.

### Magical practice

**[Late-antique/medieval accretion]** The *Testament of Solomon* makes Beelzeboul subject to a ring and divine names. This is ritual-literary demon compulsion, not evidence of surviving Ekronite worship.

**[Late-medieval grimoire tradition]** The *Book of Abramelin*, whose earliest manuscript forms are commonly assigned to the late medieval or early-modern period, lists **Belzebud** among eight sub-princes beneath four superior princes. The ritualist summons and compels him rather than worshipping him. [Primary text](https://www2.esotericarchives.com/abramelin/abramelin.htm).

The **Lemegeton/Ars Goetia** does not straightforwardly list Beelzebub among its seventy-two spirits. **Bael**, its first spirit, should not automatically be equated with Beelzebub. Modern “Goetic sigils of Beelzebub” are generally later inventions or borrowings.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### First millennium BCE: Ekronite god known through Israelite polemic

Baal-zebub appears only in 2 Kings 1. Whether the unusual name is authentic, translated, or ridiculed remains unresolved. There is no reconstructable mythology or continuous archive of the cult.

### Hellenistic and early Roman periods: “Baal-Fly”

The Septuagint translates the name through “fly.” Josephus repeats that understanding. These witnesses ensured that the fly interpretation preceded Christianity and was not created by modern English translators.

### First century CE: ruler of demons

Mark offers the earliest secure *Beelzebul* form. Matthew and Luke preserve related sayings. A name associated in scripture with a condemned foreign oracle has become the designation of a chief demon. The intervening mechanism is undocumented.

### Early centuries CE: demonic biography

The *Testament of Solomon* supplies celestial origin, rank, evil functions, magical subordination, and Christian defeat. Beelzeboul becomes a discrete character, not merely an insulting title.

### Late antiquity and Middle Ages: Satanic identification and differentiation

Latin **Beelzebub** reinforced identification with 2 Kings. Christian exegesis commonly treated the name as a designation for the Devil, while other traditions distinguished Beelzebub from Satan and Lucifer. There was no universally binding infernal hierarchy.

### Fifteenth–seventeenth centuries: grimoires and demonological bureaucracy

The *Abramelin* tradition makes Belzebud a sub-prince. Peter Binsfeld’s late-sixteenth-century demonology is widely reported as pairing Beelzebub with gluttony, but this is a learned moral taxonomy, not patristic dogma. Johann Weyer’s *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*, added to the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum* in 1577, catalogues demons while the larger work attacks witch-prosecution reasoning; it should not be misrepresented as uncomplicated demon worship. [Primary text and publication history](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

Sébastien Michaëlis’s *Histoire admirable*—French editions beginning in 1612, English in 1613—places Beelzebub among formerly exalted spirits, below Lucifer, within a hierarchy allegedly disclosed during exorcism. The work is digitized by the [Wellcome Collection](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/ts6zgua2).

### 1632–1634: Loudun

Beelzebub’s name enters judicial evidence during the prosecution of Urbain Grandier. The episode documents how demonological belief, exorcism, local conflict, royal authority, and criminal procedure could converge lethally. It does not authenticate the alleged possession or pact.

### Seventeenth–nineteenth centuries: literary and visual consolidation

Milton’s *Paradise Lost* (1667; revised 1674) treats Beelzebub as a fallen angel distinct from Satan and “next in power, and next in crime.” He is Satan’s confidant and an infernal councilor who supports the plan against humanity. This enormously influential rank is Miltonic poetry, not biblical angelology. [Primary text](https://sacred-texts.com/chr/milton/pl01.htm).

Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal*, first published in 1818 and repeatedly revised, organized inherited reports into an encyclopedic demonography. Louis Le Breton’s 1863 fly-like illustration became Beelzebub’s most reproducible modern image.

### Twentieth century to present

Beelzebub survives chiefly in literature, film, television, games, music, occult manuals, Satanist or demonolatry practice, and popular exorcistic language. There is no demonstrated institutional continuity from ancient Ekron. Modern devotional claims may be sincere contemporary religion, but they are revivals or reconstructions rather than continuation documented across the intervening millennia.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### William Golding

**[Modern literary reinvention]** William Golding’s *Lord of the Flies* appeared in 1954. The title literalizes the traditional interpretation of Beelzebub’s name. Within the novel, the fly-covered pig’s head addressed by Simon externalizes the “beast” and the boys’ capacity for violence. It is not a depiction of an ancient Ekronite ritual or a direct adaptation of 2 Kings.

The manuscript had been called *Strangers from Within*. Editorial work by Charles Monteith helped produce the published form; Faber’s account records the title’s publication history. [Faber history](https://www.faber.co.uk/journal/new-bbc-programme-sheds-light-on-the-story-behind-the-publication-of-lord-of-the-flies/).

### Gurdjieff

G. I. Gurdjieff’s *Beelzebub’s Tales to His Grandson* (1950) turns Beelzebub into a cosmic traveler recounting humanity’s errors. This is a twentieth-century philosophical-literary character, not a transmitted ancient myth.

### Modern Gnostic movements

Samael Aun Weor’s twentieth-century esoteric system includes a story in which Beelzebub, formerly associated with a “Black Lodge,” repents or is converted to a “White Lodge.” No Nag Hammadi text, ancient heresiologist, or early Christian source contains this episode. It is a **modern invention**, despite its Gnostic vocabulary.

### Occultism and demonolatry

Modern occultists combine *Abramelin*, Renaissance demonology, Collin de Plancy, ceremonial magic, Satanism, personal revelation, and newly designed correspondences. Beelzebub may be approached as:

- a restored Canaanite god;
- a fallen angel;
- an infernal prince;
- an elemental or planetary intelligence;
- a patron of decay, insects, sovereignty, or personal transformation.

These are distinct modern systems. Their claims should not be projected backward as “the original worship of Baal-zebub.” Available evidence does not support reliable estimates of the number of contemporary devotees.

### Film, television, comics, and games

Materially influential reinventions include:

- film adaptations of *Lord of the Flies*;
- *The Devils* (1971), Ken Russell’s dramatization of Loudun through Aldous Huxley and John Whiting;
- Penderecki’s opera *The Devils of Loudun*;
- *Good Omens*, where Beelzebub leads hell and, in the television adaptation, becomes Gabriel’s romantic counterpart;
- manga/anime titled *Beelzebub*;
- *Shin Megami Tensei* and related games, often using an enormous fly-demon design;
- fantasy games that transform the name into a monster, boss, devil, or political ruler of hell.

These works blend the fly etymology, Miltonic rank, grimoire monarchy, and modern character design. None transmits ancient iconography.

### Political and extremist appropriation

“Beelzebub” occurs as a generalized label for demonized political or religious enemies. Unlike some ancient symbols, it has no single well-documented continuous nationalist program. Claims of an organized ancient-to-modern “Beelzebub cult” controlling governments are conspiracy literature, not historical reconstruction.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Baal-zebub an authentic Ekronite name?

This remains the central dispute.

- The Masoretic Hebrew, Septuagint, and Josephus give early support to the fly reading.
- The absence of an independent fly-god inscription and the honorific possibilities of Northwest Semitic *zbl* support the mockery hypothesis.
- The Gospel form *Beelzebul* may preserve or reinvent *zebul*, but a first-century demonic name cannot by itself prove what an Ekronite deity was called centuries earlier.

The responsible conclusion is: **Baal-zebub is the securely attested biblical form; Baal-zebul as the original Ekronite title is plausible but unproved.**

### Is Beelzebul Satan?

The Synoptic rebuttal moves immediately from Beelzebul to Satan, encouraging identification. Yet the accusers’ grammar can also be understood hierarchically: Beelzebul is “ruler of demons,” and Jesus answers using the larger concept of Satan’s kingdom. Later sources alternately identify and separate them. No single harmonized answer covers all periods.

### Was he a healing god?

Ahaziah asks whether he will recover; this proves an oracle concerning illness, not necessarily a god of medicine. “Healing god,” “plague god,” and “fly-averting god” remain reconstructions.

### Is 4Q560 evidence?

Possibly, but not securely. The name depends on restoration of a damaged Aramaic incantation. It is valuable precisely because it could bridge the chronological gap, but it cannot bear that weight without qualification.

### Is Beelzebub Gnostic?

Not in the ancient documentary sense. He is absent from the securely identified Nag Hammadi pantheons and from the principal archon lists reported by heresiologists. Modern Gnostic and occult authors adopted him later. Grouping him with “Gnostic figures” is a modern atlas taxonomy, not evidence of ancient membership.

### Is Beelzebub Islamic?

No named Beelzebub tradition is securely present in the Qur’an, canonical hadith, early tafsir, or al-Tabari. Equations with Iblis are comparative or modern.

### Discredited or unproved popular claims

- Archaeologists found Beelzebub’s temple at Ekron.
- His indigenous name was certainly Baal-zebul.
- He was certainly identical with Hadad.
- Ancient worshippers sacrificed children to him.
- He possessed a fly-shaped idol.
- He appears among the seventy-two spirits of the *Ars Goetia*.
- His modern sigil is ancient.
- He is one of the Nag Hammadi archons.
- Binsfeld’s seven-demon scheme is official universal Catholic doctrine.
- Modern demonolatry is an uninterrupted survival of Philistine religion.

### State of the evidence

The archive is unusually asymmetrical:

1. one hostile Hebrew narrative;
2. ancient translations and retellings dependent upon it;
3. a first-century demonic name with uncertain transition history;
4. a disputed Qumran fragment;
5. a composite magical testament preserved in late manuscripts;
6. extensive medieval and early-modern Christian reception;
7. abundant modern art and fiction.

The absence of an indigenous Ekronite text naming the god is the dossier’s largest evidentiary gap.
