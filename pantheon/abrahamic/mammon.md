# Mammon: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the earliest relevant sources, *mammon* is not a god, angel, archon, or demon but an Aramaic/Hebrew common noun meaning wealth, property, or financial resources. In the canonical Gospels it becomes a rhetorically personified rival master to God. Late-antique and especially medieval Christian exegesis progressively reclassified Mammon as a devil or demon of avarice. Renaissance epic and modern occult/popular culture made that literary demon into an increasingly individualized supernatural character.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The noun *māmôn* occurs before Christianity in Jewish literature, notably Hebrew Ben Sira 31:8, composed about 180 BCE, although the pertinent Hebrew survives in medieval manuscript copies, and the sectarian Damascus Document, whose composition is conventionally placed in the late second or first century BCE and whose medieval Cairo copies preserve a warning involving “mammon of wickedness.” The earliest secure attestation of **Mammon as a personified opposing master** is Matthew 6:24, normally dated approximately 80–90 CE, with a parallel in Luke 16:13 and related uses at Luke 16:9, 11. Neither Gospel identifies Mammon as an ancient deity or supplies a genealogy, cult, body, temple, priesthood, or independent myth.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### The word before the demon

The underlying forms are Jewish Palestinian Aramaic or Syriac **מָמוֹנָא**, *māmônāʾ*, “wealth, property,” and Hebrew **מָמוֹן**, *māmôn*. The Greek New Testament transliterates the word as **μαμωνᾶς**, *mamōnas*, rather than translating it with Greek *ploutos*, “wealth.” The Vulgate similarly retains **mamona**. English *Mammon*, French *Mammon*, German *Mammon*, Italian *Mammona*, and Spanish *Mammón* descend through this Greek-Latin transmission.

The etymology beyond the Semitic noun is disputed:

- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** It is often connected with the Semitic root ʾ-m-n, “to be firm, reliable, entrusted,” hence a deposit, something entrusted, or that in which confidence is placed.
- **Alternative reconstruction:** Derivation from a form meaning “gain” or “profit” is also proposed.
- **Uncertain:** The attractive theological gloss “that in which one trusts” is compatible with the root but should not be mistaken for a demonstrated first-century dictionary definition.
- **Discredited or unsupported popular claim:** No contemporary inscription, temple record, myth, or image establishes an ancient Syrian, Chaldean, Canaanite, Babylonian, or Phoenician god named Mammon. The *Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible* accordingly treats the demonic figure as a later personification prompted by Gospel language, not as a surviving pagan deity.

### Historical classifications

| Period/source | Classification |
|---|---|
| Ben Sira, Damascus Document, rabbinic and targumic usage | Wealth, property, resources; a common noun |
| Matthew 6:24; Luke 16 | Personified “master” opposed to God, but not explicitly called a demon |
| Patristic exegesis | Usually riches figuratively enslaving their possessor; occasionally assimilated to the Devil or Beelzebub |
| Peter Lombard and later medieval commentary | “Name of a devil”; riches named after that devil |
| *The Lanterne of Liȝt* | Fifth of seven devils, governing avarice and covetousness |
| Spenser | “Great Mammon,” self-proclaimed god of riches and tempter |
| Milton | A fallen heavenly spirit under Satan, obsessed with mineral wealth |
| Collin de Plancy | “Démon de l’avarice” |
| Modern fiction and demonolatry | Prince, king, archdemon, embodiment, or “sin” of greed—usually modern systematization |

Documented titles include **“mammon of unrighteousness”** or **“unrighteous mammon”** (Luke 16:9, 11), **“the fifth devil”** (*Lanterne of Liȝt*, c. 1409–1410), **“greatest god below the sky”** and **“god of riches”** (Spenser, *The Faerie Queene* II.vii, 1590), **“the least erected Spirit that fell / From Heaven”** (Milton, *Paradise Lost* I.679–80, 1667), and **“demon of avarice”** (Collin de Plancy, 1863).

Claims that Mammon is invariably one of “seven princes of Hell,” Hell’s treasurer, ambassador to England, or ruler of a particular planetary day are not ancient or universally medieval doctrines. They belong to particular late classifications or to modern compilations.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Pre-Christian Jewish evidence

**Ben Sira 31:8.** Composed in Hebrew around 180 BCE, the passage praises the rich man found blameless who did not pursue gold and asks who has not put his hope in money/*māmôn*. This attests the noun, not a being. The Hebrew portions of Ben Sira survive mainly in medieval Cairo Geniza manuscripts, so composition and manuscript dates must be distinguished.

**Damascus Document XIV.20.** This Jewish sectarian work, known from medieval Cairo copies and overlapping Dead Sea Scroll fragments, uses *māmôn* in an ethical-regulatory context often rendered “mammon of wickedness.” Again, the referent is property or wrongful wealth, not a named demon.

**Targums and rabbinic texts.** Aramaic *māmônā* commonly means possessions or money. Targumic expressions include both neutral uses—such as honoring God with one’s property—and qualified expressions such as “mammon of falsehood.” Mishnah *Avot* and later rabbinic literature likewise use *māmôn* as an ordinary legal and ethical term. There is no securely attested rabbinic Mammon-demon.

Consequently, the Hebrew Bible, Septuagint, 1 Enoch, Jubilees, Tobit, the angel lists of the Dead Sea Scrolls, and classical hekhalot literature furnish no securely identified divine or demonic being named Mammon. Their discussions of riches, corrupt angels, or treasure cannot be assigned to Mammon merely by thematic resemblance.

### Canonical Gospels

**Matthew 6:24**, approximately 80–90 CE:

> Οὐδεὶς δύναται δυσὶ κυρίοις δουλεύειν … οὐ δύνασθε θεῷ δουλεύειν καὶ μαμωνᾷ.

“No one can serve two masters … you cannot serve God and mammon.”

The governing metaphor is slavery or household service: God and wealth are incompatible *kyrioi*, masters. Capitalizing “Mammon” is an editorial decision; ancient Greek manuscripts did not use modern capitalization to settle whether this was a proper name. The construction personifies wealth, but does not say that Mammon is an angel, pagan god, or demon.

**Luke 16:9, 11, 13**, approximately 80–90 CE, uses “mammon of unrighteousness” and repeats the two-masters saying. The parable’s interpretive difficulties generated extensive debate: wealth may be “unrighteous” because it is regularly implicated in an unjust economy, because it is unreliable or worldly, or because the phrase designates property used wrongfully. Luke also imagines wealth being converted into almsgiving and heavenly benefit; mammon is therefore not presented simply as an ontologically evil substance.

### Gospel of Thomas

Saying 47 of the *Gospel of Thomas* contains the two-masters analogy and concludes that one cannot serve God and mammon. The complete witness is Coptic Nag Hammadi Codex II, copied in the fourth century; Greek fragments of Thomas from Oxyrhynchus are earlier, although not every Coptic saying is preserved in them. The work’s compositional history is disputed, with sayings potentially earlier than the surviving collection and the final form commonly placed in the second century.

**Textual tier:** early extra-canonical reception or parallel tradition. Thomas does not turn Mammon into a Gnostic archon. No named Mammon appears in the demiurgical genealogies of the *Apocryphon of John*, *Hypostasis of the Archons*, *On the Origin of the World*, or the other Nag Hammadi cosmological texts.

### Patristic interpretation

**Jerome**, late fourth and early fifth centuries, glosses *mammon* as riches in the “language of the Syrians.” In *Against the Luciferians* 5 he juxtaposes the two-masters saying with Paul’s contrast between Christ and Belial; this encourages a diabolical reading without establishing Mammon and Belial as a formal identity. In his Matthew commentary and Letter 22.31, the straightforward referent remains riches.

**Augustine**, *De sermone Domini in monte* II, states that profit is called *mammon* in Punic: *Lucrum Punice Mammon dicitur*. This is linguistic exegesis, not testimony to a North African Mammon cult.

**Gregory of Nyssa.** Later reference works repeatedly attribute to Gregory the assertion that “Mammon” is a name for Beelzebub. This should be handled cautiously: the claim circulates far more readily in modern dictionaries than an exact, consistently cited Greek locus. Gregory’s securely edited works should be checked in the *Gregorii Nysseni Opera* rather than treating the attribution as independent evidence of a fourth-century cult or stable demonology.

### Medieval scholastic and vernacular material

**Peter Lombard, *Sentences* II, distinction 6**, c. 1150, supplied a highly influential learned formulation: riches are called by the name of a devil, Mammon; Mammon is a devil’s name, while in Syriac the same word denotes riches. This reverses the most likely linguistic history. A common noun construed as a rival master becomes the devil whose name riches supposedly bear.

**Nicholas of Lyra**, early fourteenth century, states in Gospel commentary that *Mammon est nomen daemonis*, “Mammon is the name of a demon.” This is medieval exegesis, not information from first-century Syria.

**Piers Plowman**, late fourteenth century, uses Gospel mammon within an allegorical critique of money, clerical corruption, debt, and stewardship. The poem exists in substantially different A, B, and C versions and more than fifty manuscripts; one should not treat quotations from one recension as though they represented a single authorial text.

**The Lanterne of Liȝt**, c. 1409–1410, an anonymous English Lollard tract, provides one of the clearest demonological classifications:

> “Þe fifþe deuel is Mammon, & haþ wiþ him þe auarouse … a foule synne, couetise.”

Mammon is the fifth devil and commands the avaricious and covetous. This is the secure medieval source behind much of the modern “seven princes/seven sins” scheme.

### Grimoires and Jewish mystical corpora

A dedicated check of the usual corpora produces a negative result:

- Mammon is not one of the seventy-two spirits of the *Ars Goetia*.
- No secure Mammon entry appears in the *Lemegeton*’s standard spirit catalogues.
- Mammon is not a standard chief spirit in the principal manuscript traditions of *Abramelin*.
- Mammon is absent from Johann Weyer’s *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* and the corresponding Goetic catalogue.
- No standard Mammon angel or demon is established in *Sefer Raziel*, the *Zohar*, or classical hekhalot catalogues.
- Modern “sigils of Mammon” cannot therefore be backdated automatically to Solomon, Kabbalah, or medieval ceremonial magic.

The resemblance of **Mammon** to names such as **Amaimon/Amaymon**, a directional king in some grimoire traditions, is not evidence that the beings are identical. The names, textual histories, and functions differ.

### Islamic sources

Neither the Qur’an nor the canonical hadith collections contain a supernatural being named Mammon. Al-Ṭabarī’s Qur’anic history and tafsir discuss Iblīs, satans, wealth, and human attachment to property, but do not establish Mammon as an Islamic demon. Arabic translations may transliterate the Gospel name in Christian contexts; that is transmission of Christian scripture, not an Islamic cult or jinn tradition.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

The earliest domain is simply **wealth as a claimant on human loyalty**. Medieval Christianity narrows and moralizes this into **avarice or covetousness**. Spenser expands it into hoarded treasure, worldly ambition, bribery, infernal extraction, and political dominion. Milton adds mining, metallurgy, monumental architecture, and the preference for material splendor over beatific vision.

There is no ancient evidence assigning Mammon a celestial body, weekday, sacred animal, plant, number, color, weapon, seal, temple emblem, or sacrificial substance.

### Physical appearance

The Gospels give no body or iconography.

Spenser describes Mammon as an earth-blackened, soot-dark figure associated with gold, iron chests, mines, smoke, and the underworld. His environment is more important than a fixed anatomy.

Milton calls Mammon a fallen spirit whose gaze was “always downward bent.” The defining iconographic gesture is lowered vision toward gold rather than contemplation of God.

The best-known fixed image is **Louis Le Breton’s engraving for the sixth edition of Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* (1863)**. It depicts a squat, heavy, dark demon with a leonine or grotesquely bestial head and multiple goat-like limbs. This is a nineteenth-century artistic invention, not a recovered ancient idol. De Plancy’s accompanying entry is extremely short: Mammon is the demon of avarice who, according to Milton, first taught humans to tear treasures from the earth.

Modern crowns, coin-covered bodies, serpentine forms, green coloring, dollar signs, steampunk machinery, and standardized sigils come from particular games, television series, online occult art, or recent demonolatry.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy and hierarchy

No ancient genealogy exists. Mammon has no attested parents, consort, offspring, siblings, mount, or cultic attendants.

Matthew and Luke establish only a structural opposition: **God versus mammon** as rival masters. Medieval sources place Mammon among devils, implicitly under Satan, but differ on rank. *The Lanterne of Liȝt* makes him fifth in a moral series; Spenser calls him a god of riches in an allegorical underworld; Milton makes him one of Satan’s fallen angels. These are incompatible literary systems, not fragments of a single ancient pantheon.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **God — rival claim to service:** A human cannot serve both God and mammon. Matthew 6:24, c. 80–90 CE; Luke 16:13, c. 80–90 CE. **Tier: securely attested early canonical personification.**

- **God — parallel opposition:** No one can serve God and mammon. *Gospel of Thomas* 47, final collection probably second century; surviving Coptic manuscript fourth century. **Tier: early extra-canonical parallel.**

- **Beelzebub — asserted identity:** Later reference tradition attributes to Gregory of Nyssa an identification of Mammon as another name for Beelzebub; the exact primary locus is insufficiently secure in commonly repeated citations. **Tier: disputed patristic attribution; not securely established as cultic belief.**

- **Belial — exegetical juxtaposition:** Jerome places the Gospel’s two masters beside the Pauline question concerning concord between Christ and Belial. Jerome, *Against the Luciferians* 5, fourth century. **Tier: patristic association, not explicit identity.**

- **Christ — opposed through avaricious service:** Patristic and medieval commentators construe service to riches as incompatible with service to Christ. This develops the Gospel’s “God and mammon” antithesis; it is not a narrative meeting. **Tier: later canonical exegesis.**

- **Lucifer — co-member of the seven-devil classification:** *The Lanterne of Liȝt* assigns Lucifer pride and Mammon avarice. They do not converse or fight. c. 1409–1410. **Tier: medieval learned systematization.**

- **Beelzebub — co-member of the seven-devil classification:** Beelzebub governs envy while Mammon governs avarice; no episode joins them. *Lanterne of Liȝt*. **Tier: medieval learned systematization.**

- **Satan — co-member and superior in later literature:** In the *Lanterne*, Satan governs wrath. In Milton, Mammon belongs to Satan’s fallen host and participates in the infernal polity. *Paradise Lost* I–II, 1667. **Tier: medieval classification and early-modern literary elaboration.**

- **Abaddon — co-member of the seven-devil classification:** Abaddon governs sloth; no narrative interaction. *Lanterne of Liȝt*. **Tier: medieval learned systematization.**

- **Belphegor — co-member of the seven-devil classification:** Belphegor governs gluttony; no narrative interaction. *Lanterne of Liȝt*. **Tier: medieval learned systematization.**

- **Asmodeus — co-member of the seven-devil classification:** Asmodeus governs lust; no narrative interaction. *Lanterne of Liȝt*. **Tier: medieval learned systematization.**

- **Philotime — proposed marriage:** In Spenser, Mammon offers Guyon his daughter Philotime, personification of worldly ambition, in marriage. *The Faerie Queene* II.vii, 1590. **Tier: early-modern literary invention.**

- **Plutus — modern comparative identification:** Mammon is frequently equated with the Greek wealth-god Ploutos/Latin Plutus, especially under the influence of Dante’s demonic Plutus. No ancient text identifies the two, and Dante calls his figure Pluto/Plutus, not Mammon. **Tier: modern syncretistic comparison, not an attested interaction.**

No interaction with the Gnostic demiurge Yaldabaoth, Saklas, Samael, Barbelo, Sophia, or named archons is attested. Their incorporation into a shared “Gnostic figures pantheon” would be a modern classificatory decision.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Mammon has no ancient myth cycle. His narratives are medieval and early-modern allegories built from the Gospel metaphor.

### God and Mammon

**Early canonical core:** Matthew presents two masters whose claims are mutually exclusive. The point is not a cosmic battle witnessed in heaven but the impossibility of divided service.

**Lukan elaboration:** Luke surrounds the saying with the parable of the unjust manager and instructions to make friends through “mammon of unrighteousness.” The tension between prudent use of wealth and rejection of its mastery drove later exegesis.

**Thomasine variant:** Thomas combines the saying with analogies about two horses and old and new wine. Mammon remains an alternative master, not an archon.

### The Cave of Mammon

In *The Faerie Queene* II.vii (1590), the knight of Temperance, Guyon, encounters Mammon amid heaps of treasure. Mammon offers him wealth and then conducts him underground through a polluted, infernal economy: ore, furnaces, coffers, guarded treasure, the mouth of Hell, the garden of Proserpina, and the hall of Philotime. Guyon refuses gold, political promotion, and marriage to Philotime. After three days underground without food or rest, he emerges and collapses.

Spenser’s Mammon is a speaking “god of riches,” but the canto is Christian moral allegory, not evidence for worship. Its classical underworld scenery draws Mammon into a literary network involving Proserpina, Tantalus, Pilate, and infernal punishments without making him an ancient classical deity.

### Mammon and Pandemonium

In *Paradise Lost* I (1667), Mammon is already avaricious in heaven: he looks down at its golden pavement rather than upward toward divine glory. After the fall, his brigade mines Hell’s hill, extracts ore, and enables construction of Pandemonium. Milton says that at Mammon’s suggestion humans first “rifled” Earth for hidden treasure.

In Book II’s infernal council, Mammon advocates remaining in Hell and building a rival greatness from its resources rather than renewing open war. He is therefore more than a miner: he supplies a political theology of material self-sufficiency. He does not single-handedly build Pandemonium, and Milton’s architect is Mulciber; Mammon leads extraction and advocates infernal development.

**Tier:** early-modern Protestant epic. Nearly all later claims that Mammon personally “created Hell,” invented money, or rules Pandemonium simplify or expand Milton.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

No ancient or medieval temple, altar, priesthood, festival, sacrifice, votive inscription, theophoric name, oracle, pilgrimage, or institutional cult of Mammon is securely documented.

There is likewise no evidence of:

- human sacrifice to Mammon;
- temple prostitution connected with Mammon;
- a suppressed Syrian cult;
- Christian destruction or conversion of Mammon temples;
- a continuous secret Mammon priesthood;
- a Mammon cult among ancient Gnostics;
- Islamic ritual directed to Mammon.

“Serving Mammon” in Christian discourse denotes moral allegiance to wealth, not participation in a recovered historical cult.

Modern demonolatry supplies prayers, meditations, altars, offerings of coins, green or gold candles, business rites, invocations, and purported sigils. These practices are real as modern religious behavior, but their specific ritual forms are recent and decentralized. Online claims of ancient continuity generally do not identify a premodern manuscript.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before Christianity: the noun

From approximately the second century BCE, Jewish sources attest *māmôn* as property or wealth, sometimes morally qualified but not inherently a supernatural being.

### First and second centuries: personified rival master

Matthew and Luke make wealth a rhetorically personified lord opposed to God. The *Gospel of Thomas* transmits a parallel. No cult, body, or biography appears.

### Fourth to twelfth centuries: exegetical demonization

Jerome and Augustine ordinarily explain the foreign word as riches. Other exegesis draws it toward Belial, Beelzebub, or the Devil because the Gospel calls God and mammon rival masters. By Peter Lombard, Mammon can be explicitly “the name of a devil.”

### Thirteenth to fifteenth centuries: moral taxonomy

Scholastic commentaries and vernacular allegory personalize Mammon. *Piers Plowman* embeds mammon in social criticism; *The Lanterne of Liȝt* gives Mammon a defined place among seven devils and assigns him avarice.

### Sixteenth and seventeenth centuries: literary apotheosis

Spenser creates the most extensive Mammon episode before Milton. Milton integrates Mammon into a full angelic fall narrative, gives him actions in heaven and Hell, and makes mining his defining activity. These works supplied much of the “traditional” biography repeated by later dictionaries.

### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: secular metaphor and fixed image

“Mammon” becomes a conventional English name for wealth worship, capitalism, commercial corruption, and materialism. Collin de Plancy’s dictionary codifies “demon of avarice”; Le Breton gives the demon a memorable body in 1863.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries: plural fictional Mammons

Modern fantasy, games, comics, animation, music, and demonolatry detach Mammon from detailed exegesis while retaining greed, gold, and infernal rank. Different franchises make Mammon male, female, nonhuman, comic, monstrous, entrepreneurial, royal, or one of seven embodied sins. These characters are mutually independent modern reinventions.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Theology and political language

Christian preaching continues to use “the spirit of Mammon” for idolatrous attachment to money. Some charismatic ministries treat Mammon as an actual territorial or personal demon. That ontology is a modern confessional interpretation of Matthew, not something explicitly taught in the verse.

Political and literary writers employ **Mammon**, **mammonism**, and **mammon-worship** as polemical names for unrestrained capitalism, bourgeois acquisitiveness, corruption, or material civilization. This secularized allegory became at least as influential as formal demonology.

### Occultism

Unlike Asmodeus, Bael, or Paimon, Mammon does not derive modern occult authority from an entry in the *Ars Goetia*. Modern workings for Mammon are typically synthetic: Gospel name, medieval sin classification, Miltonic characterization, de Plancy’s image, ceremonial-magic conventions, and contemporary prosperity magic.

Claims that a circulating Mammon seal is “Solomonic” require a manuscript citation. In its absence the responsible classification is **modern invention**.

### Literature and visual culture

- **Spenser, 1590:** Mammon’s subterranean trial of Guyon created the major narrative.
- **Milton, 1667:** The fallen mining spirit created the dominant English literary characterization.
- **Collin de Plancy/Le Breton, 1863:** The demon received his most reproduced historical illustration.
- **Modern fantasy gaming:** Mammon frequently becomes an archdevil or ruler of an infernal realm. Such cosmologies are proprietary fictional settings, not inherited medieval consensus.
- **Television and animation:** Seven-sins ensembles commonly include a Mammon character; personality, gender, costume, and sigil belong to each production.
- **Comics and popular novels:** Mammon may be the literal embodiment of greed or a corporate Satanic figure, extending the older metaphor into consumer society.

### Dante: an important correction

Dante’s *Inferno* does not feature Mammon by name. Canto VII places **Plutus**, the classical wealth deity transformed into a demonic guardian, over the circle of hoarders and spendthrifts. Later comparisons between Plutus and Mammon are understandable but remain retrospective syncretism.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Mammon an ancient deity?

Older dictionaries and popular works sometimes call Mammon a Syrian or Chaldean god of riches. The evidence does not support this. The Semitic word is common, the Gospel personification is transparent, and there is no external cult record. Modern critical treatments therefore begin with lexical personification and trace the later demon.

### Does Matthew name a demon?

Three positions recur:

1. **Ordinary wealth with vivid personification:** the prevailing historical-linguistic reading.
2. **Quasi-personal spiritual power:** a theological reading emphasizing the two “masters.”
3. **Named demon:** securely attested in later Christian interpretation but not explicitly in Matthew.

The grammar permits rhetorical personification; it cannot by itself establish an independently believed supernatural individual.

### Gregory of Nyssa and Beelzebub

The alleged Gregory identification is widely repeated but poorly localized in popular literature. Until an exact critical-edition passage is supplied, it should be labeled a disputed patristic attribution rather than used as the foundation of Mammon’s biography.

### “Seven princes of Hell” and Binsfeld

A popular internet genealogy credits Peter Binsfeld’s 1589 demonology with the now-familiar list Lucifer/pride, Mammon/greed, Asmodeus/lust, Leviathan/envy, Beelzebub/gluttony, Satan/wrath, Belphegor/sloth. This is frequently conflated with the different and earlier *Lanterne of Liȝt* list, whose actual assignments include Mammon as the fifth devil of avarice. The lists should not be merged or projected backward as a universal medieval canon.

### Gnostic Mammon

No Mammon appears as an aeon, archon, ruler, or demon in the standard Nag Hammadi indexes. The label “Gnostic figure” is therefore unsupported unless it means only that the *Gospel of Thomas*, found at Nag Hammadi, reproduces the Gospel saying. Even that requires caution because scholars dispute whether “Gnosticism” is an adequate category for all Nag Hammadi texts.

### State of the evidence

The primary history is unusually asymmetric:

- abundant evidence for the word “wealth”;
- a few foundational Gospel sentences;
- substantial medieval commentary and allegory;
- almost no material-cult evidence;
- increasingly elaborate modern imagery and personality.

This asymmetry explains why later literature is often mistaken for ancient mythology.

## 10. Chronology

- **c. 180 BCE:** Ben Sira composed in Hebrew; 31:8 uses *māmôn* for money or wealth. **Common noun.**
- **Late second–first century BCE:** Damascus Document uses “mammon” in sectarian ethical regulation. **Common noun.**
- **c. 80–90 CE:** Matthew 6:24 opposes service to God and mammon. **Earliest secure personification.**
- **c. 80–90 CE:** Luke 16:9, 11, 13 uses “mammon of unrighteousness” and repeats the two-masters saying.
- **Second century CE, disputed:** *Gospel of Thomas* Saying 47 transmits the opposition; complete witness copied in the fourth century.
- **Fourth century:** Jerome and Augustine explain *mammon* linguistically as riches; related patristic interpretation increasingly associates its mastery with the Devil.
- **Fourth century, disputed attribution:** Gregory of Nyssa allegedly identifies Mammon with Beelzebub.
- **c. 1150:** Peter Lombard calls Mammon the name of a devil and makes riches bear that name.
- **Early fourteenth century:** Nicholas of Lyra repeats *Mammon est nomen daemonis*.
- **c. 1370s–1380s:** *Piers Plowman* develops mammon as social, economic, and spiritual allegory.
- **c. 1409–1410:** *The Lanterne of Liȝt* makes Mammon the fifth devil, commanding avarice and covetousness.
- **1590:** Spenser publishes *The Faerie Queene* Books I–III; Book II.vii narrates Guyon’s descent into Mammon’s realm.
- **1667:** Milton publishes *Paradise Lost*; Mammon mines Hell and argues for infernal material development.
- **1818:** First edition of Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* appears.
- **1863:** Sixth edition identifies Mammon as “demon of avarice” and includes Louis Le Breton’s influential engraving.
- **Nineteenth century:** *Mammonism* and “worship of Mammon” become widespread political and social metaphors.
- **Twentieth century:** Fantasy and occult reference works increasingly standardize Mammon as an archdemon or prince of greed.
- **Twenty-first century:** Games, comics, animation, online demonolatry, and prosperity-oriented occult practice generate numerous incompatible new Mammons and purported sigils.
- **Present:** Mammon remains simultaneously a biblical word for wealth, a Christian moral personification, a literary fallen angel, an occult demon, and a commercial-fiction character. No continuous ancient cult has been demonstrated.

### Critical orientation

The most useful corrective supplied by modern scholarship, including the *Dictionary of Deities and Demons in the Bible*, is that Gospel personification does not demonstrate a pre-Christian god. Studies of the Devil by Jeffrey Burton Russell, Henry Ansgar Kelly, and Bernard Bamberger help locate Mammon within the long process by which metaphors, pagan gods, fallen angels, moral abstractions, and literary characters were reorganized as named demons. James M. Robinson’s and Marvin Meyer’s Nag Hammadi editions are relevant chiefly for establishing the *absence* of a Mammon archon outside the Thomas saying.
