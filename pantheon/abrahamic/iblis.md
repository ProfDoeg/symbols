# Iblis: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the Qur’an, Iblīs is a singular rebellious heavenly being, explicitly called “one of the jinn” in Q 18:50 but placed among the angels when they are commanded to prostrate before Adam. Later traditions variously classify him as a jinn, an angelic being who fell, the progenitor or chief of the devils, the archetypal tempter, a tragic monotheist, or a literary personification of pride, despair, imperialism, and alienated intellect.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The Arabic name **إِبْلِيس, Iblīs** first appears securely in the Qur’an, proclaimed and written in the early seventh century CE. It occurs eleven times, chiefly in the repeated narrative of his refusal to prostrate before Adam. No securely dated pre-Qur’anic inscription or Arabic text naming Iblīs is known. Earlier Jewish and Christian adversaries—Hebrew *ha-śāṭān*, Greek *diabolos*, Belial, Mastema, Satanael, and the devil who refuses homage to Adam—are historical antecedents or comparanda, not attestations of the name Iblīs.

## Evidence labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** Qur’anic material and early manuscript evidence.
- **[CE] Canonical elaboration:** canonical or broadly authoritative Islamic exegesis and hadith.
- **[RV] Regional or sectarian variant.**
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** tafsir, theology, philosophy, or demonology.
- **[SY] Syncretistic identification.**
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or literary reinvention.**

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Iblīs

**إِبْلِيس, Iblīs** is the Qur’anic proper name. It is not simply interchangeable with every occurrence of **شيطان, shayṭān**, “adversary/devil.” In the Qur’anic narrative, *Iblīs* normally designates the individual who refuses God’s command; *al-shayṭān* designates the tempter in the Adam story and can function as a title or role, while plural **الشياطين, al-shayāṭīn** denotes many devils. Later interpretation commonly makes Iblīs the first or chief shayṭān.

The Qur’an names Iblīs in Q 2:34; 7:11; 15:31–32; 17:61; 18:50; 20:116; and 38:74–75, 38:85. Nine of the eleven occurrences belong directly to the refusal episode.

### Etymology

Two explanations compete:

1. **Greek derivation [MSR].** The dominant modern philological explanation derives *Iblīs* ultimately from Greek **διάβολος, diábolos**, “slanderer/adversary,” probably through a Christian Semitic intermediary such as Syriac. The Septuagint uses *diabolos* to translate Hebrew **שָׂטָן, śāṭān**. The lack of securely attested pre-Qur’anic Arabic uses of *Iblīs* supports borrowing, although the exact transmission route remains uncertain.

2. **Arabic derivation [LS].** Muslim lexicographers and exegetes connected the name with Arabic **أبلس, ablasa**, “to despair,” “become confounded,” or “lose hope,” interpreting Iblīs as “the one who despaired of God’s mercy.” This is theologically apt and influential but generally treated by modern philologists as secondary wordplay or folk etymology.

It is therefore too strong to say the Greek etymology is absolutely proven; it is the leading reconstruction.

### Other names and titles

- **al-Shayṭān, الشيطان**, “the Satan/adversary”: Qur’anic title associated with the tempter, but also a common noun applicable to hostile beings among jinn and humans. Iblīs is a shayṭān after or through his rebellion; not every shayṭān is Iblīs.
- **al-Shayṭān al-rajīm, الشيطان الرجيم**, “the accursed/stoned Satan”: liturgical designation in the formula seeking refuge from Satan before Qur’anic recitation. It is normally understood to include Iblīs.
- **ʿaduww mubīn**, “manifest enemy”: a Qur’anic description of Satan in relation to humanity.
- **ʿAzāzīl, عزازيل**: a later exegetical name for Iblīs before his fall. It resembles Jewish **Azazel**, but the identification and etymology are uncertain. The Qur’an never calls Iblīs ʿAzāzīl. Reports transmitted under Ibn ʿAbbās and developed in *qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ* describe ʿAzāzīl as a powerful angel or jinn elevated among the angels. **[CE/MA]**
- **al-Ḥārith, الحارث**: another alleged pre-fall name in later traditions. Not Qur’anic. **[MA]**
- **Abū Murra**, “Father of Bitterness/Murra”: folkloric or demonological kunya. **[F/MA]**
- **ṭāwūs al-malāʾika**, “peacock of the angels”: a later literary title evoking his former beauty or worship. It is not Qur’anic and should not be retrojected into early Islam. **[MA]**
- **qāʾid ahl al-firāq**, “leader of the people of separation”: Iqbal’s literary characterization in the *Jāvīd-nāma*. **[MI]**

### Classifications

- **Qur’anic:** “one of the jinn” (*kāna mina l-jinn*, Q 18:50), created from fire according to his own comparison with clay-made Adam.
- **Early exegetical angel theory:** Iblīs belonged to a fiery class or “tribe” of angels called jinn; the command and grammatical exception naturally include him among the angels.
- **Early exegetical jinn theory:** associated especially with al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī: Iblīs “was never an angel, not for the blink of an eye”; he was a jinn admitted to angelic company.
- **Later mainstream popular formulation:** a jinn so pious that he stood among the angels, then became the chief devil.
- **Sufi symbolic classifications:** ego, imagination, separation, jealous lover, failed monotheist, or necessary instrument of testing.
- **Modern occult classifications:** “Islamic Lucifer,” demon king, or object of veneration. These are loose modern equivalences, not stable categories in Qur’anic or classical Islamic theology.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The Qur’an

The Qur’an is the earliest secure source for Iblīs. Its proclamations belong to the early seventh century; the received consonantal text was transmitted in seventh-century manuscripts. The Birmingham folios provide exceptionally early physical evidence for portions of suras 18–20: their parchment was radiocarbon dated to 568–645 CE, and the hand is early Ḥijāzī. Those leaves contain Q 18:17–31, however, not Q 18:50; they therefore attest the early material transmission of the surrounding sura but are not themselves a physical witness to the word *Iblīs*. Corpus Coranicum catalogues other early Qur’anic witnesses, but assigning one unique “oldest surviving Iblīs” manuscript requires manuscript-by-manuscript palaeographic work and cannot presently be stated securely.

The Qur’anic passages are:

- **Q 2:30–39 [EA]:** God announces a terrestrial *khalīfa*; teaches Adam the names; commands the angels to prostrate. All do so except Iblīs, who refuses, acts arrogantly, and becomes one of the unbelievers. Satan later causes Adam and his spouse to slip.
- **Q 7:11–18 [EA]:** Iblīs explains: “I am better than he; You created me from fire and created him from clay.” Expelled and abased, he requests delay until resurrection and promises to ambush humanity from every direction.
- **Q 15:26–43 [EA]:** Adam is made from sounding clay; the jinn had previously been made from scorching fire. Iblīs refuses, is cursed until Judgment, receives respite, and vows to adorn error for all except God’s sincere servants.
- **Q 17:61–65 [EA]:** Iblīs protests against prostrating to a clay creature, promises to seize Adam’s descendants, and is licensed to entice them by voice, cavalry, infantry, wealth, and offspring; God’s true servants remain beyond his authority.
- **Q 18:50 [EA]:** the decisive classification verse: “He was one of the jinn and departed from the command of his Lord.” It also speaks of Iblīs and his **offspring** (*dhurriyya*), without explaining their generation.
- **Q 20:115–123 [EA]:** Iblīs refuses; God identifies Satan as enemy to Adam and his spouse; Satan promises immortality and an imperishable kingdom and induces them to eat from the tree.
- **Q 38:71–85 [EA]:** the most dramatic dialogue: God says Adam was created with “My two hands”; Iblīs claims fiery superiority; God curses him; Iblīs vows to mislead all but the purified servants; God promises to fill Gehenna with him and his followers.

The Qur’an does not give Iblīs a biography before Adam, a wife, a throne, a bodily portrait, the name ʿAzāzīl, or an account of how he entered the garden.

### Pre-Qur’anic comparative corpus

These sources matter historically, but none names Iblīs:

- **Hebrew Bible:** Job 1–2 and Zechariah 3 present *the satan* as an accuser within the divine council, not an independent rebel. Genesis 3 has a serpent but does not call it Satan. Isaiah 14 addresses the king of Babylon through the image of fallen *hêlēl ben-šaḥar*; Ezekiel 28 addresses the king of Tyre. Their later application to Lucifer/Satan is reception history.
- **Septuagint:** Greek *diabolos* translates Hebrew *śāṭān* and supplies the most plausible lexical ancestor of *Iblīs*.
- **1 Enoch, chiefly third–first centuries BCE:** rebellious Watchers descend and corrupt humanity. Their chiefs are not Iblīs, and their offense is not refusal to bow to Adam.
- **Jubilees, second century BCE:** Mastema is an adversarial prince who requests authority over a remnant of evil spirits. He is a functional comparator, not Iblīs.
- **Tobit:** Asmodeus persecutes Sarah and is defeated by Raphael; there is no Iblīs.
- **Dead Sea Scrolls:** Belial/Mastema and the spirits of darkness occupy cosmic-adversary roles. No Iblīs is attested.
- **Life of Adam and Eve / Apocalypse of Moses, probably first centuries CE in a complex multilingual transmission:** in several versions the devil says he refused Michael’s command to worship or honor Adam because he was created before Adam. This is the clearest early narrative cognate.
- **3 Baruch and Gospel of Bartholomew:** develop Satan’s fall and the Adamic refusal in Christian apocryphal forms.
- **Syriac *Cave of Treasures*, probably late antique, with later manuscripts:** the adversary refuses homage to Adam and contrasts his fiery nature with Adam’s dust. This is especially close to the Qur’anic rationale. Scholarship speaks more cautiously of a shared late-antique discourse or intertextual environment than of demonstrable direct copying.
- **New Testament:** Satan/Devil tempts Jesus; Luke 10:18 depicts Satan falling; Revelation 12 identifies the dragon with “the ancient serpent,” Devil, and Satan. No Adamic prostration scene and no Iblīs.
- **Talmud and midrash:** Samael and the serpent acquire expanded roles, but the exact Qur’anic refusal narrative is not a simple transcription of a single rabbinic source.
- **Hekhalot, Sefer Raziel, and Zohar:** important for Jewish angelology and demonology, but they do not attest Iblīs by name.

### Nag Hammadi and the “Gnostic pantheon”

**Negative finding:** Iblīs does not appear by name in the Nag Hammadi codices, whose surviving Coptic manuscripts are fourth-century books translating or preserving mostly second- and third-century Greek works.

Texts such as the *Apocryphon of John*, *Hypostasis of the Archons*, *On the Origin of the World*, and *Apocalypse of Adam* feature Yaldabaoth, Saklas, Samael, archons, Sophia, and hostile creator figures. These are not securely identifiable as Iblīs. The Gnostic creator’s boast, hostility to higher humanity, and association with ignorance made later comparison tempting, but equation of Iblīs with Yaldabaoth or “the Demiurge” is a **modern syncretistic construction [SY/MI]**, not an ancient attestation.

The heresiologists Irenaeus, Hippolytus, and Epiphanius likewise describe numerous “Gnostic” systems but do not document Iblīs. They are evidence for Christian classifications of their opponents, not for an Iblīs cult.

### Tafsir and historical narrative

- **Muqātil b. Sulaymān, eighth century:** among the earliest substantially surviving Qur’anic commentaries; develops narrative links around Adam and Iblīs.
- **al-Ṭabarī, d. 923, *Jāmiʿ al-bayān* and *Taʾrīkh* [CE/LS]:** preserves rival reports. One lineage, often associated with Ibn ʿAbbās, makes Iblīs an angel or member of an angelic group called *jinn*, sometimes sent against earthly jinn. Another, associated with al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī, insists that he was a non-angelic jinn. Al-Ṭabarī’s value is precisely that he preserves disagreement rather than a single primitive doctrine.
- **al-Thaʿlabī, d. 1035, and al-Kisāʾī, *Qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ* [MA]:** expand his former worship, names, campaign against jinn, examination of Adam’s empty body, and means of entering paradise.
- **al-Zamakhsharī, al-Rāzī, al-Bayḍāwī, al-Qurṭubī, Ibn Kathīr [LS]:** debate the grammatical exception in “the angels prostrated except Iblīs,” angelic impeccability, and Q 18:50.
- **Shiʿi tafsir [CE/RV]:** generally stresses that angels obey God and therefore Iblīs was a jinn, though the inherited report tradition remains diverse.

### Hadith

Hadith collections contain further portrayals, but their compilation dates and isnāds must be distinguished from the events they report:

- **Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim 2813b [CE]:** Iblīs places his throne on water and dispatches agents; the agent who separates spouses receives special praise.
- **Ṣaḥīḥ Muslim / Sunan Ibn Mājah 1052 [CE]:** when a human recites a prostration verse and prostrates, Satan withdraws weeping because he refused the corresponding command.
- **Ramadan traditions [CE]:** “satans” are chained. These speak of plural devils and do not necessarily mean that every temptation or Iblīs himself ceases.
- Numerous reports concerning Satan’s presence at meals, sleep, prayer, sexual intercourse, and marketplaces concern *shayṭān* generically. They cannot automatically be converted into personal acts of Iblīs.

### Sufi and Persian corpus

- **Sahl al-Tustarī and Junayd, ninth century:** sayings attributed to early Sufis begin treating Iblīs as a warning about worship without obedience, or as a paradoxical monotheist.
- **al-Ḥallāj, d. 922, *Kitāb al-Ṭawāsīn* [MA/LS]:** makes Iblīs and Muḥammad parallel extreme figures: Muhammad obeys in nearness; Iblīs, described as an incomparable heavenly monotheist, refuses to bow because he will turn to no other. Hallāj does not simply acquit him. His Iblīs combines love, pride, election, delusion, and damnation.
- **Aḥmad al-Ghazālī, d. 1126; ʿAyn al-Quḍāt Hamadānī, d. 1131 [MA]:** develop the lover interpretation. Iblīs’s curse becomes the lover’s willingness to suffer separation from the beloved, though this never became ordinary creedal doctrine.
- **Sanāʾī, ʿAṭṭār, Rūmī [MA]:** employ Iblīs variously. ʿAṭṭār sometimes presents him as perceiving Adam’s hidden mystery, elsewhere as deceiver and emblem of ego. Rūmī overwhelmingly treats him as the master of self-justification: the lesson is not that Iblīs was right, but that spiritual pedigree and clever argument cannot excuse disobedience.
- **Ibn ʿArabī and later metaphysical readers [LS]:** sometimes associate Iblīs with imagination or an incomplete apprehension of divine form, but popular claims that Ibn ʿArabī simply “worshipped” or canonized Iblīs are distortions.

## 3. Domain, attributes, powers, and iconography

### Domain and characteristic actions

Iblīs has no cultic “domain” comparable to an ancient city-god. His textual functions are:

- refusing Adamic prostration;
- arguing superiority from elemental composition;
- requesting respite until resurrection;
- tempting or misleading human beings;
- beautifying wrongdoing;
- commanding or coordinating subordinate devils;
- testing the distinction between sincere devotion and pride;
- embodying despair from mercy, envy, ego, false analogy, and separation.

Q 17:65 and Q 15:42 deny him coercive authority over God’s true servants. His paradigmatic power is suggestion or enticement, not independent creation and not equality with God. Islamic theology consequently rejects a fully dualistic cosmos in which Iblīs is God’s coeternal opposite.

### Fire, clay, and false analogy

Iblīs’s argument “You created me from fire and him from clay” becomes the classic example of **qiyās fāsid**, defective analogy: he ranks substances against an explicit command. Other interpreters see his failure as inability to perceive the divine spirit breathed into Adam. The claim that fire is inherently nobler than earth is Iblīs’s argument, not a proposition endorsed by the Qur’an.

### Body and form

The Qur’an supplies no physical portrait, horns, wings, tail, color, gendered anatomy, or fixed number of limbs. Masculine grammar and later genealogical stories produce a consistently male literary identity.

Later narratives give him:

- former angelic beauty, sometimes four wings;
- a transformed demonic face after the curse;
- access to animal forms or concealment in the serpent;
- offspring, inferred from Q 18:50 but elaborated into named specialist demons.

These are exegetical, artistic, or folkloric accretions.

### Iconography

Islamic manuscript painting sometimes depicts the refusal episode despite the absence of a Qur’anic description.

- A Persian rendition of al-Ṭabarī/Balʿamī produced at Herat around 1416 depicts the angels bowing while Iblīs remains apart. **[MA]**
- British Library Or. 11837, fol. 9v, from a sixteenth-century manuscript of *Majālis al-ʿushshāq*, depicts Adam honored by angels and Iblīs refusing. Iblīs is differentiated by posture, dress, and sometimes darkened or demonic features.
- A monumental Safavid *Fālnāma* folio, Qazvin, approximately 1550s–early 1560s, shows angels bowing before Adam and Eve; the associated interpretation identifies the temptation with Satan/Iblīs. The painting belonged to an omen book, not to an illustrated Qur’an.
- Ottoman and Persian cosmological manuscripts depict jinn and demons with composite animal-human bodies, horns, claws, flames, or extraordinary colors. Such forms belong to regional visual vocabularies and are not canonical portraits of Iblīs.

There is no recognized archaeological “cult statue” of Iblīs and no continuous ancient iconographic type.

### Associations often falsely presented as canonical

- **Peacock and serpent:** later paradise-entry legends say the peacock introduced Iblīs to the serpent, which carried him into the garden. Neither animal has this role in the Qur’an.
- **Friday, Saturday, black, red, the number eleven, a planet, or a metal:** no stable canonical Iblīs correspondence exists. Modern occult tables assigning such correspondences are recent constructions.
- **Ruler of hell:** Iblīs is threatened with hell; he is not the Qur’anic sovereign or jailer of hell. Mālik is the named keeper in Q 43:77.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other beings

### Origin and offspring

The Qur’an gives no parents or birth. Iblīs says he was created from fire; Q 18:50 calls him one of the jinn and mentions “him and his offspring.” It does not state how those offspring arise.

Later commentators propose:

- he reproduces sexually through a wife;
- male and female organs exist in his body;
- offspring emerge from eggs;
- the “offspring” are followers rather than biological children.

None is securely early. Lists of named children—such as demons assigned to markets, prayer distraction, sexual immorality, false reports, or bereavement—vary across adab, tafsir, and folklore.

### Hierarchy

In the Qur’an he is subordinate to God, answerable to divine command, granted rather than possessing respite, and limited in power. In tafsir he may have been a guardian of paradise, treasurer of the lower heaven, leader against rebellious jinn, or preeminent worshipper. Those offices are later explanatory narratives.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine or supernatural being

- **God / Allah — command, refusal, interrogation, curse, respite, limitation.** Q 7:11–18; 15:28–43; 17:61–65; 38:71–85, early seventh century. **[EA]**
- **Adam — refuses to prostrate; claims elemental superiority; vows to corrupt Adam’s descendants.** Q 2:34; 7:11–18; 15:28–43; 17:61–65; 18:50; 20:116–123; 38:71–85. **[EA]**
- **Adam’s unnamed spouse — Satan tempts both members of the first couple.** Q 7:20–22; 20:120–121. The Qur’an does not name her Eve/Ḥawwāʾ. **[EA]**
- **The angels, collective — remains standing when they prostrate; his relation to their class becomes the central exegetical dispute.** Same Qur’anic passages. **[EA/CE]**
- **Moses — Hallāj’s Iblīs meets or converses with Moses and defends his exclusive devotion and acceptance of the curse.** *Kitāb al-Ṭawāsīn*, compiled by or transmitted under al-Ḥallāj, early tenth century. **[MA]**
- **Muhammad — Hallāj juxtaposes Iblīs and Muhammad as two extreme missions: Iblīs refuses prostration, while Muhammad’s gaze does not swerve during the ascent.** *Ṭawāsīn*, early tenth century. This is symbolic comparison, not a Qur’anic meeting. **[MA]**
- **Gabriel — later Sufi and literary texts contrast obedient Gabriel with rebellious or separated Iblīs; Iqbal stages “Gabriel and Iblīs” as a dialogue between celestial serenity and dangerous striving.** Iqbal, twentieth century. **[MI]**
- **Serpent — later *qiṣaṣ* and pictorial tradition makes the serpent Iblīs’s vehicle into paradise.** Medieval Islamic narrative; not Qur’anic. Whether the serpent counts as a supernatural being varies by version. **[MA/F]**
- **Peacock — later Persian and related stories make the peacock an intermediary whom Iblīs deceives.** Medieval or early-modern accretion; not Qur’anic. **[MA/F]**
- **Mālik, keeper of hell — no securely attested direct interaction found.**
- **Yaldabaoth, Saklas, Samael, Sophia, Barbelo, and the Nag Hammadi archons — no ancient textual interaction with Iblīs found.** Modern equations are syncretistic, not documentary. **[absence finding]**
- **Azazel, Mastema, Belial, Lucifer — no source stages a meeting with Iblīs.** They are comparative or later equated adversaries, not interacting characters. **[absence finding]**
- **Melek Ṭāʾūs — no secure Yazidi sacred narrative stages an interaction with Iblīs.** External identification of the two is contested and historically harmful. **[absence finding]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The refusal before Adam

**Early core [EA].** God creates Adam from clay, breathes spirit into him, and orders the angels to prostrate. Iblīs refuses because he considers fiery creation superior. God expels and curses him. Iblīs requests respite until resurrection and receives it. He promises to mislead Adam’s descendants except the sincere or purified. God restricts his authority and promises hell for him and his followers.

The Qur’anic repetitions are complementary but not identical:

- Q 2 emphasizes arrogance and unbelief.
- Q 7 emphasizes Iblīs’s four-direction ambush.
- Q 15 contrasts clay with the jinn’s earlier fiery creation.
- Q 17 expands his instruments into voice, cavalry, infantry, wealth, and children.
- Q 18 explicitly calls him a jinn and mentions offspring.
- Q 20 connects his temptation with immortality and imperishable rule.
- Q 38 emphasizes creation with God’s “two hands” and the filling of hell.

### Earlier Adamic-refusal tradition

The *Life of Adam and Eve* tradition has Satan refuse to worship Adam at Michael’s instruction, commonly because Satan was created first. The *Cave of Treasures* adds the elemental contrast between fire and dust. The Qur’an shares the refusal and elemental argument but reshapes them within its own theology.

Direct literary dependence cannot be demonstrated from an extant manuscript in Muhammad’s environment. The responsible historical conclusion is that the Qur’an participates in a widespread late-antique Adamic and angelological discourse.

### The temptation and fall

The Qur’an does not say that Iblīs becomes a snake. “Satan” whispers to Adam and his spouse, promises immortality or angelic existence, and induces the eating. Responsibility falls on both humans; the Qur’an does not construct a doctrine of inherited original sin from Eve’s act.

Later storytellers explain how expelled Iblīs re-entered paradise: he enlisted the peacock, hid in the serpent’s mouth, or called from outside. These solve a narrative question the Qur’an leaves unanswered. **[MA/F]**

### Former piety, war against the jinn, and fall

In narratives preserved by al-Ṭabarī and later *qiṣaṣ*, pre-Adamic jinn corrupt the earth. Angels defeat them under Iblīs or ʿAzāzīl. His victory and immense worship generate pride; God’s plan to create Adam exposes it. Other versions make him a jinn captured as a child and raised among angels.

These accounts cannot be combined into a single genealogy. They are rival attempts to reconcile:

- the command addressed to angels;
- Q 18:50’s designation of Iblīs as jinn;
- the idea that angels obey;
- Iblīs’s fiery nature.

### Inspection of Adam’s body

Medieval narratives say Iblīs entered or circled Adam’s still-unanimated clay body, found it hollow, and predicted that a creature dependent on appetite could be mastered. Najm al-Dīn Rāzī and Rūmī use the scene psychologically. ʿAṭṭār sometimes reverses its meaning: by refusing to bow, Iblīs sees a divine secret hidden from the prostrate angels. **[MA]**

### The Sufi “tragic lover”

Hallāj’s Iblīs argues that he knew the divine command to bow but was bound by an earlier demand to worship God alone. He accepts curse and separation rather than turn toward another. This is not a neutral celebration: Hallāj places Iblīs beside Pharaoh as a dangerous claimant to exclusive insight, and Iblīs’s assertion of “I” remains his ruin.

Aḥmad al-Ghazālī, ʿAyn al-Quḍāt, and later Persian poets intensify the lover motif. Other Sufis reject it or use it as a warning: professed love that violates the beloved’s present command is ego disguised as fidelity. Both currents must be retained.

### Iblīs and Muʿāwiya

Rūmī’s *Mathnawī* recounts Iblīs waking Muʿāwiya for prayer. After prolonged argument, Iblīs admits that if Muʿāwiya overslept, remorse might bring greater spiritual profit; waking him prevents that profit. The story presents satanic deception as capable of wearing the mask of piety. **[MA]**

### Eschatological fate

The Qur’an grants respite “until the day they are raised” or “the appointed time,” not redemption. Hell will be filled with Iblīs and those who follow him. Later universalist or mystical speculation occasionally imagines eventual reconciliation, but no Qur’anic passage states that Iblīs repents or is restored.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

No securely documented temple, priesthood, festival, sacrifice, pilgrimage center, votive inscription, or ancient organized cult of Iblīs is known. Islamic practice treats him as an enemy from whom refuge is sought, not as a recipient of worship.

Practices connected indirectly with the Iblīs narrative include:

- reciting **aʿūdhu bi-llāhi mina l-shayṭāni l-rajīm**, seeking refuge in God from the accursed Satan;
- prostration during specified Qur’anic recitations, which hadith frames as the obedient reversal of Iblīs’s refusal;
- stoning the pillars at Minā during the hajj, popularly narrated as commemorating Abraham’s rejection of Satan. The rite does not establish a cult of Iblīs, and the pillars are not literally regarded in normative law as his body;
- protective prayers and amulets against devils or jinn. Their targets are usually generic *shayāṭīn*, not necessarily Iblīs personally.

### Alleged sacrifice and “devil worship”

No credible evidence establishes human sacrifice, temple prostitution, or an institutional Iblīs cult in classical Islam. Accusations of “Iblīs worship” have instead been used polemically against heterodox Muslims, Sufis, Yazidis, and modern occultists.

The most consequential case is the external identification of **Melek Ṭāʾūs**, the Yazidi Peacock Angel, with Iblīs or Satan. Yazidis do not generally define Melek Ṭāʾūs as an evil devil. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century outsiders treated perceived narrative similarities—especially refusal to bow—as proof of “devil worship.” Scholarship on Yazidi oral religion has also questioned the antiquity and authenticity of the so-called *Black Book* and *Book of Revelation* manuscripts circulated as ancient Yazidi scripture. The equation contributed to persecution and should be labeled an external polemical identification, not an established Yazidi doctrine.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the seventh century

Jewish and Christian traditions develop several partially overlapping adversaries: the divine prosecutor, fallen Watchers, Mastema, Belial, the Eden serpent, Lucifer, and the devil who refuses Adamic homage. None is yet Iblīs, but the late-antique narrative field makes the Qur’anic figure intelligible.

### Early seventh century: Qur’anic formation

The Qur’an names Iblīs, centers his fall on Adamic prostration, and gives him a distinctive first-person argument from fire and clay. He is neither a coequal evil god nor an uncontrolled rebel: he petitions God, receives limited respite, and lacks power over sincere servants.

### Eighth–tenth centuries: exegetical expansion

Early Muslims dispute whether he is angel or jinn. Reports associated with Ibn ʿAbbās expand an angelic or quasi-angelic past; al-Ḥasan al-Baṣrī supplies the categorical jinn position. Al-Ṭabarī collects the alternatives. Narrative cycles add ʿAzāzīl, warfare against pre-Adamic jinn, former offices, Adam’s hollow body, and specialist offspring.

### Tenth–thirteenth centuries: theological and Sufi interiorization

Hallāj turns Iblīs into a paradox of love and selfhood. Persian mystical literature makes him alternately tragic lover, master of ego, jealous monotheist, delusive imagination, and proof that millennia of worship cannot replace obedience. The favorable and condemnatory readings coexist.

### Fourteenth–seventeenth centuries: visual and cosmographical elaboration

Persian and Ottoman painters visualize the prostration scene and develop regional demon iconographies. Story collections and cosmographies stabilize motifs absent from the Qur’an—serpent, peacock, monstrous form, named demonic descendants.

### Nineteenth–twentieth centuries: comparative religion and colonial classification

European Orientalists compare Iblīs with Satan, Lucifer, Azazel, Angra Mainyu, and Melek Ṭāʾūs. Some comparisons illuminate transmission; others flatten distinct traditions or reproduce the “devil-worshipper” accusation. Modern scholarship increasingly distinguishes genealogical relationship, functional analogy, and later equation.

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries: literary and occult reinvention

Iqbal converts Iblīs into a political and philosophical speaker. Modern Satanist, Luciferian, and online demonolatry communities sometimes adopt him as an Islamic mask of Lucifer or as an antinomian liberator. These movements are recent, decentralized, and poorly evidenced in scale. They do not continue an ancient Iblīs cult.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Muhammad Iqbal

Iqbal’s Iblīs is neither merely the Qur’anic tempter nor Hallāj’s tragic lover.

- **Jāvīd-nāma, 1932:** Iblīs appears as “leader of the people of separation.” His restless, oppositional energy contrasts with passive celestial perfection; nevertheless, separation from God remains his defining condition.
- **“Gabriel and Iblīs”:** a dramatic dialogue contrasts Gabriel’s secure heavenly obedience with Iblīs’s experience of conflict, passion, and historical struggle.
- **“Iblīs kī Majlis-e Shūrā” (“The Parliament of Iblīs”), 1936:** Iblīs and five advisers discuss imperialism, capitalism, communism, democracy, religion, and the threat posed by a renewed revolutionary Islam. This is political satire and philosophical drama, not transmitted demonology.
- In Iqbal, Iblīs can represent intellect severed from love, modern systems of exploitation, the dynamism of negation, and the danger of sterile rebellion.

### Literature

Dante and Milton concern the Christian Devil, not Iblīs by name, but their fallen rebel profoundly shaped later readers’ expectations. Applying Milton’s Satan directly to the Qur’an imports themes—war in heaven, rule in hell, heroic republican rebellion—that the Qur’an does not contain.

Modern Arabic, Persian, Urdu, Turkish, and anglophone writers use Iblīs for political tyranny, colonial modernity, ego, or dissent. Salman Rushdie’s *The Satanic Verses* concerns the broader Satanic-verse tradition and modern identity politics; it does not supply an authoritative Iblīs mythology.

### Occultism and grimoires

The *Lemegeton*, *Book of Abramelin*, Renaissance magic, Golden Dawn materials, and most European grimoires do not contain the Qur’anic Iblīs as a stable spirit with seal, office, and planetary correspondence. Their demons—Lucifer, Satan, Belial, Asmodeus, and the goetic kings—belong to different textual genealogies.

Recent occult manuals and websites sometimes:

- identify Iblīs with Lucifer or Satan;
- assign him a seal, color, planet, incense, or enn;
- connect him with Melek Ṭāʾūs;
- call Hallāj an “Iblīs worshipper” or proto-Satanist.

These are **modern inventions or syncretisms [MI]** unless a traceable older source is supplied. Hallāj’s paradoxical interpretation is not evidence of ritual worship.

### Politics and extremist appropriation

Muslim polemic often calls an enemy, ruler, ideology, or tempter “Iblīs” metaphorically. Extremist rhetoric may demonize opponents as *shayāṭīn*; that language does not show belief that the historical opponent literally is Iblīs.

Conversely, the accusation that Yazidis worship Iblīs/Satan has served persecution, including modern jihadist violence. The documentary finding is that outsiders’ equation played a role in stigmatization; it is not a neutral ethnographic description of Yazidi belief.

## 9. Historiography and major debates

### Angel or jinn?

This is the central traditional controversy.

**Angel evidence:**

- the command is addressed to angels;
- the Arabic exception “except Iblīs” most naturally includes him in the addressed class;
- early reports associated with Ibn ʿAbbās describe a fiery angelic group called jinn;
- some theologians allowed angelic disobedience or distinguished classes of angels.

**Jinn evidence:**

- Q 18:50 explicitly says *kāna mina l-jinn*;
- Iblīs describes himself as created from fire, while a famous hadith distinguishes angels made from light and jinn from smokeless fire;
- Qur’anic angels are characteristically obedient;
- the grammatical exception can be discontinuous: everyone addressed obeyed, but Iblīs—present among them though not one of them—did not.

**Historical conclusion:** both readings are early. The jinn interpretation became especially prominent in modern Sunni apologetic presentation, but it is inaccurate to claim classical tafsir unanimously denied angelic status.

### Iblīs and al-Shayṭān

Some interpreters treat them as two phases or titles of one being: Iblīs before/at the refusal, Satan in his function after it. Others stress that *shayṭān* is a class or role and *Iblīs* a proper name. The Qur’an permits identification in the Adam narrative but does not make every satan Iblīs.

### Name derivation

Greek *diabolos* remains the leading philological derivation. Arabic *ablasa* is influential traditional exegesis. A direct derivation through one securely documented Syriac form has not been demonstrated beyond dispute.

### Dependence on Jewish or Christian sources

Older scholarship often spoke of simple “borrowing.” Current work more often reconstructs a multilingual late-antique environment in which biblical, para-biblical, homiletic, and oral Adam traditions circulated. The *Life of Adam and Eve* and *Cave of Treasures* are close parallels, but an exact documentary chain into the Qur’an remains unproven.

### Is the Sufi Iblīs vindicated?

No single “Sufi doctrine” exists. Hallāj and some Persian mystics explore Iblīs’s exclusive love; other Sufis condemn his ego, envy, literalism, or refusal to recognize God in Adam. Even sympathetic texts preserve his curse and separation. “Iblīs as the first true monotheist” is therefore an attested paradoxical reading, not the consensus of Sufism.

### Gnostic origin claims

No Nag Hammadi text names Iblīs, and no ancient Gnostic source equates him with Yaldabaoth. Similarities—rebellion, hostility to Adam, cosmic deception—are insufficient to establish identity. Describing Iblīs as a member of a “Gnostic pantheon” is a modern cataloguing decision, not an ancient classification.

### Primary evidence and editions

The key evidentiary bodies are:

- early Qur’anic manuscripts catalogued by Corpus Coranicum and major libraries;
- critical Qur’an editions and historical commentaries;
- al-Ṭabarī’s tafsir and chronicle;
- *qiṣaṣ al-anbiyāʾ* collections;
- Hallāj’s *Ṭawāsīn*, critically associated with Louis Massignon and Paul Nwyia;
- Persian poetic editions of Sanāʾī, ʿAṭṭār, Rūmī, and Iqbal;
- illustrated manuscripts in the British Library, Smithsonian National Museum of Asian Art, Morgan Library, Topkapı Palace Library, and other collections.

Gustav Davidson’s *Dictionary of Angels* is useful as a finding aid but frequently merges late, occult, literary, and scriptural material without adequate historical separation. Bamberger, Jeffrey Burton Russell, Henry Ansgar Kelly, and modern Qur’anic scholarship are more useful for tracing the development of adversarial figures. Works on “Gnosticism” by Kurt Rudolph, Birger Pearson, Michael Williams, and the modern Nag Hammadi editorial tradition warn against treating heterogeneous movements as one religion.

## 10. Dedicated contested-material findings

- **Etymology:** Greek *diabolos* is most likely; Arabic *ablasa* is a meaningful traditional reinterpretation, not certainly the historical derivation.
- **Origin:** no pre-Qur’anic Iblīs by name is securely attested.
- **Angel/jinn dispute:** genuinely early and never completely uniform.
- **Syncretism:** equations with Lucifer, Azazel, Yaldabaoth, Angra Mainyu, or Melek Ṭāʾūs are later comparisons, not timeless identities.
- **Human sacrifice:** no credible evidence for sacrifice to Iblīs.
- **Temple prostitution:** no evidence.
- **Institutional cult:** none securely documented before modern individual occult practice.
- **Suppression:** because no historical public cult is documented, there is no documented conversion or destruction of “Iblīs temples.” Persecution of alleged “devil worshippers,” especially Yazidis, is real but rests on contested identification.
- **Occult grimoires:** traditional European grimoires do not provide an ancient Iblīs seal or office.
- **Neopagan or reconstructionist worship:** no historically continuous reconstructionist Iblīs religion is established; contemporary online veneration is modern and decentralized.
- **Gnostic identification:** absent from Nag Hammadi and the heresiologists.
- **“First monotheist”:** a medieval mystical paradox, especially associated with Hallājian reception, not Qur’anic doctrine.
- **“Ruler of hell”:** popular or literary misconception.
- **Serpent and peacock:** medieval explanatory folklore, not Qur’anic.
- **ʿAzāzīl as original name:** later exegetical tradition, not securely early revelation.
- **Distinction from Shayṭān:** Iblīs is an individual name; *shayṭān* is a role/class and only contextually the same individual.

## 11. Chronology

| Date/period | Attestation or development |
|---|---|
| Third–first centuries BCE | 1 Enoch, Jubilees, and Dead Sea Scroll traditions develop Watchers, Mastema, and Belial; none is Iblīs. |
| First centuries CE | *Life of Adam and Eve* traditions attest the devil’s refusal to honor Adam. |
| Fourth–sixth centuries | Syriac and Christian apocryphal traditions, especially the *Cave of Treasures*, develop the fire-versus-dust refusal motif. |
| Early seventh century | Qur’an names Iblīs and recounts the refusal, curse, respite, temptation, and final punishment. |
| Seventh century | Early Ḥijāzī Qur’an manuscripts demonstrate early written transmission of the Qur’anic corpus; the Birmingham leaves contain parts of suras 18–20 but not Q 18:50. |
| Eighth century | Early tafsir expands Iblīs’s prehistory and debates angel versus jinn. |
| Ninth–early tenth centuries | al-Ṭabarī records competing reports; hadith collections consolidate Iblīs as commander of tempters. |
| Early tenth century | Hallāj’s *Ṭawāsīn* gives the classic mystical treatment of Iblīs as proud and tragic exclusive lover. |
| Eleventh–thirteenth centuries | Aḥmad al-Ghazālī, ʿAyn al-Quḍāt, ʿAṭṭār, Rūmī, and others elaborate or contest the lover interpretation. |
| c. 1416 | Herat manuscript painting depicts Iblīs refusing to bow while angels honor Adam. |
| 1550s–1560s | Safavid *Fālnāma* painting visualizes the prostration before Adam and Eve. |
| Sixteenth century | *Majālis al-ʿushshāq* manuscript, British Library Or. 11837, fol. 9v, depicts the refusal scene. |
| Nineteenth century | European Orientalism and missionary writing increasingly equate Yazidi Melek Ṭāʾūs with Satan/Iblīs. |
| 1913 onward | Massignon’s work on Hallāj and the *Ṭawāsīn* makes the mystical Iblīs central to modern scholarship. |
| 1932 | Iqbal’s *Jāvīd-nāma* presents Iblīs as leader of separation. |
| 1936 | Iqbal writes “Iblīs kī Majlis-e Shūrā,” transforming Iblīs into a critic and architect of modern political-economic orders. |
| 1945 onward | Discovery and publication of the Nag Hammadi codices prompts new comparisons with Islamic demonology, but no Iblīs attestation is found. |
| 1966 onward | Modern religious Satanism emerges institutionally in the West; occasional identification with Iblīs is modern syncretism. |
| 1983 | Peter J. Awn’s *Satan’s Tragedy and Redemption: Iblīs in Sufi Psychology* provides a major study of mystical interpretations. |
| 2015 | Public announcement of the Birmingham Qur’an parchment dates intensifies study of early Qur’anic material transmission. |
| Twenty-first century | Online demonolatry and esoteric communities construct individualized Iblīs practices; no evidence demonstrates continuity with an ancient cult. |
