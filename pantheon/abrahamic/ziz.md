# Ziz: Research Dossier

**Class:** Primarily a **gigantic primordial bird/monster in rabbinic Jewish tradition**, not a god, angel, demon, archangel, jinn, or Gnostic power. In the Hebrew Bible, *zîz śāday* is an obscure zoological expression—possibly a collective designation for moving or wild creatures, possibly a mythological mountain bird—not unambiguously a proper name. Only late-antique rabbinic interpretation securely personifies Ziz as a colossal bird.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The expression **זִיז שָׂדַי, *zîz śāday*** occurs in the Hebrew text of **Psalms 50:11 and 80:14**. The psalms cannot be dated precisely. Psalm 80 may preserve an eighth-century BCE context connected with Neo-Assyrian pressure, although its final literary form and incorporation into the Psalter may be later; Psalm 50 is likewise difficult to date. Neither passage explicitly says that *zîz* is a single bird, a cosmic being, or a proper name. The earliest unambiguous identification of Ziz as a named gigantic bird occurs in **Genesis Rabbah 19:4 and Leviticus Rabbah 22:10**, Palestinian midrashim compiled approximately in the fifth century CE, followed by **Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 73b**, in the Bavli’s late-antique redaction.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

Ziz belongs securely to Jewish aggadah rather than to angelology or demonology. Rabbinic sources describe it as an enormous, ritually clean bird whose wings can obscure the sun. Medieval traditions place it alongside **Leviathan**, the paradigmatic sea creature, and **Behemoth**, the paradigmatic land creature, producing a cosmic threefold division:

- Leviathan: sea;
- Behemoth: land;
- Ziz: sky.

This symmetrical triad is a useful medieval and modern synopsis, but it should not be projected unchanged into the Hebrew Bible. Biblical Leviathan and Behemoth possess extensive and unmistakably monstrous descriptions; biblical *zîz śāday* does not.

No primary source examined calls Ziz:

- an angel or fallen angel;
- a demon;
- an aeon or archon;
- a Gnostic divine figure;
- a servant of Satan;
- a being in the hierarchies of Pseudo-Dionysius or Thomas Aquinas;
- a Qurʾanic or hadith creature;
- a Goetic spirit.

Those classifications are therefore **modern categorical inventions or confusions**, not ancient traditions.

### Names and forms

#### זִיז — *zîz*

**[Securely attested biblical expression; meaning disputed]**

The word appears in the construct phrase *zîz śāday*. Its independent meaning is uncertain. Proposed explanations include:

- “moving thing(s),” from a root associated with movement;
- teeming or abundant life;
- wild creature;
- an individual mythological bird;
- a Hebrew reflex of Mesopotamian **Anzû**.

Brown–Driver–Briggs treats the word cautiously as a masculine collective: “moving things,” hence creatures or beasts. That lexical interpretation explains why many translations suppress “Ziz” entirely.

#### זִיז שָׂדַי — *zîz śāday*

**[Securely attested biblical expression]**

The second word is the poetic construct form of שָׂדֶה, *śādeh*, normally “field/open country.” The phrase is conventionally rendered “creatures of the field,” “wild animals of the field,” or—under the rabbinic interpretation—“Ziz of the field.”

Nili Wazana instead argues that *śāday* may carry a mountainous or highland sense and that the complete expression denotes a mythological mountain bird. She compares the fixed mountain association of Mesopotamian Anzû. This is an influential **modern scholarly reconstruction**, not a settled etymology.

The form must not be confused with **שַׁדַּי, Šadday**, the divine epithet conventionally represented as “Almighty.” “Ziz-Shaddai,” meaning something like “Ziz of the Almighty,” is a recurrent popular spelling but is philologically misleading in these psalms.

#### זיזא / related personal names

Forms resembling *Ziza/Zizah* occur as human personal names in Chronicles. These names do not establish worship of Ziz and need not have the same etymology. They are not secure theophoric evidence.

#### Renanim

**[Medieval or modern synthetic identification]**

“Renanim” derives from Hebrew רְנָנִים, *renānîm*, “rejoicings/cries/songs,” in Job 39:13, traditionally associated with the ostrich or another conspicuous bird. Louis Ginzberg’s synthetic retelling calls Ziz “Renanim” and interprets it as the celestial singer. The surviving early passages do not consistently present “Renanim” as Ziz’s proper name.

#### Sekwi

**[Medieval or modern synthetic identification]**

שֶׂכְוִי, *śekhvi*, occurs in Job 38:36 and is variously understood as “mind,” “rooster,” or another creature endowed with perception. Later harmonizing accounts make “Sekwi” an epithet of Ziz, “the seer.” This is not securely stated in the early Ziz passages.

#### Bar Yokhni / Bar Yukhne

**[Related rabbinic giant bird; later identification disputed]**

בַּר יוֹכְנֵי, *bar yokhni*, conventionally “son of the nest,” is the immense bird whose discarded egg floods sixty towns and uproots three hundred cedars in **b. Bekhorot 57b**. Medieval commentators and Ginzberg identify or associate it with Ziz. The Talmudic passage itself does not call the egg-laying bird Ziz. Treating the names as simple synonyms therefore harmonizes separate traditions.

#### “Wild cock,” תרנגול ברא

**[Targumic/medieval comparison]**

Aramaic versions of Job employ “wild cock” in passages later associated with giant or cosmological birds. Ginzberg connects this creature to Ziz, but the equation is interpretive, not an explicit universal identification.

### Comparative identifications

#### Anzû / Imdugud

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction, with possible ancient literary dependence]**

Anzû is the Mesopotamian lion-headed storm bird associated with mountains, winds, cosmic disorder, and—most famously—the theft of the Tablet of Destinies. Wazana argues that biblical *zîz śāday* is a Hebrew reflex of Anzû. Under this reading:

- Psalm 80 casts the invader of Israel’s cosmic vine as a mythological highland bird;
- the image may polemically recode Neo-Assyrian imperial power;
- later rabbinic dimensions—enormous size, relation to the sun, command of the air—preserve or reinvent aspects of the older bird-monster complex.

The phonological and thematic comparison is substantial but remains an argument, not a direct inscriptional equation such as “Ziz is Anzû.” There is no surviving Hebrew narrative in which Ziz steals divine authority or battles a warrior god.

#### Simurgh

**[Medieval comparison or modern comparativism; no demonstrated identity]**

Jewish and modern reference works compare Ziz with the Persian Simurgh because both are immense supernatural birds. The equations are typological. No early Jewish text states that Ziz is Simurgh, and their narrative functions differ.

#### Phoenix, Roc/Rukh, Garuḍa, ʿAnqāʾ

**[Modern comparativism]**

These comparisons depend on the widespread giant-bird motif. They do not establish historical identity, borrowing, cultic continuity, or a shared name.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Hebrew Bible

#### Psalm 50:11

**[Securely attested early material; lexical meaning disputed]**

The Masoretic Hebrew reads:

> יָדַעְתִּי כָּל־עוֹף הָרִים וְזִיז שָׂדַי עִמָּדִי  
> *yādaʿtî kol-ʿôp hārîm, wezîz śāday ʿimmādî*

Literally, approximately:

> “I know every bird of the mountains, and the *zîz śāday* is with me.”

The verse belongs to YHWH’s declaration that every wild creature already belongs to him; he therefore does not require Israel’s sacrifices as food. The grammatical parallel is between “bird(s) of the mountains” and *zîz śāday*. It does not explicitly describe size, wings, a personal name, or the later triad.

The textual witness normally printed is the medieval Masoretic text, especially Codex Leningradensis. No extant Dead Sea Psalms fragment located in the consulted inventories preserves Psalm 50:11 itself. The composition is ancient, but the exact surviving physical witness for the Hebrew wording is medieval.

The Septuagint, translated several centuries before the rabbinic compilations, has:

> καὶ ὡραιότης ἀγροῦ μετ᾽ ἐμοῦ ἐστιν  
> “and the beauty of the field is with me.”

The Greek translator therefore did not transmit Ziz as a proper name or recognizable giant bird. This is strong evidence against treating the fully developed rabbinic monster as the only possible ancient meaning.

#### Psalm 80:14 in English/Hebrew numbering  
(Psalm 79:14 in the Septuagint)

**[Securely attested early material; possible mythological allusion]**

The Hebrew describes Israel as a vine devastated by enemies:

> יְכַרְסְמֶנָּה חֲזִיר מִיָּעַר וְזִיז שָׂדַי יִרְעֶנָּה  
> *yekharsmennā ḥazîr miyyāʿar; wezîz śāday yirʿennā*

Approximately:

> “The boar from the forest ravages it, and the *zîz śāday* feeds upon it.”

The parallelism associates *zîz* with an animal destroying a vine. Many translations consequently use “wild beast,” “creature of the field,” or equivalent wording. Wazana’s reconstruction identifies the boar with Egypt and the highland bird with Assyria, making the verse a political mythologization of imperial enemies. The historical setting is plausible but not demonstrable from the verse alone.

The Septuagint renders the second destroyer:

> μονιὸς ἄγριος  
> “a solitary wild creature.”

Again, there is no Greek proper name corresponding to Ziz.

### Genesis Rabbah 19:4

**[Later canonical rabbinic elaboration; approximately fifth century CE compilation]**

In the serpent’s argument to Eve, creation is arranged by days: firmament, vegetation, lights, birds, and finally humanity. Rabbi Judah bar Simon states:

> זִיז עוֹף טָהוֹר הוּא וּבְשָׁעָה שֶׁהוּא פּוֹרֵחַ מְכַסֶּה גַּלְגַּל חַמָּה  
> “Ziz is a clean bird, and when it flies it covers the orb of the sun.”

This is one of the earliest unambiguous texts in which Ziz is a singular, named, colossal bird. It also makes Ziz part of the fifth day’s creation and places it under humanity’s intended dominion. It does not call Ziz rebellious; Wazana’s suggestion that the context may preserve a remote memory of a rebellious primeval bird is interpretive.

### Leviticus Rabbah 22:10

**[Later canonical rabbinic elaboration; approximately fifth century CE compilation]**

This passage develops a sequence in which God balances forbidden foods with permitted counterparts:

- forbidden fish → Leviathan, a clean fish;
- forbidden birds → Ziz, a clean bird.

It quotes Psalm 50:11, adds that Ziz’s spread wings dim the sun, and offers a wordplay:

> “Why is it called Ziz? Because it contains many kinds of flavor, *mi-zeh u-mi-zeh*, ‘from this and from that.’”

This is folk etymology serving an eschatological food tradition, not historical Hebrew etymology. Ziz’s ritual cleanness matters because it will be available as food for the righteous.

### Babylonian Talmud, Bava Batra 73b

**[Later canonical rabbinic elaboration; late-antique Bavli]**

Rabbah bar bar Ḥana reports seeing a bird standing in the sea:

- the water reached only its ankles;
- its head reached the sky;
- travelers assumed the water was shallow;
- a heavenly voice warned that a carpenter’s axe had fallen there seven years before and had still not reached the bottom—not simply because of depth, but because of the current.

Rav Ashi then identifies the bird:

> הַהוּא זִיז שָׂדַי הוּא  
> “That was Ziz Sadai,”

citing Psalm 50:11.

This is the clearest canonical source for Ziz’s vertical dimensions. It does **not** say in the same episode that Ziz’s wings cover the sun; that feature comes from Palestinian midrash. Modern retellings regularly merge the passages.

### Midrash Tanchuma, Buber recension, Beshalach 24

**[Medievally transmitted rabbinic elaboration; strata and dating disputed]**

God tells Israel that in the world to come it will eat Ziz and Leviathan. The passage makes Ziz explicitly eschatological food and helps create the familiar aerial/marine pairing.

The Tanchuma-Yelammedenu corpus contains layers rather than one securely datable authorial text. Claims that this exact wording is either “biblical” or certainly tannaitic exceed the evidence.

### Bekhorot 57b: the enormous egg

**[Related rabbinic giant-bird tradition; identity with Ziz unestablished]**

A bird identified as Bar Yokhni casts away a spoiled egg:

- its contents flood sixty towns;
- its impact uproots three hundred cedars.

The episode is frequently incorporated into biographies of Ziz. The surviving passage names Bar Yokhni, not Ziz. The identification is therefore best classified as a medieval or modern harmonization.

### Midrash Konen / Baraita de-Maʿaseh Bereshit

**[Medieval cosmological systematization]**

This post-talmudic cosmological work, preserved and printed through medieval and early-modern transmission, places Ziz in an elaborate account of creation. Traditions attributed to it include:

- Ziz’s creation from light, water, and earth or mud;
- Ziz’s feet resting on Leviathan’s fins;
- its head extending toward the divine throne;
- the primordial monsters sporting before God.

The work belongs to the environment of early Jewish mystical or Hekhalot-influenced cosmology, but its exact composition and redactional history are uncertain. These details cannot safely be projected back into biblical or early rabbinic belief.

### Later commentaries and compilations

Medieval exegetes—including Baḥya ben Asher—and lexicographers consolidated Ziz, Bar Yokhni, the eschatological feast, and the three elemental monsters. Early-modern treatments include Johann Heinrich Wolf’s Latin dissertation, *Dissertatio de portentosae magnitudinis ave זיז שדי* (Leipzig, 1683).

Louis Ginzberg’s *Legends of the Jews* (1909–1938) produced the influential modern composite narrative:

- Ziz’s wings darken the sun;
- it protects the earth from southern storms;
- its egg inundates cities;
- it is called Renanim, Sekwi, and “son of the nest”;
- it is destined for the righteous.

Ginzberg carefully documented rabbinic parallels in his notes, but his running narrative merges different sources and names. It should be cited as a modern scholarly synthesis, not as one ancient text.

### Requested corpora in which no Ziz attestation was established

A dedicated check found no named Ziz in:

- **1 Enoch**;
- **Jubilees**;
- **Tobit**;
- the nonbiblical **Dead Sea Scrolls**;
- the **New Testament**;
- standard New Testament apocrypha;
- **Irenaeus**, **Hippolytus**, or **Epiphanius**;
- the **Nag Hammadi codices**;
- **Pseudo-Dionysius**;
- **Thomas Aquinas**;
- the **Qurʾan**;
- canonical hadith collections;
- al-Ṭabarī’s standard prophetic and Qurʾanic materials;
- the classical angel catalogues of Hekhalot literature;
- the spirit catalogues of the *Lemegeton*;
- the *Book of Abramelin*;
- Dante;
- Milton.

The absence matters. Ziz is not part of the Watcher myth, Christian angelic hierarchy, Gnostic pleroma, Islamic angelology, or Goetic demonology merely because modern reference projects place many supernatural beings together.

*Sefer Raziel* and the *Zohar* transmit related Jewish cosmologies and monster symbolism, but no secure evidence was found that either makes Ziz an angel, demon, independently invoked power, or central named figure. Apparent internet claims to that effect often confuse Ziz with Raziel, Metatron, or generic celestial birds.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**[Rabbinic and medieval systematization]**

Ziz’s primary domain is the **air or sky**. In the developed triad it completes the inhabited cosmos:

| Realm | Creature |
|---|---|
| Sea | Leviathan |
| Land | Behemoth |
| Sky | Ziz |

This classification is theological and literary. It is not evidence of a cult assigning Ziz jurisdiction over weather, birds, or aviation.

### Form

The sources establish only a limited iconographic dossier:

- a bird;
- ritually clean;
- of staggering height;
- head reaching the sky;
- wings able to obscure the solar disk.

No early source securely specifies:

- plumage color;
- sex;
- eagle, vulture, rooster, ostrich, or griffin anatomy;
- a lion’s head;
- multiple wings;
- crown, halo, weapon, or divine regalia;
- a fixed number of feathers;
- immortality or cyclical rebirth.

Lion-headed or griffin-like images reflect comparison with Anzû or later artistic conventions, not a standard rabbinic description.

### Powers and functions

#### Blocking or dimming the sun

**[Secure rabbinic attestation]**

Genesis Rabbah 19:4 and Leviticus Rabbah 22:10 say that Ziz’s flight or spread wings cover or dim the orb of the sun.

#### Cosmic size

**[Secure rabbinic attestation]**

Bava Batra 73b places its ankles in the sea and head in the sky. The story dramatizes scale but also warns that visual inference is unreliable: apparently shallow water remains lethally deep and turbulent.

#### Edibility and ritual cleanness

**[Secure rabbinic attestation]**

Both major Palestinian midrashim call Ziz a clean bird. Leviticus Rabbah and Tanchuma connect that cleanness to the future banquet.

#### Protection from southern winds

**[Late synthetic tradition]**

Ginzberg says Ziz’s wings shield the earth from southern storms. The motif depends on connecting Ziz with rabbinic discussions of a winged being restraining winds. The relevant passages do not uniformly name Ziz; commentators sometimes identify the actor as an angel. The function is therefore not as secure as the sun-darkening motif.

#### Protector or king of all birds

**[Modern elaboration]**

Modern bestiaries frequently call Ziz “king of birds” or say that without it all birds would perish. No early primary text located in this investigation supports that fully developed office.

#### Prophecy and celestial song

**[Late harmonization]**

These characteristics derive from the disputed Renanim and Sekwi equations. They are not part of the earliest explicit Ziz dossier.

### Major images

#### Ambrosian Hebrew Bible, Milan, Biblioteca Ambrosiana MS B.32 inf., fol. 136r

**[Medieval Ashkenazic iconography, 1236–1238]**

The best-known medieval depiction occurs in a German Hebrew Bible produced in 1236–1238. The folio presents Leviathan, Behemoth, and Ziz in a composition connected with the eschatological banquet and with surrounding medieval monster imagery. Ziz appears above the terrestrial and marine beasts as a large bird.

This image is historically important because it gives visible form to the tripartite scheme. It is not an eyewitness depiction or a preserved ancient iconographic type. Its forms belong to thirteenth-century manuscript art.

#### North French Hebrew Miscellany, British Library Add MS 11639

**[Medieval Ashkenazic iconography, approximately 1277–1286 or later for some illuminations]**

This lavish Hebrew miscellany contains a giant-bird image often identified as Bar Yokhni and, in modern captions, sometimes as Ziz. The codex postdates Isaac of Corbeil’s *Sefer Mitzvot Qatan* of 1277 and refers to Yeḥiel of Paris as still living, providing approximate chronological limits. Dating of some full-page illuminations remains debated.

The manuscript demonstrates that giant-bird lore entered Jewish visual culture, but it also illustrates the danger of making Ziz and Bar Yokhni identical without qualification.

### Symbols not attested

No securely documented ancient or medieval cultic tradition assigns Ziz:

- a sacred color;
- number;
- weekday;
- planet or zodiacal sign;
- incense;
- gemstone;
- magical seal;
- divine weapon;
- temple emblem.

Modern occult charts that supply such correspondences are inventions unless they cite a particular modern practitioner.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### Origin and family

Ziz has no securely attested parents, consort, offspring, or siblings.

Genesis Rabbah implies that Ziz belongs to the birds created on the fifth day. Midrash Konen supplies elemental materials for its creation. Neither source provides biological genealogy.

The common statement that Leviathan, Behemoth, and Ziz are “siblings” is metaphorical or modern systematization. The sources juxtapose them as representative creatures; they do not provide common divine parents or narrate sibling relations.

No securely attested source gives Ziz a mate. Unlike Leviathan, whose male and female forms are explicitly discussed in rabbinic tradition, Ziz lacks a developed sexual or reproductive myth. If Bar Yokhni is kept distinct, even the famous egg cannot securely be assigned to Ziz.

### Position in a hierarchy

Ziz is subordinate to God:

- God owns or knows all creatures in Psalm 50;
- God created it in rabbinic interpretation;
- humanity is theoretically created to rule the animals, including Ziz;
- God can reserve it as eschatological food.

Ziz is therefore not an independent deity or rival to God.

### Unattested interactions

No source located records Ziz:

- fighting Leviathan or Behemoth;
- mating with another primordial creature;
- rebelling against God;
- fighting Michael, Gabriel, Raphael, Satan, Samael, or Metatron;
- meeting Jesus, Muhammad, Enoch, Solomon, or any Gnostic revealer;
- stealing the Tablet of Destinies;
- being defeated by Ninurta;
- carrying a prophet or hero;
- receiving prayer or sacrifice.

The Ninurta–Anzû combat belongs to Mesopotamian Anzû tradition. It becomes part of Ziz’s dossier only if one accepts a historical derivation, and even then it is an antecedent parallel—not a surviving Ziz narrative.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The biblical *zîz śāday*

**[Secure early expression; interpretation disputed]**

In Psalm 50, *zîz śāday* helps express divine ownership of the animal world. God does not need sacrifices because cattle, mountain birds, and field creatures already belong to him.

In Psalm 80, *zîz śāday* participates in ravaging Israel’s vine. The immediate meaning is political and lamentational: foreign powers despoil Israel. Whether the expression invokes a recognizable mythological bird cannot be proved from the psalm alone.

Wazana’s reconstruction supplies a possible early mythic background: an Israelite poet adapted the image of the Mesopotamian mountain storm bird Anzû to describe Assyrian imperial aggression. If correct, the later colossal bird would represent both continuity and radical domestication: a former emblem of cosmic threat becomes God’s owned, edible creature.

### The sun-darkening bird

**[Later canonical rabbinic elaboration]**

Genesis Rabbah and Leviticus Rabbah describe Ziz as so large that its wings obscure the sun. The image asserts both divine creative magnitude and Ziz’s aerial identity.

The Genesis Rabbah context is especially important. The serpent argues that Adam and Eve should act before God creates still more worlds and installs other rulers over them. Ziz is one example in a chronological catalogue of creation. It does not rebel. Wazana nevertheless suggests that the association of the great bird with the serpent’s rhetoric may preserve a distant echo of a primordial opponent. This remains conjectural.

### Rabbah bar bar Ḥana’s sea voyage

**[Later canonical rabbinic elaboration]**

The travelers see a bird whose ankles stand in water and whose head reaches the heavens. They infer that the water must be shallow. A divine voice prevents them from entering and explains that an axe has not reached the bottom after seven years because of the powerful current.

Rav Ashi identifies the bird with *zîz śāday*. The story performs several functions:

- it concretizes the obscure psalmic expression;
- it portrays creation as exceeding human scale;
- it warns against trusting surface appearances;
- it places rabbinic scriptural interpretation into the marvelous travel tales of Rabbah bar bar Ḥana.

The text supplies no combat, quest, or biography. It is an encounter report followed by exegetical identification.

### The future banquet

**[Late-antique to medieval eschatological elaboration]**

Jewish traditions describe Leviathan and Behemoth as eschatological food for the righteous. Ziz enters this banquet through Leviticus Rabbah’s declaration that it is clean and through Tanchuma’s promise that Israel will eat Ziz and Leviathan in the world to come.

Later systematization distributes the menu cosmically:

- Leviathan supplies sea creature;
- Behemoth supplies land animal;
- Ziz supplies bird.

The feast signifies divine victory, abundance, reversal of present deprivation, and the transformation of dangerous creation into sustenance. No evidence makes it a historical temple festival or enacted sacrificial rite.

### The catastrophic egg

**[Related rabbinic legend; later attached to Ziz]**

In b. Bekhorot 57b, Bar Yokhni discards a spoiled egg, whose fluid floods sixty towns and whose impact uproots three hundred cedars. The episode emphasizes scale and providential restraint: such devastation occurs only because the egg was defective and discarded, not through ordinary reproduction.

Ginzberg and later bestiaries incorporate the event into Ziz’s legend. Since the source names Bar Yokhni, the responsible classification is:

- Bar Yokhni episode: securely rabbinic;
- identity with Ziz: medieval or modern harmonization.

### Ziz holding back storms

**[Late synthetic tradition]**

Modern retellings say Ziz protects the earth from winds blowing from the south. The construction combines the sun-darkening wings with Talmudic passages about winds restrained by a winged entity. Because those passages do not consistently name Ziz and traditional commentators sometimes call the restrainer an angel, this should not be presented as uncontested rabbinic canon.

### Cosmic placement in Midrash Konen

**[Medieval learned systematization]**

Midrash Konen turns Ziz into an architectural component of the cosmos: an immense creature created from primordial elements, vertically extending from Leviathan toward the divine throne. The image links the sky creature with the sea monster while keeping both subordinate to God.

This is the closest Ziz approaches a fully systematic cosmological office. It remains literary cosmography, not evidence for popular zoological belief in a specific habitat.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No cultic institution

There is no evidence for:

- a temple or shrine of Ziz;
- priests or priestesses;
- sacrifice offered to Ziz;
- votive inscriptions;
- pilgrimage;
- oracle;
- liturgical invocation;
- protective amulet bearing Ziz’s name;
- theophoric names demonstrably invoking Ziz;
- a festival dedicated to it.

The biblical phrase occurs in poetry addressed to God, not in worship of the creature. Rabbinic sources subordinate Ziz to the Creator and anticipate eating it. They do not prescribe veneration.

### Food law and eschatology

Ziz’s only substantial ritual classification is that it is **עוֹף טָהוֹר, *ʿôf ṭāhôr***, a clean bird. This status is not an instruction for ordinary hunting or slaughter. It legitimizes Ziz’s place in the imagined future banquet.

### Manuscript use

Medieval images of Ziz occur in Jewish biblical and miscellany manuscripts. Their contexts are pedagogical, exegetical, decorative, and eschatological. An image in a religious manuscript does not by itself constitute cult.

### Human sacrifice, temple prostitution, and cult violence

The dedicated contested-material search found no evidence connecting Ziz with:

- human sacrifice;
- child sacrifice;
- temple prostitution;
- sacred sexual rites;
- cultic warfare;
- persecution in its name.

Assertions of such practices would be inventions.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Possible ancient Near Eastern antecedent: second millennium–first millennium BCE

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**

Mesopotamian literature and art attest Anzû/Imdugud, the enormous storm bird associated with mountains and the divine order. Wazana argues that Israelite *zîz śāday* continues this complex. No bilingual equation or uninterrupted transmission record survives.

### Biblical phase: approximately monarchic through Persian periods

**[Secure textual expression; uncertain chronology]**

The phrase *zîz śāday* enters Psalms 50 and 80. Psalm 80 may reflect eighth-century BCE Assyrian pressure; final collection and redaction are later. At this stage the evidence permits at least two readings:

1. an obscure collective term for wild creatures;
2. an allusion to a mythological mountain bird.

The text does not yet supply the rabbinic biography.

### Greek translation phase: approximately second century BCE

**[Secure translation evidence]**

The Septuagint translators render the phrases generically—“beauty of the field” and “solitary wild creature.” This shows that a named monster was not the only ancient Jewish understanding and may not have been known to these translators.

### Second Temple Jewish literature: third century BCE–first century CE

**[Absence of evidence]**

Enochic, Jubilean, Qumran, Tobit, and related apocalyptic corpora develop elaborate angels, demons, giants, primordial animals, and cosmic geography, but no named Ziz was established. This absence argues against classifying Ziz as a standard Second Temple angelological or demonological figure.

### Palestinian rabbinic phase: approximately fifth century CE

**[Secure personification]**

Genesis Rabbah and Leviticus Rabbah provide the decisive transition:

- Ziz is singular;
- Ziz is a bird;
- it is clean;
- its wings darken the sun;
- it is interpreted through Psalm 50:11;
- it becomes eligible for eschatological consumption.

This is the earliest securely reconstructable full form.

### Babylonian rabbinic phase: approximately fifth–sixth centuries and later redaction

**[Canonical rabbinic elaboration]**

Bava Batra 73b identifies the heaven-high bird of a marvelous voyage as Ziz. Other Bavli giant-bird traditions, especially Bar Yokhni’s egg, later gravitate toward it.

### Post-talmudic and medieval phase: approximately seventh–thirteenth centuries

**[Learned systematization]**

Tanchuma traditions place Ziz in the future banquet. Midrash Konen integrates it into elemental cosmology. Medieval commentators correlate it with Job’s birds and Bar Yokhni.

This is when the balanced triad of sky, land, and sea becomes most explicit.

### Medieval visual phase: thirteenth century

**[Secure material evidence]**

The Ambrosian Bible and related Ashkenazic manuscripts visualize the triad. These illustrations are the first major surviving iconographic witnesses currently identifiable, though they do not prove the absence of earlier images.

### Early-modern antiquarian phase: seventeenth century

**[Christian Hebraist reception]**

Johann Heinrich Wolf’s 1683 Latin dissertation treated *zîz śāday* as a bird of prodigious magnitude. Ziz thereby entered European antiquarian zoology and biblical philology.

### Modern scholarly phase: nineteenth–twenty-first centuries

**[Lexicographical and historical reconstruction]**

Historical lexicographers frequently returned *zîz* to the category of moving or wild creatures. Ginzberg assembled a coherent legend from dispersed rabbinic materials. Wazana renewed the mythological interpretation by arguing for an Anzû background and an Assyrian political setting.

Modern scholarship consequently divides over whether the trajectory is:

- obscure zoological noun → rabbinic monster; or
- ancient mythological bird → obscured in translation → rabbinic transformation.

### Present

Ziz remains known mainly through:

- Jewish folklore collections;
- illustrated bestiaries;
- comparative mythology;
- fantasy games;
- online encyclopedias;
- occasional Jewish educational retellings.

No continuous organized worship was established. Any modern devotional use appears individual and recent rather than an inherited Ziz cult.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Ginzberg’s composite Ziz

**[Modern scholarly-literary synthesis]**

Ginzberg’s *Legends of the Jews* has shaped almost every modern English summary. Its seamless prose makes separately sourced details look like one continuous rabbinic narrative. The following familiar “facts” should therefore be unpacked:

- sun-darkening wings: early rabbinic;
- head in heaven, ankles in sea: Bavli;
- catastrophic egg: Bar Yokhni in another Bavli passage;
- storm protection: inferred from related wind traditions;
- Renanim and Sekwi: harmonized Job/Targum traditions;
- future food: midrashic;
- elemental composition and position on Leviathan: Midrash Konen.

### Angel and demon dictionaries

Gustav Davidson’s *A Dictionary of Angels* catalogs many beings across Jewish, Christian, Islamic, literary, magical, and folkloric sources. Its broad remit encourages readers to treat every entry as “an angel.” Ziz, however, is a monster or marvelous bird in the primary sources. Dictionary placement is not primary-source classification.

Similarly, histories by Bernard Bamberger, Jeffrey Burton Russell, and Henry Ansgar Kelly concern fallen angels, Satan, and the Devil. They provide useful comparative controls precisely because Ziz is not part of the Satan biography or fallen-angel tradition.

### Gnostic classification

**[Modern invention]**

No named Ziz appears in the Nag Hammadi tractates or standard heresiological catalogues. The Gnostic codices contain many animal images, rulers, luminaries, aeons, and archons, but resemblance is insufficient for identification.

Calling Ziz a member of “the Gnostic figures pantheon” is therefore unsupported unless “pantheon” merely denotes a modern database category embracing unrelated traditions.

### Occult and grimoire traditions

**[Absence of evidence; modern invention where claimed]**

Ziz is not among the spirits of the *Ars Goetia*, *Ars Theurgia-Goetia*, *Abramelin*, or standard Renaissance planetary magic. No traditional seal, conjuration, office, rank, or magical correspondence was established.

Modern occultists can and sometimes do adopt mythological creatures into personal systems, but such use is contemporary religious creativity, not evidence that Ziz belonged to the Golden Dawn, Thelema, chaos magic, or historical demonolatry.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist use

No organized Ziz reconstructionist movement or measurable community was located. Because Ziz had no ancient cult to reconstruct, modern worship would necessarily be a new devotional construction based on literary sources.

### Literature

Ziz never achieved the literary prominence of Leviathan or Behemoth:

- Dante uses Christian monsters and demons but not a securely identified Ziz.
- Milton uses Leviathan, Behemoth-like creatures, angels, and fallen spirits but no Ziz.
- Later fantasy often borrows the name independently of rabbinic details.

Claims that Dante or Milton canonized the triad are false.

### Games and commercial fantasy

The name appears in the *Final Fantasy* franchise, including *Final Fantasy XIV*. Game creatures called Ziz are modern fantasy designs and need not resemble the rabbinic sky-covering bird. Such appearances nevertheless make “Ziz” available to audiences who encounter the name first as a monster species rather than a Jewish exegetical figure.

Modern role-playing bestiaries commonly add:

- elemental air powers;
- lightning;
- kingship over birds;
- rivalry with Leviathan;
- combat statistics;
- summoning rituals;
- fixed colors or alignments.

These are **modern inventions**.

### Political and extremist appropriation

The contested-material search found no documented nationalist, racial-extremist, terrorist, or state-political movement for which Ziz is a significant emblem. This contrasts with the political use of Leviathan as a metaphor for sovereignty. Assertions of a historic “Ziz cult” suppressed by Judaism, Christianity, or Islam are unsupported.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is biblical *zîz* already the giant bird?

This is the central dispute.

#### Lexical/collective interpretation

Brown–Driver–Briggs and many Bible translations understand *zîz* as moving, teeming, or wild creatures. Supporting considerations include:

- obscure etymology;
- parallelism with generic animal classes;
- generic Septuagint translations;
- lack of explicit singular-bird description in either psalm.

Under this model, the rabbis converted an unusual common noun into a proper name through midrashic exegesis.

#### Mythological-bird interpretation

Wazana argues that *zîz śāday* is already a great highland bird related to Anzû. Supporting considerations include:

- the possible “mountain/highland” force of *śāday*;
- Anzû’s mountain and storm associations;
- the predatory role in Psalm 80;
- the durability of gigantic-bird imagery in rabbinic tradition;
- Psalm 80’s probable imperial-political setting.

The weakness is the absence of an explicit ancient Hebrew Ziz narrative or bilingual identification.

### Was Ziz inspired by Simurgh?

Older and popular comparisons often invoke Simurgh. Typological resemblance is real, but direct derivation is unproved. Anzû offers an earlier Mesopotamian context and a closer possible link to the Assyrian political world of Psalm 80. Neither comparison should be stated as established identity.

### Is Ziz the same as Bar Yokhni?

The equation explains how the catastrophic egg entered the Ziz story, but the Bavli uses different names. A cautious reconstruction treats them as separate giant-bird traditions later conflated.

### Is Ziz an angel?

No primary evidence supports that class. Confusion arose because:

- some wind-restraint passages mention a winged celestial agent;
- Jewish cosmology contains many immense angels;
- modern angel dictionaries collect adjacent folklore;
- “celestial bird” is easily but wrongly converted into “bird-shaped angel.”

### Does the triad exist in the Bible?

Only retrospectively. Leviathan, Behemoth, and *zîz śāday* all occur in the Hebrew Bible, but no biblical passage assembles them into a sea–land–sky triad. The triad is a rabbinic and medieval synthesis.

### State of the evidence

The surviving evidence is asymmetrical:

- biblical lexical occurrences: two;
- explicit early rabbinic Ziz passages: few;
- later compilations and retellings: numerous but derivative;
- cultic evidence: none;
- ancient iconography securely labeled Ziz: none;
- medieval iconography: rare but important;
- modern popular descriptions: abundant and frequently conflated.

The most important modern study remains Nili Wazana’s “Anzu and Ziz: Great Mythical Birds in Ancient Near Eastern, Biblical, and Rabbinic Traditions,” *Journal of the Ancient Near Eastern Society* 31 (2008), 111–135. Her historical reconstruction should be distinguished from the primary texts she analyzes.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- “Ziz is one of three monsters explicitly created as a triad in Genesis.” — **Unsupported.**
- “Ziz is an archangel of the air.” — **Modern invention.**
- “Ziz is a fallen angel or demon.” — **Modern invention.**
- “The Talmud says Ziz laid the egg that flooded sixty cities.” — **The Talmud names Bar Yokhni, not Ziz.**
- “Ziz is the Jewish phoenix.” — **Loose comparison, not textual identity.**
- “Ziz is identical with Simurgh.” — **Unproved comparativism.**
- “Ziz fights Leviathan at the end of time.” — **No securely located source; likely extrapolated from Leviathan–Behemoth combat traditions.**
- “Ziz has a Solomonic seal and can be summoned.” — **Modern occult invention.**
- “Ziz was worshipped in an ancient Israelite bird cult.” — **No cultic evidence.**
- “The wings blocking the sun come from Bava Batra 73b.” — **Conflation; the wings are explicit in Genesis Rabbah and Leviticus Rabbah.**
- “The Ambrosian miniature is ancient evidence.” — **It is medieval, dated 1236–1238.**
