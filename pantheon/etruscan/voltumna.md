# Voltumna (Veltha): Research Dossier

**Class:** Etruscan divine being, securely represented as a male god in the only unambiguous named Etruscan image; in Roman literary transmission *Voltumna* is grammatically feminine and has consequently sometimes been classified as a goddess. The evidence does not establish a fixed genealogy, consort, or narrative biography.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest surviving, direct Etruscan attestation is the name **Veltune** beside a bearded, spear-bearing male on the bronze “Tarchon mirror” from Tuscania, generally dated about **330–300 BCE** and now in the Museo Archeologico Nazionale, Florence (ET **AT S.11**). Earlier evidence exists for the sanctuary now identified as the *Fanum Voltumnae*—Campo della Fiera near Orvieto was monumentalized by the sixth century BCE—but no securely published sixth-century object from the site names Voltumna. Livy, writing about 27–9 BCE, retrospectively places Etruscan councils at the *fanum Voltumnae* between 434 and 389 BCE; that is the earliest event assigned to the sanctuary by a surviving text, not an inscription contemporary with those events.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EARLY–DIRECT]** Contemporary Etruscan inscription, image, or archaeological context.
- **[ROMAN TESTIMONY]** Greek or Latin testimony about Etruscan belief, normally centuries later than the practice described.
- **[LATER ROMAN ELABORATION]** A specifically Roman cult, poem, myth, or antiquarian construction.
- **[REGIONAL]** Evidence limited to one city or territory.
- **[SYNCRETISTIC]** Historical equation or fusion with another deity.
- **[LATE SYSTEMATIZATION]** Late-antique arrangement of purported Etruscan theology.
- **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** Scholarly inference rather than an explicit ancient statement.
- **[MODERN INVENTION]** A claim or practice unattested before modern reception.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Voltumna is known in two sharply different evidentiary profiles:

1. **Veltune**, the named Etruscan male on the Tuscania mirror, stands as a divine witness or protector at the revelation of haruspicy to Tarchon.
2. **Voltumna**, in Livy’s Latin, is the deity whose sanctuary hosted councils of “all Etruria.”
3. **Vortumnus/Vertumnus**, in Roman evidence, is “the foremost god of Etruria” associated with the Vicus Tuscus, Volsinii, seasonal produce, professional disguises, change, and eventually Pomona.

The three profiles may represent a single cult carried from Etruria to Rome, related cults subsequently equated, or a Roman god whose alleged Etruscan origin was reinforced through antiquarian interpretation. The evidence does not permit the simple formula “Veltha = Voltumna = Vertumnus” to be treated as an established ancient fact.

### Names

| Form | Language and source | Date | Evidentiary status |

| **Voltumna** | Latin genitive in *fanum Voltumnae*, Livy 4.23.5, 4.25.7, 4.61.2, 5.17.6, 6.2.2 | Livy, late first century BCE; events assigned to 434–389 BCE | **[ROMAN TESTIMONY]** |
| **Vortumnus** | Varro, *De lingua Latina* 5.46; Propertius 4.2 | first century BCE | **[LATER ROMAN ELABORATION]** |
| **Vertumnus** | Normal Augustan and imperial Latin form; Propertius and Ovid | first century BCE–first century CE | **[LATER ROMAN ELABORATION]** |
| **\*Velthumna, \*Velthumena, \*Veltumna** | Modern reconstructed Etruscan prototypes for Latin *Voltumna* | modern | **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** |
| **Veltha/Velθa** | Widely repeated modern name, often glossed “earth” or “field” | modern reference and neopagan literature | **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION; uncertain]** |

The direct Etruscan reading is **Veltune**, not “Veltha.” The latter should not be printed as though it occurred beside the god in an ancient inscription.

### Etymologies

1. **Etruscan origin.** The similarity of *Veltune* and Latin *Voltumna/Vortumnus* supports an Etruscan connection, and Propertius explicitly makes Roman Vertumnus say, *Tuscus ego et Tuscis orior*—“I am Etruscan, born of Etruscans”—and that he left Volsinian hearths. This is strong evidence for what Augustan Romans believed, but it does not establish the exact phonological derivation.

2. **Latin *vertere*, “to turn/change.”** Propertius 4.2 offers multiple playful derivations involving the turning river, the returning season, and transformations. These are ancient poetic etymologies, not historical linguistics. They profoundly shaped the Roman god’s identity as mutable and many-formed.

3. **“Earth/field” derivation.** Popular summaries derive *Veltha* from an alleged Etruscan word for earth or field. This is not a securely translated lexical item and cannot support the confident description “earth god.”

4. **Connection with Volsinii/Velzna.** The resemblance among *Veltune*, *Velzna* (the Etruscan city), and the Roman family name *Volturna/Velthurna* has encouraged theories of a territorial or civic deity. Direction of derivation cannot be demonstrated.

### Titles and classifications

- **Deus Etruriae princeps**, “foremost/principal god of Etruria”: Varro’s explanation at *De lingua Latina* 5.46. *Princeps* need not mean a theological “king of all gods”; it can mean pre-eminent, characteristic, or politically foremost. **[ROMAN TESTIMONY]**
- **Federal god of the Etruscan league:** a reasonable functional description derived from Livy’s councils at the deity’s shrine. No surviving Etruscan text calls him “federal god.” **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION from ROMAN TESTIMONY]**
- **Chthonic god:** a modern interpretation based principally on supposed links to Volsinian underworld forms of Tinia and uncertain name etymologies. It is not stated by Livy, Varro, or the mirror. **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]**
- **Vegetation, season, and change god:** securely applicable to Roman Vertumnus in Propertius and Ovid; not securely transferable backward to Etruscan Veltune. **[LATER ROMAN ELABORATION]**
- **Goddess:** the Oxford Classical Dictionary traditionally calls Voltumna an Etruscan goddess, reflecting the Latin first-declension name. The named Etruscan image is male. Grammatical gender and represented sex therefore conflict rather than resolve the matter.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The Tuscania mirror

The bronze mirror was found in a tomb at Tuscania in 1898. Its engraved reverse shows a ritual consultation of a liver:

- *pava tarχies*, a youthful or childlike seer in priestly dress;
- *avl tarχunus*, usually understood as Avle, son or descendant of Tarchon;
- *ucernei*, an otherwise unknown woman;
- *raθlθ*, probably a locative form associated with Rath;
- **Veltune**, a nude, bearded male in heroic pose carrying a spear.

The Museo Archeologico Nazionale in Florence preserves the object. Dates range from about 330 BCE to around 300 BCE. A modern catalogue review identifies Veltune as a bearded protective god and dates the mirror around 330 BCE; another archaeological study uses c. 300 BCE. The scene’s conventional interpretation—Tages teaching Tarchon the Etruscan art of haruspicy—goes back especially to Massimo Pallottino. The mirror itself does not narrate the story in sentences: it labels figures. [Treccani’s art-historical survey](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/arte-etrusca_%28Enciclopedia-dell%27Arte-Antica%29/) and the [BMCR catalogue review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2018/2018.01.21/) place the mirror in a Volsinian artistic orbit.

What it securely shows is limited but important:

- Veltune was represented as male.
- He could be depicted with spear, mantle, beard, and heroic nudity.
- He belonged in a scene concerning the foundation or authorization of Etruscan divination.
- He is not explicitly called Tinia, Voltumna, supreme, chthonic, or ruler of the league.

### Livy

Livy names the *fanum Voltumnae* five times:

- **4.23.5**, events of 434 BCE: Fidenae and Veii obtain the convocation of a council of all Etruria at Voltumna’s sanctuary.
- **4.25.7**, events of 433 BCE: another council is announced; Roman anxiety concerns possible general Etruscan war.
- **4.61.2**, events of 403 BCE: a well-attended Etruscan council meets there, but it remains uncertain whether the whole people will wage public war for Veii.
- **5.17.6**, events of 397 BCE: Capenates and Faliscans ask the Etruscan council to rescue Veii; the council refuses, partly because Veii had not consulted it before beginning the war and partly because Gallic pressure constrains members.
- **6.2.2**, events of 389 BCE: Etruscan representatives meet at the shrine following Roman recovery from the Gallic sack.

Livy does not locate the sanctuary, describe a temple image, name a priesthood, or tell a myth of Voltumna. His recurring formula does establish the shrine in Roman historical memory as the assembly place of the Etruscans. The Latin text of 5.17 is available in [Livy, Book V](https://www.ancienttexts.org/library/latinlibrary/livy/liv.5.html); the sequence of all five citations is summarized in the [BMCR review of the Campo della Fiera publications](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2023/2023.11.40/).

Livy 5.1, although not naming Voltumna in that passage, says that the Veientines’ choice of a king angered Etruria because the man had interrupted ceremonial games after failing to be elected *sacerdos*. It is often joined to the Fanum evidence. The source supports a priestly election and games connected with the federal assembly, but it does not explicitly say “priest of Voltumna.”

### Varro

Varro, *De lingua Latina* 5.46, explaining the Vicus Tuscus, says:

> *Ab eis dictus Vicus Tuscus, et ideo ibi Vortumnum stare, quod is deus Etruriae princeps.*

“From them it was called the Tuscan Street, and for that reason they say Vortumnus stands there, because he is the foremost god of Etruria.”

This is the sole ancient basis for the frequently repeated title “supreme god of the Etruscans.” The Latin text is preserved online in [Varro, *De lingua Latina* V](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/varro.ll5.html).

### Propertius

Propertius 4.2, composed in the late first century BCE, is the fullest ancient self-presentation of Roman Vertumnus. The speaking statue claims Etruscan ancestry, Volsinian origins, residence in the Roman Forum, satisfaction with a modest shrine, oversight of seasonal produce, and the ability to appear as a girl, man, soldier, reaper, hunter, fisherman, charioteer, dancer, drunkard, and bearer of various tools. He also credits the legendary craftsman Mamurius with his bronze form. A complete translation is available in [Propertius, *Elegies* IV](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/PropertiusBkFour.php).

This is decisive evidence for Augustan Vertumnus, but not a literal description of Veltune three centuries earlier.

### Ovid

- *Metamorphoses* 14.623–771, written around 8 CE, supplies the major surviving myth of Vertumnus and Pomona.
- *Fasti* 6 alludes to Vertumnus in the archaic Forum landscape and plays on river-turning etymology.

Ovid’s story is Roman literary elaboration. No Etruscan version survives. The text is accessible in [Ovid, *Metamorphoses* XIV](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidMetamorphoses14.html).

### Other Etruscan corpora: the negative result

The following are indispensable for reconstructing Etruscan religion, but none provides a secure occurrence of Veltune/Voltumna under that name:

- **Liber Linteus Zagrabiensis**, a linen ritual calendar, probably third–second century BCE, surviving as mummy wrappings in Zagreb.
- **Tabula Capuana**, an inscribed terracotta ritual calendar from Campania, first half of the fifth century BCE; the Berlin museum identifies it as the second-longest surviving Etruscan text. [Museum catalogue](https://search.smb.museum/object/obj-681084).
- **Pyrgi tablets**, about 510 BCE, two Etruscan and one Phoenician gold texts dedicating Temple B to Uni/Astarte. [Museo Nazionale Etrusco](https://www.museoetru.it/works/lamine-doro-da-pyrgi).
- **Piacenza liver**, late second century BCE, a bronze sheep’s liver divided into divine sectors for extispicy. Its inscriptions name numerous gods, but not Veltune in a secure reading. The relation of its sixteen-fold rim to Martianus Capella remains a scholarly reconstruction; a modern review traces that debate in [Persée](https://www.persee.fr/doc/antiq_0770-2817_1989_num_58_1_2274_t1_0551_0000_1?pageId=T1_552).
- **Cippus Perusinus**, third–second century BCE boundary/legal text: no Voltumna.
- **Tomb of Orcus, François Tomb, and the mirror corpus:** vital sources for Etruscan divine imagery, but only the Tuscania mirror securely labels Veltune.
- **Cicero, *De divinatione*; Pliny, *Natural History*; Seneca, *Natural Questions*:** preserve Roman accounts of Etruscan haruspicy and lightning doctrine without giving a distinctive Voltumna theology.
- **Martianus Capella, *De nuptiis Philologiae et Mercurii* 1.45–61:** a fifth-century CE sixteen-region celestial system containing gods thought to preserve Etruscan doctrine. It does not securely identify Voltumna. Comparison with the liver is discussed in the [Journal of Roman Studies](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-roman-studies/article/abs/martianus-capella-and-the-cosmic-system-of-the-etruscans/F16598081DFB5340B460FA086B251478).

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Etruscan Veltune

**Secure attributes:**

- adult or mature male;
- beard and long hair;
- heroic nudity;
- mantle;
- spear;
- presence at a foundational divinatory scene.

These suggest authority, protection, and possibly martial capacity. They do not prove a war cult.

**Probable domain:**

- authorization or protection of the Etruscan divinatory tradition;
- the collective identity of the Etruscan cities, if Veltune is Livy’s Voltumna;
- particular association with the Volsinian territory.

**Uncertain or disputed domains:**

- **Underworld:** inferred, not stated.
- **Earth/vegetation:** inferred from contested etymology and Roman Vertumnus.
- **Weather/lightning:** dependent on identifying him with Tinia.
- **Sexual or gender mutability:** Roman poetic Vertumnus can assume male and female forms; the Etruscan Veltune is male in the only labeled image. Claims of an ancient Etruscan bisexual or sex-changing Voltumna go beyond the evidence.

### Roman Vertumnus

Propertius securely associates Vertumnus with:

- first grapes;
- swelling grain;
- cherries, plums, and mulberries;
- grafted pears and apple garlands;
- seasonal or occupational transformations;
- a statue dressed with appropriate implements and costumes.

Ovid turns this adaptability into literal disguise. The god becomes reaper, haymaker, ploughman, orchard worker, soldier, fisherman, and finally an old woman. He is thus a god of changing forms and cultivated produce in Augustan poetry.

### Campo della Fiera images

A terracotta head found near the altar of Temple A has been tentatively identified as Velthum(e)na/Vertumnus because of comparison with the Tuscania mirror. This is not secured by an inscription. A Gorgon-head architectural element, bronze female head, and other votive heads attest a rich divine environment but cannot automatically be assigned to Voltumna. Simonetta Stopponi presents these finds and their proposed identifications in the [Pontificia Accademia Romana di Archeologia publication](https://www.pont-ara.org/index.php?func=display&module=Pubblicazioni&pubid=16).

No ancient evidence assigns Voltumna a sacred animal, plant, color, number, weekday, planet, or constellation. Modern lists supplying these are inventions or analogies from Roman Vertumnus.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No surviving Etruscan or Roman source supplies parents, siblings, spouse, or children for Voltumna/Veltune.

Pomona becomes Vertumnus’s beloved and eventual partner in Ovid, but this belongs to the Roman Vertumnus tradition. It is not an Etruscan genealogy. Older antiquarian claims that Voltumna was Vertumnus’s wife, or equivalent to Pomona, are unsupported by ancient Etruscan evidence.

### Position within the pantheon

Tinia is abundantly represented as the Etruscan Zeus/Jupiter-like sky and thunder god, appears on the Piacenza liver, and occupies the divine triad with Uni and Menrva. That makes Varro’s description of Vortumnus as *deus Etruriae princeps* difficult if read as “king of the pantheon.”

Three principal solutions have been proposed:

1. **Independent federal god:** Tinia remained cosmological ruler; Voltumna was politically foremost because the league assembled at his sanctuary.
2. **Tinia under a local or chthonic title:** Veltune was a Volsinian manifestation or epithet of Tinia, perhaps Tinia Calusna or an underworld Tinia.
3. **Roman conflation:** Voltumna, Veltune, and Vertumnus began as partly distinct figures and were equated by Roman antiquarians, poets, or the circumstances of Volsinii’s conquest.

None is conclusively proved.

### Every documented divine interaction

- **Pava Tarchies/Tages — presence at the revelation of haruspicy.** Veltune stands armed beside the youthful liver-diviner while Pava Tarchies examines the organ and instructs or performs before Avl Tarchunus. Source: inscribed Tuscania mirror, c. 330–300 BCE. **[EARLY–DIRECT; interpretation disputed]**

- **Rath — joint appearance in the haruspicy scene.** A youthful figure is labeled *raθlθ*, apparently “at/in relation to Rath”; whether this is Rath himself, a priest, or personification of a sacred place is disputed. Source: Tuscania mirror. **[EARLY–DIRECT; uncertain identification]**

- **Tinia — proposed identity, not an interaction.** No scene shows Veltune meeting Tinia. Their equation is a modern scholarly hypothesis based on comparable spear-bearing iconography, Veltune’s role in divine revelation, and the conflict between Tinia’s usual supremacy and Varro’s *princeps*. **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]**

- **Pomona — courtship through disguise and eventual union.** Vertumnus repeatedly disguises himself to enter Pomona’s orchard, finally taking the form of an old woman, praising himself, warning Pomona through the story of Iphis and Anaxarete, then revealing his youthful form. Ovid says she is conquered without force and reciprocates. Source: Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.623–771, c. 8 CE. **[LATER ROMAN ELABORATION]**

- **Venus — invoked indirectly.** Disguised as the old woman, Vertumnus warns Pomona of the vengeance of the Idalian goddess against hard-hearted rejection. Venus does not appear in person. Source: Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14. **[LATER ROMAN ELABORATION]**

- **Nemesis/Rhamnusia — invoked indirectly.** The same speech invokes the Rhamnusian goddess’s punitive power. No meeting occurs. Source: Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14. **[LATER ROMAN ELABORATION]**

- **Silvanus, Panes, Satyrs, and Priapus — rival suitors of Pomona.** Ovid lists them as unsuccessful competitors; no direct contest with Vertumnus is narrated. **[LATER ROMAN ELABORATION]**

No secure interaction with Uni, Menrva, Fufluns, Turan, Aplu, Sethlans, Śuri, Calu, or other Etruscan gods is documented.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The revelation of the Etruscan discipline

**[EARLY–DIRECT image; reconstructed narrative]**

The Tuscania mirror is usually read against the later Roman account of Tages: while Tarchon ploughs, a childlike being of immense wisdom springs from a furrow and dictates the rules of divination to assembled Etruscans. On the mirror, *pava tarχies* handles or studies a liver; *avl tarχunus* listens; Veltune, armed with a spear, witnesses or guarantees the proceeding.

The visual source may preserve an indigenous variant in which Veltune, rather than Jupiter’s *Genius*, authorizes Tages. But the identification of *pava tarχies* as Tages and *avl tarχunus* as Tarchon is linguistic and iconographic reconstruction, not a translation of narrative text.

### Voltumna and the federation

**[ROMAN HISTORICAL TRADITION, not a mythic episode]**

Livy’s repeated councils create a political sacred narrative:

1. the cities summon an assembly at Voltumna’s shrine;
2. representatives deliberate common war;
3. member cities request collective aid;
4. the gathering can accept or refuse;
5. ritual, priestly election, games, trade, and politics converge.

Voltumna never speaks or intervenes. The god’s narrative function is institutional: the shrine makes “all Etruria” temporarily present as a religious-political collective.

### Transfer from Volsinii to Rome

**[ROMAN TESTIMONY plus MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]**

Propertius makes Vertumnus say that he left the hearths of Volsinii in wartime. Volsinii fell to Marcus Fulvius Flaccus in 264 BCE, after which Rome transferred immense wealth and reportedly many statues.

Modern scholars commonly reconstruct an **evocatio**: Rome ritually summoned the city’s protecting deity, captured the cult, and installed the god on the Aventine. No surviving ancient narrative explicitly describes an *evocatio* of Voltumna/Vertumnus at Volsinii. The reconstruction is plausible by analogy with Juno Regina at Veii but remains unproved.

An alternative developed by Giorgio Ferri argues that Roman Vortumnus may have had a separate Latin history; his Etruscan reputation could have arisen from the statue’s location in the Vicus Tuscus and from later association with the captured Volsinian god. The alleged identity should therefore be marked **[DISPUTED SYNCRETISM]**.

### Vertumnus and Pomona

**[LATER ROMAN ELABORATION]**

Pomona admits no male suitor to her orchard. Vertumnus approaches repeatedly in occupational disguises. As an old woman he kisses her, admires an elm supporting a vine, and argues that both plants and persons flourish in union. He recommends “Vertumnus,” praising his youth, fidelity, proximity, adaptability, and shared love of fruit. He then narrates Iphis and Anaxarete as a warning against cruel rejection. Finally he casts off the old woman’s appearance and reveals himself as a beautiful youth. Pomona returns his desire.

This story is first securely attested in Ovid. It retrofits the Roman god’s poetic mutability to an erotic tale. It must not be projected into Etruscan religion.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### The Fanum Voltumnae

*Fanum* means a consecrated place or sanctuary, not necessarily a single roofed temple. Livy’s formula indicates an Etruscan institution known by the Latin name “sanctuary of Voltumna.”

Activities supported by the evidence include:

- councils of Etruscan cities;
- deliberation about common war;
- election of a priest or presiding religious official, if Livy 5.1 belongs to this setting;
- games and performances;
- sacrifice and communal ceremony by ordinary sanctuary analogy;
- markets or fairs, strongly suggested by the place-name Campo della Fiera and the installations, but not explicitly assigned to Voltumna by Livy.

The often-repeated phrase “annual assembly of the twelve-city league” requires qualification. Livy shows recurrent assemblies and later evidence establishes annual provincial ceremonies at Volsinii. The exact constitutional membership and uninterrupted annual schedule of an archaic “dodecapolis” are partly reconstructed.

### Campo della Fiera, Orvieto

Excavations began systematically in 2000 under Simonetta Stopponi, with later participation by the Universities of Perugia and Foggia. The site lies southwest of the Orvieto cliff, ancient Velzna/Volsinii.

The excavated complex includes:

- a monumental sacred road;
- Temples A, B, and C;
- altars and offering deposits;
- fountains and water installations;
- treasuries or offering repositories;
- spaces capable of accommodating large gatherings;
- Etruscan, Roman, late-antique, and medieval occupation.

Material establishes cult activity from the **sixth century BCE**. The sanctuary was violently disrupted in association with Rome’s destruction of Volsinii in **264 BCE**, then revived or reorganized in the Roman period. A Roman domus or official residence later overlaid part of the sacred way. The site ultimately included Christian and medieval occupation and a church of San Pietro *in vetere*. A University of Foggia record describes the stratigraphic span from the sixth century BCE to the fourteenth century CE and identifies the complex as the Fanum Voltumnae: [Campo della Fiera, Orvieto](https://fair.unifg.it/handle/11369/469793).

The identification is powerful but inferential. No excavated inscription reading *fanum Voltumnae* has supplied the desired epigraphic “smoking gun.” The match rests on:

- location beside Volsinii;
- scale and long duration;
- supra-local votive connections;
- sacred road and multiple monumental buildings;
- capacity for ceremony, games, and exchange;
- later survival of assemblies at Volsinii.

Accordingly, “Campo della Fiera is the Fanum Voltumnae” is a strong modern archaeological identification, not a fact stated by an in-situ dedicatory inscription.

### Temples and divine attribution

The site’s several temples need not all have served Voltumna. Temple A and its altar are central to the proposed identification, and the tentative Veltune head came from that zone. Temple B, connected with a monumental fountain, has also been proposed for the principal deity. None of these assignments is epigraphically certain.

### Priests and officers

- Livy’s *sacerdos* elected in connection with Etruscan games is often considered the league priest. His exact title and relationship to Voltumna are unknown.
- The Etruscan title *zilath mechl rasnal* has often been expanded as something like magistrate of the Etruscan people and equated with Latin *praetor Etruriae*. The translation and federal scope remain debated.
- Roman imperial inscriptions attest offices such as *praetor Etruriae* and later priests of Tuscia and Umbria, showing that supra-local Etruscan identity and ceremonial office persisted under Rome. They do not prove unchanged survival of the archaic league.

### Roman cult of Vertumnus

Varro locates a statue of Vortumnus in the **Vicus Tuscus** beside the Forum. Propertius says the god needs no ivory temple and is content to overlook the passing Roman crowd. Offerings included first fruits and garlands associated with grafting and harvest.

A second sanctuary or temple stood on the Aventine. Its foundation is conventionally linked to Fulvius Flaccus and the conquest of Volsinii in 264 BCE, and its dedication day was **13 August**, the festival conventionally called the Vertumnalia. The link is plausible but not preserved as a complete ancient foundation narrative.

### Human sacrifice, sexual rites, and violence

No source documents human sacrifice to Voltumna or Vertumnus. No source documents temple prostitution in this cult. General debates about violent Etruscan spectacle, funerary bloodshed, or Punic-Etruscan rites cannot be transferred to Voltumna without evidence.

Cultic violence is historically present in another form: Rome destroyed Volsinii in 264 BCE, looted or transferred its sacred wealth, and reorganized its population. Whether Voltumna was formally “captured” by *evocatio* is unproved.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Sixth–fifth centuries BCE: sanctuary before named god

Campo della Fiera was already a major sanctuary from the sixth century BCE. Monumental architecture and far-ranging offerings indicate more than a small neighborhood cult. No surviving text names its presiding deity in this phase.

### Fifth–early fourth centuries BCE: Livy’s federal past

Livy assigns councils at the Fanum to 434–389 BCE. His episodes portray a loose, negotiated federation rather than a centralized state: cities deliberate independently, refuse aid, and are constrained by local wars. Voltumna functions as divine patron of collective assembly.

### Late fourth–third centuries BCE: direct Veltune and the fall of Volsinii

The Tuscania mirror provides the first direct named Etruscan Veltune. Its divinatory scene connects him with sacred knowledge and the Volsinian sphere.

In 264 BCE Rome captured Volsinii after internal and external conflict. Campo della Fiera shows destruction and subsequent Roman-period renewal. Propertius’s claim that Vertumnus left Volsinian hearths in warfare is most naturally read against this event, though the poem also manipulates Roman foundation traditions.

### Republican and Augustan Rome

Vortumnus/Vertumnus was present in the Vicus Tuscus, associated with Rome’s Etruscan population or memories of Etruscan settlement. Varro defined him as Etruria’s foremost god. Propertius transformed the cult statue into a witty speaking god of plural identities, seasonal abundance, and Roman incorporation.

This period is when Etruscan Veltune, federal Voltumna, and Roman Vertumnus become most visibly entangled.

### Imperial period

The Fanum site continued to receive activity, building, offerings, and administrative investment. The old Etruscan league had ceased to be politically sovereign, but regional Etruscan identity persisted through priesthoods, games, and imperial civic competition.

### Fourth century CE: the Rescript of Spello

The Constantinian Rescript of Hispellum, CIL XI 5265, issued between 333 and 337 CE, records that Umbrians had been obliged by old custom to travel to Volsinii for annual sacred games jointly conducted with Tuscia. Constantine allowed a separate festival and temple at Hispellum, provided the new temple was not polluted by “contagious superstition”—language reflecting imperial management of traditional cult in a Christianizing age.

The inscription proves that annual, supra-local religious games at Volsinii survived into late antiquity. It does not name Voltumna. The relationship to the archaic Fanum is historically persuasive but not perfectly continuous. A discussion and translation context appear in [The Rescript and Games](https://keytoumbria.com/Spello/Rescript__Games.html).

### Christianization and medieval reuse

At Campo della Fiera a Roman residential complex was adapted, burials appeared among the sanctuary ruins, and Christian architectural fragments indicate a church by the early medieval period. Around the twelfth–thirteenth centuries the church of San Pietro *in vetere* occupied the area. Continuity of place does not mean continuity of Voltumna worship: the deity disappears from operative local cult, while the location remains sacred, inhabited, and commercially significant.

A study of the transition from Fanum to church reports occupation from the sixth century BCE through the fourteenth century CE: [From *Fanum Voltumnae* to San Pietro in Vetera](https://arpi.unipi.it/retrieve/e0d6c92a-a38e-fcf8-e053-d805fe0aa794/Menchelli_agerfirm2017.pdf).

### Renaissance to modern rediscovery

Antiquarians knew Livy’s Fanum but proposed numerous locations, including Bolsena, Montefiascone, Viterbo, and sites around Orvieto. Nineteenth-century digs at Campo della Fiera revealed monumental walls and terracottas. Geralberto Buccolini argued in 1935 for the Orvieto plain. Systematic excavation beginning in 2000 transformed the hypothesis into the leading identification.

There is no evidence for continuous worship of Voltumna from late antiquity to modern times.

---

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and heritage

Voltumna has become a symbol of pan-Etruscan unity in museums, excavation publicity, tourism, and Orvietan cultural identity. The modern importance of the deity is therefore disproportionately political and archaeological: the god represents the search for the Etruscan “national sanctuary.”

### Art and literature

Roman Vertumnus, especially with Pomona, enjoyed a substantial postclassical life. Renaissance and Baroque artists favored the moment when the young god appears as an old woman, allowing the composition to combine orchard abundance, erotic courtship, disguise, age, and gendered performance. These works transmit Ovid’s Vertumnus, not an independently preserved Etruscan Voltumna.

Giuseppe Arcimboldo’s famous portrait of Emperor Rudolf II as **Vertumnus** (c. 1590–1591) constructs the emperor’s face from fruits and vegetables. It uses the Roman god’s seasonal abundance and polymorphism as imperial allegory. It did much to establish Vertumnus’s modern visual association with composite vegetation.

### Occultism and neopaganism

No important Renaissance Hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or canonical modern grimoire tradition centers on Voltumna. Searches combining the deity with occultism, magic, human sacrifice, and ceremonial orders produce modern compilations rather than a documented initiatory lineage.

Contemporary Etruscan reconstructionists and eclectic pagans sometimes worship Voltumna or Veltha as:

- supreme god of Etruria;
- earth or underworld god;
- god of seasonal change;
- patron of transformation;
- combined Tinia-Vertumnus;
- gender-fluid or androgynous divinity.

Such practices may be religiously meaningful to their practitioners, but historically they are modern reconstructions. The ancient record does not preserve prayers, festival liturgies, sacred colors, elemental correspondences, tarot associations, planetary rulership, or a “Veltha rite.”

### Nationalist and extremist use

Etruscan heritage has occasionally been incorporated into Italian regionalism and broader ideological claims about autochthony. The available evidence does not show Voltumna as a major emblem of an organized extremist movement. Claims of a documented continuous ethnic cult should be rejected unless supported by independent evidence.

### Commercial culture

The name appears in tourism, heritage events, online myth compendia, and music. An Italian extreme-metal band named **Voltumna** explicitly uses Etruscan history and warfare as its theme. This is modern artistic reception, not cult continuity.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### The chief-god problem

Varro’s *deus Etruriae princeps* clashes with the much larger body of evidence for Tinia as the principal sky god.

- **Pallottino and successors:** treated Veltune as a major Volsinian god and interpreted the Tuscania mirror in connection with Tages and Tarchon.
- **Pfiffig, Jannot, de Grummond, Simon, and others:** variously distinguish cosmological Tinia from federal Voltumna or explore Veltune as a title or aspect.
- **Tinia-Veltune equation:** explains why the mirror’s god resembles a Zeus-like spear-bearing authority and why Varro can call him foremost.
- **Independent-god approach:** emphasizes that the mirror actually says *Veltune*, not *Tinia*, and that federal precedence need not mean cosmic kingship.

The correct conclusion is agnostic: **Veltune may be an autonomous god, a local form or epithet of Tinia, or a figure partly assimilated to Tinia.**

### The Voltumna–Vertumnus problem

The conventional position sees Roman Vertumnus as the imported Etruscan Voltumna. It rests on:

- similarity of names;
- Varro’s explicit Etruscan attribution;
- Propertius’s Tuscan and Volsinian origin claim;
- the fall of Volsinii in 264 BCE;
- a plausible Roman *evocatio*.

Objections include:

- irregularity or uncertainty in the phonological derivation;
- lack of a Roman text explicitly saying “Fulvius evoked Voltumna and renamed him Vertumnus”;
- the possibility that Latin *vertere* and a Roman change-god existed independently;
- differences between armed Veltune and orchard-god Vertumnus.

Thus identification is ancient and powerful but its mechanism is disputed.

### Gender

Older scholarship frequently called Voltumna a goddess because Latin *Voltumna* looks feminine. The Etruscan Veltune image is male. Propertius’s and Ovid’s Vertumnus is male but assumes feminine appearance. These facts generated modern theories of androgyny or sexual mutability, but the secure data are only grammatical variation, a male image, and Roman poetic disguise.

### Campo della Fiera versus alternative sites

Before systematic excavation, candidates included Bolsena—Roman Volsinii Novi—and several places in northern Lazio and Umbria. Campo della Fiera is now dominant because of its location beside archaic Velzna, its scale, sixth-century origins, sacred road, temples, regional offerings, Roman revival, and medieval place-memory.

Caution remains justified because:

- Livy never gives coordinates;
- no in-situ text names the Fanum;
- late imperial “Volsinii” might refer to Bolsena rather than destroyed Orvieto;
- a large sanctuary near Volsinii need not automatically be the federal sanctuary.

Nevertheless, the cumulative archaeological case is substantially stronger than that of rival sites.

### Primary evidence and collections

- Tuscania mirror: Museo Archeologico Nazionale, Florence; ET AT S.11.
- Campo della Fiera finds: Orvieto archaeological collections and excavation repositories.
- Tabula Capuana: Antikensammlung, Berlin.

- Piacenza liver: Musei Civici di Palazzo Farnese, Piacenza.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- “Veltha” is securely attested as the Etruscan word for Earth.
- Voltumna was unambiguously the king of every Etruscan god.
- Voltumna replaced Tinia in a documented theological revolution.
- The god was demonstrably both male and female in Etruscan cult.
- Ovid’s Pomona story was an Etruscan myth.
- The Romans certainly performed an *evocatio* of Voltumna in 264 BCE.
- Campo della Fiera has yielded an inscription reading “Fanum Voltumnae.”
- Human sacrifice or temple prostitution formed part of Voltumna’s cult.
- A continuous secret Voltumna tradition survived Christianity.
- Modern planetary, magical, or elemental correspondences are ancient.

|---|---|
| Late eighth century BCE onward | Etruscan writing begins; no securely identified Voltumna attestation from this earliest phase. |
| Sixth century BCE | Monumental sanctuary activity begins at Campo della Fiera below Orvieto. **[EARLY–DIRECT archaeology; divine attribution inferred]** |

| 433 BCE | Livy records another convocation at the shrine. |

| 264 BCE | Rome captures and destroys old Volsinii; Campo della Fiera undergoes major disruption. Later tradition connects Roman Vertumnus with departure from Volsinii. |
| Third–first centuries BCE | Roman reorganization and renewed occupation at Campo della Fiera. |
| 116–27 BCE | Varro calls Vortumnus *deus Etruriae princeps* and locates his statue in the Vicus Tuscus. |
| c. 27–9 BCE | Livy composes the books preserving the five Fanum Voltumnae episodes. |
| c. 16 BCE | Propertius 4.2 gives the Roman Vertumnus an Etruscan and Volsinian identity and catalogues his changing forms. |
| c. 8 CE | Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* gives Vertumnus the Pomona courtship myth. |
| First–third centuries CE | Imperial inscriptions attest Etruscan regional priesthoods and offices; the Volsinian sanctuary remains active in Roman forms. |
| 333–337 CE | Constantinian Rescript of Spello documents annual sacred games formerly shared by Umbria and Tuscia at Volsinii. |
| Fourth–ninth centuries CE | Pagan institutional worship disappears; Christian use and burials develop among the sanctuary remains. |
| Twelfth–thirteenth centuries | Church of San Pietro *in vetere* occupies Campo della Fiera. |
| 1348 | Conventionally cited terminal horizon for medieval occupation at the site. |
| 1733 | Rescript of Spello discovered, materially confirming late-antique annual provincial ceremonies at Volsinii. |

| 1935 | Geralberto Buccolini argues that Campo della Fiera is the Fanum Voltumnae. |
| 2000 | Systematic modern excavations begin under Simonetta Stopponi. |
| 2000s–2020s | Temples, sacred road, altar, votives, Roman occupation, Christian reuse, and medieval phases consolidate Campo della Fiera as the leading Fanum identification. |
| Present | Voltumna functions chiefly as an object of scholarship, heritage, artistic reception, and limited reconstructionist worship; no demonstrable continuous ancient cult survives. |
