# Uni: Research Dossier

**Class:** goddess (*ais*, “deity,” in Etruscan classification); supreme or major civic goddess in several Etruscan communities, consort of Tinia in Etruscan visual theology, and the deity whom Etruscan, Phoenician, Greek, and Roman observers variously identified with Astarte, Hera, Leucothea/Eileithyia, and Juno.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the divine name appears as *Uneial* on an Attic black-figure cup inscribed in Etruscan, probably from the Pyrgi–Caere area and dated to the late sixth or opening years of the fifth century BCE. The first securely contextualized monumental attestation is *Unial* on the Pyrgi gold tablets, deposited with Temple B around 510–500 BCE. A late-sixth-century dedication reading *UNIAL* from Rome and the recently recovered Uni inscriptions at Kainua/Marzabotto belong to approximately the same horizon. A possible sixth-century occurrence on the Poggio Colla stele is less secure because the damaged text remains under study.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early attested]** Direct Etruscan evidence of the sixth–fifth centuries BCE.
- **[Later Etruscan]** Direct Etruscan evidence of the fourth–first centuries BCE.
- **[Regional variant]** A locally restricted cult, image, title, or interpretation.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A datable equation between deities, not an assertion that they were always identical.
- **[Roman literary tradition]** Information preserved by Roman authors, often centuries after the purported event.
- **[Late learned systematization]** Antiquarian or philosophical ordering substantially later than living Etruscan political independence.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An inference from incomplete archaeological, linguistic, or comparative evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A claim first demonstrable in recent esoteric, neopagan, popular, or internet literature.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Uni

The nominative is conventionally restored as **Uni**, in Etruscan script approximately 𐌖𐌍𐌉. Etruscan inscriptions more often preserve declined forms:

- **Uni**: nominative or uninflected divine label.
- **Unial / Uneial**: genitive, “of Uni”; in dedicatory contexts often rendered idiomatically “to Uni.”
- **Unialθi / Uniialθi**: locative, approximately “in/at Uni’s [sanctuary or sacred place].”
- **Unial Astres** on the Pyrgi tablet: grammatically and theologically disputed. It may mean “of Uni-Astarte,” “of Uni, [that is] Astarte,” or contain two coordinated divine names. It is not a simple word-for-word translation of the Phoenician text.
- **Uni Mae** on the Piacenza liver: Uni shares a celestial compartment with *Mae*. Whether *Mae* is a separate deity, an epithet, or a calendrical or qualitative term remains disputed.
- **Uni Huinthnaia**: a local cult title in a fifth-century-BCE dedication from Fontanile di Legnisina at Vulci. The epithet’s meaning is not securely established.
- **Unialti Ursmnal / unialθi ursmnal** in the *Liber Linteus*: approximately “in the [sacred place] of Uni Ursmna.” *Ursmna* may be a local, gentilicial, or sanctuary epithet. Locating this sanctuary at Chiusi or Perugia is a scholarly hypothesis, not a translation forced by the words themselves.

The variant **Unei-** is now documented beside *Uni-* in the same broad Caeretan-Pyrgian setting around 500 BCE. It should not be made into a separate goddess. The cup bearing *uneial* is especially important because it shows that the vowel variant is ancient rather than a modern editorial conjecture. [Early attested] [Aristonothos study](https://riviste.unimi.it/index.php/aristonothos/article/view/27597)

### Etymology

No Etruscan source explains the name.

1. **Borrowing from Latin/Faliscan *Iūnō*.** This is favored by several modern linguists. The loss or non-representation of initial /j/ would produce a form resembling *Uni*. Bonfante and de Grummond accordingly describe the Etruscans as taking the name from an Italic Juno cult. [Modern scholarly reconstruction]

2. **Borrowing in the opposite direction, Etruscan *Uni* → Latin *Iūnō*.** Older scholarship sometimes proposed this, especially when Roman religion was treated as extensively derivative from Etruria. It is possible as cultural history but linguistically unproved. [Disputed reconstruction]

3. **Common Italic/Etruscan cultic convergence.** Because central Italian communities interacted before the surviving inscriptions, directionality may be unrecoverable. Latin *Iūnō* is itself usually compared with *iuvenis*, “young,” *iūnior*, and *iūnix*, “young cow/heifer,” but that Indo-European analysis does not directly etymologize Etruscan *Uni*. [Modern scholarly reconstruction]

Popular glosses such as “the One,” “universal mother,” “our dove,” or “Uni-verse goddess” have no basis in Etruscan morphology. [Modern invention]

### Identifications

- **Astarte (Phoenician 𐤏𐤔𐤕𐤓𐤕, ʿŠTRT):** explicitly corresponding deity in the Phoenician Pyrgi dedication, about 510–500 BCE. This is the strongest ancient cross-cultural equation. [Early attested syncretism]
- **Hera (Greek Ἥρα):** expressed primarily through Etruscan adoption of Greek mythic scenes—especially the Judgment of Paris, conflict with Heracles, and Athena’s birth—and through later Greek interpretation. It is not documented in an Etruscan–Greek bilingual equivalent to Pyrgi. [Archaic/Classical iconographic syncretism]
- **Juno/Iuno:** Roman identification, especially clear in Livy’s account of *Iuno Regina* at Veii and in the structural comparison between the Piacenza liver’s *Uni* and Martianus Capella’s Juno. Whether every Etruscan cult later called Juno had originally been a cult of Uni cannot be assumed. [Roman interpretatio]
- **Leucothea and Eileithyia:** Greek names associated with the Pyrgi sanctuary and its female deity in literary and archaeological interpretation. Their relationship to Uni-Astarte may reflect successive cults, functional overlap, or foreign visitors’ translation rather than a single permanently composite goddess. [Regional syncretism]
- **Juno Regina:** the Roman title “Queen Juno” applied to Veii’s protectress in Livy. No surviving Etruscan text gives Uni the translated title “Regina.” [Roman literary tradition]
- **Juno Sospita/Seispes:** an armed, goatskin-clad female fighting Hercle in Etruscan art is commonly interpreted as Uni in a Sospita-like form. The identification is iconographic, not secured by a surviving label in every example. [Regional iconographic interpretation]

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The late-sixth-century horizon

The surviving record begins abruptly around 525–500 BCE:

- The *Uneial* cup demonstrates the name in an offering or ownership formula.
- A ceramic base from Rome, dated to the end of the sixth century BCE, bears *UNIAL*, presumably “[property/dedication] of Uni.” [Italian state catalogue](https://catalogo.beniculturali.it/detail/ArchaeologicalProperty/1200951735)
- Temple B at Pyrgi was erected about 510 BCE. The three gold sheets found in 1964 had nail holes and had once been fixed to a structure, conventionally the temple’s doorway. Two are Etruscan and one Phoenician. [National Etruscan Museum](https://www.museoetru.it/works/lamine-doro-da-pyrgi)
- The damaged Poggio Colla stele, placed in a temple foundation around 525–510 BCE, may contain Uni’s name. Reports announcing the reading are preliminary; it cannot replace Pyrgi as the clearest early evidence. [Possible early attestation]

This clustering does not prove that the goddess originated around 500 BCE. It establishes only when her name first becomes archaeologically visible.

### Pyrgi tablets

The longer Etruscan tablet begins:

> *ita tmia icac heramašva vatieχe unial astres…*

A deliberately cautious rendering is: “This sacred place/these sacred objects … Thefarie Velianas dedicated/built for Uni-Astarte …”

The Phoenician tablet says that **Thefarie Velianas, king over Caere**, made a sacred installation for **Astarte** during the goddess’s month of sacrifice/burial and that Astarte had placed or sustained him in power for three years. The Etruscan and Phoenician texts concern the same dedication but are not exact translations. Consequently:

- “Uni equals Astarte at Pyrgi” is secure.
- “Uni and Astarte were everywhere one goddess” is not.
- The dedication tied divine patronage to Thefarie Velianas’s political authority.
- Proposed references to a treaty with Carthage, a coup, or the Battle of Alalia are historical reconstructions; none is stated on the tablets.
- *Unial Astres* may be a compound divine formulation, but translating it as a permanent fused goddess “Uni-Astre” goes beyond what the grammar compels.

The objects are now in the Museo Nazionale Etrusco di Villa Giulia. Their archaeological setting and differing texts are summarized in the [Metropolitan Museum’s Iron Age volume](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Assyria_to_Iberia_Art_and_Culture_in_the_Iron_Age.pdf).

### Tabula Capuana

The terracotta ritual calendar from Etruscan Capua, approximately 470 BCE, contains *unialθi* in the sequence:

> *celutule apirase unialθi turza esχaθce …*

The precise ritual remains uncertain, but the locative form securely places an operation “at/in Uni’s [sacred place].” The text proves a Uni cult in Etruscan Campania and makes her cult geographically wider than northern and coastal Etruria. [Early attested regional cult]

### Liber Linteus Zagrabiensis

The linen ritual calendar was written in the late Etruscan period, probably third or second century BCE, although its ritual language contains older material. It was later cut into mummy wrappings in Ptolemaic or Roman Egypt and is now in Zagreb.

Column XII contains *unialti ursmnal*, commonly understood as a ritual location belonging to Uni with the qualifier *Ursmna(l)*. Belfiore interprets much of the work as a liturgical calendar; van der Meer emphasizes the difficulty of recovering individual rites. The text does **not** furnish a narrative mythology or a genealogy for Uni. A Chiusi-area origin has been proposed; a Perugian connection remains possible but unproved. [Later Etruscan ritual evidence] [BMCR review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2011/2011.01.36/)

### Piacenza liver

The bronze model of a sheep’s liver, normally dated about 150–100 BCE, bears some forty divine names arranged in compartments for teaching hepatoscopy. In one outer celestial field stands:

> *uni / mae*

Uni therefore possessed a defined place in the divinatory sky. The object does not by itself say that Uni was a “fertility goddess,” nor does juxtaposition prove a mythic interaction with Mae. Comparison with Martianus Capella places the compartment in the zone later assigned to Juno and associated divine powers. [Later Etruscan priestly systematization] [Interactive inscription record](https://piacenzaliver.com/it/inscriptions/)

### Mirrors and inscribed art

Bronze mirrors of the fourth and third centuries BCE preserve more narrative evidence than surviving Etruscan prose. Names incised beside figures identify Uni securely in:

- the adoption/nursing of Hercle;
- confrontations between Uni and Hercle;
- the Judgment of Elcsntre/Paris with Menrva and Turan;
- divine assemblies including Tinia, Menrva, Turan, Mean, and Hercle;
- scenes of Menrva’s birth from Tinia’s head.

The principal publication corpus remains the **Corpus Speculorum Etruscorum (CSE)**. Dating individual mirrors depends on archaeological context, manufacture, and style; many were removed from tombs before scientific excavation.

### Cippus Perusinus

The Cippus Perusinus, dated to the third or second century BCE, is a 46-line legal agreement between the Velthina and Afuna families concerning property, access, or a tomb. Its official museum description identifies it as a juridical text, not a hymn or cult document. Claims that line 17 unquestionably contains an invocation of Uni depend on disputed segmentation and should not be used as primary evidence that Uni was Perugia’s patron. [Official museum account](https://www.musei.umbria.beniculturali.it/musei/museo-archeologico-nazionale-dellumbria/il-cippo-di-perugia/)

### Roman and late-antique witnesses

- **Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 5.21–22**, composed around the turn of the Common Era, narrates Camillus’s vow and removal of *Iuno Regina* from Veii in 396 BCE. Livy explicitly warns that miraculous details from such remote history cannot confidently be affirmed or refuted. [Roman literary tradition](https://www.latin.it/autore/livio/ab_urbe_condita/%2105%21liber_v/21.lat)
- **Cicero, *De divinatione* 1.92 and book 2**, 44 BCE, discusses Etruscan expertise in interpreting lightning, prodigies, and entrails. He does not transmit a Uni myth, but he provides institutional context for an object such as the Piacenza liver. [Roman testimony](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cicero/de_Divinatione/home.html)
- **Varro and Festus** preserve Roman antiquarian material on Juno, temples, ritual vocabulary, and Etruscan institutions. They do not supply a secure native biography of Uni.
- **Pliny the Elder** records the fame and plundering of Etruscan sanctuaries but does not provide a coherent account of Uni.
- **Martianus Capella, *De nuptiis* 1.45–61**, fifth century CE, divides heaven into sixteen regions. Juno inhabits the second, with Jupiter, Mars Quirinus, Lars militaris, Fons, Lymphae, and the lightning gods. Comparison with *uni/mae* on the liver is valuable, but Martianus’s Neoplatonic and Roman framework is nearly six centuries later. [Late learned systematization](https://lacabalesta.it/biblioteca/MarzianoCapella/Eng/MC_Eng_01_Bethrotal.html)

### Texts that do not document Uni

Neither the Tomb of Orcus nor the François Tomb supplies a securely labeled Uni figure. They are crucial evidence for Etruscan myth, death, and historical memory, but citing them as direct Uni sources would be misleading. The François Tomb, about 340 BCE, depicts Greek and Etruscan heroic violence and sacrifice; its surviving captions do not name Uni. The Tomb of Orcus preserves figures such as Velia, Aita, Phersipnai, and underworld beings, not an uncontested Uni.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Secure domains

From the combined evidence Uni was:

- a recipient of major civic and ruler-sponsored cult at Pyrgi;
- a deity of sovereignty or political legitimation there;
- a female civic deity with temples at Vulci and Kainua;
- a celestial deity in the divinatory system;
- associated iconographically with marriage, divine queenship, motherhood or adoptive motherhood, and the protection or admission of heroes;
- capable of a militant form.

“Goddess of marriage, women, childbirth, fertility, family, cities, war, and the sky” is a useful modern synthesis, but no single Etruscan source supplies that complete portfolio. Marriage and childbirth depend partly on comparison with Hera/Juno and partly on votive contexts; sovereignty is clearest at Pyrgi; celestial rank is clearest on the liver.

### Appearance

Uni is usually anthropomorphic and youthful rather than matronly:

- diadem, polos, or veil;
- long chiton or peplos and mantle;
- jewelry;
- seated throne in the nursing scene;
- exposed breast while nursing Hercle;
- occasionally armed with spear and shield and wearing an animal skin in the Hercle conflict;
- a duck or water bird in some Judgment of Paris images.

The famous terracotta head or bust from the Portonaccio sanctuary at Veii, often labeled Uni/Juno, remains an attribution rather than an inscribed identification. The same warning applies to generic enthroned female figures.

### Symbols and animals

- **Throne, diadem, veil:** divine status and queenship.
- **Breast/milk:** adoption and transformation of Hercle; not evidence for a universal “milk mystery.”
- **Spear, shield, goatskin:** martial regional form comparable to Juno Sospita.
- **Duck or goose-like bird:** used as Uni’s attribute in some Judgment scenes; it is not securely demonstrated as her universal sacred animal.
- **Celestial region:** the *uni/mae* sector on the liver.
- **Water and wells:** newly prominent at Kainua, but the connection is archaeological and local rather than a securely named pan-Etruscan “water goddess” function.

No ancient evidence securely assigns Uni a canonical color, number, gemstone, weekday, planet, herb, or magical sigil. Modern tables that do so normally borrow from later Juno, Hera, Astarte, or occult correspondence systems. [Modern invention]

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Genealogy

No surviving Etruscan theological text gives Uni’s parents.

- **Consort:** Tinia is repeatedly paired with Uni in art and cultic topography. Calling them husband and wife is supported by the Hera–Zeus pattern and visual scenes, though surviving Etruscan prose does not preserve a wedding narrative.
- **Menrva:** frequently described as their daughter. Images show Menrva emerging from Tinia’s head while Uni witnesses the event; they do not straightforwardly establish Uni as Menrva’s biological mother.
- **Hercle:** the Volterra mirror expressly calls him *Unial clan*, “son of Uni,” but the scene makes this adoptive status: he becomes her son through nursing.
- **Satre as father and Tinia, Nethuns, Aita, and others as siblings:** common in modern charts, but no secure Etruscan source supplies this family tree. It is a reconstruction from later Greco-Roman equations and the proximity of names on the Piacenza liver. [Unsupported modern systematization]

### Documented divine interactions

Because Etruscan narrative literature is lost, “interaction” here includes named figures participating in one visual scene. Co-presence in a liver compartment or temple is listed separately and is not automatically a story.

| Other being | Documented interaction | Source and date | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tinia** | Consort-like pairing; Tinia stands behind Uni during her confrontation with Hercle and sanctions Hercle’s adoption by pointing to the written tablet. | Pontic amphora, c. 540–520 BCE; inscribed Volterra mirror, fourth century BCE | Early/regional iconographic tradition |
| **Hercle** | Uni fights or disputes with Hercle in early art; later she nurses the adult hero, making him “Uni’s son.” | Pontic amphora, late sixth century BCE; several mirrors, chiefly fourth century BCE | Early conflict; later Etruscan reconciliation cycle |
| **Menrva** | Menrva supports Hercle against Uni; Uni witnesses Menrva’s birth from Tinia; both compete in the Judgment of Elcsntre. | Amphora and mirrors, sixth–third centuries BCE | Adapted Greek narratives with Etruscan variants |

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Uni and Hercle: hostility, reconciliation, adoption

**Earliest stage:** A late-sixth-century “Pontic” amphora shows an armed goddess, commonly identified as Uni in the form later called Sospita/Seispes, opposing Hercle. Tinia supports Uni; Menrva supports Hercle. This transforms the Greek Hera–Heracles hostility into an Etruscan divine confrontation. [Early iconographic adaptation]

**Later stage:** Fourth-century mirrors show the adult, bearded Hercle drinking from Uni’s exposed breast. On the best-known Volterra mirror Tinia indicates a tablet inscribed:

> *eca sren tva iχnac hercle unial clan θra sce*

The middle words remain debated, but the sentence’s sense is approximately:

> “This image shows how Hercle became/was made the son of Uni.”

The scene contains Uni enthroned, Hercle nursing, Tinia authorizing or explaining the event, and other divine witnesses. [Later Etruscan narrative, directly captioned]

The adoption confers legitimate divine membership. It may also enact reconciliation: the goddess who formerly opposed Hercle incorporates him into her family. Scholars have compared adult divine suckling with Near Eastern and Egyptian royal or adoptive imagery. This does not prove direct borrowing from a single eastern rite.

The Greek story in which Hera unknowingly nurses the infant Heracles is mostly preserved in later Greek and Roman literature and is rarely represented in early Greek art. The Etruscan image is therefore not merely an illustration of a standard Greek scene. Its adult hero, public divine witnesses, and explicit filial inscription make it characteristically Etruscan.

### The Judgment of Elcsntre

Etruscan mirrors adopt the Greek Judgment of Paris:

- **Uni** corresponds to Hera;
- **Menrva** to Athena;
- **Turan** to Aphrodite;
- **Elcsntre** to Alexandros/Paris;
- **Turms** may lead the goddesses or mediate the encounter.

The core competition is Greek, but details vary: Etruscan clothing, labeled divine names, distinctive birds, attending spirits, and scenes apparently showing the aftermath. There is no surviving Etruscan text stating what Uni offered Elcsntre or narrating the Trojan War consequence. Supplying Hera’s Greek promise of kingship is plausible but not directly Etruscan.

### Birth of Menrva

Several mirrors show Menrva springing armed from Tinia’s head. Uni sits or stands nearby, sometimes with other deities and a birth-assisting figure. These images adapt Athena’s birth from Zeus, but Etruscan artists emphasize Tinia’s bodily vulnerability and the surrounding divine group. Uni is a witness or senior goddess; the images do not show her physically bearing Menrva.

### Uni-Astarte and Thefarie Velianas

The Pyrgi tablets preserve a cultic-historical narrative rather than a myth: Thefarie Velianas dedicates a sacred structure, and Astarte/Uni grants or confirms his reign. The cult makes divine favor the foundation of political authority.

Later reconstructions connect this act to Caere’s alliance with Carthage, Punic mercantile communities, and western Mediterranean rivalry. These circumstances are historically credible, but the tablets themselves do not mention Carthage, a military alliance, or Alalia.

### Evocatio from Veii

Livy’s story runs as follows:

1. Camillus vows a tenth of the spoils to Apollo.
2. He asks *Iuno Regina*, “who now dwells at Veii,” to follow the Roman victors to a new temple worthy of her majesty.
3. After Veii falls, selected youths purify themselves and prepare to remove the image.
4. One jokingly asks whether the goddess wishes to go to Rome.
5. The statue appears to nod or replies that she is willing.
6. The image is carried to Rome, where a temple is dedicated on the Aventine in 392 BCE.

Livy presents the goddess as an agent who voluntarily changes civic allegiance. Yet he wrote about four centuries after the siege and flags the miraculous statue story as neither confirmable nor worth dogmatically refuting. The ritual itself fits the Roman category later called **evocatio**, but whether Camillus’s exact prayer, the responsive statue, or even the transfer occurred precisely as told cannot be established.

Most scholars identify Veii’s Juno with Uni, but no Etruscan inscription from the episode names her. “Uni was abducted from Veii” therefore combines a plausible identification with a Roman literary narrative. The clearest ancient recorded example of *evocatio* remains this one. [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548936920)

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Pyrgi

Pyrgi was Caere’s port and one of the most internationally connected sanctuaries of Etruria.

- Temple B was built about 510 BCE in a Greek-style peripteral or pseudo-peripteral form.
- The gold tablets publicly associated Thefarie Velianas with Uni/Astarte.
- Temple A, erected later in the fifth century, and other sacred buildings complicate any attempt to assign the entire sanctuary permanently to one goddess.
- Hundreds of votive objects and animal remains attest sacrifice and offering.
- The site’s location served maritime exchange, diplomacy, elite display, and cult.

Dionysius I of Syracuse plundered Pyrgi in 384 BCE. Literary tradition calls the sanctuary that of Leucothea and reports immense wealth. This may describe the same sacred complex through a Greek interpretive name, but it does not prove that every cult there was simply Uni under another name.

#### “Sacred prostitution”

Lucilius, preserved by Servius, referred disparagingly to *scorta Pyrgensia*, “the prostitutes of Pyrgi.” A row of rooms near Temple B has been interpreted as housing cult personnel or sacred prostitutes. Plautus may also allude to Pyrgi’s sexual reputation.

The evidence establishes a literary reputation for prostitutes at Pyrgi. It does **not** securely establish institutionalized “temple prostitution” as a formal rite of Uni-Astarte. The rooms could have had other residential or service functions, and the whole comparative category of Mediterranean “sacred prostitution” is contested. Claims that intercourse formed a required fertility rite should be labeled hypothesis, not fact. [Religion in Republican Italy](https://x-legio.com/file/31/Religion%20in%20Roman%20Italy.pdf)

### Kainua/Marzabotto

Excavations begun in the urban sanctuary in 2013 identified a Tuscanic temple beside the larger temple of Tinia. Inscriptions on vessels identify the smaller temple’s owner as Uni. The temple was founded around 500 BCE and remained active through Kainua’s Etruscan life. Uni and Tinia therefore had adjacent but architecturally distinct sanctuaries. [University of Bologna excavation history](https://site.unibo.it/kainua/it/la-ricerca/le-campagne-di-scavo)

Vei was also worshipped in Uni’s precinct. The association appears to concern human and agricultural fertility, but the two goddesses remained distinct.

#### Perinatal burial and human sacrifice

A perinatal male was buried beside a wall delimiting Uni’s sacred area. Interdisciplinary analysis placed him between the sixth and fourth centuries BCE and investigated whether the deposit represented sacrifice. Archaeological association with a temple is not sufficient to prove killing. The careful conclusion is: a ritualized infant deposition occurred in the precinct; whether death was sacrificial remains unresolved. [Peer-reviewed project record](https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/1034270)

#### The 2026 ritual-well discovery

The University of Bologna’s thirty-ninth campaign uncovered a well in Uni’s sacred area containing two bronze female statuettes and a bronze plate deposited during its foundation around the late sixth or early fifth century BCE. Two adult skeletons marked a later closure. Excavators interpret the feature as a ritual well connected with water and city foundation.

The human remains have not yet been shown to be sacrificial victims. “Ritual closure” describes deposition context, not cause of death. [University announcement, 4 August 2026](https://magazine.unibo.it/it/articoli/un-pozzo-rituale-con-reperti-intatti-e-resti-umani-scoperto-nellantica-kainua-a-marzabotto)

### Vulci: Fontanile di Legnisina

A suburban spring sanctuary yielded dedications to Uni and Vei. A bronze statuette’s inscription is commonly read:

> *ecn turce Pive Patrus Unial Huinthnaias*

“This Pive Patrus gave to Uni Huinthnaia.”

The epithet is local and unexplained. The spring setting and association with Vei support reproductive and agricultural concerns without making Uni generically a water goddess. [Regional cult]

### Gravisca

At Tarquinia’s port, Uni participated in a multicultural sanctuary network used by Etruscans and Greek merchants. A famous Greek dedication by Sostratos of Aegina was made to Apollo. The co-location of Apollo and Uni recalls Camillus’s paired invocation of Pythian Apollo and Juno Regina, but a direct institutional connection cannot be proved.

### Veii

Portonaccio is often described as a sanctuary of Menrva, while Veii’s chief Juno/Uni cult is associated with the citadel and the later Roman *evocatio*. Exact temple identification remains debated because Livy’s topography is imprecise and inscriptions do not conclusively label a surviving building “Uni’s temple.”

The Aventine temple of Juno Regina was vowed in 396 and dedicated in 392 BCE according to Roman chronology. It represents continuation under a Roman title, not demonstrable survival of an unchanged Etruscan liturgy.

### Perugia

Uni’s frequent description as “patron goddess of Perugia” is less secure than modern summaries imply. Evidence includes:

- later inscriptions containing *unial* from Perugia and neighboring inland Etruria;
- the possible geographic setting of *unialti ursmnal* in the *Liber Linteus*;
- a civic sanctuary inferred from archaeological and topographical studies;
- later identification of Perugian Juno with Uni.

The Cippus Perusinus is not secure proof. The safest finding is that Uni was worshipped at Perugia and may have held a major civic role; exclusive tutelary status is a reconstruction.

### Priesthood and offerings

No securely interpretable Etruscan text names a priesthood belonging exclusively to Uni. Thefarie Velianas performed or commissioned a royal dedication but was not necessarily her priest. Likely practices, attested at her sanctuaries, include:

- animal sacrifice and communal offering;
- deposition of ceramics, figurines, jewelry, and bronze statuettes;
- ritual calendars specifying operations at her sacred place;
- consultation of divine will within the wider haruspical system;
- water and foundation rites at Kainua;
- ruler-sponsored building and display at Pyrgi.

No securely identified evidence shows human sacrifice conducted specifically **to Uni**. The Kainua infant and adult remains require continuing investigation.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before about 525 BCE

There is no secure named attestation. Female civic cults, imported Greek imagery, and central-Italian Juno-like worship existed, but assigning anonymous earlier female figures to Uni is retrospective. [Absence of evidence]

### About 525–475 BCE: emergence in the written record

The name appears on dedications at Rome/Pyrgi and in Etruscan Campania. Major sanctuaries at Pyrgi and Kainua show that Uni was already civic, transregional, and adaptable:

- at Pyrgi she corresponded to Astarte and legitimized a ruler;
- at Kainua she formed a monumental pair with Tinia;
- at Capua her sanctuary entered a formal ritual calendar.

This is the earliest secure core.

### Fifth century BCE: local differentiation

Cult at Vulci, Gravisca, Pyrgi, Kainua, and probably other cities acquired local epithets and partners. Uni could be martial, reproductive, celestial, civic, or maritime depending on context. It is misleading to choose one of these as the original universal essence.

### Late sixth–fourth centuries BCE: mythic Hellenization and creative adaptation

Imported Greek stories gave Uni the visual identity of Hera. Etruscan artists nevertheless created distinctive developments, especially adult Hercle’s adoption. The goddess’s relationship with Hercle progressed from conflict to incorporation, perhaps reflecting the hero’s assimilation into civic and divine orders.

### 396–392 BCE: Roman appropriation of Veii’s protectress

Rome conquered Veii, and Roman tradition remembered Camillus inviting Juno Regina to Rome. The Aventine temple institutionalized the conquered city’s goddess within Roman religion. This was simultaneously religious translation and political domination.

Calling the event “forced conversion” would be anachronistic. It was, however, a conquest ritual in which the victor claimed the divine protector, sacred image, and prestige of the defeated city.

### 384 BCE: sack of Pyrgi

Dionysius of Syracuse plundered the sanctuary. This is the clearest documented act of cult violence against a major Uni-associated center, although Greek sources call its goddess Leucothea. The sanctuary subsequently continued in altered form.

### Fourth–second centuries BCE: mature visual theology

Mirrors elaborated Uni’s divine family, rivalry, and adoption of Hercle. Ritual documents and local dedications remained more functional than narrative. This discrepancy—rich images, terse texts—is fundamental to reconstructing Etruscan religion.

### Third–first centuries BCE: Romanization and priestly survival

The *Liber Linteus* preserves ritual language involving Uni; the Piacenza liver fixes her in a system of celestial regions. Meanwhile Etruscan cities entered Roman political control, Latin expanded, and Uni’s public identities increasingly converged with Juno.

Cultic continuity cannot be measured simply by language: a community could retain a deity’s sanctuary while changing the divine name, ritual personnel, or civic framework. Conversely, Roman Juno should not automatically be treated as “Uni under a Latin spelling.”

### Imperial Roman period

Uni disappears as a commonly written Etruscan name, but Juno cults persisted throughout Italy. Roman antiquarians remembered Veii’s Juno and Etruscan ritual expertise. Native Etruscan books and priestly traditions survived long enough to be consulted by Roman authorities.

### Late antiquity

Christian imperial policies progressively restricted sacrifice and temple institutions during the fourth and fifth centuries CE. No decree specifically targeting Uni is known. Martianus Capella still preserved a learned cosmic Juno in the second celestial region, showing antiquarian survival rather than evidence of active Uni worship.

### Medieval to early modern period

No demonstrable continuous folk cult of Uni survives. Local Marian, fertility, or well customs cannot be called survivals without a documented transmission chain. Renaissance writers revived interest in Etruscan divination, but Uni remained largely submerged under Juno and Hera.

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries

The discovery and publication of mirrors, the Piacenza liver in 1877, the *Liber Linteus*’s recognition as Etruscan, and especially the Pyrgi tablets in 1964 transformed Uni from a name inferred behind Roman Juno into an epigraphically documented goddess with distinct Etruscan and Phoenician contexts.

### Twenty-first century

Excavations at Kainua have supplied the first securely identified urban temple of Uni in the Po Valley, local ritual deposits, and new inscriptions. Modern Etruscan reconstructionists worship Uni, but the movement is small, decentralized, and discontinuous with ancient priesthoods.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Archaeological and museum reception

Uni’s present image is dominated by three museum objects:

1. the Pyrgi tablets;
2. the Volterra nursing mirror;
3. the Piacenza liver.

Together they produce a modern synthesis—international goddess, mother of Hercle, and queen of an ordered sky—that no ancient single source presented.

### Occult and esoteric literature

Renaissance and ceremonial occultism generally employed **Juno**, **Hera**, or **Astarte**, not Uni. There is no important Renaissance grimoire tradition centered on the Etruscan name.

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century goddess encyclopedias have supplied Uni with correspondences such as the moon, sea, thunderbolts, universal motherhood, particular colors, crystals, or calendrical days. These usually conflate:

- Roman Juno’s attributes;
- Greek Hera’s myths;
- Phoenician Astarte’s planetary associations;
- modern Great Goddess theology;
- wordplay on English “universe.”

Unless independently anchored in Etruscan evidence, these are modern constructions. One circulating page calls her the “Great Mother of the Uni-verse” and offers the unsupported gloss “Our-Dove/My-Dove”; neither is ancient. [Example of modern invention](https://www.holladaypaganism.com/goddesses/cyclopedia/u/UNI.HTM)

### Neopaganism

Self-described **Rasenna** or Etruscan polytheists now honor Uni as a chief goddess. Their practice typically combines archaeological reconstruction, Roman ritual, comparative Hera/Juno practice, and individual devotional experience. The movement’s online presence is demonstrable, but membership, institutional continuity, and scale are not reliably quantified. [Modern revival](https://etruscanpolytheism.wordpress.com/)

Calling this a “survival” is historically inaccurate; it is a reconstructionist revival after a long documentary break.

### Popular culture

Uni appears much less often under her own name than Hera, Juno, or Astarte. Modern books, websites, museum exhibitions, and mythological databases have nevertheless popularized:

- “Etruscan queen of the gods”;
- “mother of Hercules”;
- “patroness of Perugia”;
- “Etruscan fertility goddess.”

Each is a compression. “Adoptive mother of Hercle in a captioned mirror cycle” is firmer than “mother of Hercules”; “major goddess worshipped at Perugia” is firmer than exclusive patronage.

No major modern extremist appropriation centered specifically on Uni has been documented. Broader uses of Etruscan identity in Italian nationalism should not be projected onto her without direct evidence.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Recovery of the evidence

- **Etruscan mirrors:** collected and published from the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries onward; early find contexts were often lost.
- **François Tomb:** discovered in 1857; important for Etruscan historical myth but not direct evidence for Uni.
- **Piacenza liver:** found in 1877; recognized as a model sheep liver in the early twentieth century. Thulin compared its divine arrangement with Martianus Capella.
- **Liber Linteus:** brought from Egypt as mummy wrapping and recognized as Etruscan in the late nineteenth century.
- **Pyrgi tablets:** excavated in 1964 by Massimo Pallottino’s team; the Phoenician text revolutionized interpretation of Etruscan religion and elite politics.
- **Kainua temple:** excavated from 2013 onward; inscriptions fixed its attribution to Uni.
- **Kainua well:** discovered in 2026; analysis is preliminary.

### Major approaches

**Massimo Pallottino** treated Etruscan religion historically, emphasizing formation and interaction rather than an isolated “mysterious” civilization.

**Carl Olof Thulin**, in *Die etruskische Disciplin*, reconstructed the divinatory system from Roman literary witnesses and the Piacenza liver. His correlations remain foundational but can make late Roman systematization appear more directly Etruscan than the evidence permits.

**Larissa Bonfante** clarified Etruscan language, inscriptions, dress, and the cultural translation of Greek and Roman forms.

**Dominique Briquel, Jean-René Jannot, Nancy Thomson de Grummond, Erika Simon, Ingrid Krauskopf, Jean MacIntosh Turfa, and Bouke van der Meer** shifted attention toward local cults, visual narratives, ritual documents, and the autonomy of Etruscan adaptations.

De Grummond’s *Etruscan Myth, Sacred History, and Legend* is especially important for Uni because it treats the Hercle scenes as Etruscan narrative evidence rather than inferior copies of Greek originals.

### Continuing disputes

1. **Was Uni originally Etruscan or borrowed from Italic Juno?**  
   Linguistic evidence favors contact but does not settle ultimate cultic origin.

2. **Does *Unial Astres* name one fused goddess or two coordinated equivalents?**  
   The Pyrgi texts support equivalence in this sanctuary, but exact morphology and theology remain disputed.

3. **Was Pyrgi’s cult politically Punic?**  
   The Phoenician inscription demonstrates Punic participation or address. A formal Carthaginian alliance behind the dedication is plausible, not stated.

4. **Did Pyrgi practice sacred prostitution?**  
   Ancient invective and architectural interpretation support the possibility; no ritual regulation or unambiguous dedication proves it.

5. **Is Hercle literally Uni’s biological son?**  
   The mirror says “son of Uni,” but the nursing scene depicts the process of becoming her son. Adoption/initiation is the better reading.

6. **Was Uni Perugia’s patron?**  
   Widely repeated but less securely documented than her roles at Pyrgi and Kainua.

7. **Does the Piacenza liver reproduce a single pan-Etruscan theology?**  
   It documents a learned divinatory system. Local pantheons and cult arrangements need not have been identical.

8. **Human sacrifice at Uni’s Kainua sanctuary?**  
   Human deposits are real. Sacrificial killing remains to be established through taphonomic and anthropological evidence.

9. **Was Etruscan religion a direct source of the Capitoline Triad?**  
   Tinia–Uni–Menrva and Jupiter–Juno–Minerva are structurally comparable, and Rome received substantial Etruscan cultural influence. A simple one-time transfer of a fully formed triad is harder to demonstrate.

### Discredited or unsupported popular claims

- Uni was a surviving prehistoric universal Mother Goddess.
- Her name means “the One” or “universe.”
- Uni, Astarte, Tanit, Hera, and Juno were timelessly the same goddess.
- The Pyrgi tablets are an exact bilingual translation.
- The Etruscan language is wholly undeciphered.
- The Volterra nursing scene proves a secret milk-initiation rite practiced by worshippers.
- Uni was assigned fixed crystals, colors, tarot cards, weekdays, or planets in antiquity.
- Every female Etruscan temple statue represents Uni.
- The human remains at Kainua already prove regular human sacrifice to Uni.
