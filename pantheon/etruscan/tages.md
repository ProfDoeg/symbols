# Tages: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the surviving tradition, Tages is principally an Etruscan **divine child-prophet and culture hero**, later classified by Martianus Capella as a *semo* or demigod. He is sometimes called a god in modern reference works, but no surviving Etruscan text securely assigns him a conventional divine rank or documents a temple cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest **unambiguous, securely datable textual attestation** is Cicero, *De divinatione* 2.50–51, written in 45–44 BCE. Cicero says that his account came from Etruscan books already in circulation. A bronze mirror from Tuscania, conventionally dated around 325–275 BCE, labels a youthful haruspex **pava tarχies**; since Massimo Pallottino this figure has often been identified with Tages, but both the segmentation and identification remain disputed. The mirror is therefore the earliest **possible**, not incontrovertible, attestation.

## Evidence labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** Etruscan inscription or artifact, or the earliest surviving literary witness.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** A Roman or late-antique form that became standard.
- **[RV] Regional or textual variant.**
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** Antiquarian, priestly, philosophical, or scholastic ordering of older material.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification.**
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported modern claim.**

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Tages is the revealer through whom Roman authors explained the divine origin of Etruscan divination. In the central legend he suddenly emerges from a ploughed furrow in the territory of Tarquinii, looks like a child but possesses an old man’s wisdom, addresses the ploughman or Tarchon, and communicates the rules of *haruspicina*. Those present transcribe his discourse; it becomes the foundation of the *Etrusca disciplina*. The invariant core is emergence from cultivated earth, precocious revelation, and the founding of sacred knowledge. His aged features, genealogy, death or disappearance, individual interlocutor, and the precise contents of his books vary by source. A recent systematic study counts thirty-five relevant ancient literary passages—twenty-eight Latin and seven Greek—from Cicero through Isidore and John Lydus [Terrados González 2024](https://revistas.ucm.es/index.php/CFCL/en/article/download/97018/4564456570342/4564456720669).

### Names and forms

- **Latin:** *Tages*, genitive *Tagetis*, accusative *Tagen*. This is the form used by Cicero, Ovid, Censorinus, Ammianus, Martianus Capella, Festus, Servius, Macrobius, Isidore, and other Latin writers. **[EA/LC]**
- **Greek:** Τάγης (*Tágēs*), principally in the sixth-century antiquarian John Lydus. **[LC]**
- **Etruscan candidate:** **tarχies / tarχieś**, in the inscription **pava tarχies** on the Tuscania mirror. Older transcriptions often write *Tarchies* or *Pavatarchies*. **[EA, identification disputed]**
- **Italian:** *Tagete*; French *Tagès*; German and English normally *Tages*. These are modern linguistic forms, not separate ancient beings.

### Etymology

No etymology is secure.

1. **Pallottino’s interpretation:** The Tuscania inscription is divided as *pava tarχies*, interpreted “the child Tarchies/Tages.” This fits the Roman description of Tages as a *puer* and made the mirror central to twentieth-century reconstructions. De Grummond and many later treatments accept it cautiously. **[MSR]**

2. **Objection:** The meaning of *pava* is not securely established as “child,” and an interpretation derived partly from the Latin literary legend risks circularity. Moreover, *tarχies* may be a personal name related to *Tarchon/Tarquin-* rather than a direct Etruscan form of *Tages*. A 2008 study proposed a different mirror as a possible depiction of Tages and argued that this weakens the automatic equation of *pava tarχies* with him. **[MSR, contested]**

3. **Ancient folk etymology:** The Bern scholia on Lucan explain *Tages* from Greek γῆ, “earth,” or as an Etruscan expression meaning “a voice sent from the earth.” This is late antique or medieval learned etymologizing, not reliable Etruscan linguistics. **[LS/MA]**

4. **“Voice” hypothesis:** Giulio Facchetti has connected the name with an Etruscan stem interpreted as “voice.” This remains a modern linguistic proposal rather than consensus. **[MSR]**

No evidence connects the name etymologically with German *Tag*, “day,” despite superficial resemblance. **[MI rejected]**

### Titles and classifications

There are no securely attested Etruscan cult epithets.

- Cicero calls him *quidam Tages*, “a certain Tages,” and describes him as childlike in appearance but aged in wisdom.
- Ovid calls him the first to teach the Etruscan people to disclose future events.
- Censorinus makes him the singer of the discipline of extispicy.
- Ammianus calls him the *monstrator*, “revealer/instructor,” of the discipline.
- Martianus Capella includes him among *Semones* or *Semidei*, beings with celestial souls born in human form for the world’s benefit. This is the clearest surviving ancient classification, but it is a fifth-century philosophical taxonomy. **[LS]**
- John Lydus calls him the founder or first authority of the Italian discipline.
- Modern labels such as “god of wisdom,” “god of divination,” or “Etruscan Hermes” overstate the evidence. Tages is not securely equated with Hermes, Apollo, or any standard Greek or Roman god. **[MI where presented as ancient fact]**

### Interpretatio and syncretism

No ancient source supplies a formal *interpretatio graeca* or *interpretatio romana* equation for Tages. His grandfather Jupiter in Festus is probably a Roman rendering of Etruscan Tinia, but this is genealogy expressed in Roman terms, not proof that Tages himself was assimilated to a Roman deity. Late writers compare his revelatory authority with other founders of sacred wisdom—Moses, Isaiah, Orpheus, Pythagoras, Plato, and, polemically, Christ—but comparison is not identity.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The Tuscania mirror

**[EA, disputed identification]** A bronze mirror found at Tuscania and now in Florence’s Museo Archeologico Nazionale is usually dated to the late fourth or early third century BCE. The engraved scene shows a youthful, capped diviner examining a liver. Its labels identify:

- **pava tarχies**, the youthful haruspex;
- **avl tarχunus**, an older man leaning on a staff;
- **uθernei/ucernei**, a woman;
- **raθlθ**, a divine or personified figure;
- **veltune**, a divine figure;
- and, above, a solar or dawn chariot commonly associated with Thesan.

Pallottino interpreted the scene as Tarchon receiving the discipline from Tages. De Grummond presents it as the strongest Etruscan visual counterpart to the Roman legend. Yet the scene does not show a child rising from a furrow: *pava tarχies* is a standing young priest, already holding a liver. The inscription never uses Latin *Tages*. Consequently the object attests a figure named *tarχies* in a haruspical teaching scene; identifying that figure with Cicero’s Tages is probable to some scholars, rejected or qualified by others. A catalogue and discussion date it approximately 300 BCE and identify its major figures [Hammond, *Iconography of the Etruscan Haruspex*](https://era.ed.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/1842/4258/Hammond2009.pdf).

An engraved fourth-century BCE carnelian in the Museo Nazionale Etrusco di Villa Giulia shows a prophetic head emerging from the ground before two figures. It has been interpreted as Tarchon and Tages, but it is uninscribed and therefore less secure than the Tuscania mirror. Other isolated or disembodied prophetic heads have likewise been assigned to Tages, Orpheus, Silenus, or Medusa; without a label they cannot be treated as certain portraits.

### Cicero, *De divinatione* 2.50–51

**Latin, 45–44 BCE; [EA for the surviving literary tradition].** Cicero gives the oldest unambiguous account. While land in the territory of Tarquinii was being ploughed, a deeper-than-usual furrow was cut and Tages suddenly emerged and addressed the ploughman. According to the “books of the Etruscans,” he had a child’s appearance but an old man’s wisdom. The astonished ploughman shouted; a crowd gathered; soon “all Etruria” was present. Tages delivered a discourse, which his hearers took down in writing. That discourse contained the discipline of haruspicy; later observations were added by referring them back to its principles.

Cicero’s speaker relates this in a skeptical argument: the fantastic birth is used to challenge the authority of haruspicy, and the passage is immediately followed by Cato’s joke that one haruspex should laugh on seeing another. That polemical setting does not erase the testimony, but it shows that Cicero is reporting an Etruscan justificatory tradition, not endorsing it. The Latin and English text are available in the [LacusCurtius edition](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/e/roman/texts/cicero/de_divinatione/2%2A.html).

### Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 15.552–559

**Latin, completed about 8 CE; [LC/RV].** Ovid transforms Cicero’s emergence into a metamorphosis: a prophetic clod moves of itself, assumes human form, loses its earthen character, and opens a newly formed mouth to announce future destinies. The natives call it Tages, “the first who taught the Etruscan people to disclose future events.” Ovid omits the child-old-man paradox, crowd, written books, and Tarquinian location. The clod’s literal transformation belongs to the poetic architecture of the *Metamorphoses* and should not be back-projected into Cicero. See the [Latin text and translation](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0028%3Abook%3D15).

### Censorinus, *De die natali* 4

**Latin, 238 CE; [LC/RV].** Censorinus says that in the territory of Tarquinii a divinely ploughed-up boy named Tages “sang” the discipline of examining entrails, which the Etruscan *lucumones*—ruling aristocrats—wrote down. Here revelation is sung rather than spoken, and elite rulers, not an undifferentiated crowd, become its scribes.

### Ammianus Marcellinus, *Res gestae* 17.10; 21.1.10

**Latin, late fourth century CE; [LC/LS].** Ammianus treats divination sympathetically. Gods direct birds and transform sacrificial entrails into signs; Tages is the traditional instructor of the discipline, said to have suddenly emerged from the earth in Etruria. Elsewhere Ammianus cites Tagetic authority for fulgural doctrine. Unlike Cicero, he places Tages inside a theological defense of divination.

### Martianus Capella, *De nuptiis Philologiae et Mercurii* 2.157; 6.636–637

**Latin, early fifth century CE; [LS].** Martianus calls Tages a *semo* or demigod, places his emergence from furrows beside the miraculous signs surrounding Hercules, and says he immediately demonstrated national ritual and extispicy. Book 6 celebrates Etruria for fertility, remedies, its association with Aeneas, and “the ploughing-up of Tages.” Martianus is also the chief late witness for the sixteen celestial regions, but his regional list is a learned Latin system and does not name Tages as a resident deity.

### Festus, epitome of Verrius Flaccus, p. 359 Lindsay

**Latin epitome surviving through Paul the Deacon; underlying material early imperial, transmitted in the eighth century; [LC].** Festus says: “A boy named Tages, son of Genius, grandson of Jupiter, is said to have given the discipline of haruspicy to the twelve peoples of Etruria.” This is the principal ancient genealogy and the clearest connection with the Etruscan federation. Its transmission is late and compressed.

### Lucan and the Lucan scholia

**Lucan, *Bellum civile* 1.635–638, c. 60–65 CE; scholia later; [LC/LS].** Lucan names Tages as founder of the art during Arruns’ consultation of entrails. The Bern scholia expand the legend: Tarquinius, called a *flamen Dialis*, ploughs up the boy, grandson of Jupiter and son of Genius; Tages dictates the discipline to boys of the twelve Etruscan princes and disappears. The “Tarquinius” formulation may reflect confusion between Tarchon, Tarquinii, and Roman Tarquins. The Greek derivation from γῆ is a scholastic fabrication.

### Columella, *De re rustica* 10.337–347

**Latin, mid-first century CE; [RV].** In a poem on agricultural rites, Columella says Tages fixed the flayed head of an Arcadian ass at a field boundary to avert agricultural harm, while Tarchon surrounded his dwelling with white vines to ward off Jupiter’s lightning. This is the only surviving text giving Tages that apotropaic agricultural action. It may preserve ritual lore, poetic elaboration, or confusion between Tages and Tarchon; it cannot safely be treated as a central early myth.

### Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 5.19.13

**Latin, late fourth or early fifth century; [LS].** Quoting Carminius’ *On Italy*, Macrobius reports finding in the *Tagetica sacra* that Etruscans used a bronze ploughshare when founding cities. This is important evidence that late antiquity connected Tagetic authority with city-foundation ritual, but it is the only explicit witness to this particular attribution.

### Servius and the *libri Acheruntici*

**Latin commentary, late fourth/early fifth century; [LS].**

- On *Aeneid* 1.2, Servius cites a book called *litterae iuris Etruriae*, written “in the words of Tages,” concerning the fate of descendants of perjurers.
- On *Aeneid* 8.398, he says the haruspical books and Acherontic rites “which Tages is said to have composed” taught that fate could be postponed for ten years by a particular procedure.
- On *Aeneid* 2.781, he cites Tages as discoverer of Etruscan haruspicy.

These notices associate Tages not just with reading entrails but also sacred law, destiny, and afterlife ritual.

### Arnobius, *Adversus nationes* 2.69

**Latin Christian apologetic, c. 300 CE; [LS].** Arnobius refers to “Tagetic or Vegoic books” teaching that those soon to be struck by lightning become unable to hear thunder or loud noises. The paired citation shows that by late antiquity the two prophetic corpora could overlap.

### Cornelius Labeo, preserved by Fulgentius

**Underlying work probably third century CE; Fulgentius fifth/sixth century; [LS].** Fulgentius says Labeo explained the disciplines of Tages and Bacitis/Bacchetes in fifteen volumes. Fragments include a liver-color omen, ritual dragging of *manales lapides* to obtain rain, and removal of *praesegmina* from a victim. The attribution illustrates how “Tages” functioned as an authoritative name attached to technical ritual material.

### John Lydus

**Greek, sixth century CE; [LS/RV].** John Lydus is the last surviving author who claims meaningful access to Tagetic material.

- *De ostentis* 2–3 presents Tarchon as a haruspex of the Lydian migration and says a text—suspected by some to be Tagetic—took the form of a dialogue in which Tarchon asked and Tages answered. The text used archaic Etruscan letters and terminology.
- Lydus’ Tages, raised by Tarchon’s plough, is a child with teeth and gray hair or aged features. Tarchon initially laughs or is astonished; Tages rebukes him and teaches sacred doctrine.
- *De mensibus* 4.79 attributes earthquake omens to Tages.
- Other fragments attribute teachings about subordinate local daimones, sacred fire, and oracles to Tages.

The dialogue form may preserve a genuine feature of an Etrusco-Latin handbook, but Lydus wrote roughly half a millennium after Cicero and mediated his material through Roman antiquarians and Greek translation. The Greek/Latin edition is available in [*De ostentis*](https://www.documentacatholicaomnia.eu/03d/0490-0578%2C_Joannes_Lydus_Laurentius%2C_De_Ostensis_%28CSHB%29%2C_GR.pdf).

### Isidore of Seville, *Etymologiae* 8.9.34

**Latin, early seventh century; [MA preserving late-antique material].** Isidore says Tages sprang from clods while a farmer ploughed, dictated haruspicy, and died that same day; Romans translated the books from Etruscan into Latin. His explicit death is absent from Cicero and Ovid and is a late variant.

### The Etruscan ritual texts: what they do and do not prove

The following are primary evidence for Etruscan religion, ritual language, calendars, and divination, but **none securely names Tages**:

- **Liber Linteus Zagrabiensis:** an Etruscan linen ritual calendar, probably copied in the late Hellenistic period and reused in Ptolemaic Egypt as mummy wrappings. Linguistic archaisms may preserve a redactional history extending into the fourth century BCE or earlier. It survives in Zagreb’s Archaeological Museum. It demonstrates written ritual prescription but is not a surviving *Liber Tageticus* [Zagreb museum](https://amz.hr/hr/virtualni-muzej/vodici-kroz-stalni-postav/vodic-kroz-stalni-postav-egipatske-zbirke-arheoloskog-muzeja-u-zagrebu/etruscanska-soba/).
- **Tabula Capuana:** a ritual calendar on terracotta, conventionally fifth century BCE. It proves that lengthy ritual regulations existed centuries before Cicero, but not that Tages authored them.
- **Magliano lead tablet:** fifth century BCE ritual prescriptions.
- **Piacenza Liver:** bronze sheep-liver model, generally dated around 150–50 BCE, divided into inscribed divine sectors. Found near Gossolengo in 1877 and now in the Musei Civici di Palazzo Farnese. It materially confirms systematic hepatoscopy but does not name Tages [Museo/municipal account](https://visitpiacenza.it/en/art-and-culture/etruscan-liver/).
- **Pyrgi tablets:** two Etruscan and one Phoenician gold dedication, about 510 BCE, from Temple B at Pyrgi. They illuminate Uni/Astarte, kingship, and international cult but do not concern Tages [Museo Nazionale Etrusco](https://www.museoetru.it/works/lamine-doro-da-pyrgi).
- **Cippus Perusinus:** a third- or second-century BCE legal-boundary text. It is relevant to Etruscan sacred and legal writing but not directly to Tages.
- **Lar Pulena sarcophagus:** c. 200–150 BCE, showing the deceased holding a written scroll; its inscription apparently credits him with composing or handling a work concerning the liver. This is direct evidence for specialist haruspical literature.
- **Tomb of Orcus and François Tomb:** major fourth-century BCE painted tombs preserving named gods, demons, heroes, and politically charged mythological scenes. Neither securely depicts Tages.

Calling all these objects “books of Tages” is therefore a modern conflation. They establish the cultural plausibility of a written discipline but not the authorship claimed in Roman legend.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

Tages’ earliest secure domain is the **revelation and legitimation of haruspicy**. Later sources expand this to:

- extispicy and hepatoscopy;
- augury;
- lightning and thunder doctrine;
- prodigies and earthquakes;
- city foundation;
- sacred and civil law;
- agricultural protection;
- fate and methods of postponing death;
- Acherontic or afterlife rites.

This expansion is historical. Cicero’s Tages provides the principles of *haruspicina*; late antiquarians progressively make him author of much of the whole *disciplina*. The familiar claim that Tages revealed every branch of Etruscan religion is consequently **[LS]**, not the explicit content of the earliest account.

### Form

- **Child’s appearance, old man’s wisdom:** Cicero’s foundational paradox. **[EA]**
- **Child with mature physical features, gray hair or teeth:** especially developed by John Lydus. **[LC/RV]**
- **Animated clod becoming human:** unique to Ovid. **[RV]**
- **Youthful priest in long robe and pointed cap:** the *pava tarχies* mirror, if the identification is accepted. **[EA, disputed]**
- **Prophetic head emerging from earth:** uninscribed gems sometimes identified as Tages. **[MSR]**

### Recognitional attributes

No object is certainly Tages merely because it depicts a haruspex. Conditional attributes are:

- rising from earth or a furrow;
- youthful body with aged face;
- liver held for inspection;
- Etruscan priestly cap, often called an *apex* in Roman terminology;
- presence of Tarchon;
- the label *tarχies*.

The Tuscania mirror’s youth raises one foot in a ritual pose and studies a liver while older and divine witnesses attend. A dawn chariot places the event in cosmic or early-morning time. The mirror is not an illustration of Cicero in every detail; it may depict instruction, consultation, or a separate legendary scene.

### Symbols and unattested attributes

The liver, plough, furrow, sacred book, and diviner’s cap are textually or iconographically defensible. No ancient source assigns Tages a sacred animal, plant, color, planet, zodiacal sign, weekday, number, weapon, spouse-symbol, or independent temple emblem. Modern lists supplying such correspondences are **[MI]** unless transparently presented as new devotional inventions.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

The only explicit ancient genealogy is:

- **Father:** *Genius* or *Genius Iovialis*, depending on reconstruction of the compressed tradition.
- **Grandfather:** Jupiter.
- **Source:** Festus 359 Lindsay and the Bern scholia on Lucan.
- **Date/tier:** underlying Roman antiquarian material, transmitted in late epitome and scholia; **[LC]**.

If Jupiter translates Etruscan Tinia, the genealogy may originally have placed Tages within Tinia’s line, but no surviving Etruscan inscription says so. “Genius” could be a divine individual, a generic generative spirit, or a Latin rationalization. No mother, spouse, sibling, or offspring is securely attested.

Tarchon is sometimes treated as Tages’ discoverer, pupil, guardian, or counterpart. Similarity between *Tarχies*, *Tarχun*, Tarquinii, and the Tarquins encouraged ancient and modern conflation. He is not securely Tages’ father. Claims that Tages is a son of Tarchon, or conversely Tarchon’s father, are reconstructions from the Tuscania inscriptions rather than agreed genealogy.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

The narrow record is itself significant. Most relationships attributed to Tages are with humans or priestly figures.

- **Jupiter/Tinia — genealogical descent:** Tages is Jupiter’s grandson in Festus 359 and the Bern scholia; no narrated meeting occurs. **[LC]**
- **Veltune — joint appearance, if *pava tarχies* is Tages:** Veltune stands at the right of the Tuscania mirror’s haruspical scene, witnessing or framing the liver consultation. Veltune’s identification with Tinia, Vertumnus, or a distinct deity remains debated. Late fourth/early third century BCE. **[EA, conditional on identification]**
- **Raθlθ — joint appearance, if *pava tarχies* is Tages:** this named figure stands opposite Veltune in the Tuscania mirror scene. The figure’s precise identity and function are disputed; association with Rath/Apollo has been proposed but is not certain. **[EA, conditional]**
- **Thesan — possible joint appearance:** a dawn or solar chariot appears above the Tuscania mirror scene and is commonly interpreted as Thesan. The figure is not preserved with an unequivocal label in the cited scene, so this is iconographic interpretation. **[MSR, conditional]**
- **Vegoia/Vecu — co-authority, not a narrated meeting:** Arnobius cites “Tagetic or Vegoic books” together; later summaries treat Tages and Vegoia as the two great revealers of the discipline. No ancient narrative records them meeting, conversing, marrying, competing, or collaborating personally. **[LS]**
- **Vulcan — no direct interaction:** Servius, commenting on Vulcan’s words in *Aeneid* 8.398, invokes Acherontic teaching attributed to Tages about postponing fate. This is a commentator’s doctrinal comparison, not a mythic encounter. **[LS; interaction absent]**
- **Hestia/Vesta — no direct interaction:** John Lydus cites Tagetic oracles in a discussion of Numa’s dedication of virgins to Hestia’s eternal fire. Tages does not meet the goddess. **[LS; interaction absent]**
- **Hercules — comparison only:** Martianus Capella places the signs surrounding Hercules’ infancy and Tages’ emergence in the same catalogue of demigod manifestations. They do not interact. **[LS; interaction absent]**
- **Bacitis/Bacchetes — paired doctrinal authority:** Cornelius Labeo reportedly explained the disciplines of Tages and Bacitis in fifteen books. The being or prophet Bacitis is obscure, and no personal encounter is narrated. **[LS]**

There is **no documented interaction** between Tages and Uni, Menrva, Turan, Aplu, Nethuns, Hercle, Laran, Fufluns, or the named underworld demons of Etruscan tomb painting.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The revelation from the furrow

#### Earliest recoverable form

In Cicero, a plough cuts unusually deep in the territory of Tarquinii. Tages emerges and addresses the ploughman. Though shaped like a child, he has an elder’s wisdom. The ploughman’s cry gathers first nearby people and then, hyperbolically, all Etruria. Tages delivers a discourse; listeners transcribe every word. The original discourse contains the science of haruspicy, and later cases enlarge the system by analogy with its founding principles. **[EA]**

The story does several kinds of cultural work:

- It makes the discipline autochthonous: sacred knowledge comes literally from Etruscan soil.
- It authorizes written priestly tradition as revelation rather than invention.
- It connects agriculture, territorial order, and divination.
- It centralizes Tarquinii as the intellectual or sacred origin of haruspicy.
- It represents technical knowledge as both primeval and continually extensible.

These functions are scholarly interpretations based on the narrative’s structure, not statements preserved in an Etruscan commentary.

#### Ovid’s metamorphic version

Ovid makes the earth-clod move without human force and change into a human prophet. His Tages immediately speaks of future fate. There is no transcription scene. The crucial alteration is ontological: Tages is not merely hidden in or born from the earth; earth itself takes human form. **[RV]**

#### Censorinus’ aristocratic version

Censorinus’ divinely ploughed-up boy sings the discipline while Etruscan *lucumones* write it down. This version gives the ruling elite control over reception and preservation. **[LC/RV]**

#### The Tarchon dialogue

John Lydus gives the most elaborate surviving late version. Tarchon, the haruspex and culture founder, encounters the marvelous child in the furrow. Tages’ appearance combines infancy and old age. A dialogue follows: Tarchon questions; Tages answers. Tarchon’s initial incredulity or laughter is rebuked, and revelation proceeds in an archaic language or script later difficult to understand. **[LC/RV]**

Whether this preserves an old Etruscan dialogical handbook is disputed:

- J. R. Wood argued that Lydus drew on an ancient Etrusco-Latin *liber Tageticus* and that the dialogue coheres with the Tuscania mirror.
- More cautious readings stress Lydus’ late date, multiple intermediary sources, Greek philosophical interests, and the danger of assembling disparate witnesses into a single lost original.
- Helena Terrados González notes that Tarchon’s exclusive encounter with Tages is unique to Lydus; Cicero instead emphasizes the assembled Etruscans.

#### Death, disappearance, or continued instruction

- Cicero does not state what happens to Tages after he speaks.
- The Lucan scholia say he disappears.
- Isidore says he dies on the very day he appears.
- Some reconstructions infer that Tarchon took him home or to a palace and continued learning from him, but that narrative depends on combining the mirror with Lydus and is not explicit in Cicero.
- Tages has no securely attested descent to an underworld, apotheosis, resurrection, or later return.

### The founding of the haruspices

This is not a separate extended myth so much as the institutional meaning of the furrow story. Tages’ first audience becomes the ancestral body of Etruscan religious specialists.

Variants include:

- an anonymous crowd representing all Etruria—Cicero;
- Etruscan *lucumones*—Censorinus;
- boys of the twelve Etruscan princes—Bern scholia;
- Tarchon alone as first recipient—John Lydus;
- the twelve peoples of Etruria—Festus.

The “sons of the princes” version retrospectively legitimates hereditary aristocratic training. Roman evidence confirms that haruspical expertise was often associated with prominent Etruscan families, but it does not prove that the scholion’s precise educational scene was an ancient institution.

### Tagetic city foundation

Macrobius reports that *Tagetica sacra* prescribed a bronze ploughshare when cities were founded. The furrow thus becomes both the place of revelation and the instrument by which sacred urban space is created. Because Macrobius is the sole explicit Tagetic witness for this prescription, treating city foundation as an original core of the Tages myth exceeds the evidence. **[LS]**

### Agricultural apotropaia

Columella’s Tages fixes an ass’s flayed head at a field boundary; Tarchon wards off Jupiter’s lightning with white vines. The episode connects Tages to rural protection and boundary ritual, but it is isolated and embedded in didactic poetry. **[RV]**

### Acherontic teaching and postponed fate

Servius attributes *sacra Acheruntia* to Tages and says fate may be postponed ten years. Other summaries connect the *libri Acheruntici* with rites allowing a soul to achieve a transformed or divine postmortem status. Exact ritual details are lost, and the extent to which these writings were originally “Tagetic” rather than later collected under his name cannot be determined. **[LS]**

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No demonstrable personal cult

There is no securely identified temple, altar, priesthood, festival, sacrifice, votive deposit, theophoric personal name, or civic calendar day devoted specifically to Tages. He is prominent as the **source of priestly authority**, not as a securely documented recipient of worship.

“Tages cult,” “Temple of Tages,” and specific ancient invocations or offerings to him should therefore be labeled unestablished. His image on mirrors and possibly gems shows mythic or priestly importance, not necessarily cult.

### The haruspices

The historical institution associated with his legend is well documented:

- Etruscan *netsvis* and Latin *haruspex* were specialists in interpreting sacrificial entrails and prodigies.
- Roman magistrates and the Senate summoned Etruscan haruspices during crises.
- The practice used comparison, classification, and accumulated precedent—consistent with Cicero’s description of later observations being referred to founding principles.
- Under the Roman Empire, haruspices served both public and private clients.
- Claudius urged the Senate in 47 CE to preserve the discipline, and later tradition associates imperial organization with an *ordo* of sixty haruspices.
- The Piacenza Liver’s divine compartments materially attest a mapped hepatoscopic cosmos [Treccani, “Disciplina etrusca”](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/disciplina-etrusca_%28Enciclopedia-dell%27-Arte-Antica%29/).

This institution should not be called a Tages priesthood. No surviving title means “priest of Tages.”

### The books

Roman authors divide the discipline into:

- *libri haruspicini*—entrails;
- *libri fulgurales*—lightning;
- *libri rituales*—ritual rules;
- with subgroups or associated collections called *libri fatales*, *Acheruntici*, *ostentaria*, and *libri Vegoici*.

The terms are Latin classifications. It is uncertain whether Etruscan specialists themselves used an identical fixed tripartite canon. “*Libri Tagetici*” could mean writings attributed to Tages, extracts from a larger discipline, or a flexible authority-label rather than one stable book.

### Vegoia as the other revealer

Vegoia—Latin *Vegoia*, Etruscan candidate *Vecu/Vecuvia*—was credited with revelations about lightning, boundaries, land measurement, and the destiny of Etruria. A prophecy preserved in a Roman land-surveying corpus warns against moving boundaries and places territorial order within a cosmic history.

Tages and Vegoia are complementary in later systematization:

- Tages: especially entrails, foundational revelation, and sometimes Acherontic rites.
- Vegoia: especially lightning and boundaries.
- Arnobius’ paired “Tagetic or Vegoic books” demonstrates overlap.
- No evidence shows that they were consorts, siblings, rivals, or participants in a shared mythic encounter.

### Sacrifice and contested material

Haruspicy normally required killing animals and inspecting the warm organs. That is directly relevant to the institution legitimated by Tages.

Etruscan human sacrifice or ritual killing is separately debated from archaeological and literary evidence, including violent imagery, foundation deposits, and unusual burials. None of the evidence presently ties a human sacrifice to Tages, his revelation, or a personal cult. Claims that the *libri Tagetici* demanded human sacrifice are unsupported. A modern review of the broader evidence treats the question as contested rather than reducible to either universal denial or certainty [*Sacrifice and ritual killing of humans*](https://www.sidestone.com/openaccess/9789464260564.pdf).

No evidence connects Tages with “temple prostitution.” No documented cult violence was conducted in his name.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before c. 300 BCE: background, not attestation

Etruscan ritual specialists, sacrifice, divination, and written religious language are archaeologically attested before any secure image or text naming Tages. The Pyrgi tablets, Tabula Capuana, sanctuaries, tomb paintings, and ritual objects establish the world in which a revelatory myth could operate. They do not establish Tages himself.

### c. 325–275 BCE: possible Etruscan iconographic attestation

The Tuscania mirror presents *pava tarχies* conducting or teaching liver divination before named witnesses. If Pallottino’s identification is right, the essential association of Tages, Tarchon, divine observers, and hepatoscopy was already pictorially formulated in Etruscan before the surviving Roman accounts. If not, the mirror documents a separate legendary haruspex later conflated with Tages.

### Second–first centuries BCE: material discipline and Roman appropriation

The Lar Pulena inscription and Piacenza Liver show literate, technical haruspicy. Roman political institutions increasingly consult Etruscan specialists while Etruscan cities lose political independence. Tages becomes an ancestral guarantor of expertise within a Roman framework.

### 45–44 BCE: Cicero’s skeptical canonization

Cicero preserves the first unambiguous full legend precisely while criticizing it. His account fixes Tarquinii, the deep furrow, child/elder paradox, mass audience, transcription, and expandable discipline. Almost every later version selects from or modifies these elements.

### Augustan age: poetic transformation

Ovid turns the appearance into a metamorphosis. Roman literature incorporates Tages into a sequence of Italian wonders. His Etruscan origin remains explicit, but the story now serves a Roman universal poem.

### First–third centuries CE: technical and antiquarian expansion

Columella, Festus/Verrius, Lucan, Censorinus, and Cornelius Labeo attach genealogy, federation, agriculture, elite transmission, and an expanding technical corpus. Tages’ name functions increasingly like Orpheus’—an authoritative pseudonymous source for sacred books.

### Imperial institutional survival

Haruspices continue in state service. Claudius’ preservation initiative shows that Etruscan divination could be treated as an endangered but valuable part of Roman religion. This is institutional continuity of the discipline, not proof of continuing personal worship of Tages.

### Third–fourth centuries: pagan-Christian contest

Christian apologetic writers cite Etruscan books as pagan authorities to criticize them, while pagan intellectuals defend divination as rational divine communication. Etruscan revelation could be rhetorically compared with Jewish and Christian revelation. Dominique Briquel describes the late-antique positioning of Tages as a pagan counterfigure to Christ; this is ideological reception, not an ancient identification of the two.

The imperial haruspices were implicated in conflict with Christians. Lactantius says that during Diocletian’s court sacrifice, haruspices blamed Christians’ signs for preventing successful divination, an episode associated with the climate preceding the Great Persecution. Tages is not named in that incident. A detailed study of Etruscan religion’s late survival discusses Cornelius Labeo and the pagan-Christian confrontation [Briquel overview](https://bcs.fltr.ucl.ac.be/fe/14/haruspices.htm).

### Fourth–sixth centuries: learned systematization

Ammianus, Servius, Macrobius, Martianus Capella, Fulgentius, and John Lydus preserve increasingly encyclopedic Tagetic material. The figure is classified as a demigod, made authority for law and afterlife rites, and embedded in cosmic systems. John Lydus’ Constantinopolitan scholarship is the last major ancient attempt to transmit the doctrine.

### Suppression and disappearance

Christian emperors restricted sacrifice and divination unevenly during the fourth and fifth centuries. Haruspicy became vulnerable because it required sacrifice and could be prosecuted when associated with illicit prediction, magic, or political conspiracy. There is no recorded edict specifically banning Tages, no documented destruction of a Temple of Tages, and no account of forced conversion of his worshippers as a distinct group.

The Etruscan language had already ceased to be a normal literary language. Technical books disappeared through institutional collapse, changing religious practice, linguistic obsolescence, limited copying, and possibly deliberate destruction. The degree attributable to each cause cannot be quantified.

### Medieval to early modern survival

Tages survives primarily in manuscripts of Latin school authors and mythographic compilations. Isidore’s encyclopedia carried the story into medieval learning. Petrarch and later humanists knew the figure from Roman texts. Early-modern demonological readings could reinterpret the pagan revelation as diabolical deception; Johannes Praetorius is cited in Charles Godfrey Leland’s nineteenth-century folklore collection as treating Tages’ wonders as the devil’s work. That is Christian demonization, not evidence that Tages entered a stable grimoire tradition [Leland text](https://sacred-texts.com/book/etruscan-roman-remains-in-popular-tradition/shell/part-one-chapter-v-lares-lasa-and-lassi).

### Modern period

Modern Etruscology recovered Tages through archaeology, philology, and the Tuscania mirror. No continuous worship from antiquity to the present is demonstrable. Contemporary pagan interest exists, but its scale is unmeasured and its rites are reconstructions or innovations.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and cultural reception

George Dennis’ *Cities and Cemeteries of Etruria* popularized the furrow legend in nineteenth-century English-language Etruscan studies, although its interpretive notes reflect scholarship of its time [Dennis](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Places/Europe/Italy/_Periods/Roman/Archaic/Etruscan/_Texts/DENETR%2A/19.html).

Tages appears in modern histories of religion as an example of revelation, autochthony, the *puer-senex* motif, and priestly legitimation. Modernist fascination with “mysterious Etruscans” made Etruscan religion culturally attractive, but Tages never achieved the popular profile of Tinia, Turan, or Charun.

### Occult and esoteric claims

The dedicated search found no reliable evidence that Tages held a defined role in Renaissance Hermeticism, the Theosophical Society, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Thelema, or a canonical modern grimoire. Claims that he has an ancient sigil, planetary correspondence, standard invocation, demon rank, or Golden Dawn grade are **[MI]** unless a modern author explicitly acknowledges creating them.

Tages’ ancient role as source of divination makes him attractive to modern occult writers, but thematic suitability is not transmission.

### Neopaganism

Individual modern practitioners and small online communities discuss Tages as an Etruscan god, prophet, spirit of divination, or analogue to a scriptural prophet. A 2019 discussion explicitly asks Etruscan neopagans whether they understand him analogously to Jesus or Muhammad, demonstrating contemporary reception but not an organized consensus [discussion](https://www.reddit.com/r/paganism/comments/cz2y6f).

Modern offerings, prayers, festival dates, correspondences, and initiation rites are necessarily reconstructed or newly created because no ancient Tages liturgy survives. “Reconstructionist” should therefore describe method and aspiration, not uninterrupted lineage.

### Psychological and archetypal readings

Modern writers sometimes interpret Tages as:

- the “wise child” or *puer-senex*;
- unconscious wisdom emerging from the earth;
- agricultural civilization discovering symbolic order;
- a Jungian child archetype;
- indigenous Etruscan knowledge resisting Roman absorption.

These are datable modern interpretations. They are not meanings stated by Cicero or an Etruscan inscription.

### Political and extremist appropriation

No substantial evidence was found of a sustained nationalist or extremist cult specifically centered on Tages. General Etruscan imagery has been used in Italian regionalism, nationalism, and identity politics, but attributing those broader appropriations to Tages without direct evidence would be misleading.

### Commercial use

“Tages” is used as a modern organizational and commercial name in Tuscany, often invoking local Etruscan heritage. Such use belongs to regional branding, not demonstrated religious continuity. No film, game, comic, or mass-market literary version appears to have decisively reshaped the modern image of Tages.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Foundational modern works

- Carl Olof Thulin, *Die etruskische Disciplin* (1905–1909), reconstructed the discipline from Greek and Latin fragments, organizing the material around lightning, haruspicy, and ritual books. It remains foundational but reflects an early-twentieth-century confidence in reconstructing lost systems.
- Massimo Pallottino connected *pava tarχies* on the Tuscania mirror with Tages and treated the scene as a native Etruscan witness to the Tarchon legend.
- Giuliano Bonfante and Larissa Bonfante advanced the linguistic and epigraphic framework for evaluating Etruscan names and texts.
- Jean-René Jannot emphasized institutions, ritual practice, and the difficulty of separating Etruscan evidence from Roman representation.
- Nancy Thomson de Grummond’s *Etruscan Myth, Sacred History, and Legend* integrates literary, epigraphic, and iconographic evidence while clearly displaying the mirror and gem traditions.
- Jean MacIntosh Turfa’s *Divining the Etruscan World* places Tages within the surviving brontoscopic calendar and the practical religious culture of divination.
- Helena Terrados González’s 2024 article provides the most systematic recent corpus and intertextual analysis of the ancient literary witnesses.

### Debate 1: Is *pava tarχies* Tages?

**For:**

- *tarχies* is phonetically plausible behind Latin *Tages*.
- The figure is a youthful haruspex.
- He appears with *avl tarχunus*, readily associated with Tarchon.
- The divinatory scene closely matches the legend’s subject.

**Against or qualifying:**

- *pava* does not securely mean “child.”
- The youth does not emerge from earth.
- The name may belong to a distinct legendary or historical priest.
- Roman literary evidence may have influenced interpretation of the image.
- Alternative mirror identifications have been proposed.

Best judgment: plausible and important, but not secure enough to make the mirror an unqualified portrait of Tages.

### Debate 2: How old is the myth?

Cicero says the Etruscans preserved the story in their books, but the books he consulted or knew by report do not survive. The mirror may push the complex back to around 300 BCE. Etruscan ritual writing is older still, but that does not prove that the Tages attribution was equally old. A pre-Roman core is likely; its precise date is unestablished.

### Debate 3: One canon or fluid attribution?

Older scholarship often presents a stable Etruscan “Bible” divided into books revealed by Tages and Vegoia. The evidence instead suggests multiple technical collections, translations, commentaries, pseudonymous attributions, and evolving Roman classifications. “*Libri Tagetici*” is best treated as a family of attributed texts or an authority category unless a source specifies a work.

### Debate 4: Tarchon, Tages, and Tarquinii

Their similar names encouraged harmonization:

- Tarchon discovers Tages;
- *tarχies* is linked to *tarχunus*;
- Tarquinii is the place of revelation;
- Roman Tarquins enter some scholia.

This may reflect a genuine Tarquinian sacred complex, wordplay, or cumulative confusion. No surviving evidence securely proves that all names derive from one root or figure.

### Debate 5: Native revelation or Roman construction?

The legend’s autochthonous form and possible mirror evidence favor an Etruscan basis. Yet every complete verbal narrative is Roman or late antique. Consequently:

- the existence of a native revelatory tradition is probable;
- Cicero’s exact story is a Roman literary presentation;
- later universalization of Tages as author of almost every Etruscan doctrine is learned accretion.

### Archaeological discovery at Tarquinia

A high-status child buried near an early sacred structure at Tarquinia, with cranial pathology that may have caused visible deformation or seizures, has been associated by some scholars with the genesis of the Tages legend. This is an imaginative archaeological hypothesis, not an identification. There is no inscription linking the burial to Tages, and myth cannot be reduced to the biography of the excavated child. **[MSR, highly speculative]**

### Lost evidence

No original manuscript expressly titled *Liber Tageticus* survives. The source state consists of:

- Etruscan ritual inscriptions that do not name Tages;
- one disputed inscribed mirror;
- uninscribed images tentatively identified with him;
- Greek and Latin quotations, paraphrases, scholia, and testimonia;
- late translations and antiquarian compilations.

The *Corpus Speculorum Etruscorum* remains essential because drawings and early publications can misrepresent inscriptions or damaged scenes; its stated purpose is publication of all extant Etruscan and Praenestine mirrors [CSE review](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2001/2001.09.08/).

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Tages was securely the “Etruscan god of wisdom”: too categorical.
- *Pava* unquestionably means “child”: disputed.
- The Liber Linteus is the surviving Book of Tages: false.
- The Piacenza Liver belonged to Tages personally or bears his name: false.
- Tages and Vegoia were a divine couple: unattested.
- Tages was identical with Apollo, Hermes, Jesus, or a Goetic demon: unattested.
- Tages had an ancient temple, festival, sigil, planet, sacred color, or animal: unattested.
- His doctrine survives intact in modern occultism: false.
- Human sacrifice was prescribed in his books: unsupported.
- All branches of the *Etrusca disciplina* were originally revealed by him: late systematization, not demonstrable early fact.

Of these, only the Tuscania mirror contains a plausible form of Tages’ name, and that identification is disputed.
