# Tsukuyomi: Research Dossier

**Class:** *kami* (神), specifically a celestial lunar deity (*tsuki no kami*, 月神) in the early Japanese mythographic tradition; conventionally treated as a god, although the earliest narratives do not grammatically or anatomically determine sex.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the *Kojiki*, completed in 712 CE, where 月讀命 (*Tsukuyomi no Mikoto*) is born from Izanagi’s right eye and appointed to govern the night realm. The oldest surviving complete *Kojiki* manuscript is much later—the Shinpukuji manuscript of 1371–1372—so “712” dates the compilation recorded in the work’s preface, not its physical witness. The *Nihon shoki* of 720 supplies several incompatible birth accounts and the only early extended Tsukuyomi narrative, the killing of Ukemochi.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** eighth-century or earlier material preserved in the *Kojiki*, *Nihon shoki*, *Man’yōshū*, provincial records, ritual registers, or early administrative histories.
- **[CE] Later canonical elaboration:** Heian and later institutional texts accepted as authoritative by courts or shrines.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** local cult, shrine, or oral tradition whose identification with the mythographic Tsukuyomi may be secondary.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** commentary, theology, antiquarian interpretation, or modern scholarship.
- **[SY] Syncretistic identification:** Buddhist, Onmyōdō, or cross-cult equation.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore:** orally transmitted or ethnographically collected material.
- **[MR] Modern reconstruction or revival.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or fictional reinvention.**

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

**[EA]** Tsukuyomi is the lunar member of the triad later called the “three noble children” (*mihashira no uzu no miko*, 三貴子): Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, and Susanoo. In the *Kojiki*, Izanagi produces them during purification after returning from Yomi. Amaterasu comes from his left eye, Tsukuyomi from his right, and Susanoo from his nose. Tsukuyomi is commanded to govern *Yoru no Osu Kuni* (夜之食國), an obscure expression conventionally rendered “the realm of night.” Tsukuyomi then disappears from the *Kojiki* narrative. The text does not say that Tsukuyomi is banished, married, or continues pursuing Amaterasu.

**[EA]** The *Nihon shoki* preserves multiple alternatives instead of one canonical account:

1. Izanagi and Izanami together produce the sun deity and then the moon deity, whose brilliance is second only to the sun’s; the moon is sent to heaven to share the sun’s government.
2. Izanagi holds a white-copper mirror in his left hand and generates Ōhirume/Amaterasu, then holds it in his right and generates Tsukuyumi.
3. As in the *Kojiki*, purification of Izanagi’s right eye generates the moon deity.
4. In another distribution of realms, Tsukuyomi receives not merely night but the remote reaches of the ocean tides.
5. In “alternate writing” eleven, Amaterasu sends Tsukuyomi to Ukemochi, beginning the food-deity murder and sun–moon separation narrative.

The plurality is integral to the record. It is misleading to turn these witnesses into a single continuous biography. Kokugakuin University’s source survey likewise stresses that Tsukuyomi is nearly absent outside the birth and variant Ukemochi episodes and that the specific nature of ancient lunar worship remains difficult to recover ([Kokugakuin, Japanese database](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/shinmei/tsukuyominomikoto/); [English Encyclopedia of Shinto](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9136)).

| *Tsukuyomi no Mikoto* | 月讀尊 / 月読尊 | *Nihon shoki* variant | Same name with 尊, an honorific for an august deity. |
| *Tsukuyomi / Tsukuyomi-otoko* | 月読, 月夜見, 月余美; 月読壮士 | *Man’yōshū*, compiled later eighth century | Sometimes denotes the moon itself; *otoko* or *wotoko* explicitly personifies it as a male or young man. |
| *Tsukiyomi* | 月讀 / 月読 | Shrine and later standard pronunciation | Common modern shrine reading; not evidence that every ancient occurrence had precisely this vocalization. |

The Old Japanese phonograms show variation in *yo/yu*, so the apparent alternatives *Tsukuyomi*, *Tsukiyomi*, and *Tsukuyumi* cannot all be reduced confidently to modern spelling. The *Man’yōshū* writes the word several ways: 月読 in poems 4.670, 4.671, and 7.1075; 月夜見 in 13.3245; and 月余美 in 15.3599 and 15.3622. It also uses masculine personifications such as *Tsukuyomi-wotoko* and *tsukihito-wotoko* ([Kokugakuin textual inventory](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/shinmei/tsukuyominomikoto/)).

### Etymologies

**[LS: widely accepted but not proved] “Moon counter/reader.”** *Tsuki* or older *tsuku* means “moon”; *yomi* is associated with *yomu*, “to read” or “count.” The name would therefore mean one who reads or reckons the moon, particularly its phases and hence the calendar. *Man’yōshū* 20.4492 uses “reading/counting the moons” in a calendrical sense, strengthening the semantic possibility. It does not prove that the divine name was originally coined with that analysis.

**[LS] “Moon-night viewer.”** 月夜見 allows an interpretation involving seeing or manifesting the moonlit night. Because early Japanese writing frequently uses characters for sound, its graphic transparency is not decisive.

**[LS] “Moon bow.”** 月弓 may refer to the crescent moon’s bow shape. Aston glossed the variant through “bow,” but the spelling could instead be a phonetic accommodation of *yomi/yumi*. The variant is evidence of ancient name instability, not proof that Tsukuyomi carried a bow.

**[LS/MA] Association with Yomi, the dead’s realm.** Motoori Norinaga and subsequent interpreters have connected *yomi* in Tsukuyomi with 黄泉, Yomi. The early texts do not make that equation. Modern arguments joining moon, death, distant ocean, and renewal draw instead upon *Man’yōshū* 13.3245, comparative folklore, and the moon’s waxing and waning. This is an interpretive reconstruction, not an explicit ancient etymology.

### Gender

**[EA] The earliest prose is formally indeterminate.** Classical Japanese normally permits pronouns and predicates without marking sex. The *Kojiki* and relevant *Nihon shoki* passages do not describe Tsukuyomi’s body or explicitly call the deity *otokogami* (“male deity”).

**[EA] Early evidence nevertheless supports male personification.** The *Man’yōshū* calls the personified moon *Tsukuyomi-wotoko* or *tsukihito-wotoko*, “Moon-reading man/youth.” The early-ninth-century *Kōtaijingū gishikichō* describes the Ise image or divine form as a male riding a horse, clothed in purple and wearing a gold-fashioned sword. That is the clearest early institutional male representation, although it postdates the mythographies by roughly a century.

**[LS] Conventional conclusion:** Tsukuyomi is appropriately described as conventionally male, paired with the conventionally female Amaterasu. But statements that “the *Kojiki* explicitly says he is male” overstate the source. Conversely, claims that Tsukuyomi was originally a goddess generally rest on cross-cultural analogy or later yin–yang speculation, not direct early Japanese testimony. The secure formulation is: **sex unspecified in the earliest prose; masculine in early poetry and ritual iconography; overwhelmingly male in later convention.**

### Classifications and equations

- **[EA]** Celestial *kami* and personified moon.
- **[EA]** Ruler of night, heaven jointly with the sun, or a remote tidal sea, depending on the recension.
- **[LS]** Agricultural or calendrical deity, inferred from lunar reckoning and the Ukemochi episode.
- **[LS]** Mediator of death and renewed life, inferred from the food-deity death, *ochimizu* rejuvenating water, and remote-ocean imagery.
- **[SY]** The medieval religious environment permitted lunar kami to be interpreted through Buddhist lunar divinities such as Gatten/Gakkō or the moon-deva, but no universally authoritative equation of Tsukuyomi with one Buddhist deity is documented comparable to the elaborate honji-suijaku identities of Hachiman or major Ise deities. Specific local equations require shrine-specific evidence.
- **[LS]** Hattori Nakatsune and later writers proposed that Tsukuyomi and Susanoo were originally one deity or that Susanoo absorbed Tsukuyomi’s functions. Similar motifs make this a historical hypothesis, not an attested identity.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

**[EA] *Kojiki*, 712, Classical Japanese written largely in mixed Chinese-logographic and phonographic notation.** Its preface says Ō no Yasumaro presented the work to Empress Genmei in 712. Tsukuyomi appears in the purification sequence. The source says precisely that Izanagi washed his right eye, thereby generating Tsukuyomi; Izanagi commanded this deity to govern the night realm. It says nothing of Ukemochi, a quarrel with Amaterasu, weapons, spouse, offspring, temple, appearance, or later fate. Donald Philippi’s 1968 translation remains useful, though the electronic text most readily inspectable is Chamberlain’s older translation; Kokugakuin’s modern study renders the commission, “You shall rule over the night realm” ([Kokugakuin, “Studies on the Kojiki”](https://www.kokugakuin.ac.jp/assets/uploads/2023/03/Kenkyukaihatsusuisinkikou_kiyouNo15.pdf)).

**Dating qualification:** no eighth-century physical manuscript survives. The oldest complete witness is the Shinpukuji copy of 1371–1372, supplemented by later textual families. The 712 date belongs to compilation history, not parchment or paper in hand.

### *Nihon shoki*

**[EA] *Nihon shoki*, 720, Literary Sinitic.** This court chronicle is the indispensable source because its compilers deliberately preserved variant “writings” (*issho*). Aston’s 1896 translation sometimes supplies gendered English and interpretive vocabulary not demanded by the original, but it exposes the recensional structure.

The principal birth account calls the moon’s radiance next to the sun’s and says the deity was to be the sun goddess’s “consort” and share her government ([Aston, page 19](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3ANihongi_by_Aston.djvu/50)). “Consort” is Aston’s translation and has produced the modern claim that Amaterasu and Tsukuyomi were married. The Japanese/Chinese wording supports association in government but does not establish a wedding narrative, sexual union, or offspring. No early text narrates such a marriage.

Alternate writing eleven contains the Ukemochi episode in full. Amaterasu commands Tsukuyomi to visit Ukemochi; Ukemochi produces rice, fish, and game from her mouth; Tsukuyomi denounces the food as polluted and kills her with a sword. Amaterasu calls Tsukuyomi a wicked deity and refuses further face-to-face meeting. Sun and moon thereafter dwell apart by day and night. Ame-no-Kumahito inspects the corpse, from which livestock, silkworms, and cereals emerge ([Aston, pages 32–33](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3ANihongi_by_Aston.djvu/63)).

### Historical oracle notice

**[EA as a 720 textual notice; historicity of the narrated 487 event unverified]** Under the third year of the reign assigned to Emperor Kenzō—traditionally 487—the *Nihon shoki* says that Abe no Omi Kotoshiro, travelling on a mission to Mimana, was addressed through spirit possession by the Moon God. The god declared Takamimusubi an ancestor who helped fashion heaven and earth, requested land, and promised blessing. Kotoshiro reported the oracle, and the court granted Utaarasu fields in Kadono District, Yamashiro; Oshimi no Sukune, ancestor of the Iki provincial lords, served the cult. This is the earliest narrative of a Tsukuyomi-associated cult foundation, but its surviving witness is the 720 chronicle, not a fifth-century inscription. Kokugakuin reproduces the Literary Sinitic text and later administrative notices ([Kokugakuin shrine-source collection](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/jinja/011801_52945/)).

The notice calls the speaker 月神, “Moon God,” and gives a genealogy from Takamimusubi that conflicts with the cosmogonic parentage from Izanagi or Izanagi–Izanami. Whether the oracle deity was already identical with the courtly Tsukuyomi is plausible but not demonstrable independently.

### *Man’yōshū*

**[EA] *Man’yōshū*, compiled in stages and substantially closed after 759.** The anthology gives better evidence for lunar language and belief than for a cultic personality:

- 4.670–671 invoke Tsukuyomi’s light in an exchange of longing.
- 7.1075 imagines the moon travelling a distant path over the sea.
- 6.985 and 7.1372 use *Tsukuyomi-wotoko*, a male personification.
- 13.3245 wishes for a long bridge or high mountain by which the speaker might obtain *ochimizu*, rejuvenating water possessed by Tsukuyomi, and give it to an aging beloved.
- 15.3599 and 15.3622 employ 月余美 for moonlight or moon.

These poems show that *Tsukuyomi* could be an ordinary poetic name for the visible moon. They do not prove that every poetic invocation referred to the mythographic child of Izanagi. The rejuvenating-water poem is direct early evidence connecting the moon with renewal, while its relationship to later Okinawan stories remains comparative reconstruction rather than demonstrated textual descent ([poem 3245 text](https://bonjin-ultra.com/mny133245.html); [Kokugakuin survey](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/shinmei/tsukuyominomikoto/)).

### *Fudoki*

**[EA/RV]** The surviving and fragmentary provincial *Fudoki* contain possible lunar names but no secure parallel biography. An *Izumo Fudoki* place-name tradition refers to *Tsukutsumi-no-Mikoto*, a child of Izanagi. Phonetic resemblance has encouraged identification with Tsukuyomi, but the names are not identical and the text does not provide the eye-birth or Ukemochi narrative. Fragmentary Yamashiro traditions concerning a katsura tree at the lunar shrine are tied to the Kadono cult; their transmission is late and must not be treated as independent eighth-century manuscripts.

### *Kogo Shūi*

**[CE]** Inbe no Hironari’s *Kogo Shūi* (807) retells cosmogonic and ritual traditions but adds no substantial independent Tsukuyomi cycle. References to the moon deity belong to learned court mythography. Its silence about the Ukemochi episode is significant: that episode did not become a universally expanded narrative cycle.

### *Engishiki*

**[CE]** The *Engishiki*, compiled 905–927 and presented in 927, is administrative and ritual evidence rather than narrative scripture. Its shrine register lists:

- Tsukiyomi-no-miya in Watarai District, Ise;
- Tsukiyomi Shrine and related lunar establishments in Ise;
- Kadono-ni-masu Tsukuyomi Jinja in Yamashiro;
- Tsukuyomi shrines in Tsuzuki District, Yamashiro, and Iki.

The register proves recognized institutions and ranks by the early tenth century. It does not prove that all these establishments had always worshipped one identical deity or shared the *Nihon shoki* myth.

The *Engishiki*’s *norito* do not preserve a major prayer addressed narratively to Tsukuyomi comparable to prayers for several more institutionally prominent kami. That negative evidence agrees with the deity’s low narrative and liturgical visibility.

### Medieval tale collections and yōkai catalogues

**[MA: negative finding]** Tsukuyomi has no major independent episode in the standard *Konjaku monogatari-shū* corpus. Moon imagery, Buddhist lunar symbolism, and supernatural night episodes should not automatically be reclassified as appearances by Tsukuyomi.

**[MA/Early-modern: negative finding]** Toriyama Sekien’s eighteenth-century yōkai catalogues are not sources for a Tsukuyomi cult or iconography. Modern websites sometimes group Tsukuyomi with “yōkai” because both are Japanese supernatural subjects; Sekien did not thereby classify the lunar kami as a yōkai.

## 3. Domains, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**[EA] The moon and night.** This is the secure core, expressed by the name, the birth beside the solar deity, and governance of *Yoru no Osu Kuni*.

**[EA variant] Joint heavenly government.** Several *Nihon shoki* recensions place sun and moon together in heaven. This differs from the *Kojiki*’s separate realm assignments and precedes their separation in the Ukemochi variant.

**[EA variant] Remote tidal sea.** One *Nihon shoki* alternate writing gives Tsukuyomi the multitudinous reaches of the blue sea’s tides. This overlaps with Susanoo’s sea government but emphasizes remoteness. Modern scholarship uses this distinction against simply declaring the two deities identical ([2024 study](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/hcs/2024/34/2024_206/_pdf/-char/en)).

**[EA/LS] Time and calendar.** Lunar phases structured month-reckoning; the *Man’yōshū* supports the vocabulary of counting moons. “God of time” is nevertheless a modern abstraction. No early text appoints Tsukuyomi sovereign of time in general.

**[EA/LS] Agriculture and food.** The Ukemochi story makes Tsukuyomi the violent catalyst for the emergence of cereals, silkworms, cattle, and horses. Lunar calendrics also mattered to agriculture. Calling Tsukuyomi a universally worshipped “agricultural god” goes beyond the evidence.

**[EA/LS] Renewal and immortality.** *Man’yōshū* 13.3245 places rejuvenating water with Tsukuyomi. Nikolai Nevskii, Ishida Eiichirō, Orikuchi Shinobu, and later scholars compared Miyako and wider Eurasian/Pacific stories of lunar water, mortality, and renewal. Similarity is real; historical transmission to the eighth-century poem remains disputed.

### Powers and limitations

- Generates lunar brilliance second only to the sun’s: *Nihon shoki* main account.
- Governs an assigned cosmic region: *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* variants.
- Travels from heaven to the Central Land of Reed Plains: Ukemochi episode.
- Wields and kills with a sword: *Nihon shoki* alternate writing eleven.
- Acts as an oracle through possession and promises blessing for land: Kenzō-year notice.
- Possesses rejuvenating water in poetic imagination: *Man’yōshū* 13.3245.
- Is subordinate enough to accept Amaterasu’s commission in the Ukemochi story.
- Cannot or does not restore relations after Amaterasu’s rejection. No source narrates reconciliation.

Tsukuyomi is not securely attested with dream magic, illusion, telepathy, control of eclipses, white hair, a crescent forehead, twin blades, or “infinite illusion.” Those are modern artistic or fictional attributes.

### Iconography

**[EA/CE]** There is no securely identified prehistoric or Kofun-period image of Tsukuyomi. Lunar disks and moon-associated tomb murals attest lunar symbolism, not the named deity. The late-sixth-century Mezurashizuka tomb mural has been interpreted as a moon containing a frog-like figure and a boat journey toward it; assigning that figure to Tsukuyomi would be speculative.

**[CE]** The early-ninth-century Ise ritual description of a male, horse-riding divine form in purple clothing with a gold sword is the most important old textual iconographic notice. It confirms a masculine institutional form but does not establish a standard national image.

**[CE to present]** Ise worship centers on shrine architecture and enshrined presence rather than a publicly displayed anthropomorphic cult statue. The shrines themselves—their compounds, offerings, rebuilding, and ritual calendars—are therefore more reliable material evidence than modern portraits.

**[MA/MI]** Later illustrated books depict Tsukuyomi in court or warrior dress. Modern fantasy art commonly adds pale skin, silver or white hair, crescent moons, blue-black clothing, bows, or paired swords. These designs have no single ancient prototype. Even where a sword has textual warrant, its specific shape and “lunar” ornamentation are modern invention.

### Animals, plants, colors, and numbers

No early source assigns Tsukuyomi a stable sacred animal, plant, color, or number. In particular:

- The moon rabbit belongs to a wider East Asian Buddhist and folkloric complex and is not Tsukuyomi’s attested mount or servant.
- White, silver, indigo, and black are modern lunar aesthetics, not canonical cult colors.
- The katsura tree belongs to lunar and local shrine traditions but is not a universally attested personal emblem.
- Horses occur in the Ise male-form description and cattle and horses arise from Ukemochi’s corpse; neither establishes a universal “sacred animal.”
- The moon’s association with tides is ancient in observation, but modern claims that Tsukuyomi personally governs all water or menstruation are unattested elaborations.

## 4. Genealogy and divine relations

### Parentage variants

1. **[EA, *Kojiki* 712]** Parent: Izanagi alone, through purification of his right eye. Izanami is not co-parent because she remains in Yomi.
2. **[EA, *Nihon shoki* 720 main account]** Parents: Izanagi and Izanami together produce the moon deity after the sun deity.
3. **[EA, *Nihon shoki* alternate writing]** Izanagi alone produces Tsukuyumi through the right-hand white-copper mirror.
4. **[EA, Kenzō oracle as preserved in 720]** The Moon God calls Takamimusubi “my ancestor.” This lineage does not match the cosmogonic birth accounts and may preserve a separate Iki/Kadono cult genealogy.

### Siblings and household

- Amaterasu is the paired solar sibling in the eye-birth accounts.
- Susanoo is the third child in the purification triad.
- The many other children of Izanagi and Izanami can be called half- or full siblings only within particular genealogical recensions.
- No early source securely names a consort or child of Tsukuyomi.
- “Amaterasu’s husband” is too definite. Aston’s “consort” translates a passage about paired government; no marriage, sexual relationship, or offspring is narrated.
- No early source makes Ukemochi Tsukuyomi’s wife. She is a deity whom he visits and kills.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Birth from the right eye and division of rule

**[EA: *Kojiki* version]** After escaping Yomi and repudiating the pollution of death, Izanagi purifies himself. Numerous deities arise as he discards clothing and washes. When he cleans his left eye, Amaterasu is born; the right eye produces Tsukuyomi; the nose produces Susanoo. Izanagi rejoices that he has obtained three exalted children, gives Amaterasu his necklace and rule of the Plain of High Heaven, gives Tsukuyomi rule of *Yoru no Osu Kuni*, and gives Susanoo rule of the sea plain.

The source’s narrative function is structural: purification generates a celestial governing triad. Tsukuyomi is the balanced lunar term between solar heaven and the turbulent sea deity. But only Susanoo refuses his assignment and propels the plot onward; Tsukuyomi complies and exits.

**[EA: *Nihon shoki* variants]** The moon is alternatively:

- naturally born to Izanagi and Izanami after the sun;
- produced through a right-hand mirror;
- produced from the right eye during purification;
- assigned joint heavenly government;
- assigned the remote tidal sea.

These are not stages in one life. They are competing cosmogonies gathered by the compilers.

### Killing of Ukemochi and separation of sun and moon

**[EA: *Nihon shoki* alternate writing eleven, 720]** Amaterasu hears that Ukemochi dwells in the Central Land of Reed Plains and orders Tsukuyomi to visit. Ukemochi turns toward the land and brings boiled rice from her mouth; toward the sea and produces broad-finned and narrow-finned creatures; toward the mountains and produces rough-haired and soft-haired game. She arranges them on one hundred tables.

Tsukuyomi becomes furious. Aston renders his accusation, “Filthy! Nasty!” He objects that food disgorged from a mouth has been offered to him. He draws his sword and kills Ukemochi, then reports the event to Amaterasu. She calls him an evil or wicked deity and declares that she will not see him face to face. The two consequently dwell apart, separated by day and night.

Amaterasu sends Ame-no-Kumahito to investigate. Ukemochi is dead, but from her body have emerged ox and horse, millet, silkworms, panic grass, rice, wheat, large beans, and small beans, with the exact body-to-product mapping varying by translation. Takamimusubi or Amaterasu then directs their collection and use. Thus an act condemned within the story becomes the mechanism by which agriculture, sericulture, and domesticated resources reach humans.

The myth has at least three functions:

1. **Etiological:** it explains the apparent separation of sun and moon.
2. **Food-origin:** edible plants and useful animals emerge from a slain food being.
3. **Purity discourse:** Tsukuyomi reacts violently to bodily production of food, while the story transforms death and putrefaction into fertility.

It should not be sanitized: Tsukuyomi kills a female host for offering food produced from her body. The text condemns him as wicked through Amaterasu’s speech, yet also makes the corpse the source of human subsistence.

### Parallel Ōgetsuhime narrative

**[EA: *Kojiki* 712]** Susanoo asks Ōgetsuhime for food. She produces foods from her nose, mouth, and rectum. Susanoo believes she has polluted the meal and kills her. Crops and silkworms emerge from her corpse.

The structural relationship to the Ukemochi myth is unmistakable, but the named killer, victim, setting, food-production details, and aftermath differ. The *Kojiki* does not make the event separate sun and moon. Scholarship variously proposes:

- a shared underlying food-origin tale allocated differently by the two compilations;
- transfer of lunar functions to Susanoo as the triad was systematized;
- compilation from distinct local or lineage traditions;
- influence from broader East and Southeast Asian “food from a slain divine body” narratives.

None of these reconstructions can presently be demonstrated as the unique original form.

### Moon and rejuvenating water

**[EA poetic complex]** *Man’yōshū* 13.3245 imagines reaching Tsukuyomi’s *ochimizu* to restore a beloved’s youth. This is not a prose myth with a beginning and conclusion, but it attests a belief-image: the waxing moon possesses renewal.

**[F/LS]** Nevskii recorded Miyako Island traditions in which a lunar being or lunar water participates in the origin of mortality. Orikuchi and Ishida placed these beside the *Man’yōshū* poem and widespread “water of life” motifs. The comparison illuminates a possible old complex but does not establish that a single unchanged “Tsukuyomi myth” survived from Nara Japan to twentieth-century Okinawa.

### The oracle and land grant

**[EA textual notice]** The Kenzō-year episode constitutes Tsukuyomi’s only substantial appearance in purported human history. Possessing a person during an embassy, the Moon God demands land and promises blessing. The court complies, and a priestly ancestor from Iki serves the shrine.

The episode may encode the relocation or court incorporation of an Iki lunar cult into Yamashiro. Its fifth-century dating is internal to the *Nihon shoki*; there is no contemporary 487 inscription. The 701 notice in the *Shoku Nihongi* granting shrine rice to the Nakatomi provides firmer early-eighth-century institutional context, while the 856 relocation is recorded in the *Montoku jitsuroku*.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### The problem of “near-absence”

Tsukuyomi’s cult was not nonexistent. It was institutionally represented at Ise, Kadono/Kyoto, Yamashiro, and Iki and entered the *Engishiki*. What is absent is evidence for a broad, autonomous popular cult comparable to those of Hachiman, Inari, Kumano, Tenjin, or major Buddhist figures. The distinction matters:

- **Textual prominence:** extremely low.
- **Number of securely ancient dedicated institutions:** small.
- **Institutional rank at several surviving shrines:** high.
- **Evidence for mass medieval confraternities, preaching traditions, pilgrimage networks, or a large body of votives specifically to Tsukuyomi:** sparse.

### Ise: Tsukiyomi-no-miya

**[CE]** Tsukiyomi-no-miya is a detached auxiliary sanctuary or *betsugū* of the Inner Shrine. The Ise complex associates it closely with Amaterasu’s institutional sphere. The early ritual books and *Engishiki* establish its antiquity, but exact “foundation dates” supplied in shrine tradition cannot necessarily be verified archaeologically.

The present compound includes sanctuaries associated with Tsukiyomi, Tsukiyomi’s *aramitama* or active spirit, Izanagi, and Izanami. This fourfold layout is an institutional arrangement, not evidence that the *Kojiki* narrated four gods living together there.

Rites follow the Ise calendar: annual prayer for crops, monthly observances, harvest offerings, imperial offerings, and the periodic rebuilding system. These are part of Ise’s organized ritual economy rather than a surviving enactment of the Ukemochi myth.

### Ise: Tsukiyomi-no-miya of the Outer Shrine

**[CE]** The Outer Shrine’s 月夜見宮 is separately situated in central Ise. The *Engishiki* lists it as the leading subsidiary shrine of the Outer Shrine; it was elevated to *betsugū* status in 1210. Ise’s official history emphasizes its old location at Takagawara and associations with river flow and agriculture ([Ise Jingū official history](https://www.isejingu.or.jp/about/outerbetsugu/?sa=X&ved=2ahUKEwih2-Lv-c38AhWRV8UKHVq2D3YQyK4DegQIBBAy)).

The existence of Inner- and Outer-Shrine lunar sanctuaries should not be mistaken for evidence of two mythological Tsukuyomis. They are distinct institutions in the dual Ise organization.

### Kadono Tsukiyomi Shrine, Kyoto

**[EA/CE]** The shrine now associated with Matsunoo Taisha traces its foundation to the Kenzō oracle. More securely documented stages are:

- the narrative of a land grant preserved in the *Nihon shoki*;
- a 701 *Shoku Nihongi* notice placing the shrine’s rice allocation under the Nakatomi;
- relocation in 856 because river erosion threatened the old site;
- court rank of senior second rank in 859;
- later promotion and listing as a *myōjin taisha* in the *Engishiki*.

The present shrine tradition says the Iki provincial-lord lineage and later Urabe descendants supplied hereditary priests. Matsunoo’s official account preserves these claims and notes the 856 relocation ([Matsunoo Taisha](https://www.matsunoo.or.jp/guide07/)).

The “moon-extending stone” or safe-childbirth stone is a later local tradition. Its association with Empress Jingū and a mission to obtain the stone is transmitted in early-modern topographical literature, not in the eighth-century foundation notice. Safe-childbirth devotion is therefore a regional accretion, not an original universal domain of Tsukuyomi.

### Iki

**[CE/RV]** The *Engishiki* registers a Tsukiyomi Shrine on Iki, fitting the *Nihon shoki*’s claim that an ancestor of the Iki provincial lords served the Yamashiro cult. The present shrine in Iki identifies itself as the source of the Kyoto and Ise lunar shrines and calls itself the origin of ancient Shinto. Those expansive priority claims are current shrine tradition, not conclusions established by contemporary archaeology. Its currently advertised rites include observances on the first and fifteenth of each month and an annual festival on the twenty-third day of the ninth lunar month ([present shrine website](https://tsukiyomijinja.com/)).

The identification of the present physical site with the exact *Engishiki* establishment was made or strengthened in the early modern period. Continuity of location and cult before that identification is disputed.

### Other shrines and Gassan

Local Tsukiyomi/Tsukuyomi shrines exist elsewhere, but identical naming may reflect medieval or modern enshrinement rather than an independently documented ancient lunar cult.

Gassan, “Moon Mountain,” and Gassan Shrine are frequently assimilated in modern summaries to Tsukuyomi. The mountain’s ascetic and Dewa Sanzan traditions have their own complex Buddhist-kami history. The lexical moon association does not by itself prove that the ancient court deity Tsukuyomi was always Gassan’s sole or original god.

### Priests, offerings, and economics

The best-attested priestly connection is the Iki lineage at Kadono, followed by Urabe hereditary service. At Ise, Tsukuyomi’s rites were embedded within the Jingū priestly and estate system. Offerings documented for Ise auxiliary shrines largely follow the agrarian and textile pattern of the larger institution: rice, cloth, sake, and other food offerings.

No documentary evidence establishes:

- human sacrifice to Tsukuyomi;
- temple prostitution;
- a distinct Tsukuyomi oracle industry beyond the foundation possession narrative;
- a specialist nationwide Tsukuyomi priesthood;
- historically continuous secret “moon magic” rites;
- violent suppression conducted specifically in Tsukuyomi’s name.

These are negative findings, not assertions that violence or coercion were absent from Japanese religious history generally.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 700: possible lunar cults, little named evidence

Lunar reckoning, moon-viewing, and local moon worship almost certainly preceded the surviving texts, but no datable prehistoric inscription names Tsukuyomi. Kofun lunar imagery cannot be equated automatically with this deity.

The purported 487 Kadono foundation is known only from a text compiled in 720. It may preserve an old cult migration from Iki; its exact date and historicity remain unverified.

### 712–759: court mythography and poetry

The *Kojiki* establishes the eye-birth and night government. The *Nihon shoki* multiplies the birth and jurisdictional accounts and supplies Ukemochi’s killing. The *Man’yōshū* shows that Tsukuyomi was also a poetic personification of the moon and a possessor of rejuvenating water.

This period supplies nearly the entire narrative corpus. Later tradition elaborates little.

### Eighth to tenth centuries: institutional consolidation

Court records recognize the Kadono shrine and its allocations. The early-ninth-century Ise ritual record gives a masculine mounted form. The *Engishiki* integrates several Tsukuyomi institutions into the court’s ranked shrine and offering system.

This is the strongest period for documentary cult evidence. Yet even here Tsukuyomi remains a subsidiary or locally concentrated power rather than the center of a major national movement.

### Medieval period: shrine-Buddhist complexes

**[SY/MA]** Like other kami institutions, Tsukuyomi shrines existed within a Buddhist religious world of shrine temples, sutra rites, Onmyōdō calendrics, and honji-suijaku interpretation. It would be anachronistic to imagine a uniformly separate “pure Shinto” cult persisting unchanged.

Kuroda Toshio’s influential 1981 argument challenged the picture of Shinto as a single autonomous religion continuously preserved from prehistory; he situated kami worship within the medieval Buddhist order ([Kuroda, “Shinto in the History of Japanese Religion”](https://religion-in-japan.univie.ac.at/k/images/0/03/Kuroda_1981.pdf)). Breen and Teeuwen similarly distinguish historical kami, shrines, rites, and myths from the later abstraction “Shinto” ([*A New History of Shinto*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/9781444317190)).

No major medieval Tsukuyomi revelation text, pilgrimage confraternity, or iconographic mandala cycle has attained prominence comparable to those of Ise’s principal deities, Kasuga, Kumano, or Hachiman. The silence is historically meaningful.

### Early modern period: Kokugaku interpretation

Motoori Norinaga’s *Kojiki-den* (written 1764–1798) philologically elevated the *Kojiki* as a record of the ancient way and parsed Tsukuyomi through its diction and genealogical structure. Hirata Atsutane expanded nativist cosmology, placing the classical kami within a systematic account of spirit worlds and national antiquity.

These scholars did not recover an unchanged prehistoric theology. They created influential early-modern readings from classical texts, linguistic theories, polemics against Buddhist and Confucian interpretations, and contemporary religious concerns. Modern “ancient Shinto” presentations often inherit their categories without identifying them as Edo-period constructions.

### Meiji to 1945: shrine reorganization and State Shinto

In 1868 the government ordered the administrative separation of kami and buddhas. The resulting *shinbutsu bunri* and locally violent *haibutsu kishaku* removed Buddhist clergy, images, terminology, and buildings from many shrine complexes. Tsukuyomi institutions within Ise and the ranked shrine system were consequently recoded as components of an ostensibly non-Buddhist national order.

Tsukuyomi himself was not a leading ideological deity of the imperial state; Amaterasu and the imperial genealogy occupied that role. Nevertheless, Tsukuyomi’s Ise shrines and mythographic position were incorporated into the state-administered system. Claims that State Shinto generated a vast independent Tsukuyomi cult are unsupported.

### Since 1945

SCAP’s Shinto Directive of 15 December 1945 abolished governmental sponsorship and control of State Shinto, explicitly distinguishing the former state cult from voluntary religion ([official translated directive](https://dl.ndl.go.jp/view/download/digidepo_9602130_po_CRJ-25.PDF?alternativeNo=&contentNo=1)). Ise and other shrines continued as legally private religious institutions supported by offerings, donations, and shrine organizations.

Tsukuyomi remains worshipped at Ise, Kyoto, Iki, and local shrines. This is genuine living practice. It does not demonstrate uninterrupted identity of every doctrine, site, or ritual since antiquity.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Folklore scholarship

Yanagita Kunio’s *Tōno monogatari* (1910) and broader folklore program transformed local oral traditions into objects of national ethnology, but *Tōno monogatari* does not furnish a lost Tsukuyomi narrative. Hori Ichirō’s work on folk religion likewise helps contextualize mountain, ancestor, and otherworld practices but should not be cited as evidence that every lunar custom was worship of Tsukuyomi.

Nevskii’s Miyako research, Orikuchi’s interpretations, and Ishida’s comparative studies made the moon-and-immortality complex central to twentieth-century discussion. They are scholarship and collected folklore, not newly discovered chapters of the *Kojiki*.

### Modern worship and neopaganism

**[MR]** Individual practitioners in and outside Japan now construct devotional practices focused on Tsukuyomi: moon-phase observance, night prayer, water offerings, silver-colored altars, divination, and paired worship with Amaterasu. Some draw loosely on shrine etiquette; others combine Shinto vocabulary with Wicca, astrology, Jungian psychology, or eclectic paganism.

No evidence found establishes an ancient Tsukuyomi ritual system involving moonwater charging, crystals, tarot, dream spells, or prescribed full-moon initiations. Such practices may be meaningful modern religion, but they are modern synthesis rather than reconstruction from a surviving manual.

Likewise, claims that Tsukuyomi is an ancient patron of empaths, shadow work, gender transition, lucid dreaming, or occult illusion are modern theological creations unless a practitioner explicitly presents them as personal revelation.

### Literature, manga, anime, and games

**[MI]** Masashi Kishimoto’s *Naruto*, serialized from 1999, made “Tsukuyomi” internationally familiar as Itachi Uchiha’s devastating illusion technique; “Infinite Tsukuyomi” later denotes a world-enveloping illusion. The series groups Tsukuyomi, Amaterasu, and Susanoo as names of eye-related powers. This cleverly reuses the mythic eye-birth triad but has no basis in an ancient power of illusion. The popularity of this usage now distorts web searches toward fictional abilities ([overview of the adaptation](https://naruto.fandom.com/wiki/Tsukuyomi)).

**[MI]** *Final Fantasy XIV: Stormblood* presents Tsukuyomi as a moon-associated primal linked to Yotsuyu. The visual and narrative design is original to the game, not a reproduction of the *Nihon shoki* deity ([official game database example](https://na.finalfantasyxiv.com/lodestone/playguide/db/item/7e7ad1a24d3/)).

**[MI]** The multiplayer game *SMITE* introduced Tsukuyomi as a playable “God of the Moon” in August 2020, equipped with stylized crescent weapons and a militant commitment to order. The developer’s presentation is explicit modern character design ([official PlayStation trailer, 20 July 2020](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=Z7VfJMyTlss)).

Numerous other manga, novels, games, weapons, spacecraft projects, and fictional characters use the name. Frequently the only borrowed element is “moon.” These works have materially created the now-common image of Tsukuyomi as a pale, aloof male warrior, far more visually defined than the early record permits.

### Political and extremist appropriation

Tsukuyomi has not been a major independent emblem of modern Japanese ultranationalism. The deity is implicated indirectly through imperial myth, Ise, and the State Shinto canon, but Amaterasu, the emperor, Yasukuni, and foundation myths carried the primary ideological load. No well-documented major extremist movement centered specifically on Tsukuyomi was identified.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Why does Tsukuyomi disappear?

1. **Compilation-history explanation:** the court combined traditions in which solar and lunar deities formed a pair with a more active Susanoo cycle, leaving Tsukuyomi structurally present but narratively redundant.
2. **Functional-transfer theory:** Susanoo absorbed Tsukuyomi’s sea and food-death functions. Shared features support comparison, but no source describes the transfer.
3. **Hayao Kawai’s “hollow center”:** Tsukuyomi’s disappearance exemplifies a recurring Japanese triadic structure in which the middle term is empty or mediating.
4. **Critique of Kawai:** Amaterasu and Tsukuyomi form a primary sun–moon pair, so positioning Tsukuyomi as merely the middle of Amaterasu–Tsukuyomi–Susanoo may impose the desired structure.
5. **Cult disparity explanation:** Tsukuyomi may never have possessed the court and lineage constituencies that generated large narrative corpora for Amaterasu and Susanoo.

The 2024 review of the deity summarizes the Kawai debate and older Tsukuyomi–Susanoo identity theory while arguing for Tsukuyomi as a mediator between remote/dead and living worlds ([peer-reviewed study](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/hcs/2024/34/2024_206/_pdf/-char/en)).

### What does *Yoru no Osu Kuni* mean?

Common translations include “realm of night,” “night’s food-country,” and “country where night is governed.” The graph 食 can represent *osu*, “to rule/govern,” in honorific usage rather than literal “food.” Because the Ukemochi story links Tsukuyomi to food, some scholars see deliberate semantic resonance; others treat “food-country” as a misleading literalism. The safest translation is “night realm,” retaining the original in discussion.

### Was Tsukuyomi originally the sea god?

One *Nihon shoki* recension assigns the remote tidal sea to Tsukuyomi, while the *Kojiki* assigns the sea plain to Susanoo. This supports historical overlap, not the conclusion that every sea deity was originally Tsukuyomi. The unusual emphasis on the sea’s distant, many-layered tides may connect the moon with navigation, horizons, or an otherworld.

### Were Tsukuyomi and Amaterasu spouses?

Aston’s translation calls the moon deity the sun goddess’s “consort.” The passage certainly pairs their rule. It does not narrate marriage, cohabitation as husband and wife, sexual union, or children. “Sibling and governmental counterpart” is secure; “spouse” is a possible interpretation of one recension, not a pan-traditional fact.

### Was Tsukuyomi male?

The prose sources do not explicitly state sex; *Man’yōshū* male personifications and the Ise male-form record support early masculinity. Modern female or nonbinary depictions are legitimate reinterpretations but should not be passed off as the single suppressed ancient original.

### Is the deity “evil”?

Only Amaterasu’s speech in the Ukemochi recension calls Tsukuyomi a wicked deity in response to the killing. The broader tradition does not classify Tsukuyomi as a demon, fallen god, or cosmic enemy. Modern descriptions of an eternally evil, exiled, vengeful moon god expand one line into a theology absent from the sources.

### Primary-evidence condition

- No autograph *Kojiki* or *Nihon shoki* survives.
- The mythographies are court compilations, not neutral transcripts of prehistoric belief.
- No securely identified ancient anthropomorphic statue of Tsukuyomi establishes a standard appearance.
- Shrine registers prove institutions but not uniform theology.
- Later shrine origin narratives may preserve older traditions but can also legitimate land, rank, hereditary office, or ritual privilege.
- The paucity of evidence is itself a central fact; it cannot responsibly be repaired by combining every Japanese lunar motif into one personality.

### Major scholarly landmarks

- Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den*, 1764–1798: foundational Kokugaku philology, but also early-modern ideological interpretation.
- Hirata Atsutane, late eighteenth–early nineteenth centuries: systematic nativist cosmology.
- W. G. Aston, *Nihongi*, 1896: still the standard public-domain English translation, though philologically dated.
- Donald L. Philippi, *Kojiki*, 1968: major modern English translation.
- Nevskii, Orikuchi, and Ishida: comparative work on moon, water, death, and rejuvenation.
- Matsumae Takeshi and later Japanese mythologists: structural and historical comparison of sun–moon and food-origin myths.
- Kuroda Toshio, 1981: critique of an unbroken autonomous “Shinto.”
- Breen and Teeuwen, 2010: historical reconstruction of shrines, ritual, myth, and the changing Shinto category.
- Kokugakuin’s digital databases: consolidated primary references and bibliographies, while reflecting an institution embedded in modern Shinto studies.

## 11. Conclusions

The reconstructable Tsukuyomi is unusually narrow. The secure early core consists of a name bound to the moon, birth beside the sun, assignment to night or another remote realm, a handful of incompatible cosmogonic variants, poetic personification, and one violent food-origin story. Institutional evidence confirms real lunar shrines of substantial court rank, but not a large narrative cult.

The evidence does not support a richly detailed ancient warrior biography, a universal marriage to Amaterasu, a fixed appearance, children, a moon-rabbit retinue, occult illusion powers, or an elaborate surviving lunar mystery religion. Most such richness is modern compensation for ancient silence.

That silence is not proof that Tsukuyomi was unimportant, suppressed, female, foreign, identical with Susanoo, or secretly central. Each has been proposed, but no one proposal closes the evidentiary gap. The most historically faithful portrait is therefore plural: a barely narrated lunar child in the *Kojiki*; a bright heavenly counterpart, remote sea ruler, killer, and rejected envoy in competing *Nihon shoki* recensions; a male or impersonal moon in the *Man’yōshū*; a ranked presence in a few important shrines; an object of learned reconstruction; and, only in the modern period, a visually and psychologically elaborate god.
