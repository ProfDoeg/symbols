# Konohanasakuya-hime: Research Dossier

**Class:** female *kami* (女神, goddess) in the court-mythological tradition recorded in early Japan; later a mountain, volcanic-fire, childbirth, blossom, agricultural, and brewing deity through distinct local and syncretic developments.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the *Kojiki*, completed in 712, where she appears as 神阿多都比売 (*Kamu-Atatsu-hime*) and, by an alternate descriptive name, 木花之佐久夜毘売 (*Konohana-no-Sakuya-bime*). The closely following *Nihon shoki* of 720 supplies several variant accounts and name forms. A probably independent local form, 許乃波奈佐久夜比売命, occurs in the early-eighth-century *Harima no kuni fudoki*. None of these texts identifies her with Mount Fuji. The earliest securely dated documentary evidence for Fuji’s historical cult instead names the volcanic power 浅間大神／浅間明神 (*Asama no Ōkami/Asama Myōjin*); identification of that deity with Konohanasakuya-hime is demonstrable only from the early seventeenth century and became nearly universal in the nineteenth century, particularly after the 1868 separation of kami and buddhas.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Konohanasakuya-hime is first a mythic princess of Ata in southern Kyushu, daughter of the mountain deity Ōyamatsumi and wife of the heavenly descendant Ninigi. She embodies blooming prosperity and the brilliant but short duration of tree-flowers, in contrast to her enduring rock-associated sister Iwanaga-hime. Her principal myth establishes the fertility and legitimacy of the heavenly dynasty: after conceiving in one night and being accused of carrying another deity’s child, she seals herself in a doorless parturition house, sets it alight, and gives birth safely. Through later cultic history, this scene supplied her associations with protection from fire, volcanic danger, conception, safe childbirth, and children. One *Nihon shoki* variant also makes her cultivator of ritually pure rice and brewer of “heavenly sweet sake.” Her now-familiar identities as Mount Fuji’s goddess and specifically a cherry-blossom goddess are historical developments, not explicit premises of the earliest texts.

### Names

- **神阿多都比売 — Kamu-Atatsu-hime / Kamu-Atatsu-bime.** The *Kojiki*’s first name for her: “sacred/divine princess or woman of Ata.” *Ata* denotes the southern Kyushu region associated with Cape Kasasa. **Tier: securely attested early material, 712.**

- **木花之佐久夜毘売 — Konohana-no-Sakuya-bime.** Her *Kojiki* alternate name. 木花 is “tree-flower(s)”; 之 is the genitive *no*; 佐久 represents *saku*, “to bloom”; 夜 is generally treated as an emphatic or interjectional particle; 毘売 is *bime/hime*, “high-born woman, princess.” A cautious translation is “Princess Blooming Splendidly like the Flowers of the Trees,” not necessarily “Cherry-Blossom Princess.” **Tier: securely attested early material, 712.**

- **佐久夜毘売 — Sakuya-bime.** Short form within the *Kojiki*. **Tier: securely attested early material.**

- **鹿葦津姫 — Kaashitsu-hime**, **神吾田津姫 — Kamu-Atatsu-hime**, and **神吾田鹿葦津姫 — Kamu-Atakaashitsu-hime.** Forms in the *Nihon shoki* main text and variants. Aston romanized these inconsistently according to nineteenth-century conventions. The Ata element again locates her in southern Kyushu. **Tier: early canonical variants, 720.**

- **木花之開耶姫 / 木花開耶姫 / 木花開耶姫命 — Konohana-no-Sakuya-hime(-no-Mikoto).** *Nihon shoki* graphs. 開耶 is phonographic rather than an independent claim that her name means “opening.” **Tier: early canonical variants, 720.**

- **豊吾田津姫 — Toyo-Atatsu-hime.** “Abundant/fruitful Princess of Ata,” in a *Nihon shoki* variant. **Tier: early canonical variant, 720.**

- **許乃波奈佐久夜比売命 — Konohana-Sakuya-hime-no-Mikoto.** The phonographic form in the *Harima fudoki*. Here she is called wife of “the Great Deity,” apparently Iwa no Ōkami rather than Ninigi. Whether this is the same goddess transported into a local genealogy, a similar local blossom deity, or a name shared by different figures cannot be established. [The Hyōgo Prefectural Museum’s translation and study](https://rekihaku.pref.hyogo.lg.jp/wp-content/themes/rekihaku/assets/pdf/harimanokunifudoki/english/chapter_4_3.pdf) appropriately preserves the problem. **Tier: securely attested regional variant, early eighth century.**

- **浅間大神 / 浅間明神 — Asama no Ōkami / Asama Myōjin**, pronounced *Asama* in early records and commonly *Sengen* in shrine names. These originally name the volcanic deity of Fuji, not Konohanasakuya-hime. Their eventual equation with her is a datable fusion. **Tier: ancient local cult title subsequently attached through early-modern syncretistic identification.**

- **富士浅間大菩薩 — Fuji Sengen Daibosatsu.** Medieval Buddhist designation of Fuji’s divinity. In different texts its manifestation could be imagined through Amida, Dainichi, Kaguya-hime, a female mountain deity, or later Konohanasakuya-hime. It is not an ancient epithet of the *Kojiki* princess. **Tier: medieval syncretistic systematization.**

- **酒解子神 — Sakato-ke-no-ko-no-kami**, “child deity who interprets/loosens sake.” This is a later cult name at Umenomiya Taisha, paired with Ōyamatsumi as 酒解神, and grounded in the *Nihon shoki* sake episode. The title itself is not found in the 720 narrative. **Tier: later cultic interpretation.**

### The “cherry-blossom goddess” problem

The old text says *kono hana*, “the flowers of the trees.” Kokugakuin’s textual commentary notes that bare *hana* became conventionally synonymous with cherry blossom only in the Heian period; it therefore cannot simply be assumed that an eighth-century hearer understood 木花 as “sakura.” The *Man’yōshū* does attest cherry blossom as an image of flourishing beauty, notably poem 13.3305, so a cherry association is possible, but not textually required. The modern claim that *sakura* derives from *Sakuya*, or that the goddess sowed the first cherry seeds from Fuji, is unproved folklore or modern invention, not early etymology. [Kokugakuin’s philological dossier](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/shinmei/konohananosakuyabime/) lays out the dispute.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### *Kojiki* — 712

Compiled by Ō no Yasumaro from material attributed to Hieda no Are and presented to Empress Genmei in 712, the *Kojiki* survives in much later manuscripts; the oldest complete witness is the Shinpukuji manuscript of 1371–72. Donald Philippi’s 1968 English translation remains useful, though subsequent Japanese critical editions refine readings.

In Book I, Ninigi encounters Kamu-Atatsu-hime at Cape Kasasa. She identifies herself as Ōyamatsumi’s daughter and gives Konohana-no-Sakuya-bime as an alternate name. Ninigi asks her father for her; Ōyamatsumi sends her together with her elder sister Iwanaga-hime and abundant gifts. Ninigi rejects Iwanaga because of her appearance. Ōyamatsumi explains that he had sent both under a solemn pledge: with Iwanaga, the heavenly descendants would endure like rock; with Sakuya, they would flourish like tree blossoms. By rejecting the first, their lives become brief like blossoms. The text explicitly applies this to the lives of emperors.

After one night’s union, Sakuya announces her pregnancy. Ninigi doubts that a child could be his after one night and suggests an earthly deity as father. She constructs a doorless eight-fathom hall, seals it with clay, vows that an illegitimate pregnancy will perish but Ninigi’s children will survive, and ignites it. She bears Hoderi, Hosuseri, and Hoori. The ordeal establishes paternity and dynastic legitimacy.

**What the *Kojiki* does not say:** that she lives on Fuji, governs all volcanoes, scatters cherry seeds, has cherry blossom as a formal emblem, or is worshipped at Sengen shrines.

### *Nihon shoki* — 720

The court chronicle preserves a main narrative and numerous “one writing says” alternatives. Aston’s translation displays the variation in [Book II](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nihongi/Book_II).

The main account calls her Kaashitsu-hime, also Kamu-Atatsu-hime or Konohana-Sakuya-hime, and makes her a child born from Ōyamatsumi’s marriage with a heavenly deity. After the one-night conception, she undergoes the burning-house ordeal and bears three sons, but their order and names differ from the *Kojiki*: Ho-no-Susori, Hiko-Hohodemi, and Ho-no-Akari, with genealogical notices making the first ancestor of the Hayato and the last ancestor of the Owari *muraji*.

Important variants include:

- Iwanaga herself, rather than Ōyamatsumi, pronounces or curses the descendants’ mortality after being rejected.
- One version universalizes the result: “visible mankind” withers like flowers, explaining human rather than specifically imperial mortality.
- The children’s names and birth order vary; one account has four children.
- One account says neither mother nor sons are injured, adds her cutting the umbilical cords with a bamboo knife, and derives the place-name Takaya from the resulting bamboo grove.
- The same variant says she divined a pure rice field called Sanada, brewed 天甜酒 (*ame no tamuzake*, “heavenly sweet sake”), prepared rice from another field, and held a feast. This is the early textual basis for her later brewing associations—not proof that she was generally worshipped as a sake goddess in 720.
- Another variant ends with her refusing to speak to Ninigi after the ordeal; he laments in song. This preserves the marital rupture rather than a modern romantic reconciliation.

### *Harima no kuni fudoki* — compiled c. 713–715

At Uruka in Shisō district, “Konohana Sakuyahime, wife of the Great Deity,” is so beautiful that the place is etiologically connected with *uruwashi*, “beautiful.” The Great Deity is conventionally identified as Iwa no Ōkami, the regional land deity centered on Iwa Shrine. This creates a genuinely conflicting marriage tradition, not an episode that can be harmonized with Ninigi. [The surviving passage and archaeological context are translated here](https://rekihaku.pref.hyogo.lg.jp/wp-content/themes/rekihaku/assets/pdf/harimanokunifudoki/english/chapter_4_3.pdf).

### *Man’yōshū* — compiled principally in the eighth century

The anthology does not narrate Sakuya’s myths. Its importance is comparative:

- poems address Fuji itself as an awesome divine mountain;
- poem 13.3305 juxtaposes azalea-colored beauty and cherry blossom flourishing.

It documents early Fuji sacrality and blossom imagery, but not their union in Konohanasakuya-hime. Treating every early Fuji poem as worship of her is anachronistic.

### *Kogo shūi* — 807

The Inbe clan’s ritual-historical memorandum is important for the broader heavenly descent and court ritual system, but it does not furnish an independent major Sakuya cycle. Its silence matters: it is not evidence for her Fuji identity.

### *Sendai kuji hongi* — probably ninth to early tenth century

The work repeats chronicle materials under spellings 木花開姫, 木花開耶姫, and 木花之開姫 in its imperial-grandchild section. Its preface’s attribution to Prince Shōtoku is rejected by modern scholarship. **Tier: learned early-medieval compilation, derivative rather than independent early attestation.**

### *Engishiki* — compiled 905–927

The *Engishiki* registers Asama Jinja of Suruga as a major shrine and preserves court ritual and *norito*. It is evidence for institutional Asama worship, not for Konohanasakuya-hime by name. No extant *Engishiki norito* addressed specifically to her has been established.

### Medieval tale and shrine literature

The *Konjaku monogatari shū* (early twelfth century) contains Fuji and mountain-ascetic materials but no securely identified independent Sakuya narrative central to her dossier. Medieval Fuji origin texts—including *Fujisan engi*, *Sengen daibosatsu engi*, *Shintōshū* materials, and later *Sengen gohonji goyuraiki*—make Fuji’s deity part of *honji-suijaku* systems. Kaguya-hime and Buddhist beings occupy roles later assigned to Sakuya. This is evidence of competition among identities, not a stable medieval consensus.

### Early-modern scholarship

Motoori Norinaga’s *Kojiki-den* (1798 completion) philologically privileged the *Kojiki* and helped canonize its names and narratives. Hirata Atsutane extended nativist cosmology and ritual interpretation in the nineteenth century. Their works shaped subsequent “Shinto mythology,” but their reconstructions are not independent ancient evidence.

Toriyama Sekien’s eighteenth-century illustrated yōkai catalogues do **not** provide a significant Konohanasakuya-hime entry. Classifying her as a *yōkai* because she appears on modern “yōkai encyclopedia” sites is a category error: early sources call her a *hime* and divine being, and shrine worship treats her as *kami*.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains by period

- **Early court myth:** beauty, flowering prosperity, fertility, one-night conception, childbirth, dynastic legitimacy, the fragility of imperial or human life. **Securely attested, 712–720.**
- **Agriculture and first-fruits:** one *Nihon shoki* variant makes her select sacred rice fields, brew sweet sake, cook rice, and feast. **Early canonical variant, 720.**
- **Brewing:** extrapolated from that variant and institutionalized at such shrines as Umenomiya. **Later cultic specialization.**
- **Fire ordeal and childbirth:** the narrative supplies later protection against fire and prayers for conception, safe delivery, and child-rearing. **Early narrative; cultic domain later documented.**
- **Fuji and volcanic pacification:** inherited when she was identified with Asama no Ōkami. **Early-modern syncretistic identification, broadly fixed in the nineteenth century.**
- **Cherry blossoms and evanescence:** increasingly specific under Heian and later cherry symbolism, Kokugaku exegesis, Sengen shrine planting and emblems, modern art, and tourism. **Later elaboration; its exclusive antiquity is disputed.**
- **Water, navigation, fishing, agriculture, weaving:** contemporary Sengen-shrine benefit lists. These are real present-day cult claims, not all early mythic attributes. [Fujisan Hongū Sengen Taisha’s official history](https://fuji-hongu.or.jp/sengen/history/index.html) lists fire protection, safe childbirth, navigation, fishing, farming, and weaving.

### Form and symbols

The chronicles specify only an extraordinarily beautiful young woman. They give her no weapon, fixed costume, color, vehicle, sacred animal, celestial body, weekday, or number. Lists assigning such occult correspondences are modern and lack support in the early corpus.

Recognizable later imagery usually presents:

- a courtly woman in layered robes;
- Fuji rising behind or beneath her;
- flowering cherry branches;
- occasionally an infant or fire imagery;
- shrine mirrors, sakaki, and ritual implements belonging to general shrine iconography rather than uniquely to her.

The earliest safely identifiable images are much later than the texts because premodern Fuji art generally depicts the sacred mountain, Buddhist icons, or an unnamed female deity rather than a securely captioned Sakuya. A decisive early-modern visual example is Katsushika Hokusai’s “Konohana-sakuya-hime” in *Fugaku hyakkei* (*One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji*), first volume, 1834, catalogued by the [Sumida Hokusai Museum](https://hokusai-museum.jp/modules/xelfinder/index.php/view/1019/220125-1%E2%89%AA%E5%8C%97%E6%96%8E%E3%81%A7%E6%97%A5%E6%9C%AC%E5%8F%B2%20%E2%80%95%E3%81%82%E3%81%AE%E4%BA%BA%E3%82%92%E3%81%A9%E3%81%86%E6%8F%8F%E3%81%84%E3%81%9F%E3%81%8B%E2%80%95%E2%89%AB%E4%BD%9C%E5%93%81%E3%83%AA%E3%82%B9%E3%83%88.pdf). Yasuda Yukihiko’s monumental 1953 painting *Konohana-no-Sakuyabime* is an important modern historicizing image; it is described in the [Japanese cultural-heritage database](https://online.bunka.go.jp/heritages/detail/409335).

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical variants

**Kojiki lineage**

- Father: Ōyamatsumi-no-kami.
- Elder sister: Iwanaga-hime.
- Husband: Ninigi-no-mikoto.
- Sons: Hoderi, Hosuseri, and Hoori/Hikohohodemi.
- Through Hoori, she becomes an ancestress of the imperial line.

**Nihon shoki main-text lineage**

She is born to Ōyamatsumi and an unnamed heavenly deity. This differs from modern genealogies that confidently name Kaya-no-hime as her mother; no such maternal identification is established in the principal early accounts.

Her sons’ names, order, and lineage functions differ between the main text and variants: Ho-no-Susori, Hiko-Hohodemi/Ho-no-Ori, Ho-no-Akari, and in one account four unnamed or differently named children. These differences reflect competing genealogical interests, including Hayato, Owari, and imperial descent.

**Harima lineage**

She is wife of the unnamed “Great Deity,” contextually Iwa no Ōkami. This local marriage is incompatible with the court genealogy unless one assumes later equation or multiple marriages, neither of which the text states.

### Every documented divine interaction

- **Ōyamatsumi — father and marriage-giver.** Sakuya identifies herself as his daughter; he sends her and Iwanaga with gifts to Ninigi and explains the sisters’ contrasting powers. *Kojiki* (712); parallel but differently arranged *Nihon shoki* version (720). **Tier: securely attested early/canonical.**

- **Ninigi — encounter, marriage, accusation, ordeal, and estrangement.** He meets her at Cape Kasasa, obtains her through her father, impregnates her in one night, doubts the paternity, and is answered by her fire ordeal. One *Nihon shoki* variant ends with her refusing to speak to him. *Kojiki* (712); *Nihon shoki* main text and variants (720). **Tier: securely attested early with canonical variants.**

- **Iwanaga-hime — elder sister and rejected co-bride.** They are jointly sent to Ninigi. Sakuya is retained; Iwanaga is rejected. Ōyamatsumi in the *Kojiki*, but Iwanaga herself in a *Nihon shoki* variant, announces the resulting mortality of the heavenly descendants or humanity. *Kojiki* (712); *Nihon shoki* (720). **Tier: securely attested early with conflicting agency.**

- **Hoderi / Ho-no-Akari / Ho-no-Susori — son born through the fire ordeal.** Name, order, and clan affiliation vary. *Kojiki* (712); *Nihon shoki* (720). **Tier: securely attested early/canonical variant.**

- **Hosuseri / Ho-no-Susumi — son born through the fire ordeal.** *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* variants (712–720). **Tier: securely attested early/canonical variant.**

- **Hoori / Hiko-Hohodemi / Ho-no-Ori — son born through the ordeal and ancestor in the imperial genealogy.** *Kojiki* (712); *Nihon shoki* (720). **Tier: securely attested early/canonical variant.**

- **Possible fourth son — born in one *Nihon shoki* variant.** The variant says she gave birth to four children; this does not become the dominant genealogy. **Tier: early canonical minority variant.**

- **Iwa no Ōkami — husband in the Harima tradition.** The *Harima fudoki* calls her the wife of “the Great Deity”; regional context identifies him with Iwa no Ōkami. *Harima no kuni fudoki*, c. 713–715. **Tier: securely attested regional variant, identity somewhat inferential.**

- **Asama no Ōkami — identity fusion, not a narrative meeting.** Separate figures in the earliest evidence; equated in early-modern discourse and modern shrine doctrine. **Tier: syncretistic identification, early seventeenth century onward.**

- **Fuji Sengen Daibosatsu — identity relation, not a narrated interaction.** Konohanasakuya-hime eventually becomes the *suijaku* or visible kami form within some Fuji Buddhist systems, though earlier systems use other figures. **Tier: medieval/early-modern learned syncretism.**

- **Kaguya-hime — competing or overlapping Fuji-goddess identity.** Medieval and early-modern Fuji origin literature associates the bamboo-cutter princess with Sengen/Fuji; this is not an encounter between two independently acting goddesses. **Tier: medieval syncretistic equation and competition.**

- **Amaterasu — no direct meeting with Sakuya is recorded in the principal early narratives.** Amaterasu belongs to Ninigi’s heavenly genealogy, making Sakuya her descendant’s wife, but the two do not converse or share an episode. **Absence of evidence.**

- **Toyotama-hime — no documented direct interaction.** She marries Sakuya’s son Hoori in the next generation and parallels Sakuya as a non-heavenly bride and mother, but the texts do not bring them together. **Absence of evidence.**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The blossom and rock brides

**Early attested form:** Ninigi seeks the beautiful Sakuya. Ōyamatsumi sends both her and Iwanaga; Ninigi returns Iwanaga. The father explains that Iwanaga would make the heavenly descendants endure like stone, while Sakuya would make them flourish like blossoms. Because Ninigi chose only the blossom, imperial life is brilliant but short.

**Nihon shoki variants:** Iwanaga curses or declares the consequence herself; one version transfers mortality from emperors to all visible human beings.

**Development:** Scholars compare this with “banana-type” mortality myths distributed through Indonesia and New Guinea, in which humans choose a perishable object rather than stone and consequently die. This is a comparative reconstruction, not proof of a single traceable borrowing. Another scholarly proposal is that an originally general mortality tale centered on Stone Princess was incorporated into royal genealogy and paired with Blossom Princess to explain both imperial mortality and flourishing. Kokugakuin summarizes both theories in its [critical commentary](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/shinmei/konohananosakuyabime/).

**Function:** dynastic ideology. The heavenly line acquires alliance with the mountain/earth deity while its mortal embodiment is explained. The story also places fault variously on Ninigi’s aesthetic choice, Ōyamatsumi’s failed pledge, or Iwanaga’s retaliatory words.

### One-night conception and birth in the burning house

Sakuya conceives after a single night. Ninigi doubts paternity, specifically suspecting a *kunitsukami*, an earthly deity. She constructs a doorless chamber, seals it, and vows that false issue will perish but the heavenly descendant’s children will survive. She lights the structure and gives birth as the fire begins, peaks, and recedes.

The episode is simultaneously:

- a female ordeal answering sexual accusation;
- a proof of her sons’ heavenly paternity;
- a legitimation of dynastic descent;
- an extraordinary-birth tale in which fire stages divine invulnerability;
- possibly a mythic transformation of childbirth customs involving a special birth hut, fire, or the destruction of a polluted parturition structure.

The last explanation is scholarly inference; no surviving eighth-century ritual manual states that women reenacted Sakuya’s ordeal.

Modern summaries often say she and “the baby” emerged unharmed. The early texts instead give multiple sons and correlate them with stages of flame. One version explicitly says mother and all children were uninjured. Another preserves lasting anger: Sakuya refuses to speak to Ninigi, who sings a lament. Reconciliation is a modern narrative smoothing.

### Sacred rice and heavenly sweet sake

A *Nihon shoki* variant appends that Kamu-Atakaashitsu-hime divined the site of the Sanada rice field, used its rice to brew *ame no tamuzake*, prepared boiled rice from Nunada, and gave a feast. Aston connected this with first-fruits ritual. The passage makes Sakuya an agricultural and ritual-feasting agent and is the sound basis for later brewing cults. It does not say she chewed the rice, fed sake to newborns instead of breast milk, or invented modern *nihonshu*; those are later expansions.

### Harima’s beautiful wife

In the *Harima fudoki*, her beauty explains the place-name Uruka/Uruwa and she is wife of Iwa no Ōkami. The local story contains no Ninigi, rejected rock sister, paternity accusation, fire, Fuji, or sake. Its very brevity and incompatible husband suggest either a locally independent goddess, appropriation of a prestigious name, or a shared oral complex now unrecoverable.

### Fuji narratives

Fuji’s earliest record presents the mountain itself or Asama deity as active and dangerous. Medieval narratives Buddhistize the mountain: ascetics encounter manifestations, summit paradises, Dainichi or Amida realms, Sengen Daibosatsu, or Kaguya-hime. Only in the early modern period is Sakuya securely installed as the mountain’s principal named female form. Her safe delivery amid flames provided an elegant theological explanation for a benevolent goddess governing a destructive volcano. The [Japan Tourism Agency’s historical exhibit](https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/en/R2-00078.html) dates the Fuji identification to the early seventeenth century and documents its later visual dominance.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### The Asama cult before Sakuya identification

The *Shoku Nihongi* records a Fuji eruption in 781. The *Nihon Montoku tennō jitsuroku* records Asama’s elevation to junior third rank in 853. During the Jōgan eruption of 864–866, the *Nihon sandai jitsuroku* reports divination that Asama Myōjin’s anger resulted from ritual neglect. The court ordered offerings and, in 865, the establishment of official shrines and priests in Kai. The accounts are unusually valuable because they show cult as disaster response, supported by court orders, divination, priestly appointments, and geographical administration. [A Yamanashi volcanic-history study quotes the relevant chronicle entries](https://www.mfri.pref.yamanashi.jp/fujikazan/original/P119-136.pdf).

The *Engishiki* subsequently lists Asama Jinja as a *myōjin taisha*, a major official shrine. None of these records calls its deity Konohanasakuya-hime.

### Fujisan Hongū Sengen Taisha

The shrine at Fujinomiya claims legendary foundations under the ancient emperors Kōrei, Suinin, or Keikō and transfer or rebuilding by Sakanoue no Tamuramaro. These are shrine traditions, not contemporary foundation records. Its documentable history begins with early-Heian notices of Asama worship and divine rank. It became Suruga’s first shrine and received warrior patronage. The existing two-storey main sanctuary is associated with Tokugawa Ieyasu’s early-seventeenth-century patronage.

The shrine now calls itself head shrine of more than 1,300 Sengen shrines and identifies Konohanasakuya-hime as Asama no Ōkami’s manifestation. Its ritual calendar includes:

- *yabusame* mounted archery in May, connected by shrine tradition with Minamoto no Yoritomo;
- mountain-opening observances in July;
- annual great festivals in November;
- prayers and amulets for childbirth, children, fire protection, navigation, agriculture, fishing, and weaving.

The number “1,300” is an institutional estimate, not an archaeological census of ancient Sakuya shrines.

### Fuji asceticism and pilgrimage

From the late Heian and medieval periods, reduced volcanic activity allowed ascetic ascent. Matsudai Shōnin is associated with a twelfth-century summit establishment; Raison developed Murayama ascetic practice in the fourteenth century. Murayama Shugendō organized routes, lodgings, ritual sites, and the mountain as a mandalized Buddhist landscape. [Kokugakuin’s Fuji-cult entry](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9024) distinguishes these phases.

Kakugyō Tōbutsu Kū (1541–1646) became the legendary founder of early-modern Fuji devotional movements. Fuji confraternities (*Fuji-kō*) subsequently expanded in Edo. Lay leaders and *oshi* provided lodging, ritual guidance, amulets, and recruitment; this was also an economy of fees, offerings, lodging, and route access. Miniature Fuji mounds allowed vicarious ascent.

Women were long excluded from the summit, despite the mountain’s increasingly female deity and childbirth cult. Women participated through foothill worship, confraternities, miniature Fuji, and womb-cave rites; general access to summit routes followed Meiji-era changes.

### Womb caves and childbirth

Lava-tree molds at Funatsu and Yoshida were interpreted as *otainai*, the mountain’s womb. Fuji pilgrims crawled through them for purification and rebirth; women prayed for safe delivery. The Yoshida cave was reportedly found and cultically developed in 1892 and now enshrines Konohanasakuya-hime. [The official regional description](https://fujiguide.pref.yamanashi.jp/en/navi/53/) documents continuing rebirth and childbirth practice. **Tier: early-modern/modern folk and pilgrimage elaboration, not ancient mythic geography.**

### Yoshida Fire Festival

Held on August 26–27 at Kitaguchi Hongū Fuji Sengen Jinja and Suwa Jinja, the festival closes the climbing season. Its history is contested. Documentary evidence shows it was originally strongly associated with the local Suwa shrine; one legend connects its fires with Takeminakata’s battle. Only later did a Sengen explanation make the torches a reenactment of Sakuya’s burning parturition house. The [Japan Traditional Culture Promotion Organization](https://www.jtco.or.jp/bunkakan/?act=detail&c=20&id=76&p=0) explicitly preserves both accounts. Calling the festival an unchanged prehistoric Sakuya rite is therefore untenable.

### Offerings and priesthood

Standard modern offerings are rice, sake, salt, water, branches, monetary gifts, votive plaques, amulets, and festival service. Dedicated sake offerings have an evident narrative rationale. Her shrines employ the ordinary modern shrine offices—chief priest, priests, shrine maidens, parishioner and supporter organizations—rather than a unique ancient Sakuya priesthood. The Jōgan records attest *hafuri* and ritual officials for Asama, but not a hereditary priesthood explicitly serving Sakuya.

No reliable evidence was found for human sacrifice, temple prostitution, or a distinct sacrificial priesthood in her cult. Stories of entering fire concern the goddess’s voluntary ordeal, not worshippers burning victims. Claims to the contrary require a specific dated source.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before 712: unrecoverable antecedents

Ata place associations, the mountain-deity father, bride exchange, mortality myth, birth-hut imagery, and the Harima local variant probably draw on older oral traditions. Their date and original configuration cannot be securely established. Archaeological evidence for mountain or blossom rites cannot be assigned to this named goddess without inscriptions.

### 712–720: textual formation

The *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* install her in the imperial descent cycle. The texts already disagree over her name, maternity, the agent of the mortality decree, its human or imperial scope, her sons, and the aftermath of the fire. There never was one uniform early version.

### Eighth century: regional plurality

The *Harima fudoki* supplies a beautiful goddess of the same or nearly the same name married to Iwa no Ōkami. The *Man’yōshū* celebrates Fuji as divine but does not call it Sakuya. Thus named Sakuya tradition and Fuji worship coexist without documented equation.

### Ninth–tenth centuries: official Asama cult

Eruptions generate court ranks, divination, offerings, shrines, and priests for Asama. The deity is the dangerous volcanic mountain power. The *Engishiki* institutionalizes the shrine. Sakuya remains absent from this documentation.

### Eleventh–sixteenth centuries: Buddhist Fuji

Fuji becomes an ascetic and pilgrimage landscape. Buddhist cosmology, Shugendō, *honji-suijaku*, summit cults, Amida and Dainichi imagery, Sengen Daibosatsu, and Kaguya-hime traditions overlap. A single “Shinto goddess of Fuji” does not adequately describe this period. Geoffrey W. Earhart’s historical synthesis, *Mount Fuji: Icon of Japan*, stresses the multiple ascetic routes and institutions rather than a timeless unitary cult ([publisher/database record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv6wgfmg)).

### Early seventeenth century: identification with Fuji

The first secure evidence for identifying the Fuji deity with Konohanasakuya-hime belongs here. Hayashi Razan’s 1607 *Heishin kikō* is often credited with a learned genealogical argument: if Mishima/Ōyamatsumi is related to Fuji and Sakuya is his daughter, she is a suitable identity for Fuji’s deity. The exact causal priority remains debated, but contemporary material confirms the equation in this period. It coincides with systematized Fuji confraternities and an increasingly feminized mountain deity.

### Edo period: popular expansion

Fuji-kō spread widely, supported by *oshi*, lodgings, amulets, confraternity networks, and miniature Fuji. Sakuya’s birth story supported fire protection and childbirth prayer. Art made her visible: Hokusai’s 1834 figure joins elegant court femininity, blossoms, and Fuji. Yet Buddhist amulets remained common. A 1788 *goō* might display Amida above Fuji; by 1860 a female kami could replace him. That comparison materially demonstrates the shift rather than merely asserting it.

### 1868–1945: separation and State Shinto

The Meiji government ordered the separation of kami and buddhas. Buddhist images, clerics, terminology, and institutions were removed or reorganized at Fuji sites, sometimes violently or destructively. Konohanasakuya-hime offered a chronicle-based, imperially genealogical identity suited to the new shrine system. By the nineteenth century older female statues were retrospectively labeled as her, and the 1868 settlement helped make the identification nearly universal.

In 1871 hereditary priestly succession was formally abolished and shrines were ranked as state ritual institutions. The broader administrative history is summarized in [Kokugakuin’s modern Shinto chronology](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/id%3D9071). Sengen shrines therefore participated in an imperial system in which Sakuya’s marriage to Ninigi and ancestry of the emperors carried political significance. This does not mean every local worshipper approached her chiefly as a nationalist emblem; childbirth, fire, water, agriculture, and pilgrimage remained practical concerns.

### 1945–present

The Allied Shinto Directive ended state sponsorship. Sengen shrines continued as religious juridical bodies, most within or related to the postwar shrine-association system. Worship remains active; Fuji pilgrimage, fire festivals, *yabusame*, childbirth prayer, amulets, sake offerings, and tourist interpretation continue. Fuji’s inscription as a UNESCO World Heritage cultural site in 2013 further encouraged historical presentation of its pilgrimage routes and religious monuments.

There is no defensible count of individuals who worship Sakuya exclusively. Shrine visitation does not translate straightforwardly into denominational membership.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### National and aesthetic symbolism

Modern culture increasingly makes Sakuya a personification of cherry blossom, feminine beauty, motherhood, Japanese impermanence, and Fuji. These meanings draw on genuine early metaphors but combine them in a form largely stabilized after the seventeenth century and promoted by Meiji historical painting, shrine pedagogy, tourism, and school mythology.

Yasuda Yukihiko’s 1953 painting gives her an idealized classical form surrounded by cherry symbolism. Contemporary shrine and tourism art commonly depicts a serene woman before Fuji, suppressing the anger, accusation, destructive fire, and marital estrangement preserved in early texts.

### Popular media

- Capcom’s *Ōkami* (2006) features Sakuya as a tree spirit inhabiting the sacred Konohana tree who revives Amaterasu. The [official game manual](https://static.capcom.com/okami/manuals/PS2_Okami_Manual.pdf) identifies her as a “wood sprite.” This is a creative derivative, not a retelling of the chronicle goddess’s marriage or fire ordeal.
- *Persona 4* (2008) uses Konohana Sakuya as Yukiko Amagi’s Persona, adapting blossom, fire, and feminine selfhood into psychological game symbolism. **Tier: modern commercial reinvention.**
- Later *Megami Tensei*, mobile games, manga, anime, and fantasy fiction employ the name for fire, healing, flower, or Fuji characters with little obligation to the textual corpus.
- Online claims that Touhou’s Sakuya Izayoi secretly “is” Konohanasakuya-hime are fan speculation based primarily on the shared name *Sakuya*; no canonical identification has been established.
- “Goddess of every volcano,” “avatar of all Japanese life,” and fixed occult lists of her crystals, tarot cards, planets, colors, or moon phases are modern generalizations unless tied to a dated organization or author.

### Neopagan and occult use

English-language “deity work,” eclectic pagan, and New Age communities now invoke her as a goddess of cherry blossoms, volcanoes, beauty, motherhood, transformation, fire, and personal rebirth. Such practice is documented as contemporary reception but is neither a continuation of an ancient initiatory cult nor evidence of a traditional occult correspondence system. No significant role for her was found in Renaissance Hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, classical Western grimoires, or pre-twentieth-century European occultism.

### Politics and extremist appropriation

Her imperial-genealogical place made her available to State Shinto ideology, especially after shrine identities were regularized. No specific modern extremist movement centered principally on her was established in the sources reviewed. General nationalist invocations of divine imperial descent should not be misrepresented as a distinct Sakuya movement.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was she always Fuji’s goddess?

No. This is the dossier’s central historical finding:

1. The 712–720 narratives know Sakuya but do not connect her with Fuji.
2. eighth-century poetry knows Fuji as divine but does not name Sakuya.
3. ninth-century state records know Asama Myōjin but do not identify that power with Sakuya.
4. medieval Fuji religion uses Buddhist and other female identities.
5. explicit Sakuya-Fuji identification is secure from the early seventeenth century.
6. the equation becomes visually dominant by the nineteenth century and institutionally fixed after 1868.

The [Japan Tourism Agency’s exhibit documentation](https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/en/R2-00078.html) is unusually explicit on this sequence.

### What does “tree-flower” mean?

The older interpretation makes 木花 specifically cherry blossom and reads Sakuya as a vegetation or rice-field spirit whose blossom foretells crops. The counterargument is linguistic: in the Nara period, “flower” was not yet automatically synonymous with cherry blossom. The safest conclusion is that the name expresses blooming/flourishing tree-flowers; cherry became the dominant species in later reception.

### Mortality-myth origins

Comparativists locate the rock-versus-perishable choice within Southeast Asian “banana-type” mortality tales. Japanese textual scholars also propose that a general human mortality tale centered on Iwanaga was reshaped into an imperial genealogy by adding Sakuya’s flourishing fertility. These are plausible reconstructions, not directly witnessed stages.

### Fire-birth interpretation

Proposals include:

- ordeal proving sexual fidelity;
- dynastic paternity test;
- exceptional divine birth;
- ritualized destruction of a birth hut;
- fire used at childbirth;
- volcanic symbolism.

Only the first three are explicit or immediate in the early text. Reading it as a Fuji-volcano myth projects the later fusion backward.

### “Shinto” as a historical category

Kuroda Toshio’s 1981 “Shinto in the History of Japanese Religion” challenged the idea of an autonomous, unchanged indigenous religion extending from prehistory to modernity. The bibliographic record is available through [CiNii](https://cir.nii.ac.jp/crid/1360298341761191552). John Breen and Mark Teeuwen refine that critique: shrines, myths, and kami rites certainly existed, but “Shinto” as one coherent religion was repeatedly constructed and reorganized. Their [*A New History of Shinto*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/9781444317190) is especially pertinent because Sakuya’s history crosses court myth, volcanic propitiation, Buddhism, pilgrimage, Kokugaku, state administration, and modern tourism.

Accordingly, calling the 864 Asama rites “worship of the Shinto goddess Konohanasakuya-hime” commits two anachronisms: it assumes both a settled transhistorical Shinto system and an identity not documented then.

### Primary-evidence condition

- The *Kojiki* survives only in manuscripts more than six centuries later than composition.
- The *Nihon shoki* has a complex manuscript and commentary tradition; its variant quotations are deliberately preserved rather than accidental noise.
- The *Harima fudoki* is incomplete and transmitted through later copies and quotations.
- Shrine foundation legends commonly precede their first contemporary documents by centuries.
- Medieval Fuji texts exist in variant local recensions and require manuscript-specific dating.
- Images of an unnamed female Fuji deity cannot automatically be identified as Sakuya without inscription, provenance, or a linked textual tradition.

### Discredited or unestablished claims still circulating

- **“The *Nihon shoki* calls her goddess of Mount Fuji.”** False; it does not.
- **“She has always been the deity of Sengen shrines.”** False; early records name Asama without equating the two.
- **“Her name literally means cherry blossom.”** Too strong; “tree-flower” is secure, cherry is disputed and later dominant.
- **“Sakura derives from Sakuya.”** Popular etymology, not established linguistics.
- **“She invented sake.”** The text says she brewed ritual sweet sake in one variant; it does not make a technological priority claim.
- **“She breast-fed the princes with sake.”** Later legend, absent from the 720 passage.
- **“The Yoshida Fire Festival has always reenacted her birth.”** Historically misleading; the Suwa cult has an older competing claim.
- **“Kaya-no-hime is securely her mother.”** Not established in the principal early texts.
- **“She destroyed Yatsugatake for being taller than Fuji.”** A regional mountain-comparison legend is sometimes attached to a female Fuji deity, but attribution specifically to chronicle Sakuya is late and unstable.
- **“Ancient worship prescribed crystals, tarot correspondences, or modern spell work.”** Modern invention.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before 712:** Oral antecedents of the Ata bride, stone-and-flower mortality tale, and fire-birth episode are probable but undatable. No inscription securely names her.
- **712:** *Kojiki* completed; earliest secure named attestation as Kamu-Atatsu-hime / Konohana-no-Sakuya-bime.
- **c. 713–715:** *Harima fudoki* records Konohana Sakuyahime as the beautiful wife of a regional Great Deity, probably Iwa no Ōkami.
- **720:** *Nihon shoki* completed; preserves multiple names, genealogies, mortality formulas, birth orders, the bamboo episode, marital estrangement, and the sacred-rice/sweet-sake variant.
- **Eighth century:** *Man’yōshū* treats Fuji as divine and cherry blossom as flourishing imagery, but does not identify Fuji with Sakuya.
- **781:** Historical eruption of Fuji recorded in the *Shoku Nihongi*.
- **807:** *Kogo shūi* composed; supplies broader ritual and descent context but no independent major Sakuya cycle.
- **853:** Asama deity receives junior third rank according to the *Nihon Montoku tennō jitsuroku*.
- **864–866:** Jōgan eruption. Divination blames insufficient worship of Asama; the court orders propitiation.
- **865:** *Nihon sandai jitsuroku* records official Asama shrines and priestly appointments in Kai.
- **905–927:** *Engishiki* compiled; Asama Jinja listed as a major shrine. No equation with Sakuya is stated.
- **Ninth–tenth centuries:** *Sendai kuji hongi* reworks chronicle genealogy and name forms.
- **Late eleventh–twelfth centuries:** Fuji becomes increasingly accessible to Buddhist mountain ascetics; Matsudai Shōnin is linked with summit practice.
- **1317–1319:** Raison’s Fuji asceticism helps consolidate Murayama practice.
- **Medieval period:** Fuji Sengen Daibosatsu, Buddhist *honji*, Kaguya-hime, and mountain-mandala identities circulate.
- **1541–1646:** Life of Kakugyō Tōbutsu Kū, retrospectively central to Fuji-kō history.
- **Early seventeenth century:** Secure emergence of the equation between Konohanasakuya-hime and Fuji/Asama; Hayashi Razan’s 1607 travel writing is often cited in its learned articulation.
- **Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries:** Fuji confraternities expand; *oshi* networks, lodgings, pilgrimage economies, womb-cave rites, and miniature Fuji develop.
- **1788:** A surviving Fuji amulet still depicts Amida and attendants above the mountain.
- **1798:** Motoori Norinaga completes *Kojiki-den*, influential in philologically recentering the *Kojiki* mythology.
- **1834:** Hokusai publishes the identifiable Sakuya image in *One Hundred Views of Mount Fuji*.
- **1860:** A Fuji amulet shows a female kami where earlier examples showed Amida, documenting the changing visual theology.
- **1868:** Government-mandated separation of kami and buddhas restructures Fuji sites and helps fix Sakuya as the orthodox shrine identity of Asama.
- **1871:** Modern ranked-shrine system established; hereditary priestly office formally abolished; shrines declared state ritual institutions.
- **1892:** Yoshida lava-tree-mold “womb” discovered/developed as a cult site; Sakuya becomes its childbirth deity.
- **1909:** Documentary writing on the Yoshida Fire Festival still preserves a Takeminakata/Suwa origin alongside the now-dominant Sakuya explanation.
- **1939–1940:** Religious Bodies Law promulgated and enforced within the wider State Shinto order.
- **1945–1946:** Shinto Directive dismantles state sponsorship; postwar shrine organizations succeed the state system.
- **1953:** Yasuda Yukihiko exhibits *Konohana-no-Sakuyabime*, a major modern historical painting.
- **2006:** Capcom’s *Ōkami* globally popularizes a new tree-spirit character named Sakuya.
- **2013:** Fuji inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage cultural property, strengthening public interpretation of its shrine and pilgrimage traditions.
- **Present:** Konohanasakuya-hime remains principal deity at many Sengen shrines and recipient of prayers concerning childbirth, children, fire, agriculture, water, and safe travel; eclectic global pagan and commercial versions coexist with institutional shrine worship.

## 11. Sources and Assessment

The most important primary witnesses are the *Kojiki* (712), *Nihon shoki* (720), *Harima no kuni fudoki* (early eighth century), *Man’yōshū*, *Nihon Montoku tennō jitsuroku*, *Nihon sandai jitsuroku*, *Engishiki*, medieval Fuji origin texts, shrine ritual records, pilgrimage amulets, and securely captioned art. Philippi’s *Kojiki* and Aston’s *Nihongi* remain standard English points of entry, but Japanese critical editions and the Kokugakuin textual database are preferable for exact graph forms and variant control.

The result of the dedicated contested-material search is negative in several areas: no documented Konohanasakuya cult of human sacrifice, temple prostitution, classical Western occultism, or deity-specific modern extremist organization was established. Suppression is documented primarily through the Meiji destruction and removal of Buddhist elements at Fuji sites, not suppression of Sakuya herself. The historically secure transformations are instead the court construction of an imperial ancestress, the independent volcanic Asama cult, medieval Buddhist Fuji, the early-modern fusion of Sakuya with Asama, Meiji purification and state administration, postwar institutional continuity, and modern aesthetic and commercial reinvention.
