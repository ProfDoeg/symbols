# Ebisu: Research Dossier

**Class:** *kami* 神, specifically a Japanese livelihood, fishery, market, commerce, household, and good-fortune deity. In particular local traditions Ebisu may also be a visiting sea-deity, agricultural deity, dangerous or epidemic-bearing outsider, divine drift-object, deified drowned body, whale, shark, stone, or float. “Ebisu” is not a single identity already fixed in Japan’s earliest mythological texts; the historical figure is a composite produced through the medieval convergence of coastal religion, market cults, Hiruko, Kotoshironushi, Nishinomiya traditions, and Buddhist–kami systems.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest presently identifiable documentary notice of **Ebisu as a deity** is a record that Ebisu was invoked at Tōdaiji in **1163** as a market guardian. The earliest unambiguous notice of an **Ebisu shrine at the Hirota–Nishinomiya complex** is the *Nakasuke-ō ki* 仲資王記 entry for **1194**, naming a subordinate “Ebisu shrine” (*massha Ebisu-no-miya* 末社戎宮); a 1204 entry calls it “Ebisu Saburō Shrine” 戎三郎社. Hiruko, later identified with Ebisu, is much earlier—*Kojiki*, 712, and *Nihon shoki*, 720—but neither chronicle calls Hiruko “Ebisu,” says that he survived, or brings him to Nishinomiya. The equation **Hiruko = Ebisu** is a medieval development, securely visible by the fourteenth century and probably forming between the mid-Kamakura and early Nanbokuchō periods. [Kokugakuin’s Ebisu-cult survey](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9023), [Kokugakuin’s Nishinomiya Shrine database](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/jinjya/nishinomiyajinja/), [historical notices summarized by Kotobank](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E8%A5%BF%E5%AE%AE-591709)

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** contemporary or near-contemporary ancient/medieval texts, dated objects, inscriptions, or records.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** a later account that became authoritative in a shrine, school, or widely received narrative.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** locally attested but not universal.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** interpretation by commentators, priests, theologians, or antiquarians.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** historical equation or fusion of originally distinguishable figures.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore:** oral custom, ethnographic report, local legend, or customary explanation.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference or model proposed from the evidence.
- **[MI] Modern invention or reinvention.**

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

**[EA/MSR]** Ebisu developed from an originally unstable category associated with the sea and the exterior. Medieval evidence places him at markets and at Nishinomiya; later ethnography finds “Ebisu” in beings and objects that arrive unexpectedly from the sea. The common denominator is not originally “wealth” in the abstract but an outside power that can bring a catch, food, danger, disease, or profit. The smiling merchant’s god is the favorable, domesticated form of that power.

**[MA]** By late medieval times Ebisu was paired especially with Daikoku and incorporated into the Seven Lucky Gods. In the Edo period his fishery, household, and market functions consolidated into the familiar god of honest labor, daily food, and commercial prosperity. The standard group eventually became Ebisu, Daikokuten, Bishamonten, Benzaiten, Hotei, Fukurokuju, and Jurōjin, but early combinations varied. A court diary records a “dance of the Seven Lucky Gods” in 1448; the familiar membership is also found in the late-medieval kyōgen repertoire. [JapanKnowledge on the formation of the group](https://japanknowledge.com/introduction/keyword.html?i=1670), [German-language historical summary](https://religion-in-japan.univie.ac.at/Kamigraphie/Sieben_Gl%C3%BCcksg%C3%B6tter)

### Written forms and pronunciations

- **えびす / エビス — Ebisu.** Modern standard spelling and romanization.
- **ゑびす / ヱビス — Webisu/Yebisu.** Historical kana spelling. “Yebisu” survives conspicuously in the beer trademark, though modern pronunciation is *Ebisu*.
- **夷, 戎, 胡, 蛮.** Characters historically denoting outsiders, eastern or northern peoples, foreigners, or “barbarians”; applied phonetically or semantically to *ebisu*.
- **恵比須, 恵比寿, 恵美須, 恵美酒.** Auspicious ateji, respectively using characters suggesting blessing, comparison, longevity, beauty, or—in 恵美酒—“fine sake.” These spellings do not establish separate ancient gods.
- **蛭子.** Literally “leech child,” normally read *Hiruko* in the classical myths but conventionally read *Ebisu* in shrine names and later cult.
- **戎三郎 / 夷三郎 — Ebisu Saburō.** “Ebisu, third son”; documented in the medieval Nishinomiya complex in 1204. “Third” has generated several learned explanations, but no single ancient genealogy securely accounts for it.
- **西宮大神 / 西宮大明神 — Nishinomiya Ōkami / Nishinomiya Daimyōjin.** Shrine-centered titles.
- **蛭児大神 — Hiruko Ōkami.** Nishinomiya’s present designation of its principal Ebisu.
- **事代主神 / 八重事代主神 — Kotoshironushi-no-kami / Yae-Kotoshironushi-no-kami.** A distinct mythological deity identified with Ebisu at many shrines, particularly the Kotoshironushi line represented by Imamiya Ebisu and Miho-related worship.
- **おえべっさん / えべっさん — O-Ebessan/Ebessan.** Affectionate Kansai vernacular.
- **荒戎 / 沖恵美酒大神 — Ara-Ebisu / Oki-no-Ebisu Ōkami.** The vigorous or *aramitama* form worshipped at Nishinomiya’s subsidiary Oki-no-Ebisu Shrine; its earliest explicit record is “Nishinomiya Ara-Ebisu Shrine shook” in the 1419 *Go-Sukō-in gyoki*. [Nishinomiya’s shrine record](https://nishinomiya-ebisu.com/guide/okinoebisu/)

### Etymology

**[MSR: leading interpretation]** The divine name probably belongs to the same semantic field as *ebisu/emishi*: an outsider beyond the ordered Yamato world. This suits a god arriving from an external realm with unpredictable gifts. It does not prove that the god was literally derived from any one historical ethnic population.

**[MSR: maritime model]** Coastal evidence suggests that *ebisu* became a designation for whales, sharks, dolphins, corpses, stones, floats, and other potent arrivals from the sea. Scholars including Nelly Naumann therefore reconstruct the deity from maritime “visitor” and fish-calling beliefs. [Kokugakuin](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9023), [Naumann’s 1974 article](https://asianethnology.org/article/148185-whale-and-fish-cult-in-japan-a-basic-feature-of-ebisu-worship)

**[LS/SI]** Medieval exegetes joined this outsider-name to Hiruko because Hiruko had been expelled in a boat and thus could be imagined as the drifting god who later returned from the sea. This is a persuasive historical mechanism for the fusion, not a statement made by the *Kojiki*.

### Classification shifts

1. **712–720:** Hiruko is an unsuccessful or anomalous offspring, excluded from the recognized children of Izanagi and Izanami; he is not yet Ebisu.
2. **Twelfth–thirteenth centuries:** Ebisu is a market and coastal guardian and a shrine deity at Nishinomiya.
3. **Kamakura–Nanbokuchō:** Hiruko and Ebisu increasingly merge.
4. **Late medieval period:** Ebisu becomes a major *fukujin*, is paired with Daikoku, and enters the Seven Lucky Gods.
5. **Edo period:** household, occupational, agricultural, fishery, and commercial forms proliferate.
6. **Meiji onward:** shrine authorities classify local composites within a purified “Shinto” genealogy, most often as Hiruko or Kotoshironushi.
7. **Modern popular culture:** “god of luck/business” becomes the dominant shorthand, often suppressing the older dangerous, disabled, maritime, and death-related aspects.

The statement that Ebisu is “the only one of the Seven Lucky Gods born in Japan” is broadly correct **only as a description of the standard late-medieval group**: the others derive through Indian Buddhist or Chinese Buddhist/Daoist traditions, whereas Ebisu’s documented formation occurred in Japan. It should not be converted into a claim that an immutable seven-member group existed in antiquity.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The ancient texts: precursors, not yet Ebisu

#### *Kojiki* 古事記, 712

**Language/support:** Classical Japanese expressed predominantly through Chinese characters; compiled by Ō no Yasumaro from traditions attributed to Hieda no Are. No autograph manuscript survives; the oldest complete surviving manuscript tradition is medieval.

**[EA]** Izanami speaks first in the marriage exchange. The couple then produces Hiruko, judged among the “not good” children, places him in a reed boat, and floats him away. The text does not say that Hiruko lacks bones; it describes him as an anomalous child and, in the received interpretation, connects the defective birth with the woman speaking first. It does not say he became Ebisu, reached Nishinomiya, learned to walk, or became a god of fishing or commerce. [Kokugakuin’s Hiruko text and variants](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/shinmei/hiruko/), [Chamberlain translation](https://sacred-texts.com/shi/kojiki.htm)

**[EA]** Elsewhere the *Kojiki* presents Kotoshironushi, son of Ōkuninushi, fishing at Miho when summoned during the transfer of the land. Kotoshironushi accepts the heavenly demand and withdraws. Again, the text does not call him Ebisu. Donald L. Philippi’s 1968 translation remains important, though its romanization and some philological choices should be checked against modern Japanese critical editions.

#### *Nihon shoki* 日本書紀, 720

**Language/support:** Literary Chinese; multiple variant accounts are deliberately preserved.

**[EA]** Hiruko appears in several variants. In one strand he is an early malformed offspring in the land-birth sequence; in another he follows the sun and moon among the divine children. The main account says that at three he still could not stand, so he was placed in the heavenly rock-camphor boat and set adrift. The differing positions—failed land/child in one strand, sibling of great celestial deities in another—must not be harmonized.

**[EA]** The *Nihon shoki* version of the land-transfer episode gives Kotoshironushi his own relations to Ōkuninushi and the heavenly envoys. W. G. Aston’s 1896 translation is historically important but should be read alongside current Japanese editions.

#### *Kogo shūi* 古語拾遺, 807

**[EA: negative finding]** Inbe no Hironari’s ritual-political compilation retells early divine traditions to advance Inbe claims at court, but it does not supply the later Ebisu career. Its importance lies in showing that an independent Ebisu identification had still not entered this classical mythographic corpus. The work was presented in 807. [Cultural Heritage Online](https://online.bunka.go.jp/db/heritages/detail/197226)

#### *Fudoki*, *Man’yōshū*, and the early shrine liturgies

**[EA: negative finding]** The surviving provincial *Fudoki* and the eighth-century *Man’yōshū* preserve rich local, marine, and divine material, but they do not securely document the fully formed deity Ebisu. References to boats, fishing, visiting gods, or the sea are comparative context, not direct Ebisu attestations.

**[EA: negative finding]** The twenty-seven *norito* in Book 8 of the *Engishiki*—compiled 927, promulgated 967—do not give an Ebisu myth or liturgy. Nor does the *Engishiki* shrine register securely list the later Nishinomiya Ebisu establishment as such. This absence is significant: claims that Ebisu was already a named state-cult god in the classical *Engishiki* system are unsupported. [Kokugakuin’s account of the *Engishiki norito*](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=8632)

### Documentary emergence

- **1119 [EA]:** Minamoto no Morotoki’s *Chōshūki* records an offering at “Nishinomiya Shrine.” This probably belongs to the Hirota shrine complex; it does not by itself prove that the named recipient was Ebisu.
- **1142 [EA]:** Fujiwara Yorinaga’s *Taiki* mentions a “Nishinomiya treasure hall,” again within the broader Hirota setting.
- **1163 [EA]:** Ebisu was invoked at Tōdaiji as guardian of a market—the earliest secure cult notice currently identified in the consulted evidence.
- **1194 [EA]:** *Nakasuke-ō ki* explicitly records Hirota’s subordinate “Ebisu Shrine.”
- **1204 [EA]:** The same diary tradition names “Ebisu Saburō Shrine.”
- **1253 [EA]:** Ebisu was invoked as a market guardian at Tsurugaoka Hachimangū.
- **Fourteenth century [LC/SI]:** Kitabatake Chikafusa’s *Jinnō shōtōki* states that Hiruko is Nishinomiya Daimyōjin/Ebisu, furnishing explicit learned evidence for the fusion.
- **1419 [EA]:** *Go-Sukō-in gyoki* records the disturbance of Nishinomiya’s Ara-Ebisu shrine.
- **1448 [EA]:** Prince Fushimi Sadafusa’s diary records a Seven Lucky Gods dance.

### Other named corpora

**[EA/negative finding]** *Konjaku monogatari-shū*—compiled about 1120—contains numerous Buddhist, miraculous, and local narratives but is not secure evidence for the mature Ebisu dossier unless a specific story and manuscript reading are identified. General claims that the collection already tells the Hiruko-to-Ebisu story should therefore be rejected pending a precise tale number.

**[EA/negative finding]** Toriyama Sekien’s four yōkai catalogues—*Gazu hyakki yagyō* (1776), *Konjaku gazu zoku hyakki* (1779), *Konjaku hyakki shūi* (1781), and *Gazu hyakki tsurezure bukuro* (1784)—do not constitute primary evidence that Ebisu was classified as a yōkai. Sekien catalogued anomalous beings, but Ebisu’s dangerous outsider traits do not make him an entry in those works. Treating him as “one of Sekien’s yōkai” is a modern category error. [NDL catalogue for Sekien’s series](https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/books/R100000002-I000007312562)

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**[EA/MA] Maritime livelihood.** The earliest reconstructable religious stratum is maritime: catches, safe fishing, sea-borne abundance, stranded animals, and potent objects from offshore.

**[EA/MA] Markets and exchange.** The 1163 Tōdaiji and 1253 Tsurugaoka records place Ebisu at markets. His commerce function is therefore genuinely medieval, not merely a modern reinterpretation.

**[MA] General prosperity and “daily food.”** The commercial deity developed into a general bestower of household sufficiency and occupational success.

**[RV/F] Agriculture.** In farming communities Ebisu overlaps with the visiting field god and mountain god, sometimes departing and returning at seasonal thresholds. Ebisu-kō may involve rice, vegetables, money placed in a measure, or live fish.

**[RV/F] Health, childhood, and epidemic control.** Nishinomiya’s Ebisu-Hyakudayū puppet complex mediated smallpox and pollution as well as blessing. Modern summaries that make Ebisu only benevolent conceal this ambivalence.

**[RV/F] Death and dangerous fortune.** Some fishing communities recovered drowned bodies, buried or ritually transformed them, and worshipped them as Ebisu. This did not mean that every corpse was automatically Ebisu: the ritual process converted dangerous death-pollution into a source of fishing fortune. Namihira Emiko’s 1978 study documents the apparent contradiction between fishermen’s avoidance of death pollution and their reverence for recovered drowned bodies. [Namihira’s article](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/minkennewseries/42/4/42_KJ00002403203/_article/-char/ja/)

### Standard form

**[MA]** The now-defining iconographic type is a smiling, well-fed man in courtly hunting dress, often with a tall folded cap, holding a fishing rod in his right hand and a large red sea bream (*tai* 鯛) under the left arm or in a basket. The fish is both livelihood and visual pun: *tai* recalls *medetai*, “auspicious/congratulatory.” The combination became stable in late-medieval and Edo popular art; it is not described in the *Kojiki* or *Nihon shoki*.

Other attributes include:

- fishing line, hook, net, basket, or creel;
- folding fan;
- bamboo or New Year’s greenery;
- boat or treasure ship in Seven Lucky Gods scenes;
- fish markets, kitchen, shop counter, rice measure, coins, or sake;
- pronounced smile—the “Ebisu face” (*ebisu-gao*) became an idiom for genial happiness.

A seventeenth–eighteenth-century wooden figure in the Nelson-Atkins Museum already shows the mature fish-and-rod deity. Edo prints and metalwork disseminated it widely. [Nelson-Atkins sculpture](https://art.nelson-atkins.org/objects/18528/ebisu), [Metropolitan Museum tsuba](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/25716), [British Museum image](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/image/634254001)

### Sea bream and rod

**[MA/F]** The fisherman with rod and sea bream is the defining public image, but it is historically later than the maritime cult itself. A rod suggests controlled, individual fishing and honest acquisition; the prestigious red fish marks festive abundance. Puppet performances in which Ebisu catches a sea bream helped make this image mobile and recognizable.

### Animals and material manifestations

**[RV/F]**

- whales and dolphins as fish-bringers;
- sharks, including whale sharks, as Ebisu manifestations;
- driftwood, stones, floats, and other washed-up objects;
- drowned bodies ritually converted into Ebisu;
- sea bream as the canonical iconographic fish;
- the horse as a divine messenger at Nishinomiya in the nocturnal circuit legend.

There is no universally attested sacred color, number, planet, or weekday belonging solely to Ebisu. Red, gold, the number seven, and New Year symbolism arise chiefly from auspicious art and the Seven Lucky Gods rather than an ancient Ebisu-specific system.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to divine beings

### Competing genealogies

#### Hiruko genealogy

**[EA for Hiruko; SI for Ebisu identification]**

- Parents: Izanagi and Izanami.
- Position: first anomalous child in the *Kojiki*; differently positioned in *Nihon shoki* variants.
- Fate: expelled in a reed or camphor-wood boat.
- Missing ancient conclusion: neither chronicle makes him Ebisu.
- Medieval conclusion: he drifts to the Japanese coast, becomes Nishinomiya’s god, and is worshipped as Ebisu.
- Dominance: this is Nishinomiya Shrine’s principal genealogy.

#### Kotoshironushi genealogy

**[EA for Kotoshironushi; SI for equation with Ebisu]**

- Father: Ōkuninushi.
- Mother: Kamuyatatehime in the *Kojiki* genealogical sequence.
- Role: receives the heavenly demand during the transfer of the land while fishing, consents, and withdraws.
- Ebisu link: fishing and coastal cult made him a natural candidate for identification.
- Dominance: strong at Imamiya Ebisu and many shrines outside the Nishinomiya-Hiruko line. Imamiya presently identifies Kotoshironushi explicitly as Ebisu. [Imamiya Ebisu](https://www.imamiya-ebisu.jp/english)

#### Daikoku relationship

**[MA/SI]** Ebisu and Daikoku are often represented as a pair, sometimes popularly described as father and son. There is no single ancient text establishing that genealogy. It emerges from their shared household and prosperity functions and from the fusion of Daikokuten with Ōkuninushi; once Ebisu was identified with Ōkuninushi’s son Kotoshironushi, the father–son explanation became possible.

### Every documented named divine interaction

The entries distinguish interaction in an actual narrative from later joint cult or iconography.

- **Izanagi — parent who, with Izanami, judges Hiruko an unsuccessful child and consigns him to a boat.** *Kojiki* (712); *Nihon shoki* variants (720). **[EA for Hiruko; SI if applied to Ebisu]**
- **Izanami — mother whose speaking first is made the explanation of the defective first birth in the *Kojiki*.** *Kojiki* (712). **[EA/SI]**
- **Ōkuninushi — father of Kotoshironushi; asks that his son’s judgment determine the response to the heavenly envoys.** *Kojiki* (712), *Nihon shoki* (720). **[EA for Kotoshironushi; SI for Ebisu]**
- **Takemikazuchi — receives Kotoshironushi’s consent to the transfer of the land.** *Kojiki* (712). **[EA/SI]**
- **Ame-no-Torifune — sent to summon Kotoshironushi from fishing in the land-transfer episode.** *Kojiki* (712). **[EA/SI]**
- **Futsunushi — heavenly envoy in the *Nihon shoki* land-transfer account associated with Kotoshironushi’s submission.** *Nihon shoki* (720). **[EA/SI]**
- **Takeminakata — another son of Ōkuninushi who resists after Kotoshironushi has submitted; they do not directly meet in the text.** *Kojiki* (712). **[EA; indirect relation only]**
- **Daikokuten/Daikoku — paired with Ebisu as a household and prosperity god; in some late explanations Daikoku is his father.** Late-medieval kyōgen, paintings, domestic cult, and Edo commentary. **[MA/SI]**
- **Bishamonten — joint member of the Seven Lucky Gods; no independent ancient Ebisu–Bishamonten episode.** Late-medieval/early-modern group imagery. **[MA]**
- **Benzaiten — joint member of the Seven Lucky Gods; no early narrative meeting.** Late-medieval/early-modern group imagery. **[MA]**
- **Hotei — joint member of the Seven Lucky Gods; no early narrative meeting.** Late-medieval/early-modern group imagery. **[MA]**
- **Fukurokuju — joint member of the Seven Lucky Gods; no early narrative meeting.** Late-medieval/early-modern group imagery. **[MA]**
- **Jurōjin — joint member of the Seven Lucky Gods; no early narrative meeting.** Late-medieval/early-modern group imagery. **[MA]**
- **Kichijōten — occasionally substitutes for a member of the seven; this is membership variation, not a narrative interaction.** Early-modern lists and images. **[RV/MA]**
- **Shōjō — occasional substitute in variant Seven Lucky Gods groups; no independent narrative encounter.** Early-modern lists. **[RV/MA]**
- **Hyakudayū — worshipped with or through Ebisu in the Nishinomiya puppeteers’ epidemic and ritual-performance complex.** Late-medieval/early-modern shrine and performer traditions. **[RV/MA/SI]**
- **Amaterasu — co-enshrined with Ebisu/Kotoshironushi at Imamiya and with Ebisu at modern Nishinomiya, but no Ebisu-specific mythic meeting is attested.** Shrine cult. **[LC; joint cult, not narrative]**
- **Susanoo — co-enshrined at Nishinomiya; no direct Ebisu narrative is securely attested.** Modern shrine configuration. **[LC; joint cult only]**
- **Konpira/Kompira — paired with Ebisu in some “stay-at-home god” explanations during the gods’ month; no ancient narrative encounter.** Regional folklore. **[RV/F]**

**Absence finding:** There is no securely early story in which a being actually named Ebisu converses with the classical pantheon. Most apparent “Ebisu interactions” are retroactive consequences of identifying him with Hiruko or Kotoshironushi.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. The anomalous first child

**Earliest version: *Kojiki*, 712 [EA].** Izanagi and Izanami circle the heavenly pillar. Izanami speaks first; after intercourse Hiruko is born. He and the subsequently born Awa island are not counted among the proper offspring. The parents place Hiruko in a reed boat and let him drift away. Divine consultation identifies the woman’s prior speech as the ritual fault, after which the couple repeats the marriage correctly.

**Variants: *Nihon shoki*, 720 [EA].** Hiruko’s order of birth and cosmological rank differ by variant. One version says he could not stand even at three years old. The boat may be the heavenly rock-camphor vessel. The chronicle does not offer the *Kojiki*’s single smooth causal sequence.

**Later additions [LC/MA]:**

- Hiruko had no bones or lacked limbs.
- He survived at sea.
- At three he developed limbs.
- He reached Nishinomiya.
- He became Ebisu.

None belongs to the surviving wording of the *Kojiki*. Modern inspirational retellings in which the “disabled child overcomes hardship” are **[MI]** unless clearly presented as creative reinterpretations.

### B. The drifting god reaches Nishinomiya

**[LC]** A fisherman from Naruo repeatedly draws a sacred image from the sea off Wada Cape or the Muko coast. He takes it home and worships it. In a dream the deity declares himself Hiruko and asks to be installed farther west at a suitable sacred site. That site becomes Nishinomiya.

This shrine foundation story provides the missing bridge deliberately left absent by the ancient myths: what happened after Hiruko was cast away. Its motif—an image or divine object fished from the sea, followed by dream-oracle and installation—is widespread in Japanese cult foundation narratives. Nishinomiya itself acknowledges that its date of foundation is unknown. [Nishinomiya’s official history](https://nishinomiya-ebisu.com/about/)

**Historical assessment:** The story is evidence for how medieval and later worshippers understood the shrine, not evidence that an eighth-century child-god physically landed there. Documentary notices show the Hirota/Nishinomiya religious complex before the explicit 1194 Ebisu shrine entry, but the exact foundation sequence cannot be recovered.

### C. Kotoshironushi the fisherman and the transfer of the land

**[EA for Kotoshironushi; SI for Ebisu].** The heavenly deities send an envoy to demand Ōkuninushi’s land. Ōkuninushi defers to his sons. Kotoshironushi is fishing at Miho. Summoned back, he accepts the transfer and withdraws behind a ritual barrier or into concealment. His fishing setting, nonviolent assent, and connection with sea-edge cults made him available for later identification with Ebisu.

This is not a second version of Hiruko’s childhood. It is an originally separate narrative cycle later drawn into Ebisu’s identity.

### D. The visitor from beyond the sea

**[F/MSR].** Fishing communities recognize a being or object from offshore as *ebisu*. The arrival may be a whale heralding shoals, a shark, float, stone, log, sacred image, or human corpse. Proper treatment brings abundance; rejection or disrespect risks danger. Scholars interpret this as the deep structural layer beneath both the Nishinomiya drift legend and the fish-bringing god.

The traditions vary sharply. A whale may be Ebisu himself, Ebisu’s messenger, or merely an omen. A corpse becomes Ebisu only through particular local rites. No universal Japanese “Ebisu doctrine” can be reconstructed from these variants.

### E. The deaf god and the gods’ month

**[F].** During the tenth lunar month the gods are popularly said to travel to Izumo, leaving other regions in “the month without gods,” *Kannazuki*. Ebisu nevertheless remains as a *rusugami*, a god guarding the home or community. Some explanations say he did not hear the summons because he is hard of hearing; others simply exempt Ebisu, or Ebisu and Konpira, because an autumn Ebisu festival must still occur.

The ethnographic logic is secondary: the October 20 Ebisu-kō exists, and the story explains why worship is possible when other gods are absent. The evidence does not support an ancient pan-Japanese myth in which Izumo formally rejected Ebisu. At some shrines worshippers knock behind the sanctuary or make extra noise so the “deaf” Ebisu will hear them. The disability motif may echo Hiruko’s impaired body but that connection is interpretive rather than textually established. [Ebisu-kō survey](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E3%81%88%E3%81%B3%E3%81%99%E8%AC%9B-1510432)

### F. Ebisu catches the sea bream

**[MA/F].** Ritual puppetry and auspicious drama present Ebisu fishing, struggling with, and finally landing a large sea bream. The catch dramatizes the arrival of fortune. The plot became inseparable from his image and contributed to later puppet theater, including the historical environment from which Awaji ningyō and bunraku developed. It is not an episode from the ancient chronicles.

### G. Ebisu-Hyakudayū and ritual puppetry

**[RV/MA].** Nishinomiya-associated *ebisu-kaki* carried an Ebisu puppet, shrine talismans, purification, household blessing, entertainment, and traditions concerning Hyakudayū. The performers occupied socially marginal positions, and “outsider” performers mediated an “outsider” deity. By the sixteenth century they were widely documented and their performances expanded into dramatic skits. The ritual combined blessing and the pacification of dangerous powers, including epidemic disease. [Ashmore’s study](https://www.japanesestudies.org.uk/articles/2007/Ashmore.html), [Law’s history of Awaji puppetry](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctt13x17bk)

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Nishinomiya Shrine

**Location:** Nishinomiya, Hyōgo, historically within the Hirota shrine complex and near Osaka Bay and major roads.

**Documented development:**

- Foundation date unknown.
- 1119 and 1142: “Nishinomiya” records within the Hirota setting.
- 1194: explicit subordinate Ebisu shrine.
- 1204: Ebisu Saburō Shrine.
- Medieval period: growing independence and prosperity as a coastal, road, market, and fortune cult.
- 1663: Tokugawa Ietsuna sponsored reconstruction and recognized the shrine’s nationwide distribution of sanctioned Ebisu images/talismans. Nishinomiya treated its image as the “correct image,” building a network of licensed distributors.
- 1694–1870s: surviving shrine service diaries document administration, repairs, festivals, finances, and distributors. The extant group contains 215 diary units plus associated accounts. [Cultural Heritage Online](https://online.bunka.go.jp/heritages/detail/436970)
- 1945: the nationally designated main hall donated under Ietsuna was destroyed in an air raid.
- Postwar: reconstruction and revival of performance traditions.

The present main sanctuary enshrines Ebisu/Hiruko in the first hall, Amaterasu and Ōkuninushi in the second, and Susanoo in the third. This arrangement is cultic co-residence, not proof of a common ancient myth.

### Imamiya Ebisu

**[LC]** The shrine traditionally dates its establishment to 600 and Prince Shōtoku’s foundation of a western guardian for Shitennōji and the market. The secure evidence for every element of that early date requires separate archaeological and documentary demonstration; the foundation account is shrine tradition. Its Ebisu is Kotoshironushi rather than Nishinomiya’s Hiruko. The contrast demonstrates that “Ebisu” is a cult title spanning more than one mythological identity.

### Tōka Ebisu

At Nishinomiya, January 9–11 comprise:

- January 9: *yoi-ebisu*, the eve;
- January 10: principal festival;
- January 11: *nokori-ebisu*, “remaining Ebisu.”

Offerings and attractions include lucky bamboo, talismans, commercial prayers, sea products, and the modern great-tuna offering, to which worshippers attach coins. At 6:00 a.m. on January 10 the gates open and participants race approximately 200 meters to the main hall in the *Fukuotoko* selection.

**[Historical development]** Modern railways transformed the festival’s scale. After the state railway improved access, Hanshin Electric Railway actively marketed and reorganized the pilgrimage. The spectacular mass festival is therefore both inherited observance and modern transport-era production. Research on the Meiji–Taishō transition also shows that the 1872 calendar reform did not simply abolish the old-date celebration: old-calendar and new-calendar practices coexisted, and railway companies profited from both. [National Museum of Japanese History article](https://rekihaku.repo.nii.ac.jp/records/1791), [Nishinomiya official history](https://nishinomiya-ebisu.com/about/)

### Ebisu-kō

**[F/MA]** The usual eastern and rural date is the twentieth day of the tenth lunar month, with January 20, November 20, and December variants. Merchant associations held feasts, prayed for business, and hosted clients. Households placed money or a wallet in a one-*shō* measure so that wealth would “fill” the container. Rural families offered rice and produce; some placed live carp or crucian carp in a bowl and later released them into a well.

The festival’s timing generated the “stay-at-home god” explanation. In agricultural districts the January and autumn observances can be interpreted as Ebisu departing to earn wealth and returning, or as a field god moving seasonally.

### Offerings

- sea bream and other fish;
- rice, vegetables, and first fruits;
- sake;
- live fish in regional Ebisu-kō;
- money in a measuring box;
- bamboo branches decorated with lucky objects;
- tuna at modern Nishinomiya;
- shrine images and paper talismans distributed through itinerant networks.

### Priesthoods and intermediaries

The cult did not depend on a single priestly order. Its agents included:

- hereditary shrine priests and administrators;
- licensed *gannin* who distributed Nishinomiya images;
- *ebisu-kaki* or *ebisu-mawashi* puppeteers;
- merchant and occupational confraternities;
- fishing communities and boat households;
- village ritual groups;
- Buddhist institutions that invoked Ebisu as market guardian.

The puppet performers’ marginal social status is part of the evidence, not incidental embarrassment. Their later suppression was connected to Meiji policies toward itinerancy, medicine, social status, and the purification of shrine practice.

### Sacrifice, sexuality, and violence

**Human sacrifice:** No reliable source found documents institutional human sacrifice to Ebisu. Stories about drowned bodies concern accidental or unidentified dead recovered and ritually worshipped, not people deliberately killed as offerings.

**Temple prostitution:** No reliable evidence links Ebisu worship to an institution properly describable as “temple prostitution.” Commercial-sex communities could participate in Ebisu observances, including oath-remission customs, but that is not equivalent.

**Cult violence:** No evidence establishes an Ebisu priesthood conducting religious warfare. Dangerous and epidemic aspects occur in ritual symbolism; they are not evidence of organized violence.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 712: reconstructable background

**[MSR]** Maritime visitor beliefs, fishing rites, and reverence for unusual arrivals probably predate the written record, but “Ebisu” cannot be projected securely into prehistoric Japan. Archaeological fishing equipment or coastal ritual deposits do not identify a named deity without inscriptions.

### 712–927: classical myth and state ritual

Hiruko and Kotoshironushi enter the textual record as distinct figures. Neither is called Ebisu. The *Engishiki* ritual system does not yet present a Nishinomiya Ebisu cult.

### Eleventh–thirteenth centuries: documented emergence

The Hirota–Nishinomiya complex appears in court diaries; Ebisu emerges as a market deity in 1163 and as a named shrine deity in 1194. By 1253 his market function was portable enough to be invoked at Kamakura.

### Thirteenth–fourteenth centuries: fusion with Hiruko

The sea-discarded Hiruko becomes the explanatory identity of Nishinomiya’s deity. The fusion supplied genealogy and a mythic prehistory to a medieval cult. The fourteenth-century *Jinnō shōtōki* shows that the equation had entered elite learned discourse.

### Fifteenth–sixteenth centuries: fortune god and mobile cult

Ebisu joins Daikoku and the developing Seven Lucky Gods. Nishinomiya-affiliated puppeteers distribute talismans and performances. Markets, fishing, agriculture, household purification, entertainment, and fortune become mutually reinforcing.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: national diffusion

Tokugawa authorization in 1663 strengthened Nishinomiya’s claim to the authentic Ebisu image. Printed pictures, statues, talismans, puppet theater, popular drama, and merchant worship standardized the smiling fisherman with rod and sea bream. Regional Ebisu-kō customs remained much more varied than the standard image.

### Nineteenth century: scholarship and state restructuring

Motoori Norinaga’s *Kojiki-den* (completed 1798) philologically restored the *Kojiki* and interpreted Hiruko’s terminology, boat, and exclusion. Norinaga’s work is essential evidence for Edo learned reception, not an ancient source. Hirata Atsutane extended *kokugaku* cosmological and national schemes; neither scholar can be cited as proof of what eighth-century worshippers believed.

After 1868, *shinbutsu bunri*, shrine ranking, controls on itinerancy, social-status reform, and medical regulation disrupted the mixed religious and occupational world of the Ebisu puppeteers. The 1870 vaccination/medical regime particularly weakened ritual treatment of smallpox, while administrators regarded popular puppetry and mixed Buddhist-kami practices as incompatible with the new shrine order. Nishinomiya’s rites survived but were reframed. [Ashmore](https://www.japanesestudies.org.uk/articles/2007/Ashmore.html)

### State Shinto period

Nishinomiya was fitted into a centralized shrine hierarchy whose ideology privileged imperial genealogy and sharply distinguished shrines from Buddhist institutions. Ebisu’s Hiruko identity was compatible with chronicle genealogy, but his Buddhist, epidemic, itinerant, corpse, and outcaste associations were awkward and often marginalized. No evidence found shows Ebisu becoming a central militarist state deity comparable to Amaterasu or Yasukuni’s war dead. Assertions that Ebisu himself was a major fascist or imperial-war god would therefore be excessive.

### 1945 and after

The Allied Shinto Directive of December 15, 1945, ended state protection, supervision, and propagation of State/Shin­tō institutions and formally sought to eliminate militarist and ultranationalist uses. Shrines became voluntary religious juridical bodies. [Kokugakuin on the Shinto Directive](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/id%3D8852)

In 1949 a recognized Ebisu dance was again performed at Nishinomiya after a hiatus of more than forty years. Postwar revival reconstructed “traditional” puppetry through negotiation among priests, performers, scholars, and cultural-property institutions. That revival is continuity through deliberate reconstruction, not an unchanged survival.

### Present

Ebisu worship remains active at thousands of shrines and through household, fishing, merchant, and festival traditions. Nishinomiya describes itself as the head shrine of roughly three thousand Hiruko/Ebisu shrines. Such totals depend on how subsidiary shrines and Kotoshironushi establishments are counted. Tōka Ebisu remains one of the Kansai region’s largest shrine festivals.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Yebisu Beer

**[MI/commercial reception]** Japan Beer Brewery launched Ebisu Beer on February 25, **1890**, using the auspicious deity as brand image. German equipment and a German brewmaster were employed. The beer won recognition at the 1900 Paris Exposition and 1904 St. Louis Exposition.

The direction of naming is frequently reversed in popular accounts:

1. the beer was named after the god;
2. a freight station opened in 1901 to ship the beer and was named Ebisu;
3. passenger service followed in 1906;
4. the surrounding Tokyo district eventually took the beer-derived name.

Thus modern Ebisu, Tokyo, is indirectly named after the deity through a commercial product. The label preserves the historical spelling ヱビス/YEBISU and the fisherman-with-sea-bream image. [Sapporo’s official brand history](https://www.sapporobeer.jp/yebisu/brand/history/), [Sapporo English history](https://www.sapporobeer.jp/yebisu/english/)

Beer is therefore not an ancient sacramental attribute of Ebisu. Sake and festive alcohol have long been offered to kami, but the special identification “Ebisu = beer” is a highly successful modern reception. Nishinomiya’s modern summer Ara-Ebisu festival now includes a Yebisu Beer event, an explicit feedback loop from god to brand and back into shrine festival culture. [Nishinomiya summer festival](https://nishinomiya-ebisu.com/event/fuzuki/)

### EVISU clothing

**[MI]** The denim company EVISU was founded in Osaka in 1991 and named for the prosperity god. Its seagull-like painted pocket mark and later Ebisu imagery converted the deity into a global luxury-fashion sign. [EVISU official history](https://us.evisu.com/pages/about-us)

### Art and popular imagery

Edo and Meiji artists repeatedly parody or modernize Ebisu: catching an enormous fish, fishing for money, appearing with courtesans, merchants, children, or the other Lucky Gods. Kawanabe Kyōsai’s nineteenth-century drawing shows the deity catching red sea bream. [Smithsonian object record](https://asia-archive.si.edu/object/F1975.29.8/)

Ebisu appears in manga, animation, games, mascots, restaurants, finance software, and commercial logos. These characters often borrow only the name, fish, smile, or prosperity association. They must not be mined as evidence for premodern mythology.

### Occult, neopagan, and psychological reception

The contested-material search found no documented Western ceremonial-magic or Golden Dawn tradition in which Ebisu held a stable historical role. Modern occult correspondence lists that assign him planets, tarot cards, chakras, crystals, or universal “abundance frequencies” are **[MI]**, not recoveries of Japanese cult.

Contemporary Shinto and eclectic pagan practitioners may venerate Ebisu, but no reliable census isolates an international “Ebisu neopagan” movement. Jungian descriptions of him as an archetype of the wounded child, divine fool, abundance, or resilience are modern psychological interpretations.

### Nationalist and extremist appropriation

Ebisu participated institutionally in the modern shrine order, but the research located no stable extremist movement uniquely centered on him. General claims that every shrine deity was equally a symbol of State Shinto erase important differences. The better-documented political history is administrative: separation from Buddhism, ranking, purification of genealogy, restriction of performers, and postwar disestablishment.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is Ebisu originally Hiruko?

- **Shrine and traditional position [LC/SI]:** Nishinomiya’s Ebisu is Hiruko, the child cast into the sea.
- **Text-critical objection [EA]:** the *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* never make that identification or describe Hiruko’s return.
- **Historical consensus [MSR]:** the equation is medieval, probably formed between the mid-thirteenth and early fourteenth centuries, after the Ebisu cult already existed.
- **Meaning:** “Hiruko is Ebisu” is historically real as a long-lived cult identity, but not an original statement of the eighth-century texts.

### Is Ebisu originally Kotoshironushi?

Kotoshironushi’s fishing, sea-edge setting, oracle-like name, and role in submission made the identification plausible. Yet early texts still do not call him Ebisu. This represents a second major syncretic genealogy rather than a philological solution to the “true” identity.

### Is the name an ethnic slur?

The written forms and related vocabulary unquestionably belong to a historical outsider/barbarian semantic field. Scholars differ on whether the god directly personifies historical Emishi or foreign populations, or whether “outsider” is the broader operative category. Direct derivation from one ethnic group remains unproven.

### Maritime-origin theory

Nelly Naumann’s “Whale and Fish Cult in Japan” and Japanese ethnographic work assemble whales, sharks, dolphins, drift objects, and corpses into a maritime Ebisu complex. The theory explains considerable regional evidence, but the diversity of examples counsels against positing one prehistoric cult with an unchanging doctrine.

### Drowned-body worship

Namihira Emiko argued from ethnographic evidence that fishermen’s recovery and worship of drowned bodies should be understood through ritual transformation: the dead body is dangerous pollution, yet proper burial and rites create a beneficent Ebisu. Popular retellings that simply say “fishermen worshipped corpses as lucky gods” omit the central ritual problem.

### Disability

Modern disability readings emphasize Hiruko’s rejection and later success as Ebisu. They can be productive contemporary interpretations, but the specific recovery story is not in the ancient texts. Conversely, translations that hide Hiruko’s inability to stand sanitize material that the *Nihon shoki* explicitly preserves.

### “Shinto” as a timeless category

Kuroda Toshio’s 1981 critique argued that a distinct, continuous, independent “Shinto” should not be projected backward through Japanese history; medieval kami worship usually existed inside Buddhist institutional and conceptual systems. Breen and Teeuwen retain the antiquity of kami, shrines, myths, and rites while treating “Shinto” as a historically formed category. Ebisu strongly supports this approach: Buddhist market institutions, shrine worship, puppeteers, divine images, Daikokuten, epidemic rites, and folk cult cannot be separated into timeless religious boxes. [Kuroda bibliographic record](https://religion-in-japan.univie.ac.at/an/Literatur%3AKuroda_1981), [Breen and Teeuwen review](https://japanreview.jp/issues/24/3036/)

### Kokugaku

Motoori Norinaga’s *Kojiki-den* established a rigorous early-modern philological encounter with the *Kojiki*. His explanations of “reed boat,” malformed birth, and wordplay belong to eighteenth-century scholarship. Hirata Atsutane placed the divine age within a more systematic nativist theology. Their work influenced modern readings and shrine ideology but does not remove the medieval gaps in Ebisu’s record.

### Yanagita Kunio, Hori Ichirō, and folklore studies

Yanagita’s approach redirected attention from canonical myth to local practice, visiting gods, occupational groups, and oral tradition. *Tōno monogatari* (1910) is indispensable to Japanese folklore history but is not itself a principal Ebisu source; invoking it without an Ebisu-specific passage is misleading. Hori Ichirō’s studies of Japanese folk religion placed figures like Ebisu in the interaction of institutional and popular religion, seasonal rites, outsiders, and sacred-profane mediation.

Michael Dylan Foster’s *The Book of Yōkai* is useful for the construction of yōkai as a category and for understanding Sekien’s legacy; it does not justify reclassifying Ebisu wholesale as a yōkai.

### Evidence and archives

Important bodies of evidence include:

- *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* manuscript and critical-edition traditions;
- medieval court diaries such as *Chōshūki*, *Taiki*, and *Nakasuke-ō ki*;
- shrine records at Nishinomiya and Hirota;
- Nishinomiya’s 1694–1870s service diaries;
- talismans, puppets, statues, masks, prints, and household objects;
- ethnographic field reports on fishing villages and Ebisu-kō;
- modern railway, municipal, corporate, and shrine archives.

Nishinomiya maintains an Ebisu-faith reference collection including images, earthen figures, stone figures, books, and digitized portions of historical research. [Nishinomiya archive](https://nishinomiya-ebisu.com/ebisudb/)

### Discredited or unsupported claims

- **“The *Kojiki* says Hiruko grew limbs and became Ebisu.”** False; later addition.
- **“Ebisu is named in the classical creation myth.”** False.
- **“Nishinomiya Shrine has a securely documented foundation in prehistoric or early-imperial times.”** Unsupported; its foundation date is unknown.
- **“Ebisu’s rod and sea bream are ancient chronicle attributes.”** False; they belong to later iconography.
- **“The seven Lucky Gods have always been the same seven.”** False.
- **“All Ebisu shrines worship Hiruko.”** False; many worship Kotoshironushi.
- **“Toriyama Sekien catalogued Ebisu as a yōkai.”** Unsupported.
- **“Ebisu had an ancient tarot, planetary, chakra, or crystal correspondence.”** Modern invention.
- **“Drowned-body Ebisu traditions document human sacrifice.”** False; the evidence concerns found bodies and ritual transformation.
- **“Beer was an ancient defining attribute.”** False; Yebisu Beer dates to 1890.
