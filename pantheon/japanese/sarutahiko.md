# Sarutahiko: Research Dossier

**Class:** *kami* 神, specifically a terrestrial or “country” kami (*kunitsukami* 国つ神) in the earliest texts; later classified as a crossroads and boundary deity, guide, ancestral deity, road protector, phallic or fertility-associated folk god, and—through iconographic convergence—a tengu-like figure.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the *Kojiki* 古事記, completed in 712 CE. Its account names **Sarutabiko no kami** 猿田毘古神 as the terrestrial deity who waits at the heavenly crossroads and offers to guide Ninigi’s descent. The first independent parallel is the *Nihon shoki* 日本書紀 of 720, whose first variant of the descent story gives the much fuller description of his extraordinary body. “Secure” here dates the texts, not the narrated divine age. The original manuscripts are lost: the oldest surviving *Kojiki* copy is the Shinpukuji manuscript of 1371–72, while early *Nihon shoki* witnesses include ninth-century fragments, although the oldest surviving copy of its opening mythic books is later. [Kokugakuin Kojiki database](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/shinmei/sarutabikonokami/); [Nara National Museum](https://www.narahaku.go.jp/english/collection/p-1190-0.html)

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Sarutahiko’s early textual identity is narrow but striking. He is a radiant *kunitsukami* encountered at the point where Ninigi’s heavenly procession must enter the terrestrial world. He identifies himself, declares that he has come to guide the heavenly grandchild, conducts the descent, and returns toward Ise. The *Kojiki* subsequently narrates his drowning at Azaka. It does not call him Ninigi’s enemy, a tengu, the husband of Ame-no-Uzume, or a universal patron of travel. Those are later constructions, though several became important living traditions.

### Names and spellings

- **猿田毘古神 — Sarutabiko no kami.** Principal *Kojiki* spelling. *Biko/hiko* means a distinguished male, “prince,” or male child of elevated status.
- **猿田毘古大神 — Sarutabiko no ōkami.** “Great kami Sarutabiko,” also in the *Kojiki* episode.
- **猿田毘古之男神 — Sarutabiko no onokami.** “Male kami Sarutabiko,” in the *Kojiki* explanation of the Sarume name.
- **猨田彦大神 / 猨田彦神 — Sarutahiko no ōkami / Sarutahiko no kami.** *Nihon shoki* spellings; 猨 is a variant character for “monkey.”
- **衢神 — Chimata no kami.** “Kami of the crossroads,” the functional designation in the *Nihon shoki* variant.
- **猿田彦命 — Sarutahiko no mikoto.** Later honorific shrine usage.
- **猿田彦大神 — Sarutahiko Ōkami.** Standard modern shrine title.
- **道別大神 — Michiwake no Ōkami.** “Great kami who divides/opens the way,” prominent in Tsubaki shrine theology but not an early independent personal name.
- **みちひらきの大神 — Michihiraki no Ōkami.** “Great kami of opening the way,” a modern devotional and institutional formulation especially associated with Sarutahiko Shrine in Ise.
- **底度久御魂 — Sokodoku mitama; 都夫多都御魂 — Tsubutatsu mitama; 阿和佐久御魂 — Awasaku mitama.** Three process-names applied in the *Kojiki* to Sarutahiko as he sinks, the water bubbles upward, and foam bursts after his drowning. They are not securely separate gods. [Kokugakuin deity entry](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/shinmei/sarutabikonokami/)

The readings vary: **Sarutahiko**, **Sarutabiko**, **Sarudahiko**, and **Sarudabiko** occur. Kokugakuin’s philological summary notes an objection to *Saruda-* on Old Japanese phonological grounds: the following *b* in *biko* makes preceding voicing of *ta* problematic.

### Etymology

**Unresolved.** The characters 猿田 look transparently like “monkey-field,” but early Japanese divine names were frequently written partly for sound. Major proposals include:

1. **Literal monkey interpretation**—*saru* “monkey,” supported retrospectively by the red rear in the *Nihon shoki*, the shellfish episode, and Japanese macaque symbolism. Emiko Ohnuki-Tierney developed this interpretation in *The Monkey as Mirror* (1987). It is a modern anthropological reconstruction, not an ancient gloss. [Princeton/De Gruyter edition](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/9780691222103/html)

2. **Ritual performer theory**—*Saruda* from a word related to playful or licentious performance, parallel to *Sarume*, the courtly female performer lineage. This attempts to explain why the myth makes Ame-no-Uzume and the Sarume clan central.

3. **Sacred-rice-field theory**—*sa* as a sacred rice or agricultural element plus *ta*, “field”; Sarutahiko would then be a local agricultural or land deity.

4. **Place-name theory**—derivation from **Sanagata** 狭長田, the Ise destination named in the *Nihon shoki*.

5. **Sada/Sata theory**—Sarutahiko identified etymologically with **Sada no Ōkami** 佐太大神 of Izumo. This was systematized particularly by Hirata Atsutane and influenced the modern identification at Sada Shrine. Problems include the different textual genealogies and the absence of an ancient text equating the names.

6. **Ryukyuan theory**—Iha Fuyū in 1926 connected the name with a Ryukyuan form *sadaru*, interpreted as “to lead.” This remains conjectural.

No surviving Nara-period text explains the name as “monkey-field prince.” [Kokugakuin philological discussion](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/shinmei/sarutabikonokami/)

### Historical identifications

- **Crossroads/boundary gods (*sae no kami*, *dōsojin*):** later canonical and folk assimilation. It is motivated by *chimatano kami* and the meeting at *ame no yachimata*, but the nationwide equation developed after the chronicles.
- **Kōshin deity:** early-modern syncretism. Because 申, the calendrical sign in *kōshin* 庚申, also means monkey, Sarutahiko entered the mixed Daoist-Buddhist-Shinto Kōshin cult, sometimes replacing or accompanying the Buddhist Shōmen Kongō.
- **Tengu:** iconographic and festival convergence, especially early-modern and modern; not an identity stated in the *Kojiki*, *Nihon shoki*, medieval *Konjaku monogatari*, or Toriyama Sekien’s yōkai catalogues.
- **Sada no Ōkami:** learned Kokugaku identification, later adopted by Sada Shrine; not an equation made by the 733 *Izumo fudoki*.
- **Okitama no kami / Ōta-no-mikoto:** medieval Ise genealogical and shrine-system identifications, not securely present in the eighth-century Sarutahiko narratives.
- **Gyōman Daimyōjin and Shugendō ancestry:** Tsubaki shrine tradition. Its historical antiquity cannot presently be established from the early chronicles.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### *Kojiki* (712)

Language: Japanized literary Chinese with phonographic Old Japanese passages. Compilation is attributed to Ō no Yasumaro from traditions recited by Hieda no Are. The autograph is lost; the earliest complete manuscript is the Shinpukuji copy of 1371–72. [Kokugakuin manuscript discussion](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/kojiki/%E5%A4%A9%E5%9C%B0%E5%88%9D%E7%99%BA/)

The text says that when Ninigi is about to descend, a god stands at the **“eightfold crossroads of heaven”** (*ame no yachimata*), illuminating Takamagahara above and the Central Land of Reed-Plains below. Amaterasu and Takagi order Ame-no-Uzume to identify him. He says:

> “I am an earthly kami. My name is Sarutabiko no kami. I have come because I heard that the heavenly grandchild was to descend, and I wish to go before him and serve as guide.”

After the descent, Ninigi orders Uzume to send home the god whom she exposed or identified and to bear his name in service. The narrative explains the title **Sarume no Kimi** for Uzume’s descendants. Sarutahiko later fishes at Azaka; a *hirabu* shell catches his hand, and he drowns. The three *mitama* names describe phases of the sinking water.

What the *Kojiki* does **not** say:

- It gives no measurements of Sarutahiko’s nose or stature.
- It does not say he obstructed the descent maliciously.
- It does not call Uzume his wife.
- It does not narrate a wedding.
- It does not say that Uzume remains with him; she returns and asserts ritual control over the fishes.
- It does not call him a tengu, monkey god, martial-arts god, or patron of automobiles.
- It supplies no parents, siblings, consort, or offspring.

Donald L. Philippi’s 1968/1969 translation remains important; the searchable Japanese Historical Text Initiative identifies its English *Kojiki* text as Philippi’s. [JHTI translation information](https://jhti.studentorg.berkeley.edu/Kojiki%20editions%20and%20copyrights.html)

### *Nihon shoki* (720), Age of the Gods, Book II, variant 1

Language: literary Chinese. This is an imperially commissioned chronicle and deliberately preserves variant myths rather than one seamless canon. Aston’s 1896 translation remains public and useful, though modern editions supersede some philology. [Aston text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3ANihongi_by_Aston.djvu/108)

Its Sarutahiko is physically extraordinary:

- nose seven hand-spans long;
- body/back “more than seven fathoms,” with a textual issue over whether “seven feet” was intended;
- light shining from mouth and posterior;
- eyes like an eight-hand mirror;
- reddish glare like *akakagachi*, usually understood as winter cherry or a red physalis.

None of the assembled gods initially dares question him. Ame-no-Uzume confronts him, exposes her breasts, lowers her girdle beneath her navel, and laughs. He identifies himself as Sarutahiko no Ōkami and says he is waiting to guide Ninigi. In this version he announces his destination as **the upper Isuzu River at Sanagata in Ise**. Ninigi tells Uzume to escort him. The *Nihon shoki* does not include the shellfish death in this passage.

The difference matters: the grotesque long-nosed body usually treated as Sarutahiko’s defining visual form is securely attested in 720, but not in the 712 *Kojiki*.

### *Kogo shūi* 古語拾遺 (807)

Attributed to Inbe no Hironari, this court memorial defends the ritual privileges and traditions of the Inbe clan. It retells the descent episode substantially within the established chronicle tradition. Its Sarutahiko material is later canonical recapitulation, not independent evidence of a distinct cult or biography. Its clan-political purpose should remain visible.

### Provincial *Fudoki*

The extant and fragmentary eighth-century provincial gazetteers do not preserve a securely independent Sarutahiko cycle. A fragment attributed to the **Iga fudoki** names Sarutahiko, but its transmission is indirect.

The **Izumo no kuni fudoki** (733) tells of **Sada no Ōkami**, born to Kisakahime at Kaka sea cave. It does not call that deity Sarutahiko. Their equation is a later learned reconstruction that became shrine doctrine. The distinction is essential: the *Fudoki* is early evidence for Sada, not early evidence that Sada already was Sarutahiko. [Sada Shrine’s current identification](https://sadajinjya.jp/?WID=1717&m=wp)

### *Man’yōshū*

No secure passage supplies a Sarutahiko narrative or cult hymn. *Man’yōshū* 12.3000 has been invoked only comparatively in etymological arguments about animal-plus-field formations. The anthology should not be listed as a substantive Sarutahiko source.

### *Engishiki* and *norito*

The *Engishiki* was ordered in 905, completed in 927, and brought into force in 967. Its shrine register names Tsubaki/Tsubaki Ōkami shrines in Ise, but the register’s name alone does not prove that its tenth-century deity was already explicitly identified as Sarutahiko. Two Suzuka shrines later disputed which establishment the entry designated. [Engishiki chronology](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%BB%B6%E5%96%9C%E5%BC%8F-38039)

The surviving *Engishiki* *norito* contain important language about road junctions and boundary deities, but they do not preserve a major named hymn to Sarutahiko comparable to the chronicle episode. Using them as direct proof of an early national Sarutahiko road cult overstates the evidence.

### Medieval narrative and shrine literature

- **Ise shrine-origin texts**, including the *Ise nisho kōtai jingū go-chinza denki* and related medieval writings, integrate Sarutahiko with the Ujitoko priestly lineage, Ōta-no-mikoto, Okitama, and the siting of the Inner Shrine. These are medieval priestly systematizations.
- **Konjaku monogatari shū** (early twelfth century) contains abundant Buddhist supernatural material and tengu traditions but no foundational Sarutahiko episode. It documents the separate medieval tengu world, not an early identity between tengu and Sarutahiko.
- **Tengu zōshi** (1296) depicts tengu primarily as birdlike embodiments of clerical pride. This contrasts with the later long-nosed human tengu that could be visually assimilated to Sarutahiko.
- **Toriyama Sekien’s catalogues** beginning with *Gazu hyakki yagyō* (1776) systematize yōkai imagery but do not establish Sarutahiko as a yōkai or as an entry in the ancient Sarutahiko corpus. Invoking Sekien as though he directly catalogued Sarutahiko is unsupported. [Sekien publication history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gazu_Hyakki_Yagy%C5%8D)

### Early-modern scholarship

- **Yamazaki Ansai (1618–1682):** Suika Shinto elevated Sarutahiko as the initiator or ancestral teacher of “the teaching,” with Amaterasu as the source of the Way and Sarutahiko as its guide. This is seventeenth-century learned systematization.
- **Motoori Norinaga (1730–1801), *Kojiki-den*:** philological recovery and sacral interpretation of the *Kojiki* helped restore the chronicle’s authority. Norinaga is evidence for Edo Kokugaku reception, not evidence for prehistoric belief.
- **Hirata Atsutane (1776–1843):** expanded systematic relationships among deities and argued for Sarutahiko/Sada identifications. His reconstruction strongly affected later shrine identities.
- **Yanagita Kunio:** folklore studies placed roadside gods, monkey traditions, performers, and local cults into broader ethnological patterns. *Tōno monogatari* (1910) is not itself a primary Sarutahiko scripture.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains by historical layer

**Securely attested early material**

- guide of Ninigi;
- terrestrial kami at the junction between heaven and earth;
- radiant, physically overwhelming divine presence;
- associated with Ise through the *Nihon shoki* destination;
- associated with fishing and drowning at Azaka in the *Kojiki*.

**Later canonical inference**

- protector of crossroads and boundaries;
- guide for journeys;
- opener of roads, undertakings, and life transitions;
- local ancestral kami of Ise priestly families.

**Medieval and early-modern accretion**

- *dōsojin* or *sae no kami*;
- Kōshin deity;
- phallic and fertility-associated roadside god;
- teacher or originator of Suika Shinto doctrine;
- tengu-like red, long-nosed festival leader.

**Modern shrine extension**

- traffic safety;
- aviation and journey protection;
- business openings and career direction;
- land development, construction, and groundbreaking;
- martial arts, especially aikido at Tsubaki;
- marriage and artistic success when paired with Ame-no-Uzume.

### Appearance

The reliable early description is the *Nihon shoki’s*: enormous stature, seven-span nose, mirror-like red-glowing eyes, and light from both mouth and posterior. A modern image of simply a handsome bearded man with a long nose is a selective domestication of that unsettling body.

Common later iconography includes:

- red face;
- very long nose;
- thick beard and eyebrows;
- archaizing court or traveling costume;
- spear, staff, or halberd;
- tall one-toothed clogs in tengu-influenced images;
- standing at the front of a procession;
- pairing with Uzume;
- phallic emphasis in folk sculpture.

Tengu initially appear in medieval art as beaked, winged creatures; the human, red-faced, long-nosed **daitengu** developed later. Sarutahiko and daitengu therefore converged iconographically; one cannot derive the whole tengu tradition from Sarutahiko. In festivals a performer in a tengu mask may specifically represent Sarutahiko leading the portable shrine. [University of Vienna overview](https://religion-in-japan.univie.ac.at/Handbuch/Mythen/Tengu); [JAANUS](https://www.aisf.or.jp/~jaanus/deta/t/tengu.htm)

A securely catalogued early-modern artwork is Totoya Hokkei’s 1825 surimono commonly titled **“God and Cocks,”** identified by the Rijksmuseum as Sarutahiko. The museum’s accompanying claim that he helped Uzume lure Amaterasu from the cave conflates separate myths: Sarutahiko is absent from the cave episode in the early chronicles. The print is therefore evidence of nineteenth-century reception, not of the early story. [Rijksmuseum](https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/collection/object/God-and-Cocks--56a3dabc0356407403235a7e6387dc8f)

No color, weekday, planet, plant, or canonical sacred animal belongs to a securely attested early Sarutahiko system. Camellias at Tsubaki, monkeys through the spelling *saru*, and the calendrical monkey through Kōshin are historical associations, not a single ancient symbolic code.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Genealogy

The *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* give Sarutahiko **no parents, siblings, divine ancestors, children, or birth narrative**.

The familiar statement that he is **Uzume’s husband** is not made in either chronicle. The sources pair them through confrontation, escort, naming, and later cult, but do not narrate marriage or sexual union. Aratate Shrine and Tsubaki tradition treat them as a married pair; this is a regional shrine tradition and later canonical elaboration.

Sarutahiko’s position as “leader of all earthly kami” is also later theology. The chronicles call him a *kunitsukami*, but they do not install him as their genealogical chief.

The Ujitoko family of Ise claims descent from him through shrine-lineage traditions. These claims explain ritual office and sacred geography; they are not recoverable as eighth-century biological genealogy.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Amaterasu Ōmikami — command through an intermediary.** Amaterasu, together with Takagi, orders Uzume to question the radiant god at the crossroads. *Kojiki*, 712. **Tier: securely attested early material.**
- **Takagi no kami / Takamimusubi — command through an intermediary.** Jointly with Amaterasu commissions Uzume’s inquiry. *Kojiki*, 712. **Tier: securely attested early material.**
- **Ame-no-Uzume — confrontation and identification.** She questions Sarutahiko; in the *Nihon shoki* she exposes her breasts, lowers her girdle, laughs, and compels him to identify himself. *Kojiki*, 712; *Nihon shoki*, 720. **Tier: securely attested early material, with divergent textual detail.**
- **Ame-no-Uzume — escort to Ise/home territory.** Ninigi assigns her to send or accompany Sarutahiko home; the *Nihon shoki* specifies Sanagata at the upper Isuzu River. *Kojiki*, 712; *Nihon shoki*, 720. **Tier: securely attested early material.**
- **Ame-no-Uzume — marriage.** Tsubaki and Aratate shrine traditions say the pair married and lived together. No marriage occurs in the early texts. **Tier: regional shrine tradition / later canonical elaboration.** [Japanese transport ministry description of Aratate](https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/R1-01041.html)
- **Ninigi no Mikoto — offered service and guidance.** Sarutahiko says he has come to guide the heavenly grandchild and leads the descent. *Kojiki*, 712; *Nihon shoki*, 720. **Tier: securely attested early material.**
- **Ninigi no Mikoto — receives instruction concerning Uzume and Sarutahiko.** After the descent, Ninigi directs Uzume to escort Sarutahiko and bear his name in service. *Kojiki*, 712. **Tier: securely attested early material.**
- **Sada no Ōkami — equation, not narrative interaction.** Hirata-influenced scholarship and Sada Shrine doctrine identify the two as one being. The 733 *Izumo fudoki* does not. **Tier: learned systematization and modern shrine identification.**
- **Okitama no kami / Ōta-no-mikoto — equation or lineage relation.** Medieval Ise materials make these figures manifestations, descendants, or functional continuations of Sarutahiko. Versions differ. **Tier: medieval priestly systematization.**
- **Gyōman Daimyōjin — descendant relation in Tsubaki tradition.** Gyōman is presented as Sarutahiko’s descendant and an ancestor of the priestly house. **Tier: shrine genealogy; historically unverified.**
- **Amaterasu — cave dance.** Some modern captions place Sarutahiko beside Uzume during the cave episode. Neither early chronicle does so. **Tier: modern conflation, not an attested interaction.**
- **Other gods accompanying Ninigi:** they occur in the same broad descent cycle, but no individual interaction with Sarutahiko is narrated. **Absence of evidence.**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The encounter at the heavenly crossroads

**Early core:** Ninigi is preparing to descend when an unknown luminous god appears at *ame no yachimata*. The heavenly gods hesitate. Uzume identifies him. Sarutahiko declares himself a terrestrial deity who has come to guide Ninigi.

The scene’s structure is one of controlled encounter between the heavenly sovereign lineage and an indigenous/local terrestrial power. The *Kojiki* emphasizes light spanning both worlds and the etiology of the Sarume clan name. The *Nihon shoki* emphasizes bodily excess, Uzume’s sexually charged confrontation, and Sarutahiko’s Ise destination.

Modern retellings often turn him into a hostile guardian whom Uzume seduces or defeats. The early texts are subtler: his intentions are initially unknown, but he says he came voluntarily to serve as guide. Uzume’s exposure is a display of ritual audacity or coercive power; the texts do not say it romantically seduces him.

Scholarly reconstructions variously read the episode as:

- Yamato incorporation of an Ise local deity;
- a ritual boundary encounter;
- a charter for Sarume court performers;
- a memory of local elites integrated into imperial ritual;
- a mythological bridge between Ise and the southern descent tradition.

None is demonstrable as the single original meaning.

### Guidance of the heavenly grandchild

Sarutahiko leads Ninigi’s party toward the terrestrial destination. Later tradition transforms this one act into the general category of **michibiki/michihiraki**, guidance and opening the right way. The extension is intelligible but historically later: the early texts record one cosmic-political act, not a catalogue of everyday blessings.

### Return to Ise and Uzume’s naming

The *Nihon shoki* gives Sarutahiko’s intended home as Sanagata at the upper Isuzu River. The *Kojiki* has Ninigi instruct Uzume to escort him but is less explicit about the destination. Ninigi further tells her to take the god’s name into her service, explaining why her descendants are called **Sarume no Kimi**.

This is not a wedding story in the early record. It is simultaneously a divine encounter and an etiology of a courtly lineage/title.

### The drowning at Azaka

Only the *Kojiki* supplies this episode. While fishing at Azaka, Sarutahiko’s hand is caught by a *hirabu* shell; he sinks and drowns. The text successively names his spirit as it remains at the bottom, as the water rises in bubbles, and as foam bursts.

Interpretations include:

- a marine or fishing cult;
- a ritual description of drowning;
- monkey folklore concerning a hand trapped in shellfish;
- transformation rather than ordinary mortal death;
- an explanation of multiple local spirit-names.

The text itself does not explain why a powerful guide is unable to escape. Nor does it say Uzume witnesses or causes his death.

### Marriage to Uzume

At Tsubaki and Aratate, the escort becomes marriage and settlement. Aratate’s local explanation says their union was so immediate that their dwelling was erected from freshly cut, rough materials, giving the shrine its name. This is valuable regional sacred history, but it is not in the eighth-century texts.

### Sada no Ōkami’s birth

The *Izumo fudoki* birth at Kaka cave belongs textually to Sada no Ōkami. Only after the Sarutahiko–Sada equation does it become a “birth story of Sarutahiko.” It must therefore be labeled **early Sada myth plus early-modern/modern identity transfer**, not Sarutahiko’s earliest genealogy.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Tsubaki Ōkami Yashiro, Suzuka

Tsubaki Grand Shrine presents itself as Sarutahiko’s principal shrine and the head of more than two thousand affiliated shrines. Its official history says it was founded in 3 BCE under the legendary Emperor Suinin through Yamatohime’s oracle. That date is **shrine tradition**, not contemporary documentation.

The shrine’s historical claims require separation:

- **3 BCE foundation:** legendary chronology.
- **748 Daianji record:** often cited as the earliest documentary notice.
- **Engishiki listing:** tenth-century evidence for a Tsubaki-named shrine, though rival Tsubaki Shrine contests which site the register meant.
- **1871:** ranked a district/county shrine under the modern state system.
- **1927:** promoted to prefectural shrine.
- **1935:** designated or promoted institutionally as a head shrine of Sarutahiko worship, according to later shrine histories.

Its current pantheon includes Sarutahiko as principal kami, with Ninigi, Takuhatachijihime, Uzume, and Konohanasakuya-hime. Uzume has a separate subsidiary sanctuary, Tsubakikishi Jinja. Current benefits include guidance, land protection, construction, removal of directional calamity, traffic safety, commerce, marriage, study, and martial training. These are living devotional categories, not all ancient attributes. [Tsubaki official history](https://tsubaki.or.jp/about/)

### Sarutahiko Jinja, Ise

Located near the Inner Shrine pilgrimage route, Sarutahiko Shrine is associated with the hereditary Ujitoko family, which identifies Sarutahiko as its ancestor. The shrine relates that Ōta-no-mikoto, the ancestor of its priestly house, guided Yamatohime to the Inner Shrine’s site.

Its current theology emphasizes **michihiraki**—placing undertakings on the right course. The precinct includes Sarume Shrine for Uzume. Octagonal pillars, amulets, votive plaques, and the *Michibiki no mai* dance articulate the guidance symbolism. [Official shrine history](https://www.sarutahikojinja.or.jp/about/)

Sarutahiko’s location on the road to Ise made him particularly apt as a pilgrimage-road deity. However, the proposition that every premodern Ise pilgrim formally worshipped him at the present shrine is too broad. The historically prominent purification stop at Futami and its Okitama associations are related but institutionally distinct.

### Other cult centers

- **Tsubaki Jinja, Suzuka:** rival claimant to the *Engishiki* Tsubaki entry and Ise *ichinomiya* status.
- **Ōasahiko Jinja, Tokushima:** enshrines Ōasahiko/Ame-no-Futodama and Sarutahiko; its official history says Sarutahiko originally dwelt on Mount Ōasa and was later jointly enshrined. [Official history](https://www.ooasahikojinja.jp/yuisho/)
- **Sada Jinja, Shimane:** worships Sada no Ōkami as Sarutahiko under the later identification.
- **Aratate Jinja, Takachiho:** jointly worships Sarutahiko and Uzume as a married couple.
- Numerous roadside **Sarutahiko**, **Dōsojin**, **Kōshin**, and sometimes **Shirahige** sites reflect local equations of varying date; the names do not prove a common ancient origin.

### Offerings, rites, and festivals

Modern worship follows ordinary shrine practice: purification, coin offerings, bows and claps, *tamagushi*, *norito*, amulets, votive plaques, festival processions, and kagura. Tsubaki’s rites emphasize misogi, guidance, and martial disciplines; Ise’s Sarutahiko Shrine performs purification and the *Michibiki no mai*.

In local festivals Sarutahiko may be represented by a long-nosed masked leader preceding the *mikoshi*. This dramatizes his guidance of Ninigi. The mask’s tengu form is an early-modern or modern visual convention.

Kōshin groups used scrolls or stones bearing Sarutahiko, Shōmen Kongō, the three monkeys, sun and moon, and roosters in various combinations. Surviving nineteenth- and twentieth-century hanging scrolls document this syncretism materially.

### Sexual and phallic material

Some early-modern and folk representations pair Sarutahiko and Uzume with explicit genital imagery or transform the long nose into a phallus. This connects crossroads, fertility, comic performance, and protective obscenity. It is documented folk and early-modern iconography; it should not be projected backward as the original meaning of the *Nihon shoki* nose.

### Human sacrifice and cult violence

The dedicated contested-material search found **no credible primary or archaeological evidence for human sacrifice to Sarutahiko**, temple prostitution, a Sarutahiko priesthood conducting cult violence, or forced conversion specifically in his name. Japanese “monkey-god sacrifice” folktales concern other beings and narrative complexes; similarity of the word *saru* does not identify those gods with Sarutahiko.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 712: unreconstructable background

Sarutahiko may preserve an Ise local god, a boundary ritual, a performer-clan charter, or a terrestrial chief incorporated into Yamato mythology. None is directly documented before the eighth century. There are no securely identified pre-712 inscriptions, statues, theophoric names, or archaeological cult objects bearing his name.

### 712–807: chronicle formation

The *Kojiki* establishes the luminous terrestrial guide, the Uzume encounter, Sarume etiology, and drowning. The *Nihon shoki* adds the canonical grotesque body and explicitly locates his return in Ise. The *Kogo shūi* repeats the descent within an Inbe ritual-political argument.

### Eighth–tenth centuries: local shrine incorporation

Ise genealogies and Tsubaki institutions gradually attached Sarutahiko to local priestly ancestry and sacred geography. The *Engishiki* supplies evidence for named shrines and state ritual administration but not a full nationwide Sarutahiko cult.

### Medieval period: combinatory religion

Buddhist, shrine, lineage, mountain-ascetic, and Ise traditions operated in interdependent combinations. Sarutahiko was connected with Okitama, local land gods, road and boundary powers, priestly ancestors, and eventually tengu-like imagery. The medieval tengu remained substantially an independent Buddhist discourse about arrogance, possession, mountain power, and fallen clerics.

Kuroda Toshio’s critique is relevant: treating this whole period as the history of a timeless, autonomous religion called “Shinto” obscures the medieval *kenmitsu* and shrine-temple complexes. Sarutahiko’s medieval history is better described as shrine, lineage, local, and combinatory cult than as the straightforward development of one separate “Shinto denomination.”

### Early-modern period: popular and learned expansion

Road travel, Ise pilgrimage, roadside stone cults, Kōshin associations, phallic imagery, festival masks, and print culture broadened Sarutahiko’s functions. Yamazaki Ansai’s Suika Shinto made him an authoritative divine teacher. Kokugaku scholars reread the ancient texts philologically while also constructing systematic identities absent from those texts.

The long-nosed daitengu’s fully developed popular image made visual convergence easy. Sarutahiko could be costumed as a tengu without becoming, in every theological setting, literally a yōkai.

### 1868–1945: modern shrine system and State Shinto

The Meiji separation of kami and buddhas dismantled many shrine-temple combinations and encouraged fixed official祭神 identities. Local, Buddhist, Kōshin, and combinatory beings were often redescribed using chronicle kami. Sarutahiko benefited from the authoritative status of the *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* and from his role in the imperial descent narrative.

Tsubaki entered the modern shrine-ranking system and was eventually promoted. Sarutahiko’s act of guiding Ninigi could support imperial myth and national foundation rhetoric, but he was never as central to state ideology as Amaterasu, the imperial ancestors, or the war dead. Claims that his entire cult was a State Shinto invention are false; claims that its modern organization remained untouched by State Shinto are equally false.

The government’s 1906–12 shrine-merger policy consolidated many local shrines, sometimes erasing or subordinating small roadside cults. Evidence for suppression must be shown shrine by shrine; no national decree specifically targeted Sarutahiko.

### 1945–present

The December 1945 Shinto Directive ended state sponsorship and administration of shrines. The postwar constitution separated religion and state; shrines reorganized as private religious corporations. [Kokugakuin State Shinto entry](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=8865)

Postwar Sarutahiko worship remained active and adapted readily to automobile safety, business, personal development, aikido, tourism, and international shrine practice. Tsubaki Grand Shrine of America was established in California in 1987, later moved to Washington State, and ultimately closed in 2023; its *wakemitama* was transferred to Shin Mei Spiritual Centre in British Columbia. That overseas history represents a modern institutional extension, not an ancient diaspora.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Aikido

Morihei Ueshiba’s visionary religious world gave Sarutahiko a special place as guide and guardian of the “Floating Bridge of Heaven.” A report attributes to Ueshiba a 1942 vision in which gods gathered under Sarutahiko’s guidance for the manifestation of aiki. Tsubaki subsequently cultivated aikido ties and maintains martial practice facilities. This is twentieth-century new religious and martial interpretation, not an early textual attribute.

### Literature, manga, film, and games

Osamu Tezuka’s *Hi no Tori* (*Phoenix*) repeatedly uses **Saruta/Sarutahiko**, marked by a conspicuous nose, through reincarnated or parallel characters. The series began in preliminary form in 1954 and its best-known serialization from 1967 onward. Saruta’s recurrence materially shaped the modern popular image of Sarutahiko as a transhistorical, suffering, visually unmistakable figure. [Tezuka official character page](https://tezukaosamu.net/en/character/288.html)

The 1978 live-action *Phoenix* includes Sarutahiko as a Yamatai general. Numerous anime, manga, role-playing games, and commercial designs use the long-nosed guide, but they freely merge chronicle god, tengu, warrior, and comic grotesque.

The Japanese coffee company **Sarutahiko Coffee**, founded in Tokyo in 2011, is a commercial appropriation of the memorable divine name and “guidance” associations; it is not a cult lineage.

### Western occultism and neopaganism

No evidence was found that Sarutahiko held a historical role in Renaissance Hermetism, the Theosophical Society, the Golden Dawn, classical ceremonial magic, or early Wicca. Online spell lists assigning him planetary correspondences, fixed magical colors, tarot cards, chakras, or universal “gatekeeper” offices are **modern inventions unless individually sourced to a contemporary creator**.

Contemporary non-Japanese polytheists and shrine-affiliated practitioners may worship him. Such worship is real as modern religious practice, but it should not be called an unbroken Western occult tradition.

### Political appropriation

Sarutahiko’s placement in the imperial descent cycle made him usable in imperial-national narratives, particularly as the terrestrial power who accepts and guides heavenly rule. Documented modern shrine rhetoric often calls him a founder of the nation. No substantial evidence was found for a distinct contemporary extremist movement centered on Sarutahiko, and it would be misleading to infer one merely from his presence in State Shinto-era mythology.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Origin of the figure

Major interpretations include:

- indigenous Ise land or territorial deity;
- boundary and crossroads god;
- solar deity displaced by Amaterasu;
- agricultural or rice-field god;
- monkey deity;
- performer or ritual-clown figure connected with the Sarume;
- mythologized local chief;
- ritual predecessor of a masked festival leader.

The first two fit the texts and later cult particularly well, but none can be demonstrated as the unique prehistoric origin. Claims that Sarutahiko was Japan’s original supreme sun god displaced by Amaterasu are speculative modern reconstructions.

### Uzume: wife or ritual opponent?

The early texts support confrontation, identification, escort, and name transfer. They do not support marriage. Marriage emerges in shrine and regional tradition. Calling her simply “his wife” without dating the claim erases the textual history.

### Tengu resemblance

The resemblance is historically real but chronologically directional. Sarutahiko’s long nose is attested in 720; early medieval tengu are avian. Human long-nosed tengu become prominent later, after which festival and popular art draw the figures together. Sarutahiko is therefore neither securely “the first tengu” nor wholly unrelated to tengu iconography.

### Sada no Ōkami

The *Izumo fudoki* provides Sada’s separate birth narrative. Hirata Atsutane and later shrine administrators promoted the equation with Sarutahiko. It is a documented learned identification, not a fact established by the 733 text.

### Historicized local rulers

Some modern scholarship suggests that Sarutahiko and Uzume may encode real regional elites later divinized. This can explain political geography but remains inference; there is no inscription naming a historical Sarutahiko.

### Kuroda and the category “Shinto”

Kuroda Toshio argued that “Shinto” as an autonomous, continuous religion was largely a modern construction and that medieval kami worship operated within a Buddhist-dominated religious system. Breen and Teeuwen modified the strongest form of this position while likewise rejecting a timeless, unchanged Shinto essence. For Sarutahiko, this requires period-specific vocabulary: early court myth, medieval combinatory cult, early-modern Shinto theory, State Shinto administration, and postwar Shrine Shinto are not one unchanged institution.

### State of the evidence

The dossier rests disproportionately on two early literary passages. There is:

- no pre-eighth-century inscription naming Sarutahiko;
- no securely identified ancient cult statue;
- no ancient independent genealogy;
- no ancient marriage narrative;
- no early corpus of Sarutahiko hymns;
- no documented ancient human sacrifice;
- no evidence in Toriyama Sekien that transforms Sarutahiko into a catalogued yōkai;
- abundant later shrine, stone, mask, print, festival, and ethnographic evidence.

The central modern monograph is Iida Michio’s *Sarutahiko-kō: Sarutahiko shinkō no tenkai* (1998). Other indispensable frames include Philippi and Aston’s translations, Motoori’s *Kojiki-den*, Hirata’s writings, Hori Ichirō on folk religion, Ohnuki-Tierney on monkey symbolism, Kuroda’s essays, Breen and Teeuwen’s *A New History of Shinto*, and Michael Dylan Foster’s work on yōkai. These works do not agree on a single origin, and their hypotheses should not be converted into divine biography.
