# Ame-no-Uzume: Research Dossier

> **Class:** female *kami* (女神, goddess/divine being) in the early Japanese court mythologies; ancestral deity of the Sarume ritual lineage; subsequently patron kami of performance, dance, laughter, theatrical arts, spirit-pacification, and matchmaking. “Dawn goddess” is a modern interpretive title, not her class or explicit domain in the earliest texts.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** *Kojiki* 古事記, completed in 712 CE, under the name 天宇受賣命 (*Ame-no-Uzume-no-Mikoto*). Its oldest complete surviving manuscript is the Shinpukuji copy of 1371–72. The second independent early witness is the *Nihon shoki* 日本書紀, completed in 720, which writes her name 天鈿女命. No securely identified pre-712 inscription, image, archaeological cult installation, or theophoric personal name names her.

## Evidentiary labels used

- **Early attested:** securely present in the *Kojiki* (712), *Nihon shoki* (720), or closely following court records.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** transmitted in such works as the *Kogo shūi* (807), *Sendai kuji hongi*, *Engishiki* (completed 927), medieval ritual manuals, or later shrine traditions.
- **Regional variant:** localized shrine, kagura, or oral tradition not demonstrably present in the earliest texts.
- **Learned systematization:** interpretation constructed by theologians, court ritualists, kokugaku scholars, or modern scholars.
- **Syncretistic identification:** historically contingent equation with another kami, Buddhist figure, roadside god, mask, or folk deity.
- **Medieval/early-modern accretion:** post-Heian development not documented in the eighth-century sources.
- **Folklore:** orally or locally transmitted belief recorded substantially later.
- **Modern invention/retelling:** a claim, title, genealogy, or characterization unattested before modern popular religion, literature, or entertainment.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Ame-no-Uzume is one of the comparatively few female kami given sustained individual action in both early court mythologies. Her defining early actions are ritual performance and inspired speech at Amaterasu’s rock cave; confronting and identifying Sarutahiko at the heavenly crossroads; accompanying him toward Ise; acquiring the Sarume name; and compelling the creatures of the sea to submit to the heavenly descendant. The early texts do not supply parents, birth, siblings, celestial body, fixed residence, or an independent sovereignty. Her identity is functional and relational: performer, medium, negotiator, lineage ancestor, and agent of the heavenly court.

### Names and written forms

| Form | Source and date | Meaning/status |
|---|---|---|
| **天宇受賣命** (*Ame-no-Uzume-no-Mikoto*) | *Kojiki*, 712 | The principal *Kojiki* spelling. 宇受賣 is largely phonographic; 命, *mikoto*, is an honorific for an august being. |
| **天宇受売命** | Modern simplified-character form | The same name, with 賣 simplified to 売. |
| **天鈿女命** (*Ame-no-Uzume-no-Mikoto*, sometimes *Ama-no-Uzume*) | *Nihon shoki*, 720; *Kogo shūi*, 807 | 鈿 can mean ornamental metalwork or a jeweled hair ornament, but the spelling does not establish that this was the name’s original etymology. |
| **天鈿女 / 天宇受売神** | Abbreviated later forms | “Heavenly Uzume”; *kami* substitutes for the honorific *mikoto*. |
| **天乃於須女** (*Ame-no-Osume*) | *Kogo shūi*, 807, presented as an old pronunciation | Used in that work’s etymological explanation. |
| **Sarume ancestral kami** | *Kojiki*, *Nihon shoki* variant, *Kogo shūi* | A status rather than a proper alternate name. |
| **Okame / Otafuku / Ofuku** | Early-modern and modern folk and artistic usage | A comic, auspicious female mask or popular deity later associated or identified with Uzume. It is not an early name of the goddess. |
| **“Goddess of dawn”** | Principally modern English and Japanese popular description | An inference from restoring the sun/light, assisted by the long-crowing birds. Neither *Kojiki* nor *Nihon shoki* assigns her “dawn” as a formal domain. |
| **Goddess of mirth, revelry, meditation, fertility, sexuality** | Modern popular and neopagan lists | “Mirth” and sexual display summarize narrative actions; fertility is a scholarly interpretation of the cave scene; “meditation” has no identifiable classical basis. |

### Etymology

The etymology is unresolved.

1. **Early learned etymology — “formidable woman.”** The *Kogo shūi* says her old name was *Ame-no-Osume* and derives it from a word for a strong, fierce, or intrepid woman: “the deity was strong and resolute; hence the name.” This is valuable evidence for an early-ninth-century court interpretation, not proof of the name’s prehistoric derivation. [Kokugakuin’s deity database](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/shinmei/amenouzumenomikoto/) preserves the passage and modern discussion.

2. **Ornamented-medium theory.** Because 鈿 can denote a jeweled ornament or hair decoration, scholars have proposed that *Uzume* designated a female ritual specialist bearing a divine receptacle or ornament in her hair. This is compatible with the plant ornaments in the cave scene but remains a reconstruction.

3. **“Whirling” or “vortex” explanations.** Popular works sometimes connect *uzu* with 渦, “whirl/vortex,” hence “whirling woman.” The early spellings do not substantiate that derivation.

4. **“Dread female of heaven.”** W. G. Aston’s older rendering reflects an etymological interpretation, not a literal consensus translation. Karl Florenz similarly rendered the name as a frightening or repelling heavenly woman. These translations belong to nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century comparative interpretation.

### Classification and changing domain

- **Eighth century:** female heavenly kami and Sarume ancestor.
- **Ninth–tenth-century court ritualism:** prototype or divine ancestor of ritual performers, possession specialists, and participants in *chinkonsai*.
- **Medieval shrine-temple culture:** a kami interpreted within a religious world inseparable from Buddhist ritual and local cult; no evidence supports treating medieval worship as an isolated, doctrinally “pure Shinto” system.
- **Early modern period:** theatrical ancestor, comic and auspicious female figure, increasingly associated with Okame/Otafuku.
- **Modern shrine religion:** patron of dance, acting, entertainment, technical skill, sports, harmonious relationships, and matchmaking. Sarume Shrine in Ise explicitly presents her as ancestral kami of *wazaogi*, kagura, performing skill, and spirit-pacification. [Sarume Shrine](https://www.sarutahikojinja.or.jp/sarume/)

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The documentary problem

The *Kojiki* was presented in 712, but no eighth-century copy survives. The oldest complete witness is the paper manuscript copied in 1371–72 at Shinpukuji; its first volume is catalogued as a National Treasure by the Nara National Museum. [Nara National Museum manuscript record](https://imagedb.narahaku.go.jp/006312-001-000.html)

The *Nihon shoki* was completed in 720. Its oldest surviving manuscript material includes a ninth-century copy of volume 10, but the earliest extant “Age of the Gods” copies are much later—among them the Kanekata manuscript of 1286 and Kanenatsu manuscript of 1303. Thus Uzume’s earliest attestation is the securely dated composition and reception history of the eighth-century works, not an autograph physical sheet from 712 or 720. [National Institutes for Cultural Heritage manuscript account](https://emuseum.nich.go.jp/detail?content_base_id=100249)

### Primary corpus

#### *Kojiki* 古事記 — 712; Japanese represented through mixed Chinese and phonographic writing

Uzume occurs in three connected episodes:

1. **The heavenly rock chamber.** She adorns herself with clubmoss and spindle-tree foliage, carries bamboo leaves, overturns a sounding vessel or tub, stamps until it resounds, becomes divinely possessed, exposes her breasts, and lets her skirt-band fall toward her genitals. The assembled deities laugh. When Amaterasu asks why they rejoice despite universal darkness, Uzume answers that they have found a deity more august than Amaterasu. The mirror stratagem and Tajikarao’s strength then restore Amaterasu.

2. **The heavenly descent.** Uzume is one of the five hereditary company-leaders accompanying Ninigi. At the heavenly crossroads, Amaterasu and Takamimusubi send her to question the shining, intimidating Sarutahiko because she is capable of confronting him directly.

3. **Sarutahiko and the sea creatures.** Ninigi orders her to escort Sarutahiko and bear his name because she disclosed his identity. She demands the allegiance of the fishes to the heavenly descendant. All consent except the sea cucumber; she cuts or slits its mouth with a small blade. The narrative explains both the sea cucumber’s mouth and the Sarume clan’s privilege in receiving marine tribute.

Donald L. Philippi’s *Kojiki* (1968/1969) remains the English translation specified in the brief. Kokugakuin’s synchronized text and deity database identifies her appearances under “Heavenly Rock Chamber,” “Heavenly Descent,” and “Sarume no Kimi.” [Kokugakuin Ame-no-Uzume database](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/shinmei/amenouzumenomikoto/)

#### *Nihon shoki* 日本書紀 — 720; literary Sinitic

The *Nihon shoki* preserves a main narrative and variant writings rather than one harmonized account.

- In the principal rock-cave account, Uzume is characterized as skillful in inspired performance. She binds plant material around herself, kindles fires, overturns a tub, stamps, becomes possessed, and exposes herself. The multitude laughs.
- Not every variant of the cave myth includes her. Her absence from some *Nihon shoki* variants is evidence that neither her dance nor the complete familiar ensemble belonged to every transmitted version.
- In the descent-cycle variant, the heavenly gods cannot face Sarutahiko’s gaze. Uzume exposes her breasts and lowers her waistband below the navel while laughing face-to-face with him. He asks why; she counters by asking who he is and why he blocks the road. After questioning him, she reports to the heavenly descendant and accompanies him to Ise.
- The *Nihon shoki* version explains that both men and women of the relevant lineage were styled Sarume-no-Kimi, while the *Kojiki* specifies its women. This discrepancy probably reflects confusion or change between *Sarume* as a female ritual office and Sarume-no-Kimi as a lineage designation. [Kokugakuin discussion](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/shinmei/amenouzumenomikoto/)

W. G. Aston’s 1896 translation is historically important but uses Victorian diction and occasionally embeds obsolete comparative assumptions. [Aston’s relevant page](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Nihongi_by_Aston.djvu/110)

#### *Kogo shūi* 古語拾遺 — 807; literary Sinitic

Compiled by Inbe no Hironari partly to assert Inbe ritual prerogatives, this work:

- repeats and rationalizes the cave performance;
- gives the “strong/intrepid woman” etymology;
- supplies ritual-institutional continuity between Uzume, Sarume, and female religious specialists;
- retells the Sarutahiko interrogation, including her bodily exposure;
- complains that old hereditary offices had declined or passed to other practitioners.

Its claims must be read as early-Heian lineage advocacy, not as a transparent window onto remote prehistory. A translated passage describes Uzume’s escorting Sarutahiko and explains the Sarume title through their encounter. [English *Kogo shūi* page](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Kogoshui.djvu/56)

#### *Sendai kuji hongi* 先代旧事本紀 — probably ninth century, though once treated as much earlier

The work incorporates Uzume into its divine and descent genealogies. Its value is evidence for early-medieval systematization; it cannot safely be used as an independent pre-*Kojiki* witness without source criticism.

#### *Engishiki* 延喜式 — ordered 905, completed 927; implemented 967

The *Engishiki* preserves court ceremonial regulations and *norito*. Its importance is primarily institutional: Sarume and female ritual personnel participate in imperial ceremonial structures, especially spirit-pacification and enthronement-related rites. It does not simply retell an independent biography of Uzume. Later ritual commentators read the overturned vessel and stamping at *chinkonsai* as enactments of her cave performance. The surviving regulations establish court practice; the claim that every detail directly descends from a prehistoric Uzume rite is learned ritual genealogy.

#### *Hokuzanshō* 北山抄 — early eleventh century; *Gōke shidai* 江家次第 — completed 1111

These ceremonial manuals explicitly describe Sarume-associated actions involving an overturned vessel and state or imply that they reproduce Uzume’s performance. They are important evidence that the myth–ritual equation was established by the eleventh–twelfth centuries. Toshio Akima cites both manuals in his study of the cave narrative and *chinkonsai*. [Akima study](https://religion-in-japan.univie.ac.at/k/img_auth.php/0/0d/Akima_toshio_1993.pdf)

### Named corpora in which secure direct evidence is absent or limited

- **Provincial *Fudoki*:** no surviving early *Fudoki* supplies a substantial independent Uzume myth comparable to the *Kojiki* or *Nihon shoki*. Later quotations and local histories connect her and Sarutahiko with Ise and Hyūga, but their transmission dates must be stated individually. An Ise municipal chronology, for example, cites later works quoting lost *Ise fudoki* material; this is not an extant eighth-century manuscript. [Ise historical chronology](https://www.city.ise.mie.jp/_res/projects/default_project/_page_/001/018/858/kodai_kaniban.pdf)
- **Man’yōshū:** it supplies valuable evidence about early ritual vocabulary, plants, dress, Ise, and performance, but no secure independent narrative centered on Ame-no-Uzume has been identified.
- **Norito:** the surviving *Engishiki* prayers illuminate the court’s ritual language but do not constitute an independent Uzume hymn.
- **Konjaku monogatari shū:** no secure direct Uzume narrative comparable to the early myths has been located. Later scholarship connects a story about neighboring Kyoto epidemic and roadside shrines with sites eventually said to enshrine Sarutahiko and Uzume; that is evidence for medieval cult topography and conflation, not a fresh Uzume myth.
- **Toriyama Sekien’s yōkai catalogues:** Sekien does not recast Uzume herself as a yōkai. His bell-bearing Suzuhiko-hime evokes the ancient Uzume/kagura story in its inscription. Treating this figure as Uzume is an early-modern allusion or creative derivation, not identity. 

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Early domains and actions

**Early attested:**

- inspired or possessed performance (*kamigakari*);
- dance, stamping, laughter, and ritual obscenity;
- effective speech addressed to a concealed deity;
- fearless confrontation and interrogation;
- revelation of a stranger’s name and intention;
- service to the heavenly descendant;
- coercing marine tribute;
- ancestral relation to the Sarume.

The texts do not call her sovereign of the dawn, theater, fertility, marriage, or happiness. These domains developed by inference from her actions.

### Ritual costume and objects

The *Kojiki* associates her with:

- heavenly clubmoss or moss sash;
- spindle-tree foliage worn at the head;
- bamboo leaves held in the hands;
- an overturned hollow vessel, tub, or sounding board;
- exposed breasts and genital region;
- stamping feet;
- divine possession.

The vessel is variously translated “tub,” “bucket,” “trough,” or “sounding board.” Later *chinkonsai* practice used an overturned *ukefune* struck or stamped in ritual, strengthening—but not proving—a direct continuity claim. [Japanese historical dictionary summary](https://japanknowledge.com/introduction/keyword.html?i=689)

### Powers and limitations

Uzume’s power is performative rather than coercively cosmic. She creates the social and emotional condition—laughter and curiosity—within which Amaterasu can be brought out, but she does not move the stone, manufacture the mirror, or devise the whole plan alone. Omoikane plans; Ishikoridome makes the mirror; Tamanoya produces jewels; Koyane and Futodama conduct ritual; Tajikarao physically draws Amaterasu out. Popular retellings that make Uzume single-handedly “save the world” compress this ensemble.

At the crossroads she does not defeat Sarutahiko in combat. She withstands him, questions him, learns his purpose, and reports it. The widespread story that she seduces him by flashing her breasts is an interpretive or comic retelling: exposure is textual, but seduction is not identified as its purpose in the early sources.

### Iconography

No securely identified eighth-century portrait of Uzume survives. Early kami cult did not require anthropomorphic cult statues of every named deity.

Recognizable later iconography includes:

- a dancing woman before Amaterasu’s cave;
- an overturned tub or platform beneath her feet;
- bamboo or leafy branches;
- disordered or lifted clothing;
- assembled laughing kami;
- a mirror and jeweled sakaki;
- a cock or long-crowing bird;
- Tajikarao at the rock door.

Important documented works include:

- **Totoya Hokkei, *Uzume no Mikoto from The Cave Door of Spring*, 1825:** a surimono in the Cleveland Museum of Art; Uzume appears fully clothed, showing that erotic textual description did not dictate every image. [Cleveland Museum of Art](https://www.clevelandart.org/print/art/1940.990.3)
- **Tsukioka Yoshitoshi, *Amaterasu Kōtaijin*, 1879:** Meiji color triptych; Uzume dances in the center while Tajikarao opens the cave. The print reframes myth as an episode of national history. [British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_2014-3037-3-1-3)
- **Wada Yūjirō, *Origin of the Cave Door Dance*, 1889:** a Meiji triptych explicitly presenting Uzume’s act as a fertility dance, itself an interpretive label. [Museum of Applied Arts Vienna](https://sammlung.mak.at/en/collect/origin-of-the-cave-door-dance_196439)
- **Ikeda Taishin, *Otafuku*, Meiji era:** a hanging scroll whose museum description associates the popular Otafuku with the “jovial and voluptuous” Uzume. This is material evidence for the modern association, not for ancient identity. [National Museum of Asian Art](https://asia-archive.si.edu/object/F1997.20/)

### Animals, plants, colors, days, and celestial associations

- **Birds:** long-crowing birds are assembled in the cave ritual, but they are collective ritual instruments rather than Uzume’s exclusive sacred animal.
- **Plants:** clubmoss, spindle-tree, bamboo grass, and sakaki belong to the cave apparatus; only the first group is directly worn or held by Uzume.
- **Monkey:** derives indirectly from *Sarume* and Sarutahiko’s name. Neither early text makes Uzume a monkey goddess.
- **Sun/dawn:** she restores conditions under which the sun goddess appears, but she is not herself identified as the sun, Venus, a dawn star, or a calendrical dawn.
- **Colors, numbers, weekdays, planets:** no stable early system is attested. Modern correspondence lists are esoteric constructions.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

The earliest sources give Uzume no parents, siblings, birth story, or biological offspring. Her descent-group relationship is institutional: she is the ancestral deity of the Sarume-no-Kimi.

Sarutahiko is her closest named associate, but the early record does **not** plainly say “they married” or name children. The *Kojiki* orders her to accompany or “send off” Sarutahiko and bear his name. The *Nihon shoki* and *Kogo shūi* likewise connect their travel and the Sarume lineage. Later shrine and popular tradition turn proximity, escort, and shared cult into marriage.

The phrase usually translated “accompanied him on his return” can also be read in a funerary sense. A modern study argues that Uzume may be attending or dispatching Sarutahiko after his drowning, not returning from a honeymoon-like journey. [Founding Territorial Cults in Early Japan](https://brill.com/display/book/9789004686458/BP000016.pdf)

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. The heavenly rock chamber

**Early attested core.** Susanoo’s destructive acts in Amaterasu’s heavenly domain culminate in violence at the sacred weaving hall. Amaterasu withdraws into a rock chamber and seals it. Darkness and calamity follow. The gods assemble at the heavenly river, consult Omoikane, prepare birds, jewels, a mirror, offerings, and liturgy, and station Tajikarao at the entrance.

Uzume then turns ritual into spectacle. Her vegetation, stamping, possession, exposure, and the gods’ laughter make Amaterasu curious. Uzume tells her that a more august deity has appeared. The gods show Amaterasu the mirror; when she approaches, Tajikarao removes her from the chamber, and a boundary-rope prevents her return.

The scene does not say Uzume merely dances “half-naked.” The *Kojiki* describes exposed breasts and the lowering of clothing to the genital region. The exact degree of exposure differs with translation, syntax, and later pictorial modesty.

**Variants.** Some *Nihon shoki* alternatives omit Uzume and achieve Amaterasu’s return through prayers, sacred implements, or variant ritual arrangements. This demonstrates that the familiar dance was one authoritative strand, not the sole ancient form.

**Interpretive histories:**

- solar disappearance and return;
- eclipse or seasonal solstice myth;
- death-and-revival or tomb-opening ritual;
- imperial spirit-pacification charter;
- lineage charter for Sarume, Nakatomi, Inbe, mirror-makers, and jewel-makers;
- fertility performance;
- shamanic possession;
- comic inversion restoring social order.

None is stated by the eighth-century narrators as a comprehensive explanation. Breen and Teeuwen emphasize that the transmitted version maps court ritual offices into primordial myth and makes lineage service part of the divine foundation of sovereignty. [Rock Cave chapter from *A New History of Shinto*](https://is.muni.cz/el/phil/podzim2019/JPN313/um/Breen__Teeuwen_A_new_history_of_Shinto_The_Rock_Cave_Myth.pdf)

### B. Confronting Sarutahiko

At the eightfold heavenly crossroads appears an extraordinary being whose brilliance reaches heaven and earth. The heavenly company cannot look him in the eye. Uzume is selected to face him.

In the *Nihon shoki*/*Kogo shūi* strand, she bares her breasts, lowers her waistband, and laughs. Sarutahiko asks why. Rather than submitting, she interrogates him: who is he, why is he there, who will go first, and where will each party travel? He identifies himself and offers to guide Ninigi.

This is not a combat myth. Nor does the early record say her nudity magically incapacitates him or that she bargains through sexual intercourse. Those are embellishments of the confrontation’s sexual-comic tension.

### C. Escort, drowning, and the Sarume name

After the descent, Uzume is assigned to Sarutahiko because she revealed his identity. The *Kojiki* orders her to “bear his name” and calls her the ancestor of the Sarume-no-Kimi. The syntax leaves room for several historical interpretations:

- acquisition of a conquered or ritually mastered being’s power/name;
- an etiology of an existing Sarume office;
- incorporation of an Ise group into the Yamato court order;
- cult partnership between a crossroads/territorial god and female officiants.

Sarutahiko later drowns when his hand is caught by a shellfish. Uzume’s role around his departure can be translated as accompanying, sending off, or funerary dispatch. Later tradition suppresses this ambiguity and makes them a married couple.

### D. Submission of the fishes

Uzume assembles the fishes and asks whether they will serve the heavenly descendant. They assent, but the sea cucumber remains silent. Uzume slits its mouth, explaining its physical form. The episode functions simultaneously as:

- animal-form etiology;
- charter for marine tribute;
- assertion of heavenly sovereignty over coastal production;
- explanation of Sarume participation in distributions of first fruits from Shima.

The episode complicates her modern presentation as only joyful or benevolent: she can also punish nonresponse and enforce political-ritual submission.

### E. Ancestress of kagura

The early cave account never says, in a narrator’s voice, “this was the first kagura.” That genealogy develops through court ritual interpretation. By the *Kogo shūi* and medieval ceremonial manuals, Uzume’s performance had become an origin model for Sarume and ritual performance. Modern shrine discourse extends this to kagura, dance, acting, Noh, theater, and entertainment generally.

A modern ethnomusicological study describes the historical movement from spirit-possession toward ritual theater and treats Uzume’s dance as an origin claim rather than a documentary description of one unchanged performance tradition. [Yearbook for Traditional Music study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/yearbook-for-traditional-music/article/abs/from-spirit-possession-to-ritual-theatrea-potential-scenario-for-the-development-of-japanese-kagura/A31EA1A130A73796C7267072B59AECBF)

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Sarume and the court

The strongest early cultic evidence is not an excavated temple dedicated solely to Uzume but the existence of the Sarume as hereditary court performers. Texts link their office to:

- spirit-pacification;
- imperial enthronement and seasonal rites;
- dance and inspired performance;
- food and marine tribute;
- reproduction of the cave action through an overturned vessel.

The myths served as charters for hereditary status. This does not prove that a historical woman named Uzume founded the group; it shows that the eighth-century court represented the Sarume office through a divine ancestress.

The *Kogo shūi* complains about the decline or displacement of old hereditary specialists. Later records indicate that Sarume women continued to be supplied to the court, while the lineage’s distinct ritual dominance eventually disappeared. A historical compilation records that by the enthronement of Emperor Go-Saga in the thirteenth century no Sarume officiant served, presenting the ancient custom as lapsed. [Later historical compilation](https://miko.org/~uraki/kuon/furu/text/dainihonsi/dns266.htm)

### Chinkonsai

In court *chinkonsai* (“pacification” or “stabilization of the spirit”), female ritualists and Sarume danced while an overturned vessel was struck. Medieval sources interpreted this as repetition of Uzume’s cave performance. Scholars disagree about direction:

- the myth may preserve or legitimate an existing rite;
- the court rite may have been remodeled to imitate a prestigious myth;
- both may reflect an older cluster of possession, stamping, and royal-renewal practices.

The claim that every later kagura dance is an unbroken survival of Uzume’s dance cannot be demonstrated.

### Shrines

#### Sarume Shrine, Ise

The present Sarume Shrine is a subordinate shrine within Sarutahiko Shrine in Ise. It enshrines Uzume as ancestral kami of performance, kagura, skill, and *chinkon*, and is especially frequented by entertainers and athletes. The shrine also promotes her as a deity of good relationships because she mediated between the heavenly and earthly parties. These are living cultic claims, not neutral descriptions of eighth-century doctrine. [Official shrine page](https://www.sarutahikojinja.or.jp/sarume/)

Its current economy includes talismans and amulets for entertainment and good relationships. [Official shrine offerings](https://www.sarutahikojinja.or.jp/jyuyohin/)

#### Aratate Shrine, Takachiho

Aratate Shrine enshrines Sarutahiko and Uzume together. Local tradition says that they married and hurriedly built a dwelling from freshly cut timber, explaining the shrine’s name. The shrine is associated with marriage, harmonious couples, artistic success, and kagura. This is a regional shrine legend; the early descent texts do not locate their marriage or house there. [Takachiho Tourism Association](https://www.takachiho-kanko.info/sightseeing/7/)

Its summer festival includes formal worship, kagura, martial display, and a children’s procession, documenting continuing ritual rather than a reconstructed ancient ceremony. [Festival description](https://www.takachiho-kanko.info/event/24/)

#### Other enshrinements

Uzume is frequently enshrined:

- alongside Sarutahiko in roadside, guidance, and matchmaking contexts;
- as an auxiliary performance kami at theatrical and local shrines;
- at shrines associated with the heavenly rock-cave story;
- under localized names or alongside Ōmiyanome.

A shrine’s claim to be her “original shrine” (*motomiya*) is a sectarian or local tradition unless supported by datable records. No excavated eighth-century sanctuary can securely be identified as her unique original temple.

### Offerings and benefits

Modern offerings include kagura, dance, songs, stage performances, votive tablets, sake, food, leafy branches, and conventional Shinto offerings. Petitioners seek:

- artistic proficiency;
- success in acting, music, comedy, or dance;
- confidence and public expression;
- good relationships and marriage;
- spirit-pacification and emotional vitality.

There is no documented Uzume cult of human sacrifice or temple prostitution. Her ritual nudity is literary and performative evidence; it is not evidence for institutional sacred prostitution. Claims otherwise belong to obsolete comparativism or unsourced sexualized retelling.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before 712: reconstructable background, not direct attestation

Court performers, female mediums, spirit-possession, obscene or laughter-producing ritual, vegetation, drums or hollow vessels, and lineage-based ritual offices probably predate the written myths. But Uzume herself is not securely named in surviving pre-712 material. Any “prehistoric Uzume cult” is reconstruction.

### 712–807: canon formation

The *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* place her in the imperial mythic order and attach her to the Sarume. Differences between their variants show active selection rather than a single fixed oral scripture. The *Kogo shūi* then turns her more explicitly into a charter for hereditary ritual claims.

### Ninth–twelfth centuries: ritual systematization

Court regulations and ceremonial manuals correlate Sarume performance, overturned vessels, spirit-pacification, and the cave myth. Uzume becomes not merely a narrative actor but the divine precedent for ritual office. The process is interpretive and institutional.

### Medieval period: kami-Buddhist religious culture

Uzume persisted within a religious field of shrine-temple institutions, Buddhist cosmology, imperial rites, local gods, and professional performance. Kuroda Toshio’s influential critique warns against projecting a self-contained, unchanging “Shinto religion” backward over this history. Later scholars have qualified Kuroda’s strongest formulations but retain his central insight: kami worship, court ritual, Buddhist institutions, and local cults were historically interwoven. [Discussion of Kuroda’s intervention](https://nirc.nanzan-u.ac.jp/journal/6/article/1091/pdf/download)

No widely accepted medieval *honji-suijaku* equation assigns Uzume one stable Buddha as her universal original ground. Local equations may have existed, but a pan-Japanese Buddhist identity comparable to Hachiman’s or Amaterasu’s is not securely documented.

### Early modern period: theater, kokugaku, masks, and folk auspiciousness

Noh and kyōgen theory increasingly placed theatrical history within mythic genealogies. Zeami’s learned wordplay relating *sarugaku* and *kagura* is an origin theory, not linguistic proof.

Okame/Otafuku developed as a comic mask and auspicious female image with a round face, small features, full cheeks, and a smiling expression. Because Uzume was the primordial comic female performer, later artists and popular religion linked them. The sequence is therefore:

1. early Uzume myth;
2. medieval/early-modern performance and mask traditions;
3. association or identification of their comic female face with Uzume;
4. modern presentation of Otafuku as simply “another name” for her.

The Smithsonian’s Meiji-period *Otafuku* scroll documents the association while still describing Otafuku as a popular deity “associated with” Uzume. [National Museum of Asian Art](https://asia-archive.si.edu/object/F1997.20/)

### Kokugaku

Motoori Norinaga’s *Kojiki-den* 古事記伝 (completed 1798) restored the *Kojiki* to exceptional authority through philological reconstruction. His method strongly shaped later readings of Uzume’s Old Japanese vocabulary.

Hirata Atsutane attempted to reconcile or reconstruct an authentic ancient system from differing texts. Claims produced by this reconstruction are learned early-modern theology, not additional ancient witnesses. The Kokugakuin manuscript history notes Atsutane’s effort to derive a “correct” ancient account from differences between the *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki*. [Kokugakuin collection account](https://jmapps.ne.jp/kokugakuin/det.html?data_id=78816)

Atsutane and later writers also associated Hieda no Are with the Sarume and sometimes argued that Are was female and descended from Uzume. The evidence is late and inferential; neither the *Kojiki* preface nor an early genealogy securely establishes Are’s sex or such descent.

### Meiji–1945: State Shinto and national myth

The Meiji state separated shrines institutionally from Buddhism, ranked shrines, promoted imperial mythology, and treated shrine rites as public or national rather than “religious.” Uzume’s cave episode consequently appeared in education, art, national-history illustration, and accounts of the divine origins of imperial order.

Yoshitoshi’s 1879 print presents the cave opening in a series of “significant episodes” of Japanese history, illustrating how myth became national history in modern visual culture. This political framework centered Amaterasu and imperial descent; Uzume participated as a loyal ritual servant rather than becoming a central imperial cult deity.

There is no evidence that Uzume herself became a distinctive emblem of wartime extremist organizations. Her myths nonetheless belonged to the state-promoted imperial corpus. Conflating this general ideological use with a specific mass Uzume movement would exceed the evidence.

### 1945–present: disestablishment and voluntary shrine religion

The 15 December 1945 Shinto Directive ended state sponsorship and official propagation of shrine ideology. Postwar constitutional arrangements treated shrine organizations as private religious bodies. The Association of Shinto Shrines was established in February 1946 and eventually affiliated tens of thousands of shrines. [Kokugakuin institutional history](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=8874)

Uzume worship continued in local and voluntary shrine settings. Contemporary cult especially emphasizes entertainment, joy, skill, personal relationships, and female agency. These emphases partly continue the early stories and partly answer modern devotional needs.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Otafuku/Okame

The Otafuku face is the most important modern visual fusion:

- **attested relationship:** modern museums, shrines, and popular religion explicitly associate it with Uzume;
- **not established:** that the eighth-century authors imagined Uzume with the standardized round Otafuku face;
- **historical mechanism:** the primordial comic performer was retroactively given the face of later comic and auspicious performance traditions.

“Okame,” “Otafuku,” “Ofuku,” and “Uzume” may consequently be treated as interchangeable in commercial folk art, but historical analysis must keep their origins distinct.

### Art and nationalism

Edo and Meiji artists repeatedly clothed or partially clothed Uzume, transformed the hollow tub into a stage, added radiant light, and arranged the gods like a theatrical audience. Meiji works often converted a sexually disruptive possession performance into dignified national history.

### Literature, comics, animation, and games

Modern entertainment commonly recasts her as:

- a beautiful dawn goddess;
- a flirtatious or comic dancer;
- Sarutahiko’s unequivocal wife;
- a patroness of entertainers;
- a combat-capable “goddess” or summon;
- a member of a fixed RPG pantheon.

The *Shin Megami Tensei* and *Persona* franchises have been especially influential internationally, presenting Ame-no-Uzume as a collectible or summonable supernatural entity within a comparative demon system. This is modern game taxonomy, not Japanese historical classification.

Modern manga and anime use Takachiho and Aratate Shrine as places where performance, celebrity, marriage, and Uzume’s myths converge. Such works can materially redirect tourism and votive practice but do not become primary witnesses for ancient belief.

### Esoteric, neopagan, and psychological reception

English-language neopagan and “goddess spirituality” treatments often assign her dawn, joy, sacred sexuality, body confidence, fertility, feminist liberation, meditation, and creative authenticity. These can be sincere modern devotional interpretations. They are not ancient epithets.

No evidence places Uzume in the canonical systems of Renaissance Hermeticism, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, or premodern European grimoires. Modern occult correspondences connecting her to tarot cards, planets, chakras, or Western goddesses are twentieth- or twenty-first-century inventions.

Jungian readings interpret the cave as psychic withdrawal and Uzume as comic, erotic, trickster, or liberating archetype. These are modern psychological hermeneutics, not recovered Japanese doctrine.

### New religious movements

Some modern Japanese new religions incorporate Uzume through revelation, possession, or re-readings of national mythology. These claims must be dated to the movement and revelation rather than projected into antiquity. Their existence shows that divine identity in Japan remains generative, not frozen at 712.

### Commercial use

Her name and imagery occur in:

- dance and theater organizations;
- cosmetics, food, sake, and entertainment branding;
- tourism at Ise and Takachiho;
- amulets for artistic success;
- games, character merchandise, and fantasy art.

Commercial presentation tends to omit the sea-cucumber punishment, political submission, contested Sarume history, and explicit genital exposure.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Myth preceding ritual or ritual preceding myth?

Older scholarship often read the cave story as a survival of prehistoric solar ritual. More historically cautious approaches ask how the eighth-century court used myth to legitimate known offices.

Breen and Teeuwen argue that the story maps hereditary ritual lineages onto Amaterasu’s service. They further suggest that Sarutahiko may have been closer to the original object of Sarume worship in Ise and that Uzume’s present role reflects incorporation into an emergent court solar cult. This is a modern reconstruction, not textual fact. [Breen and Teeuwen chapter](https://is.muni.cz/el/phil/podzim2019/JPN313/um/Breen__Teeuwen_A_new_history_of_Shinto_The_Rock_Cave_Myth.pdf)

### Solar, funerary, and spirit-renewal theories

Matsumura Takeo and others treated the cave as a tomb and Amaterasu’s emergence as revival. Scholars have compared the episode to eclipse, solstice, seasonal renewal, funerary laughter, and royal soul-reinforcement. The eleventh-century ritual evidence shows that court intellectuals connected it to *chinkonsai*, but it cannot independently prove what the seventh-century narrators originally intended.

### Shamanism

Uzume’s *kamigakari*, inspired speech, rhythmic stamping, vegetation, laughter, and bodily exposure make “shamanic” comparison attractive. Hori Ichirō and later scholars placed her within histories of Japanese possession and female mediumship. “Shaman” remains an analytic comparative category, however; the early texts do not call her by a single profession equivalent to the modern anthropological term.

### Sexuality and fertility

Her exposure is securely textual. Fertility interpretation is plausible because exposure, laughter, solar return, and the later phallic characterization of Sarutahiko form a coherent complex. But neither early chronicle explicitly calls the act a fertility rite, depicts intercourse, or says Uzume became pregnant. “Temple prostitution” theories have no documentary support here.

### The Sarutahiko marriage problem

Later shrine traditions identify Sarutahiko and Uzume as husband and wife, and Aratate Shrine localizes their home. The earliest texts supply association, escort, name-transfer, and shared lineage explanation—not a wedding formula, named child, or consummation. “Husband and wife” should therefore be labeled later canonical/regional tradition.

### Why “dawn goddess” is weakly attested

The phrase is widespread because Uzume’s action leads to the sun’s return and the cave rite employs long-crowing birds. Yet:

- she does not personify dawn in the texts;
- no dawn liturgy addressed to her is known;
- no early calendar assigns her the morning;
- no celestial genealogy makes her a dawn body.

The title is a reasonable poetic interpretation but a poor historical definition.

### “First stripper” and “sexual trickster”

These are modern journalistic labels. They isolate exposure from possession, costume, speech, lineage politics, and the collaborative rite. Conversely, sanitizing the exposure into an innocuous dance also falsifies the sources.

### Otafuku identity

The Otafuku/Okame identification is real as early-modern and modern reception. It is not ancient. Museum objects and mask traditions document the later association; no *Kojiki* manuscript supplies the face.

### Kuroda and the category “Shinto”

Kuroda Toshio argued that an autonomous indigenous “Shinto” stretching continuously from prehistory was largely a modern construction; medieval kami worship normally operated within the Buddhist *kenmitsu* system. Later historians have criticized an overly totalizing Buddhist model, but few return to a simple timeless Shinto. Accordingly, calling the eighth-century Uzume straightforwardly a “Shinto goddess” is convenient modern taxonomy, not the terminology of a fully formed denominational system.

### Primary evidence and losses

- No autograph *Kojiki* or *Nihon shoki* survives.
- The Shinpukuji *Kojiki* is separated from composition by more than six centuries.
- The earliest surviving *Nihon shoki* manuscript is ninth century but does not contain the divine-age books; those survive in medieval copies.
- Most provincial *Fudoki* are lost.
- Performance is ephemeral: texts record the ideological shape of ritual better than actual choreography.
- Early wooden shrine buildings were rebuilt, altered, or lost, preventing direct identification of an original Uzume sanctuary.
- Later shrine records often mix datable institutional history with mythic foundation dates.

## 11. Sources and assessment

### Primary texts and editions

- Ō no Yasumaro, *Kojiki* (712); Donald L. Philippi, trans., *Kojiki* (1968/69).
- Prince Toneri and compilers, *Nihon shoki* (720); W. G. Aston, trans., *Nihongi* (1896).
- Inbe no Hironari, *Kogo shūi* (807).
- *Sendai kuji hongi*, probably ninth century.
- *Engishiki* (completed 927).
- *Hokuzanshō*, early eleventh century.
- *Gōke shidai* (1111).
- Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den* (completed 1798).
- Hirata Atsutane, *Koshi seibun* and related works.

### Modern scholarship

Particularly important are Kuroda Toshio’s critique of “Shinto” as a timeless category; Hori Ichirō’s work on Japanese folk religion and shamanism; Breen and Teeuwen’s historical analysis of the rock-cave myth; Toshio Akima on court ritual and underworld/solar interpretation; and recent Japanese literary studies of Uzume’s speech, exposure, laughter, *kamigakari*, and *wazaogi*. Kokugakuin’s Japanese deity database gives a useful bibliography including work by Tada Hajime, Tanaka Tomoki, Fujisawa Tomoyoshi, Yoshida Shūsaku, Inomata Tokiwa, and Inoue Sayaka.

The central documentary conclusion is narrow but firm: the early Uzume is a female heavenly kami whose power lies in possession-performance, laughter, speech, confrontation, and lineage-making. “Dawn goddess,” Sarutahiko’s unambiguous wife, universal mother of theater, and Otafuku herself are historically later constructions. They are authentic parts of her reception history, but not interchangeable with the evidence of 712 and 720.
