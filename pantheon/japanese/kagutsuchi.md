# Kagutsuchi: Research Dossier

**Class:** *kami* (神), specifically a fire deity; grammatically and narratively usually treated as male in the early court mythologies, although one anatomical detail in the *Kojiki* has prompted scholarly debate about gender.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the *Kojiki*, completed and presented to the court in **712 CE**, where the deity appears as **火之夜藝速男神**, **火之炫毘古神**, and **火之迦具土神**. The earliest surviving complete manuscript is much later—the Shinpukuji copy of 1371–1372—so 712 dates the compilation, not an extant physical witness. The *Nihon shoki* of **720 CE** independently preserves several variant accounts under **軻遇突智**, **火産霊**, and “the fire god.” No securely dated inscription or image naming Kagutsuchi is presently known to predate these texts.

**Evidentiary labels used below:** **[Early canonical]** eighth-century court compilation; **[Early variant]** an alternate early account preserved in such a compilation; **[Heian ritual]** court liturgy or register compiled in the ninth–tenth centuries; **[Medieval syncretism]** Buddhist–kami or mountain-religious formation; **[Early-modern cult]** Tokugawa-period development; **[Meiji systematization]** identification or restructuring produced through separation of kami and buddhas and the modern shrine state; **[Folklore]** orally or locally transmitted practice; **[Modern scholarship]** an analytical reconstruction rather than an ancient statement; **[Modern invention/retelling]** a recent creative attribution.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Kagutsuchi is the destructive and productive fire *kami* at the hinge of the Japanese divine-generation narrative. In the *Kojiki*, Izanami’s delivery of him burns her genitals and leads to her death; Izanagi then decapitates him with a ten-hand-span sword, and numerous sword, thunder, water, and mountain deities arise from his blood and dismembered body. The *Nihon shoki* preserves divergent traditions: Kagutsuchi may kill Izanami simply by being born; marry the earth goddess Haniyamahime and father Wakumusubi; be cut into three or five pieces; or have his blood explain why stones, trees, and grasses contain fire. The *Engishiki* fire-pacification liturgy makes the dangerous fire-child the object of ritual control through water, gourd, river vegetation, and clay. Kagutsuchi is therefore not merely “a volcano god” or “god of blacksmiths.” Those are interpretations or extensions; the primary category is dangerous and generative fire.

### Names

- **火之迦具土神 — Hi-no-Kagutsuchi-no-kami**, “Fire-Kagutsuchi deity.” **[Early canonical: *Kojiki*, 712]**
- **迦具土神 — Kagutsuchi-no-kami.** Short form used in commentary and later worship.
- **火之夜藝速男神 — Hi-no-Yagihayao-no-kami.** Conventionally understood as a masculine fire name, though the internal etymology of *yagi* is uncertain. **[Early canonical: *Kojiki*, 712]**
- **火之炫毘古神 — Hi-no-Kakabiko-no-kami**, approximately “shining/blazing fire-prince.” **[Early canonical: *Kojiki*, 712]**
- **軻遇突智 / 軻遇突智命 — Kagutsuchi / Kagutsuchi-no-mikoto.** Phonographic spelling in the *Nihon shoki*. **[Early canonical and early variants, 720]**
- **火産霊 — Homusubi or Ho-musubi**, “fire-producing/generative spirit.” *Musuhi* denotes generative or becoming power. **[Early variant: *Nihon shoki*, 720]**
- **火結神 — Homusubi-no-kami**, “fire-joining/binding deity,” in the fire-pacification *norito*. **[Heian ritual: *Engishiki*, presented 927]**
- **軻遇槌 — Kagutsuchi.** Spelling in a fragment attributed to the lost *Mino fudoki*. Its survival only through later quotation makes the exact date and wording less secure than the extant *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki*. **[Early regional fragment, transmission uncertain]**
- **香都知神 — Katsuchi/Kagutsuchi deity.** Preserved in the *Engishiki* shrine register in the name **香都知神社**, Kii Province, Nagusa District. **[Heian cult register, 927]**
- **火之産霊迦具突智**, **火焼速男命神**, **火火焼炭神**, **軻遇突智**, and **迦具突智** occur in the *Sendai kuji hongi* textual tradition. That work’s transmitted form is generally placed in the early Heian period, although it presents itself as older; it cannot automatically be treated as pre-*Kojiki* evidence. **[Early-medieval learned systematization]**
- Modern shrines commonly use **火之迦具土大神 — Hi-no-Kagutsuchi-no-Ōkami**, **火産霊命 — Homusubi-no-Mikoto**, or **迦遇槌命**. These often reflect modern shrine registers and Meiji-era reassignment rather than an unchanged ancient dedication.

### Etymology

The final elements are comparatively transparent: **-tsu** is commonly read as an attributive/genitival particle, and **-chi** as a numinous force or spirit, as in other divine names. The meaning of **kagu** is disputed.

1. **Motoori Norinaga’s interpretation:** *kagu* belongs with *kagayaku*, “to shine.” **[Early-modern philology: *Kojiki-den*, eighteenth century]**
2. **Luminous/flickering interpretation:** *kagu* is connected with *kagayō*, *kage*, *kaguya*, or *kagerō*, suggesting wavering light or flame. **[Modern philological reconstruction]**
3. **Fragrance/smoke interpretation:** *kagu* is related to *kanbashii*, “fragrant,” referring to the odor produced by burning. The spellings with **香**, “fragrance,” are cited in support. **[Modern philological reconstruction]**
4. **Kurano Kenji’s objection:** Old Japanese *kagayaku* had the form *kakayaku*, and no secure ancient example establishes *kagu* as “shine”; moreover that reading would make Kagutsuchi nearly synonymous with the immediately preceding name Kakabiko. **[Modern scholarly critique]**

No etymology is universally accepted. Claims that the name demonstrably means “incarnation of fire,” “flame controller,” “shining earth,” or “lava god” exceed the linguistic evidence.

### Gender and classification

The elements **-o** (“male”) in Yagihayao and **hiko** (“prince/male”) in Kakabiko make masculine classification explicit in the *Kojiki*. Yet one deity, Kurayamatsumi, is born from Kagutsuchi’s **陰**, conventionally read *hoto*, often “female genitals.” Proposed solutions include reading the graph as hidden parts, male genitals, or a gender-neutral anatomical region; another hypothesis regards it as a vestige of an originally female fire being. None is proven. The early text does not supply a stable anthropomorphic body, portrait, personality, or speaking role.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### *Kojiki* — 712

Ō no Yasumaro’s preface dates presentation of the work to 712. Donald L. Philippi’s critical English translation appeared in 1968. Kagutsuchi occurs in the upper scroll’s divine-generation sequence.

The text states that Izanagi and Izanami produced the boat deity Torinoiwakusufune, the food deity Ōgetsuhime, and then Hi-no-Yagihayao, also called Hi-no-Kakabiko and Hi-no-Kagutsuchi. In delivering the fire deity, Izanami’s genitals are burned. While sick she generates deities from vomit, feces, and urine; she then “divinely retires.” Izanagi weeps, buries her, draws his ten-hand-span sword, and cuts off Kagutsuchi’s neck. Eight deities arise from blood on different parts of the sword and eight mountain deities from portions of the corpse. **[Early canonical]**

This is the fullest integrated ancient narrative, but not necessarily the oldest form of every component. Kokugakuin’s textual analysis observes that it appears to assemble motifs found separately among the *Nihon shoki* variants. The history of the underlying oral or written traditions remains unrecoverable.

### *Nihon shoki* — 720

William George Aston’s English translation, *Nihongi*, appeared in 1896. The first scroll juxtaposes a principal narrative and numerous **一書**, “one writing says,” rather than enforcing one account.

- **Main narrative:** Kagutsuchi’s birth burns Izanami and causes her death. **[Early canonical]**
- **Variant 2:** Izanami gives birth to Kagutsuchi; while dying she produces Haniyamahime and Mizuhanome. Kagutsuchi marries Haniyamahime; their child is Wakumusubi, from whose head come silkworms and mulberry and from whose navel the five grains. **[Early variant]**
- **Variant 3:** the deity is Homusubi. The dying Izanami produces Mizuhanome, Haniyamahime, and a heavenly gourd—materials capable of checking fire. **[Early variant]**
- **Variant 4:** Izanami becomes feverish while delivering Kagutsuchi; her vomit becomes Kanayamahiko, urine becomes Mizuhanome, and feces becomes Haniyamahime. **[Early variant]**
- **Variant 5:** Izanami dies and is buried at Arima in Kumano, Kii, where seasonal floral worship is described. This passage concerns Izanami’s tomb cult, not demonstrably a Kagutsuchi shrine. **[Early regional variant]**
- **Variant 6:** Izanagi cuts Kagutsuchi; blood produces divine beings associated with rocks, swords, and Futsunushi. Other portions become thunder, mountain, and water beings. **[Early variant]**
- **Variant 7:** Kagutsuchi is cut into three pieces, which become Ikazuchi, Ōyamatsumi, and Takaokami. **[Early variant]**
- **Variant 8:** he is cut into five pieces, producing five mountain deities; the splashed blood stains rocks, plants, and trees, explaining why they naturally contain fire. **[Early variant and explicit fire-origin myth]**

The *Nihon shoki* thus contradicts any claim that one classical genealogy was universally canonical.

### *Fudoki*

The provincial gazetteers were commissioned beginning in 713. The extant major *fudoki* do not furnish a securely identifiable Kagutsuchi narrative comparable to the two chronicles. A later-preserved fragment attributed to the *Mino fudoki* names **軻遇槌**, but its fragmentary transmission prevents strong conclusions. **Absence finding:** no verified Kagutsuchi episode has been located in the surviving *Izumo*, *Harima*, *Hitachi*, *Hizen*, or *Bungo fudoki* texts.

### *Man’yōshū*

The eighth-century anthology contains abundant fire, mountain, and Kagu-yama imagery, but no securely identified poem narrating or invoking Kagutsuchi under his attested divine names was located. Similar-looking forms involving **Kagu-yama** are not evidence for Kagutsuchi. **Absence finding.**

### *Kogo shūi* — 807

Inbe no Hironari’s lineage and ritual memorial is important for early court ritual and competing sacerdotal claims. No independent Kagutsuchi narrative comparable to those of the *Kojiki*, *Nihon shoki*, and fire-pacification *norito* was verified. It should not be cited generically as if every divine-generation episode appeared in it. **Absence finding.**

### *Engishiki* — compiled 905–927, presented 927

Two components matter:

- The **Jinmyōchō** or shrine-name register lists **Katsuchi/Kagutsuchi Shrine (香都知神社)** in Nagusa District, Kii. This is direct evidence that a cult site bearing the name had official recognition by the early tenth century, although it does not reveal the shrine’s foundation date, icon, or complete ritual.
- The **Fire-Pacification Festival Norito (鎮火祭祝詞)** retells the birth of Homusubi. Izanami, having left a dangerous child in the upper world, produces or appoints the water deity, gourd, river vegetation, and Haniyamahime/clay to pacify him when he rages. The Urabe performed the rite at the four outer corners of the imperial palace on the last days of the sixth and twelfth months. **[Heian ritual]**

This liturgy is the clearest early link between the mythic fire-child and an operational fire-prevention rite.

### Later literary and visual corpora

No significant Kagutsuchi episode was verified in the *Konjaku monogatari-shū* or in Toriyama Sekien’s eighteenth-century illustrated yōkai catalogues. Sekien catalogued anomalous creatures; Kagutsuchi was a classical *kami*, not a standard *yōkai*. Lists that imply Sekien supplied Kagutsuchi’s canonical appearance confuse two different corpora. **Absence finding.**

Likewise, no early Ise, Izumo, or Usa shrine record was found making Kagutsuchi a central deity of those institutions. Those archives are indispensable for Japanese religious history, but relevance cannot be assumed merely because Kagutsuchi belongs to court mythology.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Secure domains

- **Fire itself:** explicit in both eighth-century chronicles.
- **Fire’s lethal danger:** the deity’s birth burns and kills Izanami.
- **Fire’s latent presence in natural materials:** *Nihon shoki* variant 8 explains fire in rocks, trees, and plants through Kagutsuchi’s blood.
- **Fire control and prevention:** made explicit by the *Engishiki* fire-pacification liturgy and later Atago and Akiba institutions.
- **Productive transformation through heat:** metallurgy, clay working, cultivation, sericulture, and grain have been read from the gods produced around the episode. This is a defensible structural interpretation, not a textual list of offices granted to Kagutsuchi.

### Modernly exaggerated domains

“God of blacksmiths,” “god of volcanoes,” and “god of warriors” are not formal offices assigned in the early texts. Metallurgical gods arise in the birth/death complex, and volcanic and fire-drill interpretations are scholarly theories. Warrior functions entered Atago devotion through Shōgun Jizō and military patronage, not through a direct classical statement that Kagutsuchi governed war.

### Form and iconography

The early texts provide **no stable visual description**—no skin color, number of arms, costume, animal, vehicle, emblem, or facial type. No securely identified Nara-period image of Kagutsuchi is known. This negative evidence is important.

Later shrines generally enshrine the *kami* aniconically through a *shintai*, mirror, sword, tablet, or inaccessible sacred object rather than a public anthropomorphic portrait. Images encountered in Atago and Akiba contexts frequently depict:

- **Shōgun Jizō**, the armored or martial bodhisattva;
- **Atago Tarōbō**, a tengu-like mountain figure;
- **Akiba Sanjakubō**, commonly a winged or tengu-like ascetic, sometimes standing upon a white fox.

These are products of medieval and early-modern combinatory religion. They are not ancient portraits of the being described in the *Kojiki*.

### Symbols and substances

Securely associated items are contextual, not universal insignia:

- **Fire/flame:** intrinsic domain.
- **Ten-hand-span sword:** Izanagi’s instrument against Kagutsuchi, not Kagutsuchi’s own weapon.
- **Blood:** generative substance producing other deities.
- **Water, gourd, river vegetation, and clay:** fire-pacifying materials in the *Nihon shoki* variants and *Engishiki norito*.
- **Rocks, trees, and grasses:** materials said to contain fire after contact with his blood.
- **Mountain pieces:** the dismembered body produces mountain gods.
- **Fire-protection talismans and sacred Japanese star anise (*shikimi*):** characteristic of later Atago distribution networks.
- **Swords offered at Akiba:** documented votive practice, but not evidence that the mythical Kagutsuchi originally bore a sword.

No ancient source securely assigns Kagutsuchi a sacred color, animal, planet, weekday, number, or zodiacal sign. Modern correspondence tables supplying these are **[Modern invention]** unless a specific late ritual source is named.

### Principal genealogies

|---|---|---|---|
| *Kojiki* (712) | Izanagi and Izanami | None stated | Gods arise from blood and corpse after Izanagi kills him |
| *Nihon shoki*, variant 2 (720) | Izanagi and Izanami | Haniyamahime | Wakumusubi |

The *Kojiki*’s corpse-born mountain gods should not be silently equated one-for-one with the three- or five-part deities in the *Nihon shoki*. The texts preserve different enumerations.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The fatal birth

**[Early canonical: *Kojiki*, 712]** Izanami’s divine generation culminates in the fire god. The fire burns her *hoto*, conventionally her genitals, and she lies ill. From her bodily emissions come metal, clay/earth, water, and agricultural deities. She then dies. The sequence connects childbirth, bodily pollution, death, and productive substances without explaining Kagutsuchi’s intentions; he neither attacks nor speaks.

**[Early canonical and variants: *Nihon shoki*, 720]** Some versions simply say that Izanami was burned and died. Others separate the production of earth, water, metal, gourd, and crops differently. Aston judged the silkworm-and-five-grains version comparatively late because of its Chinese-style formulation, but that is a nineteenth-century philological opinion, not an explicit ancient date.

### Izanagi’s vengeance and the divisible divine body

In the *Kojiki*, Izanagi’s grief becomes anger. He draws the sword worn at his side and cuts off his son’s neck. Blood landing on the sword’s tip, guard, and hilt becomes successive groups of deities. Eight mountain gods emerge from the head, chest, belly, genitals, hands, and feet.

The *Nihon shoki* does not reproduce this as a single uniform narrative. In different “one writing” accounts, Kagutsuchi is divided into three or five portions. Blood stains heavenly river rocks and produces deities including Futsunushi. One account explicitly interprets the blood as the reason stone, grass, and wood contain combustible fire.

The modern phrase “Izanagi cut Kagutsuchi into eight pieces” compresses incompatible versions. In the *Kojiki*, eight mountain deities correspond to body regions; the *Nihon shoki* has three- and five-piece variants.

### Marriage and agricultural generation

Only *Nihon shoki* variant 2 says that Kagutsuchi marries Haniyamahime. Their child Wakumusubi produces silkworms, mulberry, and grains from the body. The *Kojiki* instead makes Wakumusubi a product of Izanami’s urine during her illness. These are competing genealogies, not complementary stages.

### The ritual taming of fire

The *Engishiki* *norito* turns cosmogony into ritual precedent. Izanami realizes she has left a dangerous child in the upper country and supplies water, a gourd, river vegetation, and clay/earth for his pacification. Performed at the palace boundaries, the rite translates the myth into fire-prevention technology: water quenches; a gourd carries it; wet plants beat or contain flame; clay smothers it.

### Interpretive theories

All remain **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**:

- **Fire-drill theory:** the male drill and female hearth-board are sexualized; generated fire consumes its “mother.”
- **Volcanic theory:** burning birth and bodily dismemberment encode eruption and transformed landscape.
- **Swidden-agriculture theory:** controlled fire, soil, water, mountains, and crop generation reflect slash-and-burn cultivation.
- **Metallurgical/ceramic theory:** the birth of metal and clay beings expresses heat’s productive technologies.
- **Parturition-house theory:** the story reflects customs of burning or abandoning a birth hut.
- **Sword-ritual theory:** Izanagi’s blade magically suppresses dangerous fire.
- **Comparativist theories:** parallels have been proposed with Oceanic fire-origin myths, Hainuwele-type body-origin myths, the Chinese Pangu and fire-deity traditions, and the Vedic Puruṣa. Structural resemblance is not proof of borrowing. No Sanskrit, Persian, Akkadian, Sumerian, or Old Norse source is part of Kagutsuchi’s historical corpus.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Early court and registered cult

The strongest institutional evidence is:

1. **Fire-Pacification Festival:** performed by Urabe diviners at the imperial palace’s four outer corners at the ends of the sixth and twelfth months. Its text survives in *Engishiki* Book VIII. This was a court protection rite, not evidence for a mass nationwide Kagutsuchi church.
2. **香都知神社, Katsuchi/Kagutsuchi Shrine:** included in the *Engishiki* shrine register for Nagusa District, Kii. The entry proves official recognition by 927 but supplies no foundation legend or archaeological sequence.
3. **Domestic and occupational fire rites:** later hearth, kitchen, smithing, pottery, and fire-brigade devotions often invoke fire *kami*, but local names such as Kojin, Kamado-gami, Atago, and Akiba must not automatically be collapsed into Kagutsuchi.

No documented human sacrifice, temple prostitution, or sacrificial killing specific to Kagutsuchi was found in the examined ancient texts, shrine materials, or scholarship. Izanami’s death and Kagutsuchi’s killing are divine mythic violence, not records of human ritual sacrifice.

### Atago

Kyoto’s Mount Atago became a major fire-protection center. Its own foundation traditions place mountain opening in the Taihō era (701–704) and associate it with En no Gyōja and Taichō, but those are later institutional traditions, not contemporary foundation documents.

By the medieval period Atago was a Buddhist–kami mountain complex governed through **Hakuunji**, involving Tendai and Shingon elements, Shugendō ascetics, Atago Gongen/Tarōbō, and **Shōgun Jizō** as a principal Buddhist form. Warrior devotion emphasized victory; early-modern itinerant ascetics distributed fire-protection talismans and *shikimi* to households.

At the Meiji separation of kami and buddhas, Hakuunji was abolished, Buddhist elements were removed, and Shōgun Jizō was transferred to Konzōji. The modern Atago Shrine names Izanami and Kagutsuchi among its principal deities. This is genuine modern Kagutsuchi worship, but it should not be retrojected unchanged into the medieval complex. **[Medieval syncretism → Meiji restructuring]**

Tokyo’s Atago Shrine, founded in 1603 by order of Tokugawa Ieyasu according to its institutional history, venerates **Homusubi-no-Mikoto** as principal deity together with water, mountain, and martial deities. It burned in the 1923 earthquake and 1945 air raids and was rebuilt in 1958. **[Early-modern branch cult with modern reconstruction]**

### Akiba

Mount Akiba in present-day Hamamatsu became a powerful center of fire-protection religion. Premodern Akiba worship centered on **Akiba Sanjakubō Daigongen**, a mountain ascetic/tengu-like figure embedded in Shugendō and Sōtō Buddhist networks. It expanded nationally, especially in the early-modern period.

After the Meiji separation, the Buddhist temple and shrine institutions contested the inheritance. The shrine was reorganized around **Hi-no-Kagutsuchi-no-Ōkami**, while the Sanjakubō image was transferred to Kasuisai; Akihasan Shūyōji’s own institutional history explicitly disputes the shrine’s claim to continuity and states that Kagutsuchi was imported from Atago during the Meiji upheaval. The present Akihasan Hongū Akiha Jinja, conversely, describes Kagutsuchi as its ancient principal deity. These are competing institutional memories. The safest historical conclusion is that **the clear exclusive identification of the Akiba cult with Kagutsuchi is substantially a Meiji-era reclassification of an older combinatory fire-protection complex**.

The present shrine’s December festival includes bow, sword, and fire dances. Such practices are living Kagutsuchi worship, but their current attribution does not prove an eighth-century Kagutsuchi origin.

### Other sites

- Mukōjima Akiha Shrine traces a local Inari establishment to 1289 and says an early-modern monk installed an Akiba image; its present principal fire deity is Homusubi/Hi-no-Kagutsuchi, while an Edo-period Sanjakubō image remains. The layered dedication is visible rather than hypothetical.
- Beppu’s volcanic region preserves worship of male and female fire deities at Hinoko–Hime Shrine; later local identification includes Kagutsuchi. The older paired names and the *Engishiki* record should not simply be replaced by the single court-myth deity.
- Numerous local Atago, Akiba, and kamado shrines presently list Kagutsuchi. Each dedication needs shrine-specific dating; many were renamed or assigned canonical *Kiki* deities after 1868.

### Offerings and priesthood

No Kagutsuchi-specific hereditary priesthood or universal offering code is attested. Court rites used the Urabe; medieval Atago and Akiba propagation relied heavily on ascetics and temple networks; modern shrines employ ordinary shrine priests. Fire-prevention plaques, talismans, sacred branches, lamps, and votive swords are locally documented. Claims of a uniform ancient “Kagutsuchi priesthood” are modern invention.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before 712: unrecoverable prehistory

Fire use, hearth rites, mountain worship, metallurgy, and regional fire beings certainly predate the chronicles, but none can securely be called “Kagutsuchi” without a name-bearing source. Archaeological hearths or burned figurines are not self-identifying images of this deity.

### 712–720: court mythographic formation

The *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* place the fire deity within an imperial cosmogony. Their differences show that multiple traditions remained available. The figure explains mortality, latent fire, mountain and sword deities, and the productive/destructive ambiguity of heat.

### Ninth–tenth centuries: ritual and bureaucratic incorporation

The *Engishiki* preserves a court fire-calming rite and registers a Kii shrine with the deity’s name. Myth becomes a precedent for palace protection. This is the earliest firm evidence connecting the narrative figure to a recurring official ritual system.

### Medieval period: combinatory religion

Kagutsuchi did not simply remain the isolated protagonist of a court myth. Fire protection was practiced through temple-shrine complexes, mountain asceticism, local hearth gods, gongen, Jizō, tengu, and ritual specialists. Atago’s Shōgun Jizō/Tarōbō formation is exemplary. Kuroda Toshio’s critique is essential here: treating this world as timeless, autonomous “Shinto” obscures the Buddhist institutions within which many kami cults operated.

### Early-modern period

Urbanization and flammable wooden construction made fire prevention an urgent household and civic concern. Atago and Akiba talismans circulated widely through pilgrimage and itinerant networks. Military, domestic, and firefighting meanings accumulated. The Tokyo Atago foundation belongs to Tokugawa urban policy and patronage. This was a high point of fire-protection religion, though not necessarily of worship under the specific name Kagutsuchi.

### 1868–1945: separation and State Shinto

The separation of kami and buddhas dismantled complexes, abolished or converted temples, transferred images, and required shrines to present purified pantheons. Atago and Akiba institutions were reclassified; canonical names such as Kagutsuchi supplied acceptable “Shinto” identities where gongen, tengu, and Buddhist images had previously stood.

In 1871 the government described shrines as sites of state ritual, ended hereditary proprietary control, and constructed a ranked national shrine system. Later bureaucratic distinctions treated shrines as nonreligious state institutions while regulating other religions separately. Kagutsuchi was not a principal ideological deity comparable to Amaterasu, but shrines dedicated to him operated within this system. No evidence found makes Kagutsuchi a distinctive object of imperial militarist propaganda; general claims about State Shinto should not be personalized without shrine-level documentation.

### 1945–present

The 1945 Shinto Directive abolished the state shrine apparatus. Jinja Honchō was founded in February 1946 as a voluntary umbrella body; it reports that most of Japan’s approximately 80,000 shrines are affiliated. Atago, Akiba, and local fire shrines continue festivals, talisman distribution, fire-safety prayers, and household devotion.

This is living worship, but it is institutionally discontinuous from state administration and historically layered. Modern practitioners may understand Kagutsuchi as both beneficial energy and destructive flame; this ethical balance is largely modern explanatory theology built upon genuine classical ambiguity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Kokugaku

Motoori Norinaga’s eighteenth-century *Kojiki-den* treated the *Kojiki* as linguistically recoverable ancient tradition and proposed the “shining” etymology of *kagu*. Hirata Atsutane further systematized the divine world and treated Homusubi/Kagutsuchi as closely identified with the elemental reality of fire. These were historically influential learned readings, not transparent preservation of prehistoric doctrine.

### Folklore scholarship

Yanagita Kunio, including in *Tōno monogatari* (1910) and broader folklore work, redirected attention toward local spirits, household rites, and oral tradition. Hori Ichirō analyzed Japanese folk religion and its interaction with institutional forms. Neither licenses labeling every village fire rite “Kagutsuchi worship.” Local names, functions, and transmission histories remain primary.

Michael Dylan Foster’s *The Book of Yōkai* is useful for the modern construction of the yōkai category, but Kagutsuchi is not properly reduced to a yōkai. Toriyama Sekien likewise supplies no classical Kagutsuchi portrait.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated search found no evidence that Kagutsuchi held a defined role in Renaissance Hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or premodern European grimoires. Online systems assigning him chakras, planets, tarot cards, “demonic ranks,” or universal elemental correspondences are **[Modern invention]** unless they identify a recent author and date.

Contemporary non-Japanese polytheists and eclectic practitioners sometimes venerate Kagutsuchi through reconstructed prayers or internet devotional material. No reliable demographic estimate or centralized Kagutsuchi movement was located. Such worship is real as modern religion but cannot be projected backward as a continuous esoteric lineage.

### Popular culture

Modern works often use “Kagutsuchi” simply as a culturally resonant name for flame:

- *Naruto* uses Kagutsuchi for Sasuke’s manipulation of black Amaterasu flames. **[Modern fictional naming; not an ancient myth]**
- *Demon Slayer*’s *Hinokami Kagura* is often claimed online to be Kagutsuchi worship, but the title means “Dance of the Fire God”; the narrative does not establish the historical Kagutsuchi cult. **[Unverified popular identification]**
- *Shin Megami Tensei*, *Persona*, *Monster Strike*, *Noragami*, *Yu-Gi-Oh!*, and other games, manga, and anime employ Kagutsuchi or Hino-Kagu-Tsuchi as a character, power, boss, or collectible. Designs, weapons, temperaments, and cosmologies in these works are modern creative properties.
- The metal project Ibaraki released a song titled “Kagutsuchi.” This is reception, not cult evidence.

The now-common image of Kagutsuchi as an armored, sword-wielding male surrounded by lava is primarily modern illustration. The ancient texts give him no armor or personal sword.

### Political and extremist appropriation

Kagutsuchi belongs to a mythic corpus used in modern national education, but no well-documented extremist movement centered specifically on him was found. It would be misleading to transfer controversies surrounding Amaterasu, Yasukuni, or imperial descent onto Kagutsuchi merely by association.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Myth formation

A central debate concerns whether the *Kojiki* preserves the oldest coherent form or a comparatively late synthesis. Kokugakuin’s research survey notes that the *Kojiki* narrative combines motifs found separately among *Nihon shoki* variants—illness and excretions, mourning tears, burial, killing, blood births, and corpse births. This favors analysis of the two chronicles as distinct textual projects rather than fragments of one seamless “original myth.”

### What the myth encodes

Proposals include fire drilling, volcanic eruption, metallurgy, pottery, swidden agriculture, childbirth practices, and ritual fire suppression. Each explains part of the evidence; none commands consensus. A composite interpretation—multiple culturally important properties of fire organized into one court myth—is currently safer than choosing a single prehistoric event.

### Comparative mythology

Takagi Toshio and later comparativists examined fire-birth traditions across cultures. Hainuwele, Puruṣa, Pangu, Oceanic fire, and Chinese fire-deity comparisons illuminate recurring structures but have sometimes been presented as direct descent without demonstrable transmission. Resemblance alone cannot date a borrowing.

### Gender

Masculine names conflict with the *Kojiki* graph conventionally read as female genitals. Theories of scribal wording, broader anatomical meaning, and an earlier goddess remain open. No independent inscription or image resolves the question.

### “Shinto” as category

Kuroda Toshio’s 1981 article argued that an independent, organized, primordial Shinto was substantially a modern construction and that medieval kami worship operated within the exoteric-esoteric Buddhist establishment. Breen and Teeuwen refine rather than simply repeat this thesis: ancient myths, shrines, and kami rites existed, but their later bundling into one timeless religion must itself be historicized. This matters especially for Kagutsuchi because Atago and Akiba were profoundly combinatory before their Meiji presentation as shrines of a classical fire deity.

### Primary evidence and survival

- Original eighth-century *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* manuscripts do not survive.
- The *Kojiki*’s oldest complete surviving manuscript is medieval.
- The *Nihon shoki* survives through a complex manuscript and commentary tradition.
- Most provincial *fudoki* are lost; reported Kagutsuchi material in a *Mino fudoki* fragment therefore has weaker textual security.
- Shrine origin traditions often survive in documents produced centuries after the alleged foundation.
- Atago and Akiba material culture was altered by fires, warfare, Meiji separation, temple abolition, relocation of images, earthquake, and wartime bombing.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Kagutsuchi’s birth “introduced death into the universe”: an interpretive summary, not an explicit early statement.
- Izanagi always cut him into eight pieces: conflation of incompatible textual versions.
- Kagutsuchi is securely a volcano deity: scholarly hypothesis.
- He is the ancient patron of samurai or swordsmanship: later Atago military association retrojected into myth.
- Akiba was always exclusively a Kagutsuchi shrine: contradicted by the documented Sanjakubō Buddhist–Shugendō complex and Meiji reorganization.
- Sekien depicted Kagutsuchi as a yōkai: not verified.
- Kagutsuchi has an ancient wife named “Haniyasu-hime” in every version: only one *Nihon shoki* variant makes Haniyamahime his wife.
- Kagutsuchi and Amaterasu fought, or Kagutsuchi lives in Yomi: no classical source found.
- Human sacrifice was offered to Kagutsuchi: no evidence found.
- Modern elemental, astrological, or tarot correspondences are ancient: no evidence found.
