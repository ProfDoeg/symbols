# Raijin and Fūjin: Research Dossier

**Class:** A historically composite pair of Japanese weather divinities. *Raijin* 雷神 is a generic “thunder deity” or “thunder kami,” later visualized as an oni-like Buddhist guardian with drums; *Fūjin* 風神 is a generic “wind deity,” later visualized as an oni-like guardian carrying a wind bag. In ancient court religion, thunder and wind belonged to several distinct kami rather than to one inseparable pair. In medieval Buddhist art they became guardian figures associated especially with Thousand-Armed Kannon; in early-modern art they became an independent pair.

**Earliest secure attestation:** For the traditions later gathered under these names, the earliest surviving textual witness is the *Kojiki*, completed in 712: it names the wind kami Shinatsuhiko 志那都比古神 and eight thunder kami, *Yakusa-no-ikazuchi-no-kami* 八雷神, occupying Izanami’s corpse. The first dated event concerning an official wind-god cult is the *Nihon shoki*’s retrospective entry for 675, preserved in the chronicle completed in 720, when the court sent envoys to worship the wind deity at Tatsuta. Neither work contains “Raijin and Fūjin” as the familiar drum-and-bag pair. That paired iconography is securely documented only in medieval Buddhist art; the surviving Sanjūsangendō sculptures are conventionally dated to the mid-thirteenth century.

**Evidentiary labels used below:** **[Early attested]**, **[Later canonical]**, **[Regional variant]**, **[Learned systematization]**, **[Syncretistic identification]**, **[Medieval/early-modern accretion]**, **[Folklore]**, **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**, and **[Modern invention]**.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### 1.1 What the names actually denote

**Raijin** 雷神, Sino-Japanese *rai-jin*, means “thunder deity”: 雷 “thunder” plus 神 “kami, deity, numinous being.” It may denote thunder divinity generically, any one of several named thunder kami, a Buddhist guardian in thunder-god form, or the particular drum-bearing figure standardized by medieval and early-modern art. It is therefore unsafe to treat every occurrence of a thunder god as a reference to a single biographical individual.

**Fūjin** 風神, Sino-Japanese *fū-jin*, similarly means “wind deity.” It may denote the court-cult wind kami of Tatsuta, the mythic Shinatsuhiko, the Buddhist wind deity derived from continental iconography, a local wind spirit, or the bag-bearing figure paired with Raijin.

The two terms describe offices or types before they identify fixed personalities. Popular statements that Raijin and Fūjin are “brothers,” “twins,” or two named sons born together from Izanami are **[Modern invention or unsourced harmonization]**. The *Kojiki* makes Shinatsuhiko a child produced during Izanagi and Izanami’s generation of the land and deities, while its eight thunder gods arise upon the dead Izanami’s body in Yomi. It does not make Shinatsuhiko and a singular Raijin siblings or companions.

### 1.2 Thunder names

Documented Japanese thunder vocabulary includes:

- **雷神 — Raijin:** “thunder deity,” a generic and later iconographic designation.
- **雷の神 / 雷神 — ikazuchi no kami:** “kami of thunder.” *Ikazuchi* is the old native word used in the classical texts.
- **鳴神 — Narukami:** “sounding/rumbling kami,” common in literary and theatrical usage.
- **雷様 — Kaminari-sama:** “Lord Thunder,” the polite folk designation.
- **雷電様 — Raiden-sama:** “Lord Thunder-and-Lightning.”
- **雷公 — Raikō:** “Duke/Lord Thunder,” reflecting Chinese 雷公 terminology.
- **火雷神 — Honoikazuchi-no-kami:** “Fire-Thunder Kami.” In the lost *Yamashiro no kuni fudoki* tradition, this deity assumes the form of a red-painted arrow and fathers Kamo Wakeikazuchi through Tamayorihime.
- **賀茂別雷神 — Kamo Wakeikazuchi-no-kami:** the deity of Kamigamo Shrine. The etymology of *wake-ikazuchi* has been parsed as “young thunder,” “divided/distinct thunder,” or a personal name whose original sense is no longer recoverable. The surviving *fudoki* fragment does not identify him with the later drum-bearing Raijin.
- **八雷神 — Yakusa-no-ikazuchi-no-kami:** the “Eight Thunder Kami” of Izanami’s corpse. Philippi renders their names approximately as Great-Thunder, Fire-Thunder, Black-Thunder, Cleft/Crack-Thunder, Young-Thunder, Earth-Thunder, Sounding-Thunder, and Reclining/Couchant-Thunder. The exact semantic force of several names remains disputed because the text supplies names, bodily locations, and action but no theological commentary. [Kokugakuin’s textual entry preserves the variant witnesses and research history](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/shinmei/yakusanoikazuchinokami/).
- **建御雷之男神 — Takemikazuchi-no-o-no-kami:** “Brave-Awful-Thunder-Male Kami,” or comparable renderings. Despite *mikazuchi* in the name and later popular classification as a thunder god, the *Kojiki* primarily presents him as a sword-born, martial envoy in the land-transfer myth. He is not automatically identical with the drum-bearing Raijin. [The *Encyclopedia of Shinto* accordingly treats him as a distinct deity](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9145).
- **火雷天神 — Karai Tenjin:** “Fire-Thunder Heavenly Kami,” a designation within the cultic assimilation of Sugawara no Michizane’s vengeful spirit to thunder and heavenly calamity. This is a historical syncretism, not evidence that Michizane was the primordial Raijin.

The familiar derivation of *kaminari* as *kami-nari*, “the sounding of the kami,” is traditional and semantically attractive but should not be presented as uncontested historical linguistics. Old spellings and cognate thunder vocabulary make the word’s development more complicated than a transparent modern compound.

### 1.3 Wind names

- **風神 — Fūjin:** “wind deity.”
- **風の神 — kaze no kami:** native-Japanese “kami of wind.”
- **風伯 — Fūhaku:** “Earl of the Wind,” borrowed from Chinese *Fengbo* 風伯. It belongs to the Chinese cosmological and iconographic layer.
- **志那都比古神 — Shinatsuhiko-no-kami:** the *Kojiki* spelling of the wind kami.
- **級長津彦命 — Shinatsuhiko-no-mikoto:** *Nihon shoki* spelling.
- **級長戸辺命 — Shinatohe/Shinatobe-no-mikoto:** a *Nihon shoki* variant produced from the divine breath that dispels the morning mist. Grammatically and textually, the passage oscillates between a male-named form and a form read as female.
- **天御柱命 — Ame-no-mihashira-no-mikoto** and **国御柱命 — Kuni-no-mihashira-no-mikoto:** “August Pillar of Heaven” and “August Pillar of the Land,” the two principal names used at Tatsuta. Later liturgical and shrine traditions identify these with Shinatsuhiko and Shinatsuhime/Shinatobe. This equivalence is a **[Learned systematization]**, not stated by the *Kojiki*.
- **志那都比売神 / 級長津姫命 — Shinatsuhime:** a feminine counterpart in later liturgical and shrine nomenclature.
- **風天 — Fūten:** Buddhist Wind Deva, the Japanese form of Sanskrit **Vāyu** or **Vāta**, included among the Twelve Devas. Fūten and the bag-bearing Fūjin overlap iconographically but should not be made timelessly identical.
- **風三郎 / 風の又三郎 — Kaze no Saburō / Kaze no Matasaburō:** regional, often ambivalent folk wind beings, especially in northeastern traditions; these are not ancient canonical names of Sōtatsu’s Fūjin.

The element *shina* in Shinatsuhiko has traditionally been connected with “long breath” or blowing breath. This is plausible, particularly beside the *Nihon shoki* birth-from-breath account, but is not demonstrable enough to erase alternative historical phonological explanations.

### 1.4 Classifications by period

- **Ancient court sources:** plural *kami* or divine natural agencies, not an inseparable couple.
- **Buddhist art:** *tenbu* 天部 or Dharma-protecting deities surrounding Kannon; the number and identity of the attendants vary.
- **Medieval narrative:** awesome or demonic-looking *kishin* 鬼神, yakṣa-like beings whose frightening shape does not make them doctrinally evil.
- **Early-modern art and folklore:** kami, comic figures, dangerous weather spirits, and occasionally yōkai-adjacent beings.
- **Modern museums and popular culture:** “gods of wind and thunder,” usually treated as a fixed complementary pair.

An oni-like body is an iconographic form, not proof that either figure was classified simply as an *oni*. The porous boundary between deity, demon, guardian, revenant, and yōkai is historically important.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 2.1 *Kojiki* (古事記, 712)

**Language and support:** Classical Japanese encoded largely through Chinese characters; the work survives through much later manuscript transmission, not an autograph of 712. Donald L. Philippi’s 1968 translation remains a major English reference. An accessible scan contains the Yomi passage and its textual notes. [Philippi translation](https://www1.udel.edu/History-old/figal/Hist138/Text/er/kojiki.pdf).

**Wind:** During the generation of deities, Izanagi and Izanami produce “the wind kami, whose name is Shinatsuhiko-no-kami.” No body, bag, color, cult, deeds, or relationship with a thunder god is specified.

**Thunder:** When Izanagi enters Yomi and illuminates Izanami’s corpse, eight thunder kami occupy its head, breast, belly, genitals, hands, and feet. Izanami later sends the eight thunders, the hags of Yomi, and the forces of the dead in pursuit of him. This is the earliest surviving Japanese textual cluster that can securely be called thunder-kami mythology.

**What it does not say:** It does not name a single Raijin, give that god drums, make Fūjin his brother, or narrate the two as a paired team.

### 2.2 *Nihon shoki* (日本書紀, 720)

**Language and support:** Literary Chinese with variant myths presented as *issho* 一書, “one writing says.” W. G. Aston’s nineteenth-century translation remains useful but must be checked against modern editions.

**Cosmogonic wind variant:** After Izanagi and Izanami generate the islands, a deity is produced from breath used to blow away the morning mist: Shinatohe, also called Shinatsuhiko, “the wind deity.” The difference from the *Kojiki*—birth sequence, divine breath, and gendered naming—must not be silently harmonized.

**Court ritual:** Under the fourth year of Tenmu, corresponding to 675, envoys are sent to Tatsuta to worship the wind deity. This is the first dated state-cult notice, although the report survives only in the chronicle compiled in 720. Later notices repeat court concern with Tatsuta’s wind kami, especially during destructive or excessive weather.

**Thunder:** The chronicle preserves thunder prodigies and divine associations but does not furnish the later drum-bearing Raijin’s biography.

### 2.3 Provincial *Fudoki*

The surviving and fragmentary provincial gazetteers, ordered in 713, record local divine geographies rather than a unified pantheon.

- A fragment of the lost **Yamashiro no kuni fudoki** tells how Tamayorihime finds a red-painted arrow floating downstream, places it by her sleeping place, conceives, and gives birth to Kamo Wakeikazuchi. At a feast, the child is asked to offer wine to his father; he breaks through the roof and ascends. The arrow is revealed as Honoikazuchi. The fragment documents a distinct Kamo thunder genealogy, not the origin of the Buddhist Raijin.
- The **Hitachi no kuni fudoki** and related early tales preserve encounters with violent local deities and thunder-child motifs, but attribution and exact textual relationships require story-by-story treatment. A thunder deity killing or impregnating a mortal in a local tale is not automatically the Sōtatsu figure.

The Kamo mother-child configuration and its surviving fragment are summarized in the [*Encyclopedia of Shinto* entry on mother-child kami](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9452).

### 2.4 *Man’yōshū* (万葉集, eighth century)

The anthology supplies early poetic evidence for *ikazuchi*, wind, divine wind, Tatsuta, and weather as numinous experience. The formula *kamikaze no Ise*, “divine-wind Ise,” predates the Mongol invasions by centuries. It is therefore false to claim that *kamikaze* was coined in 1274 or 1281. The anthology does not narrate Raijin and Fūjin as a pair.

### 2.5 *Kogo shūi* (古語拾遺, 807)

Inbe no Hironari’s court memorandum preserves alternative traditions concerning ritual lineages and the ancient kami. It is important for the historiography of court ritual but supplies no early biography of the later paired figures. Its silence matters: the famous pair was not a central dyad of the early mythographic canon.

### 2.6 *Nihon ryōiki* (日本霊異記, approximately 810–823)

Kyōkai’s Buddhist collection of miraculous-retribution tales is fundamental to thunder folklore.

- **Upper fascicle, tale 1:** Chiisakobe no Sugaru, ordered by Emperor Yūryaku to summon/catch thunder, calls upon the thunder deity in the emperor’s name. The being falls or is brought to court amid fearsome light; the emperor ultimately has it released. After Sugaru’s death, thunder strikes his marker but becomes caught in a crack, creating a reciprocal “capturing” of thunder. An experimental modern translation identifies the work and date [here](https://poemsandpoetics.blogspot.com/2010/04/from-nihon-ryoiki-of-monk-kyokai-oral.html). This story should not be projected directly into the *Nihon shoki*: the chronicle’s Sugaru episode concerns a pun involving children and silkworms, and the thunder-catching form belongs to the *Ryōiki*’s separate transmission.
- **Upper fascicle, tale 3:** A farmer saves or aids a thunder deity fallen to earth. The deity rewards the household with a miraculously conceived or bestowed child of immense strength, later identified with Dōjō Hōshi of Gangōji. Subsequent tales concern the descendants’ extraordinary strength. This is an early Buddhist literary domestication of thunder divinity, not evidence for a “son Raitarō” in ancient Shinto genealogy.

### 2.7 *Engishiki* and the Tatsuta norito

The *Engishiki*, completed in 927 and promulgated in the tenth century, codifies court institutions, offerings, shrine lists, and ritual prayers. Volume 8 preserves the **Festival of the Wind Deities of Tatsuta** norito. The prayer recounts how destructive winds and water threatened grain; divination identified the divine cause, after which the sovereign established offerings at Tatsuta. The rites sought restrained winds and a successful harvest.

The court dispatched officials of fifth rank or above; the festival occurred in the fourth and seventh lunar months alongside the Hirose water/food rite. It was classed as a minor state festival but was institutionally important. [Kokugakuin’s ritual history](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9285) and Felicia Bock’s critical translation, available through [JSTOR](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv10vm241), are the essential modern entry points.

The surviving norito does not describe a green demon carrying a sack. The official wind cult and the later image are related through historical identification, not pictorial continuity.

### 2.8 *Konjaku monogatari shū* (今昔物語集, late eleventh–early twelfth century)

This large setsuwa collection transmits Buddhist, Chinese, and Japanese marvel tales, including thunder-child and divine-boy material related to the *Ryōiki*. Its exact compiler is unknown. It enlarges the narrative field in which thunder beings can fall, speak, bargain, punish, or generate superhuman offspring. It still does not supply the modern “Raijin and Fūjin are rival brothers” plot.

### 2.9 Kannon ritual and iconographic texts

The medieval pair belongs especially to the visual and ritual orbit of Thousand-Armed, Thousand-Eyed Kannon and the **Twenty-Eight Attendants** (*nijūhachi bushū*). Lists of these guardians were not stable. Images of the attendants occur in Chinese Buddhist painting at Dunhuang by the mid-Tang and early Song periods; Japanese art sometimes adds wind and thunder gods outside the nominal twenty-eight. [JAANUS explicitly notes both the unstable lists and the addition of Fūjin and Raijin](https://www.aisf.or.jp/~jaanus/deta/n/nijuuhachibushuu.htm).

This is crucial: the Sanjūsangendō ensemble contains thirty peripheral guardian statues—twenty-eight attendants plus Wind God and Thunder God. Fūjin and Raijin are not securely two of a universally fixed canonical list.

### 2.10 *Taiheiki* (太平記, fourteenth century)

In its retrospective account of the Mongol invasions of 1274 and 1281, the *Taiheiki* depicts divine responses at Japanese shrines. At Ise’s wind sanctuary, a green or blue *kishin*, yakṣa/rākṣasa-like in appearance and carrying a sack, releases destructive wind and fire. This is a major textual witness to the medieval bag-bearing, demon-bodied wind god and to the religious interpretation of the invasions. It is later than the invasions and must not be treated as eyewitness evidence.

### 2.11 *Kitano Tenjin engi emaki*

The Kitano Tenjin illustrated legends, especially the Jōkyū version of 1219, depict Sugawara no Michizane’s angry spirit and catastrophic lightning at the Seiryōden. A thunder deity attacks the palace while Fujiwara no Tokihira confronts it with a sword. The National Theatre’s digital guide identifies the scene [here](https://www2.ntj.jac.go.jp/dglib/contents/learn/exp2/exp2_new/w/143.html).

The scroll helped establish the oni-like, cloud-borne thunder figure in Japanese visual memory. It represents Michizane/Tenjin’s vengeance within a shrine-legend cycle, not necessarily a portrait of every Raijin.

### 2.12 Early-modern catalogues and scholarship

- **Toriyama Sekien, 1776–1784:** *Gazu hyakki yagyō*, *Konjaku gazu zoku hyakki*, *Konjaku hyakki shūi*, and *Hyakki tsurezure bukuro* systematized many yōkai in illustrated-book form. The National Diet Library describes their publication history and influence [here](https://www.ndl.go.jp/imagebank/column/sekienyokai). Contrary to a frequent bibliographic assumption, Sekien’s four core catalogues do not constitute a primary canonical source for the Sōtatsu Raijin–Fūjin pair. They are relevant to the neighboring categories of thunder beasts, oni, transformed objects, and the early-modern visualization of marvels.
- **Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den* (古事記伝, 1798 completion):** philological commentary seeking the ancient meanings of the *Kojiki*. Norinaga helped establish Shinatsuhiko and the eight thunders as elements of a supposedly recoverable ancient Japanese order. His readings are foundational but are Edo-period scholarly reconstruction, not ancient testimony.
- **Hirata Atsutane:** extended kokugaku cosmology through philology, spirit lore, and revelatory claims. Short prayers transmitted in his lineage sometimes equate the Tatsuta pillar deities with Shinatsuhiko and Shinatsuhime. A modern edition correctly warns that such a prayer is distinct from the ancient *Engishiki* norito [here](https://norito.sumeragi-iyasaka.com/en/norito/tatsuta-fujin-haishi/).

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### 3.1 Domains

**Raijin:**

- thunder, lightning, and the sudden violence of storms;
- rain and therefore agricultural fertility;
- divine punishment, ominous prodigies, and vengeful spirits;
- in Buddhist contexts, protection of Kannon, the Dharma, temples, and worshippers;
- in folklore, a dangerous being who falls from the sky, seizes bodies or navels, fathers or bestows superhuman children, or is captured by bold humans.

**Fūjin:**

- beneficial and destructive wind;
- dispersal of mist, clouds, disease, and pollutants;
- agricultural weather and protection of crops from destructive storms;
- maritime and later aviation safety;
- in Buddhist contexts, a weather-controlling guardian;
- in medieval national-protection narrative, the force that destroys invading fleets.

Neither deity has a securely ancient monopoly on these domains. Wind, rain, thunder, water, pestilence, and fertility overlap with Shinatsuhiko, Tatsuta’s pillar deities, Kamo Wakeikazuchi, Honoikazuchi, Takemikazuchi, Tenjin, Susanoo, dragon kings, water kami, Indra/Taishakuten, and numerous local beings.

### 3.2 Early forms

Ancient literary thunder can be:

- plural beings occupying a corpse;
- serpent-like, consistent with water, mountain, and lightning imagery;
- a small child or divine boy;
- a falling or capturable numinous force;
- the activity of a vengeful dead person.

Ancient wind receives almost no anthropomorphic description. It is divine breath and an atmospheric power requiring ritual restraint.

### 3.3 The Buddhist pair

By the medieval period both gods have muscular, dynamic, oni-like bodies, wild hair, fangs or tusks, claws, loincloths, cloud pedestals, and airborne poses.

**Raijin’s diagnostic object:** an arc or complete ring of linked drums, often marked with the triple-comma *tomoe*, struck with mallets. The drums make audible thunder visible. Earlier images can give a whip, hand-drum, gong, or less standardized percussion instrument. The ring is therefore a medieval development, not an attribute in the *Kojiki*.

**Fūjin’s diagnostic object:** a large cloth sack stretched behind his shoulders and held at both ends. Opening it releases wind. His clothing and hair stream in the same force he controls.

The visual opposition—contained air versus explosive sound, billowing bag versus circular drums—encouraged their later treatment as an aesthetically balanced pair.

### 3.4 Continental and Indian antecedents

The usual popular claim that “Raijin and Fūjin both came from Vajrapāṇi” is too simple.

- **Fūjin:** The Buddhist Wind Deva ultimately incorporates Indian Vāyu/Vāta. The bag-bearing running figure also participates in a long trans-Eurasian image-history: Hellenistic Boreas-like figures, Gandharan and Central Asian wind deities, and Chinese Buddhist paintings. The precise chain is debated because similar fluttering mantles, bags, and directional wind gods were repeatedly reinterpreted. A recent Japanese study traces relevant images through Gandhara, Kucha, Dunhuang, Chinese Kannon ensembles, and Japan [here](https://kanazawa-bidai.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/714/files/%E5%8D%83%E6%89%8B%E8%A6%B3%E9%9F%B3%E8%AB%B8%E7%9C%9E%E5%83%8F%E3%81%AB%E3%81%8A%E3%81%91%E3%82%8B%E9%A2%A8%E7%A5%9E%E3%81%AB%E9%96%A2%E3%81%99%E3%82%8B%E4%B8%80%E8%A9%A6%E8%AB%96.pdf).
- **Raijin:** Indian Indra wields the *vajra*, and Vajrapāṇi is the “vajra-bearer”; both contributed to East Asian repertoires of thunderous divine force. Chinese Leigong and other thunder officials supplied further forms. Japanese Buddhist Raijin belongs to this shared iconographic environment, but no source establishes a simple one-to-one descent “Vajrapāṇi became Raijin.”
- **The pair:** Their placement in Thousand-Armed Kannon ensembles is a Chinese-Buddhist and Japanese-medieval development. The strongest evidence concerns the transmission of guardian iconography, not the survival of two unchanged Indian personalities.

The “Boreas became Fūjin” formula is likewise a **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**, useful as a hypothesis about iconographic genealogy but misleading when stated as a single unbroken personal identity.

### 3.5 Sanjūsangendō

Rengeō-in, commonly Sanjūsangendō, was founded under the retired emperor Go-Shirakawa in 1164. The original hall burned in 1249; the present hall dates to 1266. The surviving Wind God and Thunder God are polychromed wooden sculptures with crystal eyes, approximately life-sized, attributed to the Kei-school environment and conventionally dated to the mid-thirteenth century.

Fūjin crouches on a cloud with the inflated bag over his shoulders. Raijin occupies the opposite end, surrounded by his drums. Together with the twenty-eight guardians they protect the 1,001 images of Thousand-Armed Kannon. A Japanese government guide emphasizes both their Indian-background guardian context and the medieval character of Raijin’s drum ring [here](https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/common/001562171.pdf).

They are often called the oldest surviving Japanese sculptures of the familiar pair. That is an art-historical claim about surviving objects, not proof that no earlier examples existed; the original 1164 ensemble and earlier paintings complicate any absolute “first.”

### 3.6 Sōtatsu’s screens

Tawaraya Sōtatsu’s unsigned **Wind God and Thunder God Screens**, usually dated to the early seventeenth century, are a pair of two-fold screens in ink and color on gold leaf, each about 154.5 × 169.8 cm, owned by Kennin-ji and conserved/displayed through the Kyoto National Museum.

Sōtatsu isolates the two figures from Kannon and the other attendants. Fūjin, green-bodied and gripping the wind bag, enters from the viewer’s right; the pale or white Raijin appears at left with drums and mallets. Vast gold space separates them. The museum considers the attribution secure despite the absence of signature or seal. [Kyoto National Museum record](https://www.kyohaku.go.jp/eng/collection/meihin/kinsei/item10/).

This is the decisive transformation from subordinate Buddhist guardians into an autonomous pictorial dyad. It did not invent their attributes, but its visual authority made this particular pairing canonical in modern memory.

Ogata Kōrin copied the composition in the eighteenth century; Sakai Hōitsu later painted *Summer and Autumn Plants* on the reverse and produced further homage. The Tokyo National Museum records the Kōrin screens and their later separation from Hōitsu’s reverse paintings [here](https://emuseum.nich.go.jp/detail?content_base_id=100321&content_part_id=1&content_pict_id=0&langId=ja&webView=null).

### 3.7 Other important iconographies

- **Kitano Tenjin scrolls:** thunder as vengeful divine assault.
- **Kannon paintings:** wind and thunder in the upper corners, subordinate to Kannon.
- **Ōtsu-e:** Raijin becomes comic—most famously fishing for a dropped drum. These inexpensive paintings could retain amuletic value while making a fearsome deity ridiculous. [University of Michigan example](https://umma.umich.edu/objects/otsu-e-the-thunder-god-fishing-for-his-drum-1987-1-368/).
- **Temple gates:** paired Wind and Thunder gods guard thresholds. Sensō-ji’s outer gate is formally Fūraijinmon 風雷神門, abbreviated Kaminarimon.
- **Ukiyo-e and satirical prints:** falling thunder gods are pulled into rivers, lose drums, or encounter modern technology. Comic defeat converts uncontrollable weather into urban entertainment.
- **Irezumi:** their opposing bodies, wind bag, drums, clouds, lightning, and circular motion suit large paired tattoo compositions. This is an early-modern/modern artistic reception, not proof of an ancient dual cult.

### 3.8 Colors, animals, numbers, and supposed correspondences

There is no universal ancient canonical color scheme. Medieval and later art commonly makes Fūjin blue-green and Raijin red, white, or flesh-colored; Sōtatsu’s green Fūjin and pale Raijin were especially influential.

No securely ancient sacred day, number, planet, or animal belongs uniquely to the pair. The following popular claims require qualification:

- **Raijū as Raijin’s pet:** **[Early-modern folklore/modern systematization]**. Thunder beasts are a heterogeneous family of creatures said to fall with lightning. Sources do not establish one canonical animal companion.
- **Tiger-skin loincloth:** later iconographic convention shared with oni and fierce guardians.
- **Tomoe as Raijin’s personal symbol:** the motif occurs widely in shrine, martial, and drum decoration; its appearance on thunder drums does not make it his exclusive emblem.
- **Fūjin’s bag contains all winds:** consistent with later art and narrative, but not stated in early Japanese scripture.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### 4.1 Competing genealogies

#### Wind lineage

1. **[Early attested: *Kojiki*, 712]** Shinatsuhiko is generated by Izanagi and Izanami among the natural-domain kami.
2. **[Early attested variant: *Nihon shoki*, 720]** Shinatohe/Shinatsuhiko arises from divine breath dispersing morning mist.
3. **[Later liturgical systematization]** Tatsuta’s Ame-no-mihashira and Kuni-no-mihashira are identified with Shinatsuhiko and Shinatsuhime.
4. **[Buddhist syncretism]** Fūten/Vāyu and continental bag-bearing wind imagery merge with Japanese wind-kami language.
5. **[Modern invention]** Fūjin is made the named brother or twin of a single Raijin.

#### Thunder lineages

1. **[Early attested: *Kojiki*]** Eight thunder kami manifest upon Izanami’s corpse.
2. **[Regional *fudoki* lineage]** Honoikazuchi, as a red arrow, fathers Kamo Wakeikazuchi through Tamayorihime.
3. **[Sword/martial lineage]** Takemikazuchi is born from blood on Izanagi’s sword after Kagutsuchi’s killing and acts as heavenly conqueror.
4. **[Vengeful-spirit lineage]** Sugawara no Michizane becomes Tenjin and is assimilated to destructive thunder.
5. **[Buddhist iconographic lineage]** a thunder guardian develops within Kannon and Dharma-protector art.
6. **[Folklore]** thunder appears as a child, serpent, falling being, divine father, or thunder beast.
7. **[Modern harmonization]** all are collapsed into one Raijin with one genealogy.

No early source makes the drum-bearing Raijin a singular son of Izanami who is simultaneously Kamo Wakeikazuchi, Takemikazuchi, Tenjin, and a Buddhist guardian.

### 4.2 Every documented divine interaction

The following list separates actual scenes from mere co-membership in a pantheon.

- **Izanami — Eight Thunder Kami:** The eight occupy distinct parts of her decomposing body in Yomi and later act among her pursuers of Izanagi. *Kojiki*, 712. **[Early attested]**
- **Izanagi — Eight Thunder Kami:** They pursue the fleeing Izanagi from Yomi after he sees Izanami’s corpse. *Kojiki*, 712. **[Early attested]**
- **Izanagi and Izanami — Shinatsuhiko:** They generate the wind kami during the birth of natural deities. *Kojiki*, 712. **[Early attested]**
- **Izanagi and Izanami — Shinatohe/Shinatsuhiko:** Their breath, used to disperse mist, produces the wind deity. *Nihon shoki*, 720, variant account. **[Early attested variant]**
- **Honoikazuchi — Tamayorihime:** Honoikazuchi assumes the form of a red-painted arrow; Tamayorihime keeps it beside her and conceives Kamo Wakeikazuchi. Lost *Yamashiro fudoki*, quoted in later sources. **[Regional early tradition]**
- **Honoikazuchi — Kamo Wakeikazuchi:** Father and son in the red-arrow narrative. Same source. **[Regional early tradition]**
- **Kamo Wakeikazuchi — Kamo Taketsunumi:** Grandson and maternal grandfather in the Kamo genealogy. Same source. **[Regional early tradition]**
- **Shinatsuhiko/Shinatsuhime — Ame-no-mihashira/Kuni-no-mihashira:** Identification of the wind deities under alternative names, not a narrative meeting. Tatsuta liturgical and shrine tradition, clearly expressed in later prayers. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Fūjin and Thousand-Armed Kannon:** Fūjin appears as a guardian attendant in Kannon ensembles. Chinese medieval prototypes; Japanese examples by the late Heian/Kamakura periods. **[Buddhist syncretistic identification]**
- **Raijin and Thousand-Armed Kannon:** Raijin likewise protects Kannon and the Dharma; the Sanjūsangendō ensemble is the monumental surviving instance. **[Buddhist syncretistic identification]**
- **Fūjin and the Twenty-Eight Attendants:** Joint appearance in Kannon paintings and sculpture; Fūjin is often added outside the unstable nominal list of twenty-eight. **[Medieval Buddhist elaboration]**
- **Raijin and the Twenty-Eight Attendants:** Same relation. **[Medieval Buddhist elaboration]**
- **Raijin and Fūjin:** Joint guardian appearance in Kannon art and, later, as an isolated pair. The interaction is normally juxtaposition rather than a narrated conversation, kinship, combat, or friendship. **[Medieval iconographic pairing]**
- **Sugawara no Michizane/Tenjin — thunder deity:** Michizane’s vengeful spirit is manifested and visualized through destructive lightning and the thunder god in the Kitano legends. Tenth-century cult development; thirteenth-century illustrated witnesses. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Raijin/Michizane — Fujiwara no Tokihira:** The thunder figure attacks the Seiryōden; Tokihira confronts it with a drawn sword in the *Kitano Tenjin engi emaki*. Jōkyū scroll, 1219. Tokihira is a deified-cult antagonist only in the broad sense, not originally a god. **[Medieval shrine legend]**
- **Fūjin — Amaterasu/Ise divinities:** The *Taiheiki* locates the bag-bearing wind apparition at Ise during divine defense against the Mongol invasion, placing it within the agency of Ise’s gods. Fourteenth century. **[Medieval national-protection legend]**
- **Fūjin — Hachiman, Sumiyoshi, Suwa, and other shrine deities:** These beings act in parallel at their own shrines in the *Taiheiki* invasion cycle. It is a joint national-defense sequence, not a personal meeting. **[Medieval literary elaboration]**

**Absences worth recording:**

- No classical text documents a sibling relationship between Raijin and Fūjin.
- No early text records them fighting each other.
- No early source makes Susanoo their commander, sibling, or enemy.
- No primary source establishes Amaterasu as their mother.
- No secure premodern source makes Raijū a singular pet of Raijin.
- “Raitarō, son of Raijin” belongs to later folktale and modern retelling, not canonical genealogy.
- Their placement in the same image is far better documented than any mythic dialogue between them.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### 5.1 The eight thunders on Izanami

**Source:** *Kojiki*, 712.

Izanagi travels to Yomi seeking Izanami. She tells him she has eaten food of that land but will consult its powers; she forbids him to look. He breaks the taboo, lights a tooth of his comb, and sees her decomposing body alive with maggots and occupied by eight thunder deities. Horrified, he flees. Izanami sends the hags of Yomi, then the eight thunders and a mass of warriors after him. Izanagi escapes and seals the slope of Yomi with a boulder.

**Historical significance:** Thunder here belongs to corpse, pollution, underworld pursuit, and dangerous animation. The episode explains none of the later drums, bag, colors, or paired iconography.

**Variant discipline:** The *Nihon shoki* supplies several Yomi and death accounts, but its variants do not reproduce one completely uniform eight-thunder arrangement. “Raijin was born from Izanami after her death” is an oversimplification of this plural, body-distributed scene.

### 5.2 The birth of the wind kami

**Sources:** *Kojiki*, 712; *Nihon shoki*, 720.

In the *Kojiki*, Shinatsuhiko is simply generated amid the natural-domain kami. In a *Nihon shoki* variant, wind is divine breath blowing away morning mist. The latter is an especially clear etiological image: invisible breath becomes atmospheric motion.

Neither account gives Fūjin a sack or connects him with thunder. Their brevity also shows why later ritual, Buddhist art, and shrine theology could develop the figure in divergent directions.

### 5.3 The Kamo red-arrow conception

**Source:** lost *Yamashiro no kuni fudoki*, preserved in quotation.

Tamayorihime encounters a red-painted arrow floating down the Kamo River. She takes it home and places it near her bed. She becomes pregnant and bears a son. At a feast, the child is instructed to give a cup to his father; he breaks through the roof and ascends, revealing celestial paternity. The arrow is Honoikazuchi, and the child becomes Kamo Wakeikazuchi.

This is a local river, fertility, and thunder genealogy. The arrow can be read as a phallic or divine-arrival object, but such interpretations are modern analysis, not statements in the fragment. The story grounds the paired Upper and Lower Kamo cults: mother and maternal grandfather below, thunder child above.

### 5.4 Sugaru catches thunder

**Source:** *Nihon ryōiki*, upper fascicle, tale 1, c. 810–823.

After inadvertently intruding on Emperor Yūryaku and the empress, Sugaru is commanded to summon the thunder god. He invokes imperial authority and brings the luminous, terrifying thunder presence to court. The emperor fears it and orders its release. After Sugaru dies, thunder strikes his grave marker, becomes trapped, and is released after receiving reverence.

The tale negotiates imperial authority and divine power: the emperor can order thunder summoned, yet recoils from its presence; thunder can kill Sugaru, yet is itself caught by the dead retainer’s monument. It is Buddhist setsuwa framing older court and local lore, not a simple historical report.

### 5.5 The thunder child and Dōjō Hōshi

**Source:** *Nihon ryōiki*, upper fascicle, tale 3; later related tales in the *Konjaku monogatari* tradition.

A farmer encounters a fallen thunder deity and spares or shelters it. In gratitude, thunder arranges the birth or bestowal of a child distinguished by a snake-like sign and extraordinary strength. The boy eventually becomes Dōjō Hōshi at Gangōji and overcomes the temple’s murderous oni.

Variants change the means of conception, the thunder being’s appearance, and the child’s later career. The tale participates in an East Asian divine-child complex and in Buddhist conversion of local marvel lore. Later “Raitarō” stories—where a thunder child lives with poor peasants, brings rain, then departs as a dragon—are literary-folkloric developments, not verbatim ancient scripture.

### 5.6 Michizane becomes thunderous Tenjin

**Historical core:** Sugawara no Michizane died in exile in 903. Political deaths, epidemics, fires, and lightning were interpreted as signs of his angry spirit. In 930 lightning struck the Seiryōden palace and killed or injured courtiers; Emperor Daigo died soon afterward. Appeasement culminated in Kitano worship and the development of Tenjin.

**Legendary cycle:** The *Kitano Tenjin engi* makes Michizane’s spirit a cosmic agent of punishment. The palace thunder scene provides one of medieval Japan’s most influential images of a muscular, demonic thunder deity.

**Classification:** The 930 strike is a court-historical event; the causal attribution to Michizane is documented belief; the pictorial thunder assault is medieval shrine legend. None should be collapsed into a meteorological claim or treated as the origin of all thunder worship. [Kokugakuin summarizes the thunder–goryō–Tenjin association](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9976).

### 5.7 The divine wind against the Mongol invasions

**Events:** Mongol-Yuan-led forces attacked in 1274 and 1281. Severe storms damaged the fleets, especially in 1281. Japanese religious institutions interpreted deliverance through the action of their deities.

**Medieval narrative:** The *Taiheiki* describes portents at multiple shrines and an apparition at Ise’s wind sanctuary: a blue-green, fierce being with a bag releases devastating wind. This is an explicit narrative ancestor of the familiar Fūjin.

**Development:** *Kamikaze* already existed as a poetic epithet of Ise in the *Man’yōshū*. After the invasions it acquired a historical-national-protection application. Ise’s wind shrines were elevated in status in connection with the deliverance tradition. Modern retellings that say “Fūjin alone sank the Mongol fleet” suppress the multi-shrine, politically produced character of the medieval accounts.

### 5.8 Raijin takes navels

“Cover your navel or Kaminari-sama will take it” is one of the best-attested modern Japanese childhood warnings but one of the least securely dated mythic narratives.

Documented explanations include:

1. **[Folklore]** Thunder or a thunder beast descends through the vulnerable navel.
2. **[Iconographic inference]** The half-naked thunder god’s prominent belly or navel prompted stories about his appetite for navels.
3. **[Modern rationalization]** Thunderstorms bring cold rain and sudden temperature drops; telling children to cover their stomachs induces them to dress.
4. **[Modern pseudo-scientific rationalization]** Lightning burns were supposedly concentrated around metal objects near the waist. Evidence for this as the custom’s historical origin is lacking.

A Japanese library reference service lists these as competing explanations rather than establishing one origin [here](https://crd.ndl.go.jp/reference/entry/index.php?id=1000295069&page=ref_view). The International Research Center for Japanese Studies preserves numerous local variants, including a tale in which thunder physically removes a woman’s navel [here](https://www.nichibun.ac.jp/cgi-bin/YoukaiDB3/youkai_card.cgi?ID=1730028).

Thus the navel-taking motif is **[Folklore, date and origin uncertain]**, not securely attested in the *Kojiki*, *Nihon shoki*, or *Engishiki*.

### 5.9 “Kuwabara, kuwabara”

The phrase is uttered as protection from lightning or misfortune. Competing traditions include:

- lightning would not strike Michizane’s land at Kuwabara;
- a thunder being captured in a well promised never to strike the locality called Kuwabara;
- *kuwa* “mulberry” acquired apotropaic force through sericulture and wordplay.

These explanations are local etiologies, not mutually confirmable history. The charm’s existence is more secure than any single origin story.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### 6.1 Tatsuta

Tatsuta Shrine, now Tatsuta Taisha in Nara Prefecture, is the clearest institutional center of ancient wind worship.

- **First documentary notice:** the *Nihon shoki* entry for 675.
- **Deities:** Ame-no-mihashira and Kuni-no-mihashira, later equated with Shinatsuhiko and Shinatsuhime.
- **Ritual:** the court’s Fūjin-sai in the fourth and seventh lunar months, paired with the Hirose Ōimi festival.
- **Purpose:** protection of grain from violent winds and rain, favorable agricultural conditions, and restoration after crop damage.
- **Personnel:** imperial ritualists of relatively high court rank.
- **Institutional history:** observed through Nara and Heian court ritual; continued into the medieval period; the old state form ceased during the Northern and Southern Courts.
- **Modern continuation:** the Fūchin Taisai, held on the first Sunday in July, includes offerings, prayer, dance, drumming, fire rites, and fireworks. The modern festival is a revival/continuation shaped by later shrine practice, not an unchanged performance of the *Engishiki* rite. [Current official tourism record](https://www.visitnara.jp/venues/E02051/).

Tatsuta’s location near a western approach to the Yamato basin offers a plausible geographical reason for the cult, but that explanation is scholarly inference.

### 6.2 Ise wind sanctuaries

Ise Jingū contains wind shrines associated with Shinatsuhiko and Shinatobe:

- **Kazahinomi-no-miya** at the Inner Shrine;
- **Kaze-no-miya** at the Outer Shrine.

They concern wind and rain essential to agriculture. Their promotion following the Mongol-invasion deliverance tradition tied local weather cult to national protection. The modern Ise complex comprises 125 shrines centered on Amaterasu and Toyouke [according to Ise Jingū’s institutional account](https://www.isejingu.or.jp/en/about/index.html/index.html).

The cult object here is not necessarily the green bag-bearing Fūjin as a temple statue. Shrine nomenclature and Buddhist iconography overlapped historically without becoming completely identical.

### 6.3 Kamo thunder cult

Kamo Wakeikazuchi Shrine—Kamigamo Shrine—enshrines Kamo Wakeikazuchi. The neighboring Shimogamo complex centers on Tamayorihime and Kamo Taketsunumi. The red-arrow genealogy relates the two institutions.

The Aoi Matsuri and Kamo rites belong to this major court-supported shrine complex, but calling Kamigamo simply “the principal temple of Raijin” is misleading. Its deity has a particular local genealogy, ritual organization, and shrine history. The familiar drum-bearing icon is not its ancient cult image.

### 6.4 Tenjin

Kitano Tenmangū and the expanding Tenjin network transformed feared thunder and vengeful-spirit power into a cult of pacification, protection, and eventually scholarship. This is one of the clearest examples of a divine figure changing domain:

1. dead courtier;
2. dangerous *onryō/goryō*;
3. thunderous punisher;
4. pacified heavenly deity;
5. patron of learning and examinations.

Raijin’s imagery enters this history, but Tenjin is not reducible to Raijin.

### 6.5 Buddhist temples

At Sanjūsangendō the pair functions within Kannon worship, not as the principal objects of an independent weather cult. Similar images occur at temple gates and in Kannon paintings.

At Sensō-ji in Asakusa, Wind God and Thunder God guard the Fūraijinmon/Kaminarimon. Modern gate statues belong to a rebuilding history and should not be represented as ancient survivals from the temple’s legendary foundation.

### 6.6 Offerings and rites

Documented practices include:

- textiles, grain, sake, prepared foods, ritual implements, and court offerings specified through the ritsuryō system;
- norito petitioning for controlled winds and crops;
- rainmaking and storm-aversion rites involving Buddhist monks, sutras, esoteric ritual, dragons, and weather deities;
- thunder-avoidance charms and formulas;
- village “sending-away” rites for wind or epidemic spirits;
- votive prayer for agricultural, maritime, and modern aviation safety;
- amuletic images, including popular Ōtsu-e.

No good evidence establishes a paired Raijin–Fūjin priesthood, paired human sacrifices, temple prostitution, or a regular sacrificial cult centered on the Sōtatsu-type figures. Searches for such claims yield modern occult pages and derivative mythology sites rather than inscriptions, shrine regulations, archaeological reports, or premodern ritual texts. **Absence of evidence is the finding.**

### 6.7 Cult economics and politics

Ancient wind ritual was funded and administered by the court. Kamo, Ise, Kitano, and major temples controlled land, labor, offerings, pilgrimage, and political privilege. Weather protection was therefore never only private belief: crop survival, taxation, transport, legitimacy, and military defense all gave storm rites public importance.

The Mongol-invasion narratives allowed Ise, Hachiman, Suwa, Sumiyoshi, and Buddhist institutions to claim efficacy and rewards. The Tenjin cult converted fear of an elite political victim into durable shrine patronage. These processes explain the expansion of particular divine identities more securely than a timeless “Japanese worship of nature.”

## 7. Historical Evolution

### 7.1 Before 712: reconstructable but undocumented prehistory

Archaeology and comparative folklore make indigenous weather ritual certain in a general sense, but no surviving inscription securely names Raijin or Fūjin before the written chronicles. Claims about a prehistoric paired storm cult are **[Modern reconstruction without direct evidence]**.

### 7.2 Eighth century: plural kami and state weather management

The *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* record multiple wind and thunder powers. The court directs envoys to Tatsuta and incorporates weather rites into government. The *fudoki* preserve local genealogies such as Kamo’s red arrow. There is still no standard anthropomorphic pair.

### 7.3 Ninth–tenth centuries: codification, Buddhist tales, and vengeful thunder

The *Nihon ryōiki* places thunder beings within Buddhist karmic narrative. The *Engishiki* codifies Tatsuta worship. Meanwhile the Michizane crisis ties thunder to vengeful political death and placatory cult.

This period cannot be cleanly divided into “Shinto” and “Buddhist” compartments. Kami, court divination, Buddhist rites, Chinese cosmology, and local traditions already operated together.

### 7.4 Eleventh–thirteenth centuries: guardian iconography crystallizes

Kannon worship, imported iconographic manuals, continental paintings, and Japanese sculptural workshops establish demon-bodied Wind and Thunder guardians. The Sanjūsangendō sculptures provide the greatest surviving embodiment. The *Kitano Tenjin* scrolls elaborate thunder as vengeful apparition.

This is the most defensible period for the formation of the recognizable pair.

### 7.5 Thirteenth–fourteenth centuries: national protection and “divine wind”

The Mongol invasions and subsequent memorialization connect Ise’s wind kami and a bag-bearing wind demon with military deliverance. The *Taiheiki*’s scene is literary theology composed after the events, but it powerfully historicalizes the divine wind.

### 7.6 Fifteenth–sixteenth centuries: shrine–temple systems and changing “Shinto”

Weather gods remained embedded in shrine-temple complexes. Yoshida and other theologians increasingly articulated “Shinto” as a distinct system, but this was itself a medieval/early-modern intellectual production.

Toshio Kuroda’s influential argument rejects the idea of an autonomous, unchanged Shinto religion extending from prehistory. He locates most medieval kami worship within the exoteric-esoteric Buddhist order and treats independent Shinto discourse as a later development. [Kuroda’s essay](https://religion-in-japan.univie.ac.at/k/images/0/03/Kuroda_1981.pdf). Later scholars have modified rather than simply discarded this thesis: distinct kami rites certainly existed, but “Shinto” as one transhistorical religion cannot be assumed.

### 7.7 Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: pictorial independence and popular comedy

Sōtatsu detaches the pair from Kannon and monumentalizes empty space between them. Kōrin and Hōitsu canonize the composition as a Rinpa genealogy. Ōtsu-e, popular prints, and jokes make thunder manageable through comedy.

Kokugaku scholars return to the *Kojiki* and distinguish supposedly native kami from Buddhist accretions. This encouraged later readers to identify the medieval Buddhist pair directly with ancient Shinatsuhiko and the eight thunders, even though the textual evidence does not make that equation.

### 7.8 Meiji to 1945: separation, state classification, and nationalism

From 1868 the government ordered separation of kami and buddhas, reorganized shrines, ranked institutions, standardized liturgy, and promoted imperial rites. Buddhist images and shrine objects were separated or destroyed during *haibutsu kishaku*. Raijin and Fūjin’s mixed history made them resistant to neat classification: they remained in Buddhist temples and art even while related wind and thunder kami occupied state-ranked shrines.

State Shinto did not create the pair, but it encouraged presentations of Japanese kami as a pure, continuous national system. The “divine wind” of the Mongol-invasion memory was mobilized in modern nationalism; in 1944–45 *Shinpū/Kamikaze* designated special-attack units. Equating those units with ancient Fūjin worship would nevertheless be anachronistic: this was modern political reuse of a medievalized phrase.

John Breen and Mark Teeuwen describe 1868 onward as the formative construction of modern Shinto rather than the restoration of an unchanged ancient system. [Book record and synopsis](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/9781444317190).

### 7.9 1945–present: disestablishment, shrine religion, heritage, and popular culture

The Allied occupation’s Shinto Directive of 15 December 1945 abolished state sponsorship, support, supervision, and propagation of State or Shrine Shinto. [Kokugakuin’s documentary entry](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/id%3D8852). Shrines reorganized as private religious juridical bodies; Jinja Honchō emerged in 1946.

Tatsuta, Ise, Kamo, and Tenjin worship continue, but there is no measurable mass denomination devoted specifically to the Sōtatsu pair. Today their strongest continuous presence lies in:

- shrine rites to particular wind or thunder kami;
- Buddhist guardian imagery;
- heritage tourism and museum display;
- seasonal folklore;
- amulets and commercial design;
- tattoos, manga, anime, games, advertising, and sports branding.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### 8.1 Folkloristics

Yanagita Kunio treated thunder-child, water-child, small-god, agricultural, and wind-spirit traditions comparatively. His “transformations of thunder-god belief” helped reposition dispersed tales as historical strata of folk religion. This remains influential, but reconstruction from widely separated folktales can imply genetic relationships stronger than the documentary record permits.

*Tōno monogatari* (1910) belongs to Yanagita’s project of fixing orally transmitted marvels in literary ethnography. It is evidence for traditions around the time they were collected, not transparent access to prehistoric Japan.

Hori Ichirō’s work on Japanese folk religion emphasized itinerant holy figures, village boundaries, spirits, and the coexistence of institutional and local practice. Michael Dylan Foster’s *The Book of Yōkai* places such beings within changing media and warns against treating “yōkai” as an eternal, fixed biological catalogue.

### 8.2 Esotericism and neopaganism

The dedicated contested-material search found no documented Renaissance Hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or early chaos-magic tradition centered on Raijin and Fūjin. Their occasional inclusion on modern occult websites, deity lists, correspondence charts, or eclectic altars is **[Modern eclectic practice]**, not continuity from an initiatory Japanese cult.

Online claims that:

- Raijin and Fūjin are elemental archangels;
- they are cosmic twins with a fixed rivalry;
- they correspond to Western astrological planets;
- Raijin commands all electricity while Fūjin commands “air elementals”;
- a surviving ancient rite invokes them together through reconstructed chants

are unsupported unless a particular modern author or group is named. Contemporary worship by individual neopagans may be sincere living religion, but its scale and continuity cannot presently be established from institutional or ethnographic sources.

### 8.3 Literature

Miyazawa Kenji’s *Kaze no Matasaburō* draws on northeastern wind-spirit naming rather than retelling Sōtatsu’s Fūjin biography. Modern fantasy increasingly merges Fūjin with every Japanese wind power and Raijin with Takemikazuchi, Tenjin, Susanoo, and thunder beasts.

Translations and children’s books popularized “Raitarō, Child of Thunder.” Frederick Hadland Davis’s *Myths and Legends of Japan* and earlier English-language fairy books transformed local tales into a unified national mythology. These retellings are valuable reception evidence but cannot establish early genealogy.

### 8.4 Film, comics, games, and animation

The pair’s visual attributes are unusually portable:

- **Mortal Kombat:** Raiden is a thunder god whose name uses the Japanese thunder-and-lightning term, but his conical hat, characterization, and fictional cosmology are modern hybrid design, not a representation of a single traditional Raijin.
- **Pokémon:** Tornadus and Thundurus evoke the Fūjin/Raijin opposition, with cloud-borne bodies and weather powers; this is design influence, not doctrinal adaptation.
- **Naruto:** *Hiraishin no Jutsu* means “Flying Thunder God Technique,” while other techniques borrow names such as Amaterasu and Susanoo. The works use divine names as evocative labels, not as evidence of the deities’ traditional relationships.
- **Monster Hunter Rise:** Wind Serpent Ibushi and Thunder Serpent Narwa reorganize the pair into sexed serpentine monsters and a reproductive narrative. That plot is a modern invention.
- **Numerous games and anime:** “Fujin” and “Raijin/Raiden” have become internationally legible shorthand for wind and electricity.

### 8.5 Commercial and graphic use

Sōtatsu’s composition appears on packaging, clothing, posters, restaurant décor, tourist goods, tattoos, album covers, and corporate graphics. The pair often represents balance or rivalry analogous to yin and yang. No early source assigns them that philosophical symbolism. “They are the Japanese yin and yang” is **[Modern interpretive invention]**.

### 8.6 National and political appropriation

The Mongol-invasion *kamikaze* narrative became a model of divinely protected Japan and was reused in imperial nationalism. Fūjin himself was less often a direct political emblem than the concept of divine wind. There is no strong evidence that either figure has become the central icon of a specific modern extremist organization; generic nationalist use of storm gods or *kamikaze* language should not be inflated into an organized Raijin–Fūjin cult.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### 9.1 Is this “Shinto mythology”?

Older reference works often place Raijin and Fūjin in one timeless “Shinto pantheon.” The evidence instead shows several intersecting histories:

1. ancient kami names and court weather rites;
2. local Kamo and Tatsuta cults;
3. Buddhist guardian iconography;
4. Chinese and Central Asian visual forms;
5. goryō and Tenjin worship;
6. medieval national-protection narratives;
7. early-modern painting and popular humor;
8. modern state classification and commercial mythology.

Kuroda’s critique explains why “Shinto with later Buddhist influence” is inadequate: for much of the relevant period, Buddhist institutions were constitutive of kami worship itself. Breen and Teeuwen refine this by tracing how shrines, myths, and rites were repeatedly reorganized.

### 9.2 Were the pair originally Indian?

Three positions should be distinguished:

- **Strong diffusionist claim:** the Japanese pair is simply Vāyu and a thunder god imported intact from India. This overstates continuity.
- **Iconographic-transmission model:** individual poses, cloth/bag motifs, fierce guardian bodies, and placement around Kannon traveled through Indian, Central Asian, Chinese, and Japanese Buddhist art. This is well supported.
- **Composite model:** continental images were interpreted through existing Japanese wind, thunder, crop, vengeful-spirit, and local-shrine traditions. This best fits the evidence.

### 9.3 Were they derived from Vajrapāṇi?

Vajrapāṇi’s name and attribute connect him to the *vajra*, and Greco-Buddhist protector imagery influenced East Asian guardian art. But:

- Vāyu is the clearer Indian analogue for wind;
- Indra/Taishakuten, Chinese thunder gods, Kannon attendants, and indigenous thunder traditions all matter for Raijin;
- no primary source says “Vajrapāṇi divided into Raijin and Fūjin.”

The “Buddhist Vajrapāṇi origins of the pair” should therefore be labeled **[Partial iconographic comparison, not an established direct genealogy]**.

### 9.4 Did Sōtatsu invent the pair?

No. Medieval sculpture and painting predate him. What Sōtatsu did was compositionally revolutionary: he extracted the two guardians from a larger Buddhist ensemble and made their charged opposition across gold space the whole subject. His screens established the format copied by Kōrin, Hōitsu, and modern commercial art.

### 9.5 Are Takemikazuchi and Raijin the same?

The *Kojiki* gives Takemikazuchi a thunder-bearing name but portrays him primarily as a sword-born martial envoy. Kamo Wakeikazuchi has a separate Kamo genealogy; the eight thunders have another; the Buddhist Raijin another iconographic history. Later classifications may call all of them thunder gods, but identity cannot be presumed.

### 9.6 Is Fūjin Shinatsuhiko?

They can be identified in modern shrine teaching and mythology handbooks, and both govern wind. Yet the *Kojiki*’s Shinatsuhiko has no bag or demon body, while the Buddhist bag-bearer has continental precedents. “Fūjin” is best treated as a historical convergence of these lines, not a single unchanged being.

### 9.7 The reliability of familiar modern claims

The following are poorly supported or false:

- Raijin and Fūjin are canonical twin brothers.
- They were both born from Izanami’s corpse.
- They have an ancient, detailed rivalry myth.
- Raijin is always red and Fūjin always green.
- Raijin has one canonical son named Raitarō.
- Raijū is his fixed pet.
- Sōtatsu created their attributes.
- Toriyama Sekien’s yōkai books are the principal source for them.
- Vajrapāṇi alone is the origin of both.
- “Kamikaze” was coined during the Mongol invasions.
- Navel-taking has one proven ancient explanation.
- They possessed a paired human-sacrifice cult.
- A continuous Western occult tradition worshipped them before the twentieth century.

### 9.8 State of the evidence

The record is uneven:

- early chronicles survive through later manuscripts;
- several *fudoki* are lost and known only from quotations;
- ritual codes preserve formulas but not full performance;
- the original 1164 Sanjūsangendō ensemble was damaged in the 1249 fire;
- medieval sculptures are often unsigned;
- Sōtatsu’s screens are unsigned and undated;
- oral folklore was usually written down long after its alleged origin;
- shrine origin narratives often combine institutional memory with retrospective legitimation.

The strongest claims are therefore those anchored simultaneously in dated texts, surviving objects, institutional records, and critical art history.

### 9.9 Contested-material audit

Dedicated searches were conducted for etymology, origin, syncretism, sacrifice, suppression, occultism, neopaganism, and political appropriation.

Findings:

- **Etymology:** transparent Sino-Japanese titles coexist with disputed native names such as Shinatsuhiko and Wakeikazuchi.
- **Origin:** composite; neither pure indigenous continuity nor simple Indian importation explains all evidence.
- **Syncretism:** abundant and central—Kannon guardians, Vāyu/Fūten, Indra/Vajrapāṇi-related imagery, Chinese wind/thunder figures, local kami, Tenjin, and court ritual.
- **Human sacrifice:** no verified paired cult, inscription, shrine rule, or archaeological context.
- **Temple prostitution:** no relevant evidence.
- **Suppression:** broad shrine–temple separation and anti-Buddhist destruction affected the religious environments containing the figures, but no documented campaign targeted Raijin and Fūjin specifically.
- **Occult and neopagan use:** recent, scattered, and generally non-institutional.
- **Extremist appropriation:** the divine-wind concept was politically weaponized, especially in 1944–45; a specific extremist cult of the pair was not established.
- **Modern misinformation:** sibling rivalry, fixed parentage, Raijū as pet, unified ancient biography, and a direct dual descent from Vajrapāṇi are the principal recurring inventions.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Attestation or development |
|---|---|
| Before the eighth century | Weather rites and beliefs must have existed, but no secure written “Raijin and Fūjin” pair survives. |
| 675 | *Nihon shoki* retrospectively records court worship of the wind deity at Tatsuta. **[Dated event preserved in 720 text]** |
| 712 | *Kojiki* names Shinatsuhiko and describes eight thunder kami on Izanami’s corpse. |
| 713 onward | Provincial *fudoki* compilation; the lost Yamashiro text preserves the Kamo red-arrow thunder genealogy through later quotation. |
| c. 759 | Conventional completion horizon of the *Man’yōshū*; poetic *kamikaze* and weather language attest divine-wind vocabulary before the Mongol invasions. |
| 720 | *Nihon shoki* completed; includes wind-from-breath variant and court wind-cult notices. |
| 807 | *Kogo shūi* completed; important court-ritual source but no familiar pair. |
| c. 810–823 | *Nihon ryōiki* records Sugaru’s thunder capture and the thunder-child/Dōjō Hōshi cycle. |
| 903 | Sugawara no Michizane dies in exile. |
| 927 | *Engishiki* completed; codifies Tatsuta’s Wind-God Festival and preserves its norito. |
| 930 | Seiryōden lightning strike kills or injures courtiers; subsequently associated with Michizane’s vengeful spirit. |
| 947 | Traditional foundation date of Kitano worship in Kyoto. |
| Late 11th–early 12th c. | *Konjaku monogatari shū* transmits thunder and divine-child setsuwa. |
| 1164 | Original Rengeō-in/Sanjūsangendō founded under Go-Shirakawa; Kannon and guardian ensemble created. |
| 1219 | Jōkyū version of the *Kitano Tenjin engi emaki*, including thunder assault imagery. |
| 1249 | Sanjūsangendō complex burns; some images reportedly survive, while others must be remade. |
| Mid-13th c. | Surviving Sanjūsangendō Fūjin and Raijin sculptures produced in the Kei-school environment. |
| 1266 | Present Sanjūsangendō hall completed. |
| 1274 and 1281 | Mongol-led invasions; storms later interpreted as divine protection. |
| Late 13th c. | Ise wind sanctuaries gain enhanced status in relation to invasion-deliverance traditions. |
| 14th c. | *Taiheiki* narrates a blue-green bag-bearing wind deity acting against the invasion fleet. |
| 14th–15th c. | Wind and Thunder gods spread through Kannon paintings, shrine legends, and temple art. |
| Late 15th c. | Yoshida Shinto articulates a distinct, systematized “one-and-only Shinto,” changing the conceptual environment of kami. |
| Early 17th c. | Sōtatsu paints the *Wind God and Thunder God Screens*, isolating the pair from Kannon. |
| 18th c. | Ogata Kōrin copies Sōtatsu; popular prints and Ōtsu-e comic treatments flourish. |
| 1776–1784 | Toriyama Sekien publishes his four major yōkai catalogues. They shape later monster taxonomy but are not the pair’s principal primary source. |
| 1798 | Conventional completion date of Motoori Norinaga’s *Kojiki-den*, establishing influential kokugaku readings of ancient wind and thunder kami. |
| Early 19th c. | Sakai Hōitsu reinterprets Kōrin and Sōtatsu; Rinpa genealogy further canonizes the pair. |
| 1868 onward | Meiji shrine–temple separation, shrine ranking, and construction of modern State Shinto reclassify kami and Buddhist images. |
| Late 19th–early 20th c. | English translations by Chamberlain, Aston, and folklore collections present dispersed Japanese traditions as a national mythology. |
| 1910 | Yanagita’s *Tōno monogatari* helps found modern Japanese folklore studies. |
| 1944–1945 | *Shinpū/kamikaze* special-attack terminology politically reuses the medieval divine-wind complex. |
| 15 Dec. 1945 | Shinto Directive abolishes state sponsorship and propagation of State Shinto. |
| 1946 | Postwar shrine organization consolidates around Jinja Honchō. |
| 1968 | Donald Philippi publishes a major modern English translation of the *Kojiki*. |
| Late 20th c. | Museums, tattoos, film, manga, advertising, and games internationalize the Sōtatsu-derived pair. |
| 21st c. | Tatsuta and Ise wind rites, Kamo and Tenjin cults, Buddhist guardian images, museum exhibitions, and popular-media reinventions continue simultaneously. |

## 11. Sources

### Primary and near-primary textual witnesses

- *Kojiki* (712), especially the generation of Shinatsuhiko and the Yomi episode; Donald L. Philippi translation.
- *Nihon shoki* (720), mythic variants and the 675 Tatsuta notice; W. G. Aston translation, checked against later scholarship.
- Surviving fragments of *Yamashiro no kuni fudoki*.
- *Man’yōshū*, eighth century.
- Inbe no Hironari, *Kogo shūi* (807).
- Kyōkai, *Nihon ryōiki* (c. 810–823).
- *Engishiki* (927), volume 8, Tatsuta Wind-God Festival norito.
- *Konjaku monogatari shū*, late eleventh–early twelfth century.
- *Kitano Tenjin engi emaki*, especially the 1219 Jōkyū scrolls.
- *Taiheiki*, fourteenth century.
- Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den*, completed 1798.
- Toriyama Sekien’s illustrated catalogues, 1776–1784, used primarily as comparative evidence for early-modern yōkai taxonomy.

### Material witnesses

- Sanjūsangendō/Rengeō-in Wind and Thunder God sculptures, mid-thirteenth century.
- Medieval Thousand-Armed Kannon and Twenty-Eight Attendant paintings.
- Tawaraya Sōtatsu, *Wind God and Thunder God Screens*, early seventeenth century.
- Ogata Kōrin, *Wind God and Thunder God Screens*, eighteenth century.
- Sakai Hōitsu, related copies and reverse-screen paintings.
- Ōtsu-e thunder-god paintings and Edo-period popular prints.

### Critical scholarship and reference works

- Felicia Gressitt Bock, *Engi-shiki: Procedures of the Engi Era* and *Norito: A Translation of the Ancient Japanese Ritual Prayers*.
- Toshio Kuroda, “Shinto in the History of Japanese Religion.”
- John Breen and Mark Teeuwen, *A New History of Shinto*.
- Inoue Nobutaka, ed., *Encyclopedia of Shinto*.
- Yanagita Kunio’s thunder-god, divine-child, water-spirit, and wind-spirit studies.
- Hori Ichirō’s studies of Japanese folk religion.
- Noriko T. Reider, *Japanese Demon Lore*.
- Michael Dylan Foster, *The Book of Yōkai*.
- Japanese art-historical research on Kannon’s attendants, the Sanjūsangendō ensemble, and the Sōtatsu–Kōrin–Hōitsu lineage.
