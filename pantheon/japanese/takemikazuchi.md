# Takemikazuchi: Research Dossier

**Class:** male *kami* (神), specifically an *amatsukami* or heavenly deity in the court mythologies; subsequently a conquest and sword deity, the principal deity identified with Kashima, a member of the collective Kasuga deities, a Buddhist *gongen* or manifested deity in medieval interpretation, a martial patron, and—only in later folklore—an earthquake-subduing god.

**Earliest secure attestation by name:** the *Kojiki*, completed in 712, as **建御雷之男神** (*Takemikazuchi-no-o-no-kami*), also called **建布都神** (*Takefutsu-no-kami*) and **豊布都神** (*Toyofutsu-no-kami*). The text survives not in an eighth-century original but principally through the Shinpukuji manuscript copied in 1371–1372. The *Nihon shoki*, completed in 720, writes **武甕槌神** (*Takemikazuchi-no-kami*) and preserves substantially different genealogical and land-subjugation traditions. The approximately contemporary *Hitachi no kuni fudoki* mentions the “Great Heavenly Deity of Kashima” but does **not** name that deity Takemikazuchi. The first surviving text explicitly equating Takemikazuchi with the Kashima deity is the *Kogo shūi* of 807.

## Evidence labels used

- **Early attested:** named in an eighth- or early-ninth-century text.
- **Variant early canon:** an alternative account within the *Nihon shoki* or another early court source.
- **Later canonical:** established in court ritual, shrine records, or influential commentarial tradition after the first chronicles.
- **Regional tradition:** local shrine, provincial, or lineage account not independently established by the central chronicles.
- **Learned systematization:** theological, antiquarian, or philological organization imposed on older material.
- **Syncretistic identification:** a historical equation made within Buddhist–kami or other combined systems.
- **Medieval/early-modern accretion:** demonstrably later narrative or iconographic development.
- **Folklore:** orally or popularly transmitted material whose age or textual pedigree is uncertain.
- **Modern reconstruction:** a scholarly hypothesis rather than an ancient statement.
- **Modern invention/retelling:** a recent attribution or narrative presented in popular culture, sometimes incorrectly as ancient.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

In the *Kojiki*, Takemikazuchi is a being generated from the blood on Izanagi’s sword after the killing of the fire god Kagutsuchi. He becomes the decisive heavenly envoy in the “transfer of the land” (*kuni-yuzuri*) and overcomes Takeminakata in a test of strength. In the *Nihon shoki*, Futsunushi generally occupies the leading position in the same conquest, while Takemikazuchi accompanies him; in one variant Takemikazuchi protests against being passed over. During Jimmu’s campaign, Takemikazuchi does not descend personally but sends the sword Futsu-no-Mitama through Takakuraji.

Thus the earliest texts securely support a being associated with heavenly military coercion, a miraculous sword, and—in the *Kojiki* spelling—thunder. They do not themselves give a systematic portfolio such as “god of thunder, swords, sumo, martial arts, earthquakes, victory, and ice.” That familiar list combines different periods.

### Attested names

- **建御雷之男神 — Takemikazuchi-no-o-no-kami.** *Kojiki*, 712. The final *o* means “male/man.” *Take* is commonly understood as “brave, fierce, martial”; *mi* is honorific; *ikazuchi* is thunder. “Brave/August Thunder-Male” is therefore reasonable, but the characters in early divine names are not always straightforward semantic spellings.
- **建御雷神 — Takemikazuchi-no-kami.** A shortened *Kojiki*-type spelling.
- **武甕槌神 — Takemikazuchi-no-kami.** *Nihon shoki*, 720. The graphs can suggest “martial” (*take*), “jar” (*mika*), and “mallet” (*tsuchi*), but their function may be partly phonographic. One scholarly hypothesis takes **甕**, “jar,” seriously and proposes a vessel deity or a cult involving great jars; another sees the *Kojiki*’s 雷, “thunder,” as a meaningful interpretation of an older opaque name. Kokugakuin’s philological commentary records the jar-deity hypothesis without treating it as settled. The once-common romanization *Takemikatsuchi* reflects a disputed segmentation/pronunciation; *Takemikazuchi* is now usual.
- **武甕雷男神 — Takemikazuchi-no-o-no-kami.** A mixed variant reported in the *Nihon shoki* textual tradition.
- **建布都神 — Takefutsu-no-kami.** *Kojiki*, 712. *Futsu* has been interpreted as the sound or force of cutting; it is also the crucial element in Futsunushi and Futsu-no-Mitama. The name associates Takemikazuchi with sword-force but does not prove that Takemikazuchi and Futsunushi were originally one god.
- **豊布都神 — Toyofutsu-no-kami.** *Kojiki*, 712; “Abundant/Excellent Futsu.”
- **鹿島神 / 鹿島大神 — Kashima-no-kami / Kashima Ōkami.** “Deity/Great Deity of Kashima.” Secure as an identification with Takemikazuchi only from the *Kogo shūi* (807) onward. Earlier *Hitachi fudoki* language—**香島之天之大神**, “Great Heavenly Deity of Kashima”—does not itself name Takemikazuchi.
- **鹿島大明神 — Kashima Daimyōjin.** A medieval and early-modern “Great Illustrious Deity” title, operating in a Buddhist–kami religious environment.
- **健御賀豆智命 — Takemikazuchi-no-mikoto.** Orthography in the *Engishiki* Kasuga Festival *norito*, compiled by 927.
- **春日第一神 — Kasuga daiichi-shin**, “First Deity of Kasuga.” Ritual and art-historical designation because Takemikazuchi occupies Kasuga’s First Sanctuary.
- **春日大明神 / 春日権現 — Kasuga Daimyōjin / Kasuga Gongen.** Usually collective designations for the Kasuga deities rather than an exclusive personal name for Takemikazuchi.

### Etymological disputes

1. **Thunder interpretation.** The explicit graph 雷 in 建御雷 and the word *ikazuchi* strongly encouraged the reading “thunder deity.” This is ancient as a textual interpretation because it is already present in the *Kojiki* orthography.
2. **Jar or vessel interpretation.** 武甕槌 contains 甕, “jar.” Some modern scholars connect this with traditions that Kashima once revered a great vessel. This is a hypothesis, not a statement in the chronicles.
3. **Sword-noise interpretation.** *Futsu* may represent the sound or force of a blade cutting. The alternative names Takefutsu and Toyofutsu, the Futsu-no-Mitama episode, and proximity to Futsunushi support a sword complex. Whether this means common origin, borrowing, or later editorial redistribution remains disputed.
4. **Composite-deity theory.** A major modern reconstruction holds that the chronicle figure Takemikazuchi and the older local Kashima deity were originally separate, then identified through Nakatomi/Fujiwara expansion and court myth-making. The decisive evidence is the silence of the *Hitachi fudoki* regarding the name Takemikazuchi, followed by the explicit equation in 807.
5. **Futsunushi displacement theory.** Since the *Nihon shoki* gives Futsunushi primacy while the *Kojiki* centers Takemikazuchi, scholars have proposed rivalry or transfer of mythic functions between Mononobe-associated sword traditions and Nakatomi/Fujiwara traditions. This remains reconstruction; the sources do not narrate such an institutional takeover.

### Classifications through time

- **712–720:** heavenly male kami and subjugator.
- **Eighth–tenth centuries:** Kashima/Kasuga state-protective deity and recipient of court offerings.
- **Medieval period:** Kashima or Kasuga *daimyōjin/gongen*, simultaneously kami and manifestation of a Buddha or bodhisattva.
- **Warrior traditions:** martial and sword patron.
- **Early-modern folklore:** suppressor of earthquakes and the subterranean catfish.
- **Modern shrine discourse:** deity of national foundation, victory, martial arts, safe travel, industry, protection, and earthquakes. Many of these benefits are historically layered rather than original.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest named evidence

The earliest secure named source is the *Kojiki* of 712, compiled by Ō no Yasumaro from traditions associated with Hieda no Are and the early imperial court. It is written in a mixed system employing Chinese characters semantically and phonetically. No 712 manuscript survives. The oldest complete witness is the Shinpukuji copy, produced by Buddhist monks in 1371–1372, 659 years after compilation. That transmission gap requires caution, although comparative manuscripts show a stable core. [Kokugakuin on the Shinpukuji witness](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/kojiki/%E5%9B%BD%E5%9C%9F%E3%81%AE%E4%BF%AE%E7%90%86%E5%9B%BA%E6%88%90/)

The *Nihon shoki* was completed in 720 as a court-sponsored chronicle in Literary Sinitic. Its mythic books survive in medieval manuscripts; the oldest surviving manuscript portion of any book is the ninth-century Tanaka manuscript of Book X, not the divine-age books containing Takemikazuchi. The mythic books therefore also have a substantial manuscript gap. [Nara National Museum manuscript description](https://www.narahaku.go.jp/english/collection/p-1190-0.html)

### Primary corpus

#### *Kojiki* (古事記), 712 — early attested

Takemikazuchi appears in three connected complexes:

1. **Generation from Kagutsuchi’s blood.** Blood from Izanagi’s sword falls on rocks and produces Mikahayahi, Hihayahi, and Takemikazuchi. The text also gives the names Takefutsu and Toyofutsu.
2. **Land transfer.** Takagi and Amaterasu seek another envoy after earlier missions fail. Ame-no-Ohabari, conceived as both sword and deity, recommends his son Takemikazuchi. Takemikazuchi and Ame-no-Torifune descend to Inasa in Izumo. Takemikazuchi plants a ten-hand sword upside down on the wave-crest and sits cross-legged on its point while demanding Ōkuninushi’s submission.
3. **Contest with Takeminakata.** Takeminakata challenges him to a strength contest. Takemikazuchi lets Takeminakata grasp his arm, transforms it first into an icicle and then a sword blade, then takes Takeminakata’s hand, crushes it “like a young reed,” and throws him. He pursues him to the “Sea of Suwa” in Shinano, where Takeminakata submits and promises not to leave.
4. **Jimmu’s sword.** Takemikazuchi’s conquest sword reaches Takakuraji’s storehouse and is given to the future Emperor Jimmu; the poisoned army recovers.

Chamberlain’s public-domain translation can be checked online, while Donald L. Philippi’s *Kojiki* (University of Tokyo Press, 1968) remains the requested modern scholarly English translation. [Complete Chamberlain translation](https://sacred-texts.com/shi/kj/index.htm)

#### *Nihon shoki* (日本書紀), 720 — early canonical variants

The work preserves multiple “one writing says” variants rather than one stable account:

- In one cosmogonic variant, Mikahayahi is parent of Hihayahi, and Hihayahi is parent of Takemikazuchi. Elsewhere the blood sequence differs.
- In the principal land-subjugation account, Futsunushi is selected; Takemikazuchi indignantly asks whether Futsunushi alone is considered valiant, and accompanies him.
- In another version, both are directly sent by the heavenly deity.
- Kotoshironushi consents to the transfer. Ōnamuchi/Ōkuninushi submits after negotiations that include provision of a palace and ritual status.
- A variant says that before the descent, the evil star deity Amatsu-Mikaboshi/Ame-no-Kagaseo must be dealt with. The text’s allocation of that task is not perfectly consistent; it should not be simplified into a securely narrated duel between Takemikazuchi and the star.
- In Book III, Amaterasu asks Takemikazuchi to descend again during Jimmu’s crisis. He says his sword will suffice and tells Takakuraji in a dream where Futsu-no-Mitama will appear. [Aston’s Book II](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nihongi/Book_II) and [Book III](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nihongi/Book_III)

The plural versions make the *Nihon shoki* particularly important: they show that the court did not inherit a single canonical relationship between Takemikazuchi and Futsunushi.

#### *Hitachi no kuni fudoki* (常陸国風土記), compiled c. 713–721 — early local evidence, identification uncertain

The Kashima district section calls the cult object **Kashima’s Great Heavenly Deity**, describes three associated sanctuaries, reports ancient offerings of swords, spears, iron bows, armor, and horses, and places the establishment of the Kashima sacred district in 649 under Nakatomi personnel. The early date of some offerings belongs to the text’s legendary imperial chronology; the compilation itself is eighth-century evidence.

Crucially, the surviving text does not call this deity Takemikazuchi. It establishes an early martial Kashima cult but not the identity later taken for granted. [Japanese text of the Kashima section](https://miko.org/~uraki/kuon/furu/text/fuudo/hitachi/hitachi02.htm)

#### *Kogo shūi* (古語拾遺), 807 — later canonical identification

Compiled by Inbe no Hironari, this work explicitly states that Takemikazuchi is the deity now at Kashima in Hitachi. It is the earliest surviving unambiguous bridge between the chronicle god and Kashima’s cult. It also gives a genealogy in which Takemikazuchi descends from Mikahayahi/Hihayahi, paralleling a *Nihon shoki* variant.

Because the *Kogo shūi* was written partly to defend Inbe ritual precedence against Nakatomi dominance, its testimony is indispensable but institutionally interested.

#### *Shoku Nihongi*, *Shoku Nihon kōki*, and court rank records — historical cult evidence

Court chronicles document offerings, divine ranks, and the political importance of Kashima and Katori. In 836, *Shoku Nihon kōki* records elevation of the Kashima deity under the name **建御賀豆智命** to senior second rank, alongside the Katori deity Iwainushi. This confirms that the equation had entered official nomenclature by the ninth century.

#### *Engishiki* (延喜式), submitted 927 — ritual canon

Book VIII preserves the Kasuga Festival *norito*. It addresses:

> “Takemikazuchi-no-mikoto dwelling at awesome Kashima; Iwainushi-no-mikoto dwelling at Katori; Ame-no-Koyane-no-mikoto and Himegami dwelling at Hiraoka.”

It then describes the establishment of their sanctuary at Mount Mikasa and offerings of mirrors, swords, bows, spears, horses, textiles, sake, and food for imperial and aristocratic peace. This is direct evidence of Takemikazuchi’s incorporation into court ritual and the Fujiwara-centered Kasuga complex. [Text, romanization, and translation](https://norito.sumeragi-iyasaka.com/en/norito/kasuga-sai/)

#### *Man’yōshū* (万葉集), eighth century — negative finding

The anthology contains poetry concerning Kashima, eastern travel, frontier guards, and Mount Mikasa/Kasuga, but no securely identified narrative appearance by the named Takemikazuchi comparable to the chronicles. References to Kashima or Kasuga should not automatically be converted into direct mentions of him.

#### Ise, Izumo, and Usa records — negative or indirect evidence

Takemikazuchi is absent as a principal deity from Ise, Izumo, and Usa’s central cult systems. Izumo shrine traditions intersect with the land-transfer myth through Ōkuninushi; Ise theology supplies the Amaterasu-centered sovereign framework; Usa/Hachiman later becomes the more nationally prominent warrior cult. None establishes an independent early cult of Takemikazuchi at those sites.

#### Medieval setsuwa and shrine literature

- **Kasuga shrine records and *Koshaki*.** An early-thirteenth-century record dates the four-deity Kasuga sanctuary to 768 and describes Takemikazuchi’s transfer from Kashima.
- **Kasuga Daimyōjin oracle texts**, especially traditions recorded around Myōe and Jōkei in the thirteenth century, present the Kasuga deity as a Buddhist protector and oracle.
- **Kasuga Gongen genki-e**, completed in 1309, is a twenty-scroll illustrated miracle cycle sponsored by Fujiwara/Kasuga interests. Individual identities frequently yield to collective Kasuga Daimyōjin.
- **Konjaku monogatari-shū**, c. late eleventh–early twelfth century: no major securely identifiable Takemikazuchi cycle comparable to the Kasuga miracle corpus. General references to Kasuga or Kashima require case-by-case identification.

#### Early-modern antiquarian and folklore sources

- **Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den*** (古事記伝, written 1764–1798): a foundational philological commentary that helped establish the *Kojiki* reading and elevated its myths as evidence of an ancient Japanese way. Its readings are learned Edo-period reconstruction, not independent ancient testimony.
- **Hirata Atsutane** (1776–1843): incorporated the chronicle gods into a more systematized, national cosmology and afterlife teaching. His formulations belong to late *kokugaku*, not early cult evidence.
- **Toriyama Sekien’s yōkai catalogues** (1776–1784): important for early-modern monster iconography, but they do not constitute a primary ancient source for Takemikazuchi. Namazu folklore must instead be traced through Kashima lore and earthquake prints.
- **Yanagita Kunio, *Tōno monogatari*** (1910) and folklore studies: crucial for the disciplinary recovery of local spirits and oral tradition, but not a source for an independent Tōno Takemikazuchi cycle.
- **Hori Ichirō:** his studies of Japanese folk religion help frame pilgrimage, wandering religious specialists, and regional cult, but should not be cited as primary evidence for eighth-century Takemikazuchi.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains by period

**Early attested**

- Heavenly enforcement of the transfer of terrestrial rule.
- Martial power and intimidation.
- Miraculous bodily transformation: arm to ice pillar/icicle and blade.
- Possession or command of the conquest sword Futsu-no-Mitama.
- Assistance to the sovereign’s conquest through that sword.

**Later canonical and institutional**

- Protection of Kashima, Kasuga, the court, and the Fujiwara.
- Military victory and state pacification.
- Sword ritual and warrior discipline.
- Protection of journeys and expansion into eastern Japan through networks of Kashima branch shrines.

**Medieval and early modern**

- Buddhist protection as a Kasuga/Kashima *daimyōjin* or *gongen*.
- Patronage of martial lineages.
- Protection against earthquakes.

**Modern shrine portfolios**

Victory, martial arts, household and national protection, safe travel, industry, agriculture, health, and earthquake prevention. These broad benefits reflect the normal expansion of a major shrine deity’s efficacy.

### Thunder

Thunder is ancient in his *Kojiki* name, but the surviving narratives do not show him hurling lightning, controlling storms, or acting like the later drum-bearing Raijin. Takemikazuchi and Raijin are therefore not interchangeable names for one securely attested god. “Thunder god” is defensible; the elaborate electrical combat powers common in modern games and anime are modern iconography.

### Sword and Futsu-no-Mitama

The blade is central, but source roles differ:

- Takemikazuchi is born from sword blood.
- At Inasa he displays a sword and sits on its point.
- His hand becomes a blade against Takeminakata.
- His conquest sword, Futsu-no-Mitama, saves Jimmu’s army.
- Futsu-no-Mitama later belongs to the ritual world of Isonokami Shrine.

Calling Futsu-no-Mitama simply “Takemikazuchi’s personal katana” is misleading. The texts call it a divine sword, not a medieval curved katana, and its *futsu* name overlaps Futsunushi’s identity. The sword’s ritual afterlife involves Isonokami and Mononobe traditions as well as Kashima. [Aston’s Jimmu passage](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nihongi/Book_III)

### The “first sumo match”

The *Kojiki* calls the encounter a **力競べ / chikara-kurabe**, a contest or comparison of strength. It includes grasping, transformation, crushing, and throwing. It does not call the event *sumō*, prescribe sumo rules, or make Takemikazuchi the formal inventor of the sport.

The description is reasonably treated as an early mythic precedent for wrestling. The claim “the first sumo match” is a later retrospective label. The *Nihon shoki*’s explicitly etiological human match between Nomi no Sukune and Taima no Kehaya is a separate origin narrative.

### Ice

Takemikazuchi turns his arm into an ice pillar or icicle in the *Kojiki*. This is a securely attested action, but “god of ice” is a modern portfolio inferred from a single feat. No early cult of Takemikazuchi as controller of winter or frozen water is documented.

### Earthquakes, the keystone, and Namazu

At Kashima lies the **要石, kaname-ishi**, a visible stone traditionally said to extend deep underground. Medieval evidence associates the Kashima deity with stabilizing the land; the fully developed story that the stone pins the head of a giant subterranean catfish became widespread especially in the Edo period.

After the Ansei Edo earthquake of 1855, mass-produced **namazu-e** showed Kashima restraining, punishing, or negotiating with the catfish. Some prints say Kashima was absent at the annual gathering of gods in Izumo when the earthquake occurred; Ebisu had been left in charge. Others make the catfish apologize or redistribute wealth. These prints are social commentary as well as religious images: the catfish could destroy elite property while providing work for laborers.

The Agency for Cultural Affairs notes that belief in the keystone’s earthquake-preventing force reaches into the medieval period, while widespread catfish-earthquake imagery consolidated much later. It is therefore false to project the entire Namazu narrative back into the *Kojiki*. [Agency for Cultural Affairs survey](https://www.bunka.go.jp/seisaku/bunkazai/shokai/pdf/94354801_03.pdf) and [University of Tokyo namazu-e record](https://iiif.dl.itc.u-tokyo.ac.jp/repo/s/ishimoto/document/9f40daaa-913c-4dd6-a6c6-80e9b2a11bcc)

### Animals and objects

- **Deer:** Takemikazuchi’s vehicle in medieval Kasuga foundation legend. Kasuga deer consequently became sacred messengers. The white-deer journey is not in the eighth-century chronicles; current scholarship places the legend’s developed form in the Kamakura period.
- **Sword:** earliest and most stable emblem.
- **Rock/keystone:** Kashima landscape cult; earthquake meaning expands over time.
- **Horse:** historically offered in elite ritual; also part of the *Engishiki* Kasuga offerings.
- **Sakaki and wisteria:** prominent in Kasuga mandalas. Wisteria also signifies Fujiwara identity.
- **Catfish:** adversary or restrained creature only in late folklore and prints.
- **No securely ancient sacred color, weekday, planet, or fixed number** belongs uniquely to Takemikazuchi.

### Iconography

There is no securely identified eighth-century anthropomorphic portrait of Takemikazuchi. Early shrine worship did not require a public cult statue, and his identity could reside in place, sword, offering, or concealed sacred object.

Important later visual forms include:

1. **Kasuga shrine mandalas, thirteenth–fourteenth centuries:** show the Kasuga landscape, shrines, Buddhist originals, or a deer carrying a sakaki tree, mirror, or circular sacred field.
2. **Kashima-departure mandalas:** depict Takemikazuchi riding a deer from Kashima to Mount Mikasa, sometimes accompanied by Futsunushi and priestly attendants. [Nara National Museum example](https://www.narahaku.go.jp/collection/767-0.html)
3. **Kasuga Deer Mandala, Nanbokuchō period:** a white spotted deer on clouds supports a sakaki tree entwined with wisteria. The deer functions as Takemikazuchi’s vehicle rather than as a portrait of the god himself. [Metropolitan Museum object](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/53187)
4. **Warrior-like portraits:** medieval and early-modern paintings increasingly give him court or martial dress, sword, armor, and commanding posture.
5. **Yashima Gakutei’s nineteenth-century surimono:** an Edo-period learned image of Takemikazuchi as a heroic martial deity.
6. **Namazu-e, 1855 onward:** Kashima/Takemikazuchi with sword or keystone restraining a giant catfish.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogical versions

**Kojiki, 712:** Izanagi kills Kagutsuchi with the ten-hand sword Ame-no-Ohabari. Blood from the sword produces Mikahayahi, Hihayahi, and Takemikazuchi. The sequence can be read as co-generation, although the later dispatch episode calls Ame-no-Ohabari Takemikazuchi’s father.

**Nihon shoki variant, 720:** Mikahayahi is the parent of Hihayahi, and Hihayahi the parent of Takemikazuchi. This makes Takemikazuchi a third-generation descendant of the blood event.

**Kogo shūi, 807:** similarly makes him a descendant or son within the Mikahayahi/Hihayahi line and identifies him with Kashima.

No early source supplies a consort or children. Assertions that Himegami is Takemikazuchi’s wife or daughter belong to local or later speculative traditions and conflict with the more common Kasuga arrangement in which Himegami accompanies Ame-no-Koyane.

Futsunushi is neither securely his brother nor an alternative name in all sources. They arise from closely parallel sword-blood complexes and become paired conquerors, but the texts preserve distinct names, cult centers, and roles.

| **Kasuga Daimyōjin** | Takemikazuchi becomes both an individual component and an aspect of the collective Kasuga deity. | Medieval shrine-temple literature | Syncretistic collective identity |
| **Namazu** | Kashima/Takemikazuchi restrains the earthquake-causing catfish with the keystone; sometimes punishes or negotiates with it. | Developed medieval–Edo lore; especially 1855 prints | Folklore/early-modern accretion |
| **Ebisu** | In some post-1855 prints and stories, Ebisu substitutes for the absent Kashima deity in guarding the keystone. | *Namazu-e*, 1855 | Early-modern popular invention |

No securely early interaction is documented with Susanoo, Raijin, Hachiman, Inari, or Tsukuyomi. Their occasional association with Takemikazuchi in modern pantheon lists is thematic, not textual.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. Birth from the blood of Kagutsuchi

**Earliest source:** *Kojiki*, 712.

After Kagutsuchi’s fiery birth causes Izanami’s death, Izanagi draws a ten-hand sword and kills him. Blood adhering to or spurting from different parts of the sword falls upon rocks and becomes deities. Takemikazuchi appears in the group generated from the blood, with the alternate names Takefutsu and Toyofutsu.

The *Nihon shoki* distributes the resulting gods differently among multiple variants. In one, Takemikazuchi is not an immediate blood-born peer but descendant of Mikahayahi through Hihayahi. Futsunushi’s ancestry is generated from another portion of the blood. These variants show editorial efforts to relate multiple sword deities without fully merging them.

**Function:** The narrative ties martial divine power to death, blood, rock, and the sword’s capacity to transform destructive fire into protective force. More specific ritual explanations are scholarly inference.

### B. Selection as envoy

In the *Kojiki*, earlier envoys sent to secure the terrestrial realm fail or defect. The heavenly assembly turns to Ame-no-Ohabari, who blocks access at the Heavenly River but offers his son Takemikazuchi. This makes Takemikazuchi the answer to failed persuasion.

In the *Nihon shoki*, Futsunushi is initially chosen. Takemikazuchi demands to know whether Futsunushi alone is a brave warrior. This variant preserves competition or at least status negotiation between the two gods. It is important evidence against later seamless presentations of them as an eternal, equal pair.

### C. The descent to Inasa and transfer of the land

Takemikazuchi and Ame-no-Torifune in the *Kojiki*, or Takemikazuchi and Futsunushi in the *Nihon shoki*, descend to the beach of Inasa/Itasa in Izumo. The *Kojiki*’s image is deliberately intimidating: Takemikazuchi unsheathes a ten-hand sword, plants it upside down on the wave crest, and sits cross-legged on its tip while announcing the heavenly claim.

Ōkuninushi does not immediately answer but refers the decision to his sons. Kotoshironushi accepts. Takeminakata resists. Following their outcomes, Ōkuninushi yields on conditions concerning his dwelling and ritual status.

The *Nihon shoki* versions redistribute speeches and prominence. Futsunushi often leads; Takeminakata is absent; Ōnamuchi’s palace and role in “hidden” or divine matters receive emphasis.

**Historical function:** The cycle legitimates the transfer of rule from earthly to heavenly lineages and ultimately the imperial order. It also incorporates major regional gods—especially Izumo’s—within a court-centered hierarchy. Whether it records a specific historical conquest cannot be demonstrated.

### D. The contest with Takeminakata

Takeminakata arrives carrying a stone said to require a thousand men to pull and challenges Takemikazuchi to compare strength.

1. Takeminakata grasps Takemikazuchi’s arm.
2. Takemikazuchi turns it into an icicle or upright ice formation.
3. He then transforms it into a sword blade.
4. Takeminakata recoils.
5. Takemikazuchi grasps Takeminakata’s hand, crushes it like a young reed, and throws him.
6. Takeminakata flees; Takemikazuchi pursues him to the Sea of Suwa.
7. Takeminakata begs for his life and promises to remain there and obey.

Only the *Kojiki* contains this episode. It is not evidence for a pan-Japanese prehistoric “first sumo tournament.” Suwa traditions also preserve Takeminakata as an arriving and conquering god rather than merely a defeated exile. Scholars therefore debate whether the *Kojiki* reverses a Suwa migration myth, dramatizes court domination of peripheral cults, or serves Nakatomi/Fujiwara interests. None is proven.

### E. The star deity

A *Nihon shoki* variant says that before terrestrial pacification can be completed, the hostile heavenly star deity Amatsu-Mikaboshi/Ame-no-Kagaseo must be subdued. Another deity, Takehazuchi/Shitsurihime, receives the decisive role in at least one version.

Modern retellings frequently stage a duel in which Takemikazuchi kills the star god. The source is less explicit. The safe conclusion is that Takemikazuchi’s mission is narratively linked with the star deity’s suppression, not that a detailed personal combat is preserved.

### F. Jimmu and Futsu-no-Mitama

During Jimmu’s eastern expedition, hostile gods produce a poisonous vapor that incapacitates the army. Takakuraji dreams that Amaterasu orders Takemikazuchi to descend and restore order. Takemikazuchi replies that his conquest sword can do so without his bodily descent. He tells Takakuraji that Futsu-no-Mitama will be found in the storehouse. Takakuraji awakens, finds the sword standing point-down, and presents it to Jimmu. The army recovers.

This episode makes divine presence transferable through a weapon. It also connects Takemikazuchi’s mythic subjugation with royal conquest and the ritual prestige of Isonokami.

### G. The deer journey to Kasuga

Later Kasuga tradition says that Takemikazuchi left Kashima riding a white deer, accompanied by Nakatomi attendants, and arrived at Mount Mikasa to protect the capital and Fujiwara house. The historical core is the transplantation of the Kashima deity into the Kasuga complex in the eighth century; the picturesque deer journey is medieval elaboration, prominently represented in Kasuga mandalas.

### H. The keystone and catfish

The Kashima keystone stabilizes the earth. In the developed legend, its buried portion pins the head of Namazu, whose movements cause earthquakes. When Kashima leaves for the gods’ gathering at Izumo, the guard may fail.

The story is absent from the *Kojiki*, *Nihon shoki*, and *Hitachi fudoki*. Medieval evidence supports Kashima as an earthquake protector; the recognizable catfish cycle belongs chiefly to later folklore and exploded into print after 1855.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Kashima Jingū

**Location:** Kashima, Ibaraki Prefecture, on the eastern Kantō coast.

**Earliest evidence:** the *Hitachi fudoki*, compiled c. 713–721, describes Kashima’s Great Heavenly Deity and a sacred district reportedly organized in 649 by Nakatomi officials. It mentions the combined cult of three sanctuaries and legendary offerings of weapons, armor, mirrors, and horses.

**Identity problem:** the *fudoki* does not name Takemikazuchi. The shrine’s present assertion that Takemikazuchi was enshrined from the legendary first year of Jimmu is institutional tradition, not contemporary evidence. The secure documentary sequence is:

1. eighth-century Kashima deity;
2. explicit equation with Takemikazuchi in 807;
3. ninth-century official rank under a Takemikazuchi name;
4. tenth-century ritual inclusion in the *Engishiki*.

**Political and economic position:** Kashima controlled a sacred district, estates, ritual personnel, and networks of branch shrines. Its proximity to Katori created an eastern military-sacral pair. Court offerings and high divine rank tied the site to the central state.

**Rituals and objects:** weapons, horses, textiles, sake, agricultural and marine food, purification, pilgrimage, and martial devotions. The keystone and deer park are important landscape features. Claims that the shrine’s sacred body was always a particular surviving sword cannot be demonstrated continuously from the eighth century.

**Grand Imperial Procession:** the *Mifune Matsuri*, held in the Horse year every twelve years, ceremonially joins Kashima and Katori through a maritime procession. Its extant form has accumulated medieval and modern layers.

### Kasuga Taisha

Kasuga in Nara enshrines:

1. Takemikazuchi from Kashima;
2. Futsunushi from Katori;
3. Ame-no-Koyane from Hiraoka;
4. Himegami from Hiraoka.

The official foundation date is 768, recorded in the early-thirteenth-century *Koshaki*. Evidence suggests cult activity on Mount Mikasa before the formal four-sanctuary foundation, so “founded in 768” should be understood as the shrine’s canonical institutional date. [Kasuga’s official historical account](https://www.kasugataisha.or.jp/en/guidance_en/modal-01/)

Kasuga became the Fujiwara tutelary shrine, while Kōfukuji was the family temple. Their integrated shrine-temple institution accumulated estates, ritual monopolies, artistic patronage, and political authority. Takemikazuchi’s position as First Deity made him “the Fujiwara’s god,” but Ame-no-Koyane remained the clan’s explicit ancestor. These are complementary, not identical, roles.

### Kōfukuji and medieval shrine-temple ritual

Kasuga and Kōfukuji formed a combined religious polity. Buddhist monks administered, interpreted, and represented the kami; shrine personnel participated in a Buddhist institutional world. Takemikazuchi was identified principally with **Fukūkenjaku Kannon**, whose deerskin garment supported the deer association, and alternatively with **Śākyamuni**. The *Kasuga Gongen genki-e* and Kasuga mandalas are products of this integrated order.

### Isonokami

Futsu-no-Mitama is enshrined at Isonokami, traditionally deposited there after Jimmu’s conquest. This does not make Isonokami an early Takemikazuchi-only sanctuary. The site belongs to a broader sword cult involving Mononobe traditions, Futsunushi/Futsu terminology, royal armories, and spirit-bearing weapons.

### Martial schools

Kashima became a center for martial lineages:

- Kashima no Tachi traditions;
- Kashima Shintō-ryū;
- Kashima Shinryū;
- later “Kashima-transmitted” schools.

Lineage documents claim revelation from Takemikazuchi to Kuninazu no Mahito in the seventh century, but the oldest cited scrolls are much later; Kashima Shinryū’s extant lineage evidence includes eighteenth-century manuscripts. The secure conclusion is that medieval and early-modern martial systems sought divine authorization from Kashima. The exact antiquity of individual techniques is disputed. [Kashima-Shinryū lineage account](https://www.kashima-shinryu.jp/English/history.html)

### Sacrifice and contested practices

No reliable source located for this dossier documents **human sacrifice**, “temple prostitution,” or sexual servitude in Takemikazuchi’s cult. Animal offerings, especially horses and deer symbolism, require separate treatment from sacrifice: horses could be offered ceremonially or represented by substitutes, while Kasuga deer were protected messengers.

Cult violence is nonetheless historical in a broader sense. The Kasuga–Kōfukuji institution used armed personnel, estate power, and coercive demonstrations in medieval politics. Takemikazuchi’s myth also sacralized conquest and coerced transfer of land. These facts do not establish a Takemikazuchi-specific sacrificial cult.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 712: reconstruction only

There is no inscription or datable image naming Takemikazuchi before the *Kojiki*. A local Kashima cult likely preceded the chronicles, but calling its pre-eighth-century god Takemikazuchi is retrospective. Archaeology and the *Hitachi fudoki* indicate an important eastern cult with martial offerings and Nakatomi connections; its divine name and original character remain uncertain.

### 712–807: formation and identification

The *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* incorporated Takemikazuchi into competing versions of court conquest mythology. The *Hitachi fudoki* independently described Kashima’s deity without naming him. During the Nara period the Kashima cult was transferred or invited to Kasuga and integrated into Fujiwara religious geography. By 807, the *Kogo shūi* explicitly equated Takemikazuchi and the Kashima god.

This is the strongest period for a “fusion” model: a textual heavenly warrior, an eastern territorial deity, and Nakatomi/Fujiwara institutional interests became one recognized figure.

### Ninth–tenth centuries: court canonization

Official rank grants and the *Engishiki* established Takemikazuchi as the named deity of Kashima and First Deity of Kasuga. The court offered weapons, horses, mirrors, textiles, and food. Kashima and Katori became an eastern pair; Kasuga became a central ritual complex.

### Eleventh–fourteenth centuries: Kasuga–Buddhist transformation

Takemikazuchi became a *daimyōjin* and *gongen*, with Fukūkenjaku Kannon or Śākyamuni as Buddhist original ground. Collective Kasuga identity often mattered more than an isolated divine biography. Oracles, miracle tales, pilgrimage, mandalas, and shrine-temple estate networks expanded the deity’s field.

The deer journey and mature Kasuga iconography belong chiefly here. The Kashima deity’s earthquake-preventing character also appears by the medieval period, although the catfish imagery had not yet reached its later popular form.

### Fifteenth–seventeenth centuries: warriors and martial revelation

Kashima’s deity became a source of authorization for sword schools. Warrior patronage intensified, while Kasuga remained tied to aristocratic and Buddhist institutions. Local Kashima shrines spread with eastern settlement, military families, and pilgrimage.

The label “god of martial arts” is most historically at home in this later reception, not as a verbatim eighth-century classification.

### Edo period: antiquarian recovery and popular folklore

Kokugaku philology restored the *Kojiki* to exceptional authority. Motoori Norinaga’s *Kojiki-den* helped fix readings and encouraged interpretation of Takemikazuchi as part of an uncorrupted ancient divine order. Hirata Atsutane developed a more systematic national theology.

Kashima’s earthquake protection entered mass culture. After the 1855 Ansei Edo earthquake, *namazu-e* disseminated the image of Kashima/Takemikazuchi restraining the catfish. Government attempts to suppress some prints show that the genre’s redistributionist satire mattered politically.

### 1868–1945: separation and State Shinto

The 1868 separation orders required shrines to remove Buddhist titles, images, bells, scriptures, and *honji* identifications. At Kasuga this forcibly severed the long-standing relationship with Kōfukuji; Kōfukuji was temporarily devastated and abandoned by many monks. [National Archives separation order](https://www.archives.go.jp/ayumi/kobetsu/m01_1868_03.html)

Takemikazuchi was re-presented as a purely national kami rather than a manifestation of Kannon or Śākyamuni. Kashima received first-rank imperial-shrine status in the modern hierarchy. The land-transfer and Jimmu myths suited national-foundation ideology, military education, and imperial legitimation.

There is no basis for saying that every worshipper or every Takemikazuchi shrine promoted militarism identically. Institutionally, however, the deity’s conquest mythology was incorporated into a state-directed shrine system that treated imperial mythology as public orthodoxy.

### 1945–present: religious法人 and heritage deity

The December 1945 Shinto Directive ended government sponsorship and the classification of Shrine Shinto as a nonreligious state cult. The 1947 Constitution guaranteed religious freedom and separation of religion and state. Kashima and Kasuga continued as religious institutions within the postwar shrine system rather than organs of state administration. [Text and discussion of the Shinto Directive](https://dl.ndl.go.jp/view/download/digidepo_9602130_po_CRJ-25.PDF?alternativeNo=&contentNo=1)

Today Takemikazuchi receives active worship at Kashima, Kasuga, branch shrines, household altars, and martial organizations. This is institutional continuity through radical legal and theological transformation—not an unchanged prehistoric “Shinto” surviving intact.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and museum culture

Modern editions, archaeological studies, shrine histories, and digitization have separated the source layers more sharply than shrine narratives generally do. Conversely, tourism literature often compresses the layers into a single timeless biography.

### Martial culture

Kendō, sword schools, aikidō-related circles, and other martial groups invoke Takemikazuchi as divine patron or source of technique. Historically secure medieval and early-modern Kashima associations coexist with lineage claims projected into the seventh century. Those ancient revelation dates should be labeled lineage tradition.

### Sumo

Popular histories routinely call Takemikazuchi and Takeminakata’s encounter the first sumo match. This has strongly shaped the god’s modern identity. The ancient source supports wrestling-like combat but not the institutional sport, ring, referee, or formal rules.

### Neopagan and occult reception

A dedicated search found modern individual devotional references, generalized “working with deities” discourse, and internet pantheon profiles. It did **not** locate evidence for a substantial historical Takemikazuchi tradition in Renaissance Hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or classical Western grimoires. Claims that he possesses an ancient Western occult correspondence—planet, tarot card, demonological rank, or fixed elemental signature—are modern inventions unless a specific recent author is cited.

Contemporary non-Japanese polytheist worship exists but its scale is not reliably quantified. It is distinct from shrine-based Japanese practice and should not be retroactively called ancient reconstruction.

### Literature, manga, anime, and games

Takemikazuchi has become a portable thunder-warrior archetype:

- **Shin Megami Tensei and Persona**, including Take-Mikazuchi as Kanji Tatsumi’s initial Persona in *Persona 4*: emphasizes lightning and martial masculinity.
- **Noragami:** reimagines Takemikazuchi as a personified war/thunder deity within a modern fictional divine bureaucracy.
- **Is It Wrong to Try to Pick Up Girls in a Dungeon?** uses Takemikazuchi as a martial divine character with a human “familia.”
- Numerous games use the name for swords, attacks, mecha, or lightning techniques.

These works reinforce the synthesis “thunder + sword + warrior + sumo.” Their visual electricity, armor, supernatural combat systems, and personal relationships are modern retellings, not evidence about early cult.

### Nationalism and political use

The ancient land-transfer story establishes rightful rule through heavenly command and coerced submission. In modern imperial ideology this could be read as a sacred charter of state expansion. Kashima’s official status and Jimmu association made Takemikazuchi available to State Shinto’s national-foundation narrative.

No evidence located establishes a distinct contemporary extremist organization centered specifically on Takemikazuchi. General nationalist use of imperial mythology should not be misreported as a unique Takemikazuchi movement.

### Commercial image

Shrine amulets, martial-arts branding, tourism, earthquake-protection souvenirs, deer imagery, and character merchandise commercialize different historical strata. A lightning-wreathed samurai labeled “Takemikazuchi” is a modern composite, not a reproduction of ancient iconography.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Kashima’s god always Takemikazuchi?

This is the central historical dispute.

**Traditional position:** Kashima has enshrined Takemikazuchi since mythic or Jimmu-era antiquity.

**Critical position:** the *Hitachi fudoki* knows Kashima’s Great Heavenly Deity but not the name Takemikazuchi; the *Kogo shūi* first explicitly identifies them in 807. The equation was probably produced or formalized through Nakatomi/Fujiwara ritual politics between the eighth and early ninth centuries.

The critical position better fits the surviving documentary sequence, but it cannot reveal the local deity’s lost name or prove that no oral identification existed earlier.

### Takemikazuchi versus Futsunushi

The *Kojiki* gives Takemikazuchi the starring role and omits Futsunushi from land transfer. The *Nihon shoki* gives Futsunushi priority and makes Takemikazuchi his companion. Possible explanations include:

- different clan archives;
- Nakatomi/Fujiwara promotion of Takemikazuchi;
- Mononobe/Katori or sword-cult traditions behind Futsunushi;
- editorial preservation of rival regional versions;
- functional duplication followed by differentiation.

No source says explicitly, “the Nakatomi stole Futsunushi’s myth,” so that formulation is an explanatory model, not a fact. A Japanese study indexed by the National Diet Library argues that Futsunushi was originally close to the Mononobe and was later appropriated into Nakatomi ideology. [NDL bibliographic record](https://ndlsearch.ndl.go.jp/books/R000000004-I5577184)

### Was Takemikazuchi originally a thunder god?

The *Kojiki* spelling strongly supports a thunder interpretation by 712. Yet the narrative’s dominant motifs are sword, blood, ice transformation, and conquest. The *Nihon shoki* spelling permits jar and mallet associations, and Kashima’s early local character may have involved navigation, frontier control, or vessels.

The responsible conclusion is that “thunder” is ancient but not exhaustive; a simple nature-god origin cannot be proved.

### Was the Takeminakata contest historical conquest?

Earlier comparativist scholarship often read the myth as a transparent record of Yamato conquest. More recent approaches treat it as political mythology produced in the court’s own period, possibly reflecting cult integration rather than a single military event. Suwa’s local traditions complicate any literal migration map.

### Is Takemikazuchi a “sumo god”?

The *Kojiki* supplies a wrestling-like contest; the formal association with sumo is retrospective. The claim is meaningful as reception history but imprecise as textual description.

### Kuroda’s critique of “Shinto”

Toshio Kuroda argued that “Shinto” should not be imagined as an autonomous, unchanged indigenous religion running continuously from prehistory. Medieval kami cults functioned largely within the Buddhist *kenmitsu* order; an independent Shinto system was substantially constructed in the late medieval and early-modern periods and transformed again by the modern state. [Kuroda, “Shinto in the History of Japanese Religion”](https://religion-in-japan.univie.ac.at/k/images/0/03/Kuroda_1981.pdf)

Takemikazuchi is an unusually clear case:

- chronicle god;
- possibly fused local Kashima deity;
- Fujiwara/Kasuga protector;
- Buddhist manifestation;
- martial patron;
- earthquake god;
- State Shinto deity;
- postwar shrine and popular-culture figure.

Calling all these forms simply “the ancient Shinto god Takemikazuchi” hides the history that needs explanation.

### Motoori, Hirata, Yanagita, and modern folklore studies

Motoori reconstructed ancient readings through philology but also elevated the *Kojiki* within a national intellectual project. Hirata expanded that project into systematic theology. Yanagita and later folklorists redirected attention toward regional and oral traditions. Hori Ichirō emphasized the complexity of folk religion, pilgrimage, and religious specialists. Breen and Teeuwen’s *A New History of Shinto* follows the historical-construction approach, while Michael Dylan Foster’s *The Book of Yōkai* helps distinguish named chronicle kami from early-modern and modern yōkai taxonomies.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Takemikazuchi is simply “Japanese Thor”: obsolete comparativist shorthand.
- He and Raijin are the same god: unsupported.
- He invented institutional sumo exactly as practiced today: unsupported.
- He always fought with a katana: anachronistic.
- He personally killed Amatsu-Mikaboshi in a fully narrated duel: source exaggeration.
- Namazu and the keystone appear in the *Kojiki*: false.
- Kashima’s deity is explicitly named Takemikazuchi in the *Hitachi fudoki*: false.
- He has a securely ancient wife, children, planetary correspondence, or occult seal: unestablished.
- The Kasuga cult was originally “pure Shinto” and later contaminated by Buddhism: historically misleading; the mature medieval institution was structurally Buddhist–kami.
- The white-deer journey is contemporary documentation of 768: it is later foundation legend.
- A continuous manuscript of the *Kojiki* survives from 712: false; the oldest complete witness is 1371–1372.

### Evidentiary conclusion

The reconstructable Takemikazuchi is not a single unchanged “thunder-and-sword god.” The record shows at least seven historically distinguishable configurations:

1. a sword-blood and conquest deity in the *Kojiki*;
2. Futsunushi’s companion or competitor in *Nihon shoki* variants;
3. a deity retrospectively identified with the older Kashima cult;
4. the Fujiwara-protecting First Deity of Kasuga;
5. a Buddhist *gongen* identified with Kannon or Śākyamuni;
6. a warrior and earthquake-protecting deity of medieval and Edo Japan;
7. a purified State Shinto figure and, later, a postwar shrine deity and global fictional thunder-warrior.

The differences among those forms are not corruptions of a recoverable single original. They are the documented history of the god.
