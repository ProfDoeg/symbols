# Izanagi and Izanami: Research Dossier

**Class:** A paired male and female *kami* (神), respectively Izanagi and Izanami; cosmogonic progenitors in the court mythologies, with Izanami subsequently classified as a dead/chthonic deity and Izanagi as a creator, purifier, and divine patriarch.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The *Kojiki* (古事記), presented to the Yamato court in 712 CE, is the earliest securely dated text naming and narrating the pair. The *Nihon shoki* (日本書紀), completed in 720, is the second and preserves multiple competing accounts. No securely dated inscription, image, shrine object, or archaeological deposit naming either deity predates 712. The oldest complete surviving *Kojiki* manuscript is the Shinpukuji manuscript copied in 1371–1372, 659–660 years after compilation; the oldest surviving manuscript of the mythological books of the *Nihon shoki* is the Yoshida manuscript of 1286. The narratives therefore have eighth-century documentary attestation but materially much later textual witnesses. [Nara National Museum](https://imagedb.narahaku.go.jp/006312-001-000.html), [Kyoto National Museum](https://knmdb.kyohaku.go.jp/eng/361.html)

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early canonical]** *Kojiki* (712) or *Nihon shoki* (720), including a *Nihon shoki* “one writing” (*issho*) whose survival is owed to the 720 compilation.
- **[Early ritual/administrative]** Nara- or Heian-period liturgy, law, gazetteer, poetry, or shrine register.
- **[Regional tradition]** A locally transmitted shrine, landscape, or folk claim, not necessarily evidence of the event or foundation date asserted.
- **[Medieval/early-modern accretion]** Material first documented after the classical chronicles, especially Buddhist–kami combinatory theology.
- **[Learned systematization]** An interpretation constructed by commentators, theologians, antiquarians, or folklorists.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A historical, linguistic, comparative, or archaeological hypothesis.
- **[Modern reinvention]** A recent religious, political, literary, commercial, occult, or popular-cultural use.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

In the *Kojiki*, Izanagi and Izanami are the last pair in the “seven generations of the age of the gods” (*kamiyo nanayo*). The heavenly deities order them to “complete and solidify” the drifting land and give them the jeweled heavenly spear. They form Onogoro, marry, generate the principal islands of the court’s imagined Japan, and produce numerous elemental and territorial *kami*. Izanami dies after bearing the fire deity; Izanagi travels to Yomi to recover her, recoils from her decomposing body, escapes, and seals the boundary. Izanami then becomes the Great Deity of Yomi and promises death; Izanagi answers with greater numbers of births. His ensuing purification produces further gods, including Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, and Susanoo. [*Kojiki*, sections 4–11](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Kojiki_(Chamberlain,_1882))

The *Nihon shoki* does not preserve one canonical version. Its main narrative and numerous “one writing says” variants redistribute the initiative in creation, the failed child, Izanami’s death, the Yomi episode, and the birth of the luminaries. In its main account, the pair deliberately generate the sun, moon, Hiruko, and Susanoo; in a variant closer to the *Kojiki*, Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, and Susanoo emerge from Izanagi’s purification. [*Nihongi*, Book I](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nihongi/Book_I)

### Names and scripts

- **Izanagi:** *Izanagi no kami*, *Izanagi no mikoto*, 伊邪那岐神 / 伊邪那岐命 in the *Kojiki*; 伊弉諾尊 and related graphic variants in the *Nihon shoki*. Historical and modern romanizations include Izanagi, Izanaki, Izanagi-no-Mikoto, and the older English forms Izanagi-no-Mikoto or “Male-Who-Invites.”
- **Izanami:** *Izanami no kami*, *Izanami no mikoto*, 伊邪那美神 / 伊邪那美命 in the *Kojiki*; 伊弉冉尊 or 伊弉冊尊 in later orthographic usage and shrine records. Romanizations include Izanami, Izanami-no-Mikoto, and “Female-Who-Invites.”
- **尊 (*mikoto*) / 命 (*mikoto*):** honorific titles, not personal-name components with a single stable theological meaning.
- The characters 伊邪那岐, 伊邪那美, 伊弉諾, and 伊弉冉 function primarily for their sounds. Their graphic meanings should not be treated as transparent definitions of the deities.

### Etymology

**[Learned systematization; probable but not certain]** The conventional derivation connects *izana-* with Old Japanese *izanapu* / later *izanau*, “to invite, solicit, or entice,” and treats *-ki/-gi* and *-mi* as paired masculine and feminine elements. Hence Chamberlain’s “Male-Who-Invites” and “Female-Who-Invites.” Kokugakuin’s reference work calls this the usual interpretation but acknowledges multiple theories. [Kokugakuin, “Izanami”](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9384)

The endings are not productive, unambiguous gender suffixes in historical Japanese, and the exact formation remains disputed. Proposals connecting the names with waves and calm (*nami/nagi*), snakes, Austronesian vocabulary, Korean forms, or a primordial “parent” root are comparative hypotheses, not established etymologies. Takasaki Masahide’s snake proposal and Southeast Asian comparison are particularly speculative; the fact that a sixteenth-century text makes Izanami serpentine cannot establish an eighth-century etymology. [Comparative discussion](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/12/10/896)

### Titles acquired in the Yomi narrative

The *Kojiki* gives Izanami two postmortem titles:

- **Yomotsu Ōkami** (黄泉大神), “Great Deity of Yomi.”
- **Chishiki no Ōkami**, conventionally understood as “Road-Pursuing” or “Road-Reaching Great Deity,” because she personally pursues Izanagi.

The boulder closing Yomi is itself divinized as **Chigaeshi no Ōkami**, “Great Deity Turning-Back-at-the-Road,” and **Sayari-masu Yomido no Ōkami**, the deity blocking Yomi’s entrance. These are separate beings or sacralized objects, not epithets of Izanagi. [*Kojiki*, Yomi episode](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Kojiki_(Chamberlain,_1882)/Section_9)

### Classification changes

- **Before Izanami’s death:** paired heavenly/primordial *kami*, land-generators, divine spouses.
- **After her death:** Izanami is corpse, Yomi inhabitant, pursuer, and “Great Deity of Yomi.” Calling her simply “the Japanese goddess of death” compresses a narrative transformation into a timeless office.
- **After Yomi:** Izanagi is the exemplary performer of *misogi* purification and sole progenitor of the three noble children in the *Kojiki*.
- **Medieval combinatory systems:** both could be treated as primordial father and mother principles, correlated with the Diamond and Womb mandalas and ultimately with Mahāvairocana.
- **Modern popular culture:** frequently reduced to a “creator god” and “death goddess,” or turned into paired magical techniques. These are selective modern abstractions.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The *Kojiki* (712)

**Language and form:** A complex written register employing Chinese prose, logographic Japanese, phonographic characters, and embedded songs. Its preface says Ō no Yasumaro committed to writing traditions memorized by Hieda no Are at Empress Genmei’s command.

**Physical transmission:** No eighth-century copy survives. The oldest complete witness, Shinpukuji-bon, was copied by the Buddhist monk Ken’yū in 1371–1372 and is preserved by Hōshōin in Nagoya. Its colophons date the upper and middle books to 1371 and the lower to 1372. [Wakayama Prefectural Museum](https://jmapps.ne.jp/hakubutuwakayama/det.html?data_id=198)

**Role of the pair:** Sections 3–11 contain their commission, Onogoro, marriage, Hiruko, island-birth, god-birth, Izanami’s death, Kagutsuchi’s killing, Yomi, purification, and Izanagi’s investiture of the three noble children. Chamberlain’s 1882 translation is freely accessible; Donald Philippi’s 1968 translation remains preferable for modern English philology but is not freely reproducible in full. [Chamberlain text](https://sacred-texts.com/shi/kj/index.htm)

### The *Nihon shoki* (720)

**Language and form:** A court chronicle in literary Sinitic, completed under imperial patronage. The mythological books characteristically set a main account beside divergent *issho*. This structure is evidence that multiple learned or regional traditions were available and were not completely harmonized.

**Transmission:** A ninth-century manuscript of Book 10 is the oldest surviving *Nihon shoki* witness overall, but it does not contain the creation myths. The Yoshida manuscript of 1286 is the oldest surviving copy of the opening mythological books. Earlier readings are indirectly witnessed by court lectures and quotations. [Nara e-Museum](https://emuseum.nich.go.jp/detail?content_base_id=100249)

**Role of the pair:** Book I supplies the genealogy, Onogoro and marriage accounts, island and deity generation, alternate births of the sun and moon, Izanami’s death, Yomi variants, and Izanagi’s retirement. Aston’s 1896 translation remains useful because it preserves the alternate accounts instead of producing a synthetic narrative. [Aston edition](https://library.si.edu/digital-library/book/nihongi1asto)

### *Fudoki*

The provincial gazetteers ordered in 713 preserve local topographies, cult explanations, and genealogies. Their surviving and fragmentary material occasionally links landscape formation with Izanagi or the divine couple—for example, an *itubun* associated with Tango explains Amanohashidate as a ladder Izanagi made for passage between heaven and earth. Such notices are **regional etiologies**, not parts of the *Kojiki*’s narrative sequence. Attribution and authenticity must be assessed fragment by fragment because some “lost *Fudoki* quotations” survive only in much later books.

### *Man’yōshū*

The eighth-century anthology is important evidence for the religious and poetic vocabulary surrounding Japan’s islands and sacred landscapes, but Izanagi and Izanami are not major speaking mythic characters in it. Later commentaries sometimes use *Man’yōshū* language to interpret divine names. The anthology should not be cited as though it independently narrates the complete creator-pair cycle.

### *Kogo shūi* (古語拾遺, 807)

Compiled by Inbe no Hironari to defend Inbe ritual prerogatives, the *Kogo shūi* reorders early mythology around clan and court ritual. It is early Heian evidence for reception and institutional competition, not an independent prehistoric transcript. Its value lies in what an early ninth-century ritual lineage selected and reinterpreted.

### *Engishiki* and *norito*

The *Engishiki* was compiled from 905 and presented in 927. Its shrine register names **Awaji Isanagi Jinja** in Tsuna District as a *myōjin taisha*, securely demonstrating official recognition of an Izanagi-named shrine by the early tenth century. This is the strongest early institutional evidence for a specific cult site.

Book 8 preserves twenty-seven official *norito*. Their language contains older layers, but the collection is textually a tenth-century codification. Purification liturgy develops a theology of pollution-removal compatible with Izanagi’s *misogi*, yet not every later purification prayer that invokes Izanagi belongs to the ancient *Engishiki* corpus. Modern generic *harae norito* should not automatically be advertised as unchanged ancient texts. [Oxford study of the *Engishiki Norito*](https://ora.ox.ac.uk/objects/uuid%3Ad2534335-c276-4b95-876f-85921d671445)

### Other texts

- **Sendai Kuji Hongi:** A Heian-period compilation traditionally attributed to Prince Shōtoku. It recycles and reorganizes earlier court mythology and may preserve some lineage material, but it is not a pre-*Kojiki* witness.
- **Konjaku monogatari shū** (early twelfth century): vital for Buddhist tale literature and interactions of local deities, monks, demons, and the dead, but not an independent principal source for the creator-pair cycle.
- **Medieval Shintō writings:** *Jingi hishō*, Watarai/Ise texts, Yoshida Shintō writings, and combinatory temple documents map Izanagi and Izanami onto Buddhist cosmology, embryology, mandalas, and the generative union of male and female.
- **Shosha nemotoki** (sixteenth century): describes Izanami as becoming a serpent. This is a medieval/early-modern accretion, not in the eighth-century chronicles.
- **Toriyama Sekien’s yōkai catalogues** (1776–1784): important for the visual taxonomy of *yōkai*, but they are not early witnesses for Izanagi or Izanami and do not justify classifying the pair as *yōkai*.
- **Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den*** (44 fascicles, composed 1764–1798): reconstructed the Old Japanese reading and elevated the *Kojiki* as a privileged repository of ancient Japanese sensibility.
- **Hirata Atsutane:** extended kokugaku cosmogony, spirit-world doctrine, and national theology in the early nineteenth century.
- **Yanagita Kunio, *Tōno monogatari* (1910):** central to Japanese folklore studies, but not evidence that the court creation narrative was unchanged oral folklore in Tōno.
- **Hori Ichirō:** interpreted Japanese folk religion through continuities in pilgrimage, otherworld belief, sacred geography, and ritual practice; his continuity model must be read beside Kuroda Toshio’s critique.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**Joint domains, early canonical:** generation of islands; generation of natural and functional *kami*; divine marriage; sexual reproduction; ordering unstable land.

**Izanami:** maternity, dangerous childbirth, death, bodily decomposition, Yomi, and the daily deaths of humans. “Earth mother” is a scholarly typology supported by her generative role, not an attested ancient title.

**Izanagi:** creation, paternal authority, boundary making, escape from death, purification, rectification, and the production and investiture of Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, and Susanoo.

Neither text appoints the pair universal creators *ex nihilo*. Preceding deities and the heavenly realm already exist, the earth is already present in an unstable condition, and the heavenly gods commission their work. “Creators of the universe” is therefore an overstatement; “generators and organizers of the Japanese islands and many gods” is textually safer.

### Objects and symbols

- **Ame no nuboko**, the heavenly jeweled spear: jointly used to stir the brine beneath the Floating Bridge of Heaven.
- **Ame no mihashira**, the heavenly pillar: circled during the marriage rite.
- **Yahiro-dono**, the “eight-fathom hall”: constructed on Onogoro in the *Kojiki*.
- **Reed boat:** used to expose or abandon Hiruko.
- **Ten-grasp sword:** Izanagi’s weapon against Kagutsuchi and the pursuing Yomi host.
- **Comb tooth as fire:** Izanagi lights a broken tooth from his hair-comb to see Izanami’s corpse.
- **Grapes and bamboo shoots:** magically generated distractions eaten by the Yomi pursuer.
- **Three peaches:** repel the thunder gods and Yomi warriors; Izanagi names the peaches Ōkamuzumi and commissions them to assist living people in distress.
- **Chibiki-no-iwa:** the enormous boulder sealing Yomi.
- **Water:** not a mere emblem but the physical medium of Izanagi’s *misogi*.

Later lists assigning fixed colors, weekdays, planets, crystals, or numerological correspondences to the pair are generally modern esoteric inventions unless tied to a specified medieval ritual text.

### Physical form

The chronicles present anthropomorphic, sexually differentiated bodies: Izanagi has a bodily “excess,” Izanami a bodily “lack,” and their union is explicitly generative. Izanami’s Yomi body is a decomposing corpse containing maggots and eight thunder deities. The texts do not give stable facial features, clothing colors, skin color, or a canonical portrait type.

### Iconography

There is no securely identified prehistoric or Nara-period image of the pair. Early cults commonly centered on places, ritual objects, or hidden *shintai*, so the absence of anthropomorphic images is significant.

The now-standard scene—two robed deities poised over a cloudy sea with a long spear—is largely a modern pictorial convention. Kobayashi Eitaku’s hanging scroll **“Izanami and Izanagi Creating the Japanese Islands,”** usually dated circa 1885, is among the most influential surviving formulations: Izanagi and Izanami stand amid clouds and churn the sea with the spear. Its historicizing costume is Meiji-period imagination, not archaeological evidence of ancient divine dress.

Modern shrine art, textbooks, manga, anime, and tourist graphics reproduce this formula, often softening or omitting the sexual anatomy, corpse pollution, maggots, and violent marital rupture.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Origin

The *Kojiki* presents Izanagi and Izanami as the last paired generation in the divine succession but does not name parents. The *Nihon shoki* main account likewise has them come into being; one variant makes both children of Aokashikine/Ayakashikone, while another genealogy runs Kuni-no-Tokotachi → Ame-no-Kagami → Ame-no-Yorozu → Awanagi → Izanagi and does not equivalently explain Izanami. These are competing textual genealogies, not pieces of a single complete family tree. [*Nihongi*, Book I](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Nihongi/Book_I)

The texts call Izanami Izanagi’s *imo*. In early Japanese the word can mean younger sister, wife, or beloved woman according to context. “Sibling-spouses” is common and possible, but the English assertion that they are biologically brother and sister is more definite than the *Kojiki*’s genealogy permits.

### Children jointly generated in the *Kojiki*

The joint offspring include the personified islands and the following direct divine children: Ōkoto-oshi-o; Iwatsuchi-biko; Iwasu-hime; Ōtohi-wake; Ame-no-fukio; Ōyabiko; Kazamotsuwake-no-oshi-o; Ōwatatsumi; Hayaakitsu-hiko; Hayaakitsu-hime; Shinatsuhiko; Kukunochi; Ōyamatsumi; Kayanohime/Nozuchi; Torinoiwakusufune/Ame-no-Torifune; Ōgetsuhime; and Kagutsuchi. The *Kojiki* counts thirty-five jointly generated gods before Izanami’s death but includes descendants produced by paired children in its running total. [*Kojiki*, deity births](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Kojiki_(Chamberlain,_1882)/Section_6)

From Izanami’s vomit, feces, and urine during her fatal illness come Kanayamahiko, Kanayamahime, Haniyasuhiko, Haniyasuhime, Mitsuhanome, and Wakumusubi; Wakumusubi’s child is Toyoukehime. These are births from Izanami’s body, not ordinary joint children. [*Kojiki*, Izanami’s death](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Kojiki_(Chamberlain,_1882)/Section_7)

Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, and Susanoo are Izanagi’s children alone in the *Kojiki* and one *Nihon shoki* variant. The *Nihon shoki* main account instead makes the luminaries intentional children of both spouses. Izanami’s motherhood of Amaterasu is therefore **canonical in one early source but explicitly absent in another**.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The jeweled spear and Onogoro

**[Early canonical]** In the *Kojiki*, the heavenly deities order the pair to repair and consolidate the drifting land and grant them the jeweled spear. From the Floating Bridge of Heaven they lower and stir the spear. Brine dripping from its tip accumulates into Onogoro, “self-coagulating” island. They descend, erect a pillar and hall, and prepare to reproduce.

The *Nihon shoki* main account has the pair themselves ask whether land exists below and probe the sea. This change matters: the *Kojiki* emphasizes heavenly commission; the *Nihon shoki* main text gives the pair greater initiative. Neither text securely locates Onogoro. Claims for Nushima, Eshima, Tomogashima, or another island are regional identifications.

### Marriage, female speech, and Hiruko

Izanagi asks how Izanami’s body is formed. She says one place is incomplete; he says one part of his body is excessive and proposes inserting it into hers to generate land. They circle the pillar in opposite directions. Izanami speaks first, praising the man; Izanagi objects that a woman should not speak first. Their union produces Hiruko, whom they abandon in a reed boat, and Awashima, likewise not counted.

They consult the heavenly gods, who use divination and explain the failure by the woman’s speaking first. Repeating the circuit with Izanagi speaking first yields successful islands. [*Kojiki*, marriage](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Kojiki_(Chamberlain,_1882)/Section_4)

The *Nihon shoki* preserves both the gendered ritual error and variants without the same sequence. Its main text has Izanagi object before consummation and repeat the circuit. One variant says the pair learned intercourse by observing wagtails moving their heads and tails. The wagtail episode is therefore **early but variant**, not part of the *Kojiki*.

Later interpreters have variously read the episode as a court marriage ritual, patriarchal prescription, sexual etiological narrative, explanation of defective birth, or political ordering of male and female precedence. These are interpretations; the text itself explicitly attributes the failed birth to female-first speech through divine divination.

Hiruko’s later identification with Ebisu, a prosperous fishing and commerce deity, is a medieval development. It does not retroactively make the abandoned *Kojiki* infant a wealth god in 712.

### Birth of the islands

After correcting the rite, the pair generate Awaji, Shikoku, Oki, Kyushu, Iki, Tsushima, Sado, and Honshu/Yamato, creating the “Great Eight-Island Land,” followed by six lesser islands. Hokkaidō and the Ryukyus are absent; the list maps an eighth-century Yamato political geography, not modern national borders. [*Kojiki*, island births](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Kojiki_(Chamberlain,_1882)/Section_5)

The *Nihon shoki* main text and five alternate accounts vary the sequence, names, and number. “The pair created Japan exactly in its present form” is a modern national-geographic harmonization.

### Birth of the gods and Izanami’s death

The pair generate divine embodiments of rocks, doors, houses, wind, sea, estuaries, trees, mountains, fields, boats, food, and fire. Kagutsuchi burns Izanami’s genitals during birth. Her illness generates metal, clay, water, and productive-growth deities from vomit, feces, and urine. She dies—euphemistically, “divinely retires”—and is buried on Mount Hiba at the boundary of Izumo and Hōki.

Izanagi mourns, generating Nakizawame from his tears, then decapitates Kagutsuchi. The blood on the sword and the pieces of Kagutsuchi’s body generate martial, water, and mountain gods. This makes creation proceed through sex, excretion, illness, grief, killing, and dismemberment—not through a sanitized act of harmonious design.

The *Nihon shoki* preserves a variant locating Izanami’s burial at **Kumano’s Arima village**, where people worship her with flowers and music. Hana-no-Iwaya in present-day Kumano maintains this identification. It is an early textual localization but does not demonstrate an excavated eighth-century grave.

### Descent to Yomi

Izanagi enters Yomi because the lands are not finished and asks Izanami to return. She says he is too late: she has eaten at the hearth or cooking place of Yomi. She nevertheless agrees to consult the Yomi gods and orders him not to look.

He waits, breaks a tooth from his comb, lights it, and enters. He sees her putrefying, maggot-filled body occupied by eight thunder gods. Ashamed and enraged at being seen, Izanami sends Yomotsu-shikome. Izanagi turns his headdress into grapes and his comb into bamboo shoots, which the pursuer stops to eat. Izanami then sends the eight thunders and 1,500 Yomi warriors. Izanagi drives them off with three peaches and finally seals the pass with a boulder.

Across the stone, Izanami threatens to strangle 1,000 people daily; Izanagi promises 1,500 birth huts daily. The episode thus supplies an etiology for mortality coupled with demographic surplus. It also permanently divides living land from Yomi and transforms spouses into antagonists.

The *Nihon shoki* variants alter who pursues, how the boundary is defended, and the precise dialogue. One account emphasizes Izanagi urinating to form a river barrier and producing a boundary deity. Another gives Izanami’s “do not look” taboo but a less elaborate chase. The full popular version normally combines *Kojiki* details with *Nihon shoki* variants.

### Purification and the three noble children

After escaping, Izanagi declares Yomi filthy and purifies himself at Awagihara by the Tachibana river-mouth in Himuka, Tsukushi. Removing clothing and ornaments generates twelve gods. Washing away Yomi pollution generates Magatsuhi deities; rectification produces Naobi deities. Bathing at different depths produces sea and Sumiyoshi gods. Washing his left eye produces Amaterasu, the right eye Tsukuyomi, and the nose Susanoo. He assigns them heaven, night, and sea. [*Kojiki*, purification](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Kojiki_(Chamberlain,_1882)/Section_10)

This is the principal mythic precedent for *misogi*. It does not mean every historically practiced purification rite descended lineally from a single uniform prehistoric ritual. The narrative and court liturgies participated in a wider historical complex of bodily washing, boundary control, pollution avoidance, and ritual rectification.

### The Orpheus comparison

**[Modern comparative interpretation]** The resemblance is real at motif level: a bereaved male descends to the dead seeking a woman; a visual taboo is violated; recovery fails; the boundary becomes final. Izanami’s eating in Yomi also invites comparison with Persephone.

The differences are decisive. Orpheus uses song to persuade underworld rulers and loses Eurydice by looking back during an authorized ascent. Izanagi enters Izanami’s dwelling, violates her command not to inspect her, discovers a corpse, flees active pursuit, and seals the world of death. Izanami becomes antagonist and death-deity; Eurydice generally does not. No early Japanese source mentions Orpheus, and no documentary chain demonstrates transmission from Greece. Similarity supports typological comparison, not common origin.

Erich Fried explicitly fused the comparison in his 1960 NDR radio play *Izanagi und Izanami*, calling the Orpheus parallel unmistakable. That is a documented modern reception event, not evidence for the ancient myth’s origin. [German Broadcasting Archive](https://hoerspiele.dra.de/detailansicht/1419663)

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Izanagi Jingū, Awaji

The *Engishiki* lists “Awaji Isanagi Shrine” in Tsuna District as a major shrine. By the late Heian period it was treated as Awaji’s *ichinomiya*. The present shrine’s foundation narrative identifies it with Izanagi’s hidden retirement palace at Taga after completing creation.

The textual problem is explicit. The *Kojiki* says Izanagi dwelt at “Taga in Awaji/Ōmi,” depending on the reading of 淡海; the normal *Kojiki* spelling for Ōmi is 近淡海, while the *Nihon shoki* says Awaji. The Awaji shrine tradition therefore argues that 淡海 here is a scribal error for 淡道, Awaji. Taga Taisha in Shiga developed the rival Ōmi identification.

Secure milestones are:

- Early tenth century: shrine listed in the *Engishiki*.
- Late Heian: recognized as Awaji *ichinomiya*.
- 1871: designated *kokuhei chūsha*.
- 1885: promoted to *kanpei taisha*.
- 1932: Izanami formally co-enshrined.
- 1954: renamed Izanagi Jingū.

Thus present joint worship at this shrine is not evidence that Izanami was always co-enshrined there; her formal inclusion dates to 1932. [Hyōgo Association of Shinto Shrines](https://www.hyogo-jinjacho.com/data/6328110.html)

The annual major festival is held on 22 April. The paired camphor tree, now associated with marital harmony, conception, and safe childbirth, represents later devotional development rather than early textual iconography.

### Taga Taisha, Shiga

Taga Taisha venerates Izanagi and Izanami and claims Izanagi’s retirement at Taga in Ōmi. The cult became strongly associated with longevity—summarized in the popular saying that pilgrimage to Ise should be followed by Taga. The Awaji and Ōmi claims should remain parallel historical traditions; the ambiguous textual reading does not permit a certain original location.

### Hana-no-Iwaya and Kumano

Hana-no-Iwaya Shrine in Kumano identifies its great rock with Izanami’s tomb, drawing on the *Nihon shoki* alternate account locating her burial in Arima, Kumano. Its flower festival recalls the chronicle’s notice that local people honored her with seasonal flowers and music. The site is material and the regional ritual is real; identification of the rock as a mythic grave is a sacred-place tradition, not an archaeologically verified burial.

### Hiba and Izumo–Hōki

The *Kojiki* locates Izanami’s burial on Mount Hiba at the border of Izumo and Hōki. Multiple mountains and shrines have claimed the referent. The text supplies an early geographical formula, but it does not allow a unique modern archaeological identification.

### Other cults and Izanagi-ryū

Izanagi-ryū in the Monobe River basin of Kōchi is a syncretic village ritual system involving ritualists, *saimon*, Onmyōdō, Shugendō, esoteric Buddhism, ancestor cult, and occupational rites. Its name does not prove direct descent from an eighth-century Izanagi priesthood. Some origin texts call the founder “Great King Izanagi of Tenjiku [India],” illustrating medieval cosmological bricolage rather than historical biography. [Study of Izanagi-ryū](https://papers.ssrn.com/sol3/Delivery.cfm/7297998.pdf?abstractid=7297998&mirid=1)

### Sacrifice, sexuality, and violence

No reliable evidence establishes human sacrifice, temple prostitution, or a sacrificial priesthood specifically devoted to Izanagi and Izanami. The absence should not be filled with analogies from other cultures.

Documented violence is principally narrative—Hiruko’s exposure, Izanami’s death in childbirth, Izanagi’s killing of Kagutsuchi, the Yomi pursuit, and the threatened thousand daily deaths. The pair’s myth was politically useful in imperial cosmogony and modern national education, but no securely documented cult violence uniquely attributable to their worship has been established.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 712: reconstructable limits

No text naming the pair survives from prehistoric or Kofun-period Japan. Archaeology documents ritual landscapes, mirrors, weapons, beads, tombs, and exchanges with Korea and China, but none can securely be labeled “Izanagi” or “Izanami.” Claims that the pair are straightforward survivals of Jōmon religion are modern reconstruction.

Comparisons with Austronesian sibling couples, Chinese Fuxi and Nüwa, Korean myths, Southeast Asian serpent traditions, or Eurasian underworld journeys identify structural possibilities. Without a transmission chain, they do not establish origin.

### 712–927: court canonization and ritual registration

The *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* place the pair at the opening of a genealogy culminating in Amaterasu and imperial descent. The myths organize territory, sex, political precedence, life and death, and ritual purity. The *Fudoki* project localized related cosmogonies; the *Kogo shūi* reframed myth around ritual-lineage claims. The *Engishiki* finally gives secure institutional evidence for an Izanagi-named Awaji shrine.

### Medieval period: Buddhist–kami combinations

The deities were not insulated in an autonomous, unchanged “Shinto.” Buddhist temples, shrine estates, mountain cults, esoteric ritualists, and kami theologians reinterpreted them through *honji suijaku*, mandalas, embryology, and Buddhist cosmology.

Izanagi could represent the Diamond Realm or masculine principle; Izanami the Womb Realm or feminine matrix. Both might be manifestations of Dainichi. Izanami was identified with Hakusan Gongen. Sixteenth-century material could make her serpentine. These are historically real religious developments, not corruptions to discard and not evidence of the eighth-century meaning. Medieval research stresses the complexity of these networks rather than a simple “Shinto plus Buddhism” mixture. [Medieval scholarship overview](https://compass.onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/j.1749-8171.2010.00243.x)

### Edo period and kokugaku

Motoori Norinaga’s *Kojiki-den* made the archaic text newly readable and privileged its language over the more Sinitic *Nihon shoki*. His commentary profoundly shaped the now-familiar naming, segmentation, and theological reading of the pair. Norinaga treated the *Kojiki* as access to the ancient Japanese heart and rejected rationalizing or moralizing reductions.

Hirata Atsutane expanded the divine genealogy into a systematic account of souls, afterlife, Japan, and the emperor. Edo-period commentaries are indispensable evidence for reception but must not be quoted as though they merely reproduce the eighth century.

### Meiji to 1945

The Meiji state forcibly separated kami institutions from Buddhism through *shinbutsu bunri*, damaged or reclassified combinatory sites and objects, abolished *gongen* terminology in official shrine contexts, ranked shrines, and placed mythology within imperial civic education.

In 1871 shrine offices were removed from hereditary control and shrines declared institutions of state ritual; Izanagi Jingū entered the national rank system and was promoted in 1885. The creator pair became convenient figures in a national history leading from divine island generation through Amaterasu to the imperial house. This was a new administrative and pedagogical configuration, even though it drew on ancient texts.

“State Shinto” is itself periodized differently. A broad usage covers 1868–1945; a narrow institutional usage emphasizes 1900–1945, after the Shrine Bureau separated shrine administration from recognized “religions.” [Kokugakuin, “State Shintō”](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=8865)

### 1945 to the present

The 1945 Shinto Directive ended state sponsorship and compulsory public status. The 1946 Association of Shinto Shrines organized most shrines as private religious corporations; the 1951 Religious Juridical Persons Law supplied the continuing legal framework. [Japanese Ministry of Education history](https://www.mext.go.jp/b_menu/hakusho/html/others/detail/1317483.htm)

Izanagi Jingū, Taga Taisha, Hana-no-Iwaya, and numerous local shrines remain active. Contemporary rites emphasize national origins, purification, longevity, marriage, conception, safe birth, and local identity. This is continuity of sites and transmitted practice through radical institutional changes, not proof of an administratively unchanged religion from prehistory.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship, psychology, and gender

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century readings have treated the cycle as cosmogony, ritual charter, gender ideology, mortuary myth, pollution narrative, shamanic journey, political territorialization, or psychoanalytic drama. These approaches illuminate different textual structures but are not ancient doctrines.

Modern gender criticism highlights that female-first speech is blamed for defective offspring and that the male deity survives death-contamination through purification while the female becomes the polluted dead. Counter-readings emphasize Izanami’s overwhelming generative agency and her acquisition of sovereign chthonic power. Both are modern interpretations anchored in genuine textual asymmetries.

No evidence places Izanagi or Izanami in Renaissance European Hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, or classical Western grimoires. Internet lists of them as ancient occult planetary spirits or fixed tarot correspondences are modern inventions.

### Literature and broadcasting

Erich Fried’s 1960 German radio play *Izanagi und Izanami*, directed by Egon Monk with music by Hans Keller, openly retold the *Nihon shoki* material through an Orphic comparative frame. It is a particularly clear case of modern European mythographic reception.

### Games, manga, and anime

- **Atlus, *Persona 4* (2008):** the protagonist’s initial Persona is Izanagi; Izanami is central to the final revelation, and Izanagi-no-Ōkami becomes the climactic transformed Persona. This recasts Yomi’s confrontation as a psychological struggle over truth, illusion, and self-knowledge. The official PlayStation page confirms Izanagi as the protagonist’s initial Persona. [PlayStation](https://www.playstation.com/ja-jp/games/persona-4-golden/)
- **Masashi Kishimoto, *Naruto*:** Izanagi and Izanami become paired Uchiha ocular techniques. Izanagi rewrites an unfavorable outcome; Izanami traps a target in repetition until self-recognition. These powers are newly invented and only metaphorically related to creation, fate, and reciprocal opposition. The official anime synopsis describes Izanami as developed to restrain abuse of Izanagi. [Official *Naruto* site](https://naruto-official.com/en/anime/naruto2/list/01_782)
- **Megami Tensei and derivative games:** repeatedly turn the pair into summonable or combat entities with standardized designs and statistics. Those images have no claim to traditional iconographic authority.
- Modern anime criticism also finds echoes of Yomi, pollution, forbidden looking, female sacrifice, and return-from-the-dead in *Spirited Away*, *Your Name.*, *Weathering with You*, and *Suzume*. Such intertextual readings range from demonstrable naming to broad structural analogy; not every Japanese underworld plot is a deliberate Izanagi adaptation.

### National, regional, and commercial uses

Awaji tourism markets the island as “the birthplace of Japan,” often presenting the *Kojiki*’s first successfully generated island as geographical history. Shrine amulets and wedding, fertility, and longevity products selectively emphasize the harmonious creator couple, leaving the divorce and death-threat outside the commercial frame.

No well-documented modern extremist movement appears to center uniquely on Izanagi or Izanami. They do, however, participate in broader nationalist uses of the *Kojiki*, divine imperial genealogy, and sacred-national territory. The distinction matters: association with nationalist cosmology is documented; a separate “Izanagi extremist cult” is not.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Motoori Norinaga and Hirata Atsutane

Norinaga’s philological recovery established the *Kojiki* as the preferred text for many later accounts. His proposed readings remain influential but are interpretations of a difficult writing system. Hirata transformed kokugaku into a wider theology of creation, spirits, and national destiny. Older English translations often reproduce their solutions without making the dependence clear.

### Tsuda Sōkichi and political myth

Tsuda treated the island-birth narrative not as universal cosmogony but as political mythology centered on the territories and legitimacy of the Yamato state. The absence of Hokkaidō, Ryukyu, and a universal creation strengthens that reading, although it does not reduce the text to a single propagandistic purpose.

### Folklore and continuity

Yanagita Kunio and Hori Ichirō emphasized continuities among myth, local custom, sacred geography, otherworld belief, and folk religion. Their work recovered practices neglected by state-centered textual history. The danger is circularity: a twentieth-century custom cannot automatically authenticate an eighth-century myth, and a court narrative cannot automatically explain every local rite.

### Kuroda Toshio’s critique

Kuroda’s 1981 “Shinto in the History of Japanese Religion” challenged the view of Shinto as a single, autonomous indigenous religion continuously existing from prehistory. He argued that medieval kami worship ordinarily operated within the Buddhist *kenmitsu* system and that “Shinto” as an independent religion was substantially a later formation. His article identifies Norinaga, Yanagita, and Hori as contributors to different continuity models. [Kuroda article](https://religion-in-japan.univie.ac.at/k/images/0/03/Kuroda_1981.pdf)

Breen and Teeuwen’s *A New History of Shinto* similarly approaches “Shinto” as changing institutions, myths, sites, and ritual regimes rather than a timeless essence. [Publisher record](https://uat.store.wiley.com/en-us/a-new-history-of-shinto-p-9781444357684)

Applied here, this means that the *Kojiki* pair, the Engishiki shrine, medieval mandala parents, Edo kokugaku deities, State Shinto national ancestors, postwar shrine gods, and game characters are connected historically but are not one unchanged religious object.

### Origin theories

Proposed parallels include:

- Fuxi and Nüwa as male-female culture creators;
- Southeast Asian or Austronesian primordial couples;
- Korean linguistic or mythic influence;
- Orpheus and Eurydice;
- Persephone’s consumption of underworld food;
- mortuary chambers as models for Izanami’s dark corpse-palace;
- agricultural, childbirth, or initiation rituals underlying the marriage episode.

Some comparisons are structurally productive. None currently demonstrates a complete source-to-source genealogy. “The Japanese copied the Orpheus myth,” “Izanami was originally a snake goddess,” and “the story is proved Indo-European” exceed the evidence.

### Textual limitations

- No autograph *Kojiki* or *Nihon shoki* survives.
- The complete *Kojiki* witness is fourteenth century.
- The earliest mythological *Nihon shoki* manuscript is thirteenth century.
- The *Nihon shoki*’s variants are preserved by the compiler, but their individual dates and provenances are mostly unknown.
- Shrine foundation dates are often known first from much later records.
- Archaeological objects cannot be assigned divine names without inscriptions or a controlled contextual argument.
- Philippi and Aston/Chamberlain remain useful translations, but none removes the need to distinguish original name, gloss, conjectural reading, and translator’s interpretive English.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- The pair created the entire universe from nothing.
- Izanami was always the fixed “Shinto goddess of death.”
- Amaterasu is unambiguously their joint daughter in all sources.
- Onogoro has been archaeologically identified.
- The Awaji shrine was demonstrably founded at the beginning of the world.
- The pair’s costumes in Meiji paintings reproduce ancient clothing.
- The Orpheus parallel proves Greek transmission.
- Izanagi and Izanami belonged to an autonomous, institutionally continuous “Shinto religion” unchanged from prehistory.
- Modern crystal, zodiac, tarot, or “divine masculine/divine feminine twin flame” systems are ancient Japanese teachings.
