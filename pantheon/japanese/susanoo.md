# Susanoo: Research Dossier

**Class:** *kami* 神—male deity in the classical court mythologies; an *amatsukami* 天神 (“heavenly kami”) expelled from heaven, an Izumo culture hero and ruler of Ne-no-Katasukuni, and, in medieval combinatory religion, a wrathful plague deity assimilated to Mutō Tennō and Gozu Tennō. Modern “storm god” is a defensible but incomplete scholarly classification, not the title used consistently by the earliest texts.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the *Kojiki* 古事記, completed in 712 CE, where he appears as 建速須佐之男命, Takehaya-Susanoo-no-Mikoto. The *Nihon shoki* 日本書紀, completed in 720, independently supplies multiple main and variant accounts under spellings including 素戔嗚尊. No securely dated pre-712 inscription or image naming Susanoo is known. The myths may contain older oral and regional materials, but their age before the eighth-century texts cannot be demonstrated directly.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early:** eighth-century texts or comparably early documentary evidence.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** material accepted into later authoritative textual, ritual, or shrine traditions.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** locally attested narrative, cult, or genealogy.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** exegetical or scholarly arrangement not explicit in the earliest evidence.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** historically datable combination with Buddhist, continental, or other gods.
- **[MA] Medieval/early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore or customary tradition.**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an interpretation rather than a primary-source statement.
- **[MI] Modern invention or popular retelling.**

“Attested” below means that a source records a tradition; it does not mean that the narrated supernatural event is historical.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Susanoo is not one unchanging “storm god.” In the *Kojiki* he is generated from Izanagi’s nose after the latter’s return from Yomi, receives dominion over the sea, refuses to govern it, cries for his dead mother, devastates Amaterasu’s heavenly domain, undergoes expulsion, kills the eight-forked serpent in Izumo, establishes a household, kills the food goddess Ōgetsuhime, and later rules the underworld country through which Ōkuninushi must pass. The *Nihon shoki* makes him a child of Izanagi and Izanami in its principal account, assigns him the sea or the world below, preserves numerous alternative versions, and alone preserves the explicit Silla–Soshimori episode. The *Izumo fudoki* of 733 knows Susanoo and his descendants in local place traditions but does not tell the court chronicles’ full heavenly rebellion or serpent cycle. Medieval Gion religion then combined him with pestilence gods, above all Gozu Tennō. Meiji separation policies reversed the presentation: the Buddhist-combinatory Gozu Tennō was officially displaced by the supposedly original Susanoo.

The frequently repeated English formula “god of storms and the sea” compresses different textual roles. The sea occurs in the *Kojiki* division of realms; wind, rain, storm, epidemic, agriculture, forestry, poetry, marriage, protection from disaster, and the underworld arise in different texts or historical cults. “Storm god” became particularly influential through late nineteenth- and twentieth-century comparative mythology. It should not be treated as an ancient, exclusive office. [EA/MSR]

### Documented forms

- **建速須佐之男命 — Takehaya-Susanoo-no-Mikoto**: principal *Kojiki* form. *Take-haya* suggests martial/fierce-swift power; *mikoto* is an honorific. [EA, 712]
- **速須佐之男命 — Haya-Susanoo-no-Mikoto** and **須佐之男命 — Susanoo-no-Mikoto**: shortened *Kojiki* forms. [EA]
- **素戔嗚尊 — Susanoo-no-Mikoto**: characteristic *Nihon shoki* graphing. The characters principally represent sound; extracting a literal Chinese phrase from them is unsafe. [EA, 720]
- Variants include **素戔鳴尊**, **神素戔嗚尊**, **須佐能袁命**, **須佐乃乎命**, and historically varying kana or romanizations: *Susanoo*, *Susa-no-o*, *Susanowo*, *Susano-o*, *Susanō*. [EA/LC]
- **熊野大神櫛御気野命 — Kumano Ōkami Kushimikenu-no-Mikoto** is identified with Susanoo in an *Izumo fudoki* tradition associated with Kumano, although the precise relation between the names and cults remains debated. [EA/RV]
- **牛頭天王 — Gozu Tennō**, “Ox-Head Heavenly King”; **祇園天神 — Gion Tenjin**; and **武塔天神/武塔天王 — Mutō Tenjin/Mutō Tennō** are not primitive aliases. They belong to a medieval combinatory pestilence complex subsequently identified with Susanoo. [SI/MA] The *Encyclopedia of Shinto* describes Gozu Tennō as a product of kami–Buddha combinatory religion whose legends successively placed his origins in India, China, and Japan.
- **Gion-sha no kami**, “deity of the Gion shrine,” becomes effectively Susanoo only after a historical sequence of identifications and Meiji renaming. [SI/LC]
- **Susa no ō** has been parsed as “male/man or lord of Susa,” referring to the Susa district of Izumo. This is an etymological hypothesis, not a translation secured by the phonographic spellings. [MSR]
- Derivations from *susabu/susamu*—to rage, proceed violently, or become wild—interpret the name through the deity’s destructive character. These compete with the place-name theory; neither is demonstrable as the single original etymology. Kokugakuin’s textual commentary explicitly lists both families of explanation. [MSR]
- Proposals that the name is Korean, connected to a Silla royal name, or that *Soshimori* means Korean “ox-head” are hypotheses built around one *Nihon shoki* variant. Phonetic resemblance and the story’s Korean geography are evidence for a remembered continental connection, but do not establish Susanoo as a historical Korean immigrant or Silla king. [MSR]

### Classification through time

1. **Court chronicles:** heavenly male *kami*, brother of Amaterasu and Tsukuyomi, later expelled; culture hero and underworld ruler. [EA]
2. **Izumo gazetteer traditions:** regional land, settlement, and lineage deity. [EA/RV]
3. **Medieval Buddhism and shrine-temple religion:** *araburu kami* or wild/wrathful deity, protector precisely because he can cause or command calamity; assimilated to Gozu Tennō and Mutō. [SI/MA]
4. **Early-modern nativist interpretation:** a figure whose violence had to be explained within an increasingly purified reading of the classical myths. Motoori Norinaga attributed his evil disposition to pollution remaining at Izanagi’s nose; that is *Kojiki-den* exegesis, not a statement made by the *Kojiki*. [LS]
5. **Meiji and State-Shinto presentation:** restored as a “pure” classical kami and national mythic hero after Buddhist names and rites were removed. [LS/MI in its claim of simple restoration]
6. **Contemporary shrine religion:** protector against epidemic and disaster, patron of family welfare and marriage, local tutelary deity, and protagonist of festival and theatrical traditions. [LC/F]

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The first evidence

The first securely datable named evidence is literary, not archaeological: the *Kojiki*, presented in 712 by Ō no Yasumaro. It survives through much later manuscripts—the oldest substantially complete witness is the Shinpukuji manuscript of 1371–72—so the 712 date belongs to compilation, not to the physical copy now extant. Its language combines Classical Chinese prose conventions with phonographic representation of Old Japanese, especially in songs and names.

The *Nihon shoki*, completed in 720 under Prince Toneri and other court compilers, is written principally in Literary Sinitic. It deliberately preserves “one writing says” (*issho ni iwaku*) alternatives. These variants are primary evidence for competing eighth-century compilatory traditions; they should not be collapsed into a single canonical story. Its Susanoo section includes different births, realms, expulsions, descents, serpent combats, swords, spouses, and genealogical distances from Ōkuninushi. The Chinese text is accessible in the first scroll; Aston’s 1896 translation remains useful but reflects Victorian language and older philology.

No object identifiable as a portrait of Susanoo has been securely dated before these textual attestations. Archaeological evidence for early Izumo cult does not, by itself, identify a particular excavated object or site with the named deity.

### Primary corpus

#### *Kojiki* (712)

**Language:** mixed Classical Chinese and Old Japanese phonography.  
**Role:** the fullest continuous early Susanoo narrative. [EA]

It records:

- birth from Izanagi’s nose during purification;
- appointment to rule the sea;
- prolonged weeping for his mother in Ne-no-Katasukuni;
- banishment by Izanagi;
- ascent to bid farewell to Amaterasu;
- the sibling *ukehi* oath and generation of five male and three female deities;
- ravaging of Amaterasu’s fields, hall, and sacred weaving house;
- divine penalties, nail extraction, and expulsion;
- the killing of Ōgetsuhime and generation of foodstuffs from her corpse;
- descent to Torikami on the Hi River;
- defeat of Yamata-no-Orochi, recovery of the sword later called Kusanagi, and its presentation to Amaterasu;
- marriage to Kushinadahime, palace at Suga, and the “eightfold fence” song;
- genealogy through Yashimajinumi to Ōkuninushi;
- later rule in Ne-no-Katasukuni, testing of Ōkuninushi, and the latter’s flight with Suseribime, Susanoo’s daughter.

Kokugakuin provides a modern annotated Japanese and English edition; Philippi’s 1969 translation remains a standard English reference.

#### *Nihon shoki* (720), scroll 1

**Language:** Literary Sinitic.  
**Role:** principal narrative plus multiple competing archival variants. [EA]

Its distinctive evidence includes:

- birth by Izanagi and Izanami in the main account, while variants use objects or gestures by Izanagi;
- characterization as fierce and cruel;
- alternative assignments to sea, heaven, or Ne-no-Kuni;
- variant explanations of the *ukehi* children;
- several accounts of the serpent myth, including Aki rather than Izumo;
- variant names for the serpent-killing sword and different custodial shrines;
- the explicit descent with Isotakeru to **Silla**, residence at **Soshimori**, rejection of that land, clay boat, and crossing to Izumo;
- generation of tree species from Susanoo’s body hair and their assignment to uses, with Isotakeru, Ōyatsuhime, and Tsumatsuhime distributing them;
- differing genealogical intervals between Susanoo and Ōnamuchi/Ōkuninushi.

The Silla episode occurs in an alternative account, not in the *Kojiki* and not as the *Nihon shoki*’s sole narrative. Claims that “both chronicles say Susanoo came from Korea” are false. The surviving text says he descended to Silla and then left; whether this encodes migration, metallurgy, forestry, a cult itinerary, or court ideology is unresolved. [EA plus MSR]

#### Provincial *fudoki* (eighth century)

- **Izumo no kuni fudoki** 出雲国風土記, completed 733: local place-name and shrine traditions involving Susanoo, his children, or associated deities. At Susa, Susanoo is said to have declared the land small but good and to have left his spirit there, explaining the place-name in the text’s own direction—from god to place. Scholars may reverse that derivation historically, but the reversal is reconstruction. The gazetteer does not narrate the heavenly devastation or full Orochi combat. [EA/RV]
- **Bingo no kuni fudoki**, lost except for later quotations: the *Shaku Nihongi* preserves a fragment about Somin Shōrai. A traveling pestilence deity called Mutō gives protection to the poor Somin, who hosted him, and destroys the inhospitable wealthy brother’s household; in the preserved version Mutō identifies himself as Susanoo. Because the eighth-century original is lost and the quotation is medieval, the early date of the underlying wording requires caution. [EA? through LC manuscript transmission]
- Other surviving or fragmentary *fudoki* attach Susanoo’s descendants and travels to local geography. These are not automatically evidence of a unified nationwide Susanoo cult.

#### *Kogo shūi* 古語拾遺 (807)

Compiled by Inbe no Hironari, this court ritual and clan memorandum revisits myth to defend Inbe ritual prerogatives. It names the sword used against Orochi as **Ame-no-Habakiri** and systematizes ritual consequences of the heavenly offenses. [LC/LS]

#### *Engishiki* 延喜式 (compiled 927)

The *Engishiki* records state ritual regulations, shrine lists, offerings, and *norito*. It is evidence for the tenth-century court ritual system, not a narrative biography of Susanoo. Its Great Purification formula catalogs “heavenly sins”—breaking ridges, filling irrigation channels, skinning animals backward, and defilement—that later commentators correlate with Susanoo’s acts. The prayer itself does not need to name him for the parallel to be historically important, but it should not be quoted as a direct Susanoo narrative. [LC/LS]

Shrines bearing Susa-, Kumano-, or related names appear in the *jinmyōchō*, but identical or similar shrine names do not prove that their tenth-century principal deity had exactly the identity assigned in modern shrine registers.

#### *Man’yōshū* 万葉集 (late eighth century)

The anthology is indispensable for early Japanese language and divine geography but supplies no extended Susanoo narrative comparable to the chronicles. The “Yakumo tatsu” song is transmitted as Susanoo’s composition by the *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* and later celebrated as the first *waka*; it is not simply an independently documented Susanoo poem in the *Man’yōshū*. [EA/LC]

#### Medieval narrative and commentary

- **Shaku Nihongi** 釈日本紀, Urabe Kanekata, late thirteenth century: preserves lost quotations, including *fudoki* fragments, and is crucial to the Somin-Shōrai tradition. [LC/MA]
- **Kokinshū** prefaces and medieval poetic commentaries: elevate Susanoo’s “eightfold fence” verse into an origin point for Japanese poetry. This is canon formation, not proof that a historical poet composed it. [LC/MA]
- **Gion-sha engi**, ritual texts, *saimon*, talismans, and Gozu-Tennō origin narratives: construct elaborate journeys, marriages, offspring, plague powers, and Buddhist cosmologies. Their Susanoo equations are medieval syncretism. [SI/MA]
- **Konjaku monogatari shū** 今昔物語集, early twelfth century: important for the Buddhist narrative environment of kami, demons, epidemics, and miracles, but not a primary source for the classical Susanoo cycles. A claim attributed specifically to this collection requires tale-number verification; none of the core heavenly, Orochi, or Soshimori episodes originates there.
- **Sendai kuji hongi** 先代旧事本紀, probably ninth–tenth century despite its claim to greater antiquity: rearranges *Kojiki*, *Nihon shoki*, and clan materials. It cannot be used as pre-*Kojiki* evidence. [LC/LS]

#### Early-modern scholarship and folklore

- **Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den*** 古事記伝, completed 1798: philological recovery of the *Kojiki* and a foundational Kokugaku interpretation. Norinaga’s pollution theory and moral characterization of Susanoo are commentary. [LS]
- **Hirata Atsutane** (1776–1843): expanded Kokugaku cosmology, spirit-world speculation, and nativist theology. His constructions affected Restoration Shinto but are not ancient testimony. [LS/MA]
- **Toriyama Sekien’s yōkai books** (1776–84): fundamental to early-modern yōkai taxonomy and image-making, but they are not evidence that Susanoo himself was a yōkai. Their relevance is the visual-literary environment in which Orochi, oni, serpents, and supernatural beings were reclassified. [MA]
- **Yanagita Kunio, *Tōno monogatari*** 遠野物語 (1910) and folklore studies: evidence for modern folklore methodology and local spirit traditions, not a primary source for ancient Susanoo. Yanagita’s ritual and poetic interpretations must be labeled scholarship. [MSR]
- **Hori Ichirō** placed Japanese folk religion, sacred visitors, and calamity-averting cults into comparative historical frameworks; useful for Susanoo’s popular cult, but interpretive rather than primary evidence. [MSR]

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains by source and period

- **Sea:** Izanagi assigns him the sea in the *Kojiki*. His refusal to govern it is as important as the appointment. [EA]
- **Ne-no-Katasukuni/Ne-no-Kuni:** desired land of his mother and later his own ruling realm in the Ōkuninushi cycle. It overlaps narratively with Yomi at the escape slope, but the texts do not consistently make the two places identical. [EA]
- **Heavenly disorder:** his actions destroy agricultural boundaries and ritually defile Amaterasu’s establishment. [EA]
- **Izumo order and kingship:** after expulsion he destroys Orochi, marries, issues commands, founds a palace, and generates or anchors a local lineage. [EA]
- **Storms:** violent motion, cosmic disturbance, sea, rain, and serpent-river interpretations support this classification, but “storm god” became a dominant analytic formula only in modern comparative scholarship. [MSR]
- **Forestry and ships:** a *Nihon shoki* variant creates cedar, cypress, pine, and other trees from his beard, chest hair, eyebrows, and body hair and assigns their uses for ships, coffins, and buildings. [EA/RV]
- **Agriculture and food:** his field offenses threaten cultivation; killing Ōgetsuhime produces silkworms and grains. These make him an ambivalent transformer, not simply a grain god. [EA]
- **Poetry and marriage:** the Suga song becomes the traditional first *waka*; marriage to Kushinadahime supports later matchmaking and domestic associations. [LC]
- **Epidemic and protection:** inherited through Mutō/Gozu Tennō and Somin Shōrai traditions. The same god threatens plague and teaches its avoidance. [SI/MA]
- **Disaster protection:** dominant in contemporary Yasaka and related shrine presentations. [LC]

### Powers and limitations

He causes earthquakes or cosmic disturbance by ascending to heaven; generates gods through a ritual oath; destroys fields and buildings; transforms Kushinadahime into a comb; intoxicates and kills Orochi; produces wood from his body in one version; inhabits or governs an otherworld; and imposes lethal tests on Ōkuninushi. Yet he is judged and expelled by the collective gods, pays ritual penalties, loses sacred property to Ōkuninushi, and does not retain Kusanagi. The sources therefore portray immense but politically bounded power. [EA]

### Symbols and objects

- **Totsuka-no-tsurugi**, the “ten-hand-breadth sword,” used against Orochi. [EA]
- **Ame-no-Habakiri/Ame-no-Hahakiri**, later name for the serpent-cutting sword. [LC]
- **Kusanagi-no-tsurugi** 草薙剣, found in Orochi’s tail; initially called Ame-no-Murakumo in later tradition and eventually one of the three imperial regalia. The *Nihon shoki* links its custody to Atsuta. Susanoo discovers and surrenders it; it is not normally his continuing personal weapon. [EA/LC]
- **Eight vats of strong sake**, fences/platforms, and eight gates used to incapacitate Orochi. [EA]
- **Comb:** Kushinadahime transformed and placed in his hair. [EA]
- **Trees and wooden ships:** specific to the forestry/Silla variant. [EA/RV]
- **Chinowa** or reed ring and the formula **蘇民将来之子孫也**, “a descendant of Somin Shōrai,” as epidemic talismans. Their connection comes through the Mutō–Gozu–Susanoo complex. [SI/MA/F]
- **Ox head, horns, armor, sword, wrathful face:** properly Gozu-Tennō iconography, sometimes transferred to Susanoo in syncretic or early-modern works. [SI/MA]

No early source securely assigns Susanoo a canonical color, number, weekday, planet, or zodiac sign. Modern lists giving such correspondences without a dated ritual text are modern systematizations.

### Iconography

The earliest narrative texts describe acts rather than prescribing a stable portrait. Later images commonly show a bearded or long-haired warrior, sword raised, before a multiheaded serpent and sake vats. This is recognizable narrative iconography, not an eighth-century cult statue type.

Important examples include:

- early-modern Gozu-Tennō sculptures and paintings, often armored, wrathful, and ox-headed or horned; their equation with Susanoo is syncretistic; [SI/MA]
- Kawanabe Kyōsai’s early-Meiji hanging scroll of Susanoo subduing Orochi, interpreted by art historian Yamaguchi Seiichi as possibly symbolizing Shinto’s victory amid anti-Buddhist persecution; this interpretation is contextual, not an inscription on the work; [MSR]
- Tsukioka Yoshitoshi’s 1887 *Brief History of Japan* print of Susanoo killing Orochi, which turns myth into national visual history; [LS/modern nationalist reception]
- shrine festival drama and **Iwami kagura**, where elaborate serpent bodies, masks, swords, and vats make Orochi’s defeat the defining modern visual form. [F/modern performance]

The Smithsonian’s “Susanoo summoning clouds” painting and museum prints show how modern art joined storm imagery, the dragon fight, Kusanagi, and imperial legitimacy. There is no basis for calling the now-familiar samurai-like costume Susanoo’s ancient appearance.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing origins

| Source | Parentage/origin | Assigned realm |
|---|---|---|
| *Kojiki* (712) | Generated from Izanagi’s nose during purification after Yomi | Sea; later longs for his mother’s Ne-no-Katasukuni |
| *Nihon shoki* main account (720) | Child produced by Izanagi and Izanami after Amaterasu and Tsukuyomi | Sea or the world below, depending on passage |
| *Nihon shoki* variants | Produced through Izanagi’s gaze, mirrors, or other unilateral acts | Variant cosmic domains |
| Medieval Gion materials | Recast through Gozu-Tennō Buddhist/continental genealogies | Jetavana, plague cosmology, dragon-palace narratives, or Gion |

The notion of a single canonical birth is therefore misleading.

### Consorts and descendants

- **Kushinadahime/Ki-Inadahime:** bride rescued from Orochi and principal classical consort. [EA]
- **Kamu-Ōichihime**, a daughter of Ōyamatsumi: Susanoo’s consort and mother of Ōtoshi and Ukanomitama in the *Kojiki*. [EA]
- **Yashimajinumi:** child of Susanoo and Kushinadahime in the *Kojiki*, beginning a multi-generation genealogy leading to Ōkuninushi. [EA]
- **Ōnamuchi/Ōkuninushi:** direct son in one *Nihon shoki* genealogy, sixth-generation descendant in another, and sixth-generation descendant through Yashimajinumi in the *Kojiki*’s sequence. The later father–son shorthand suppresses genuine variation. [EA]
- **Suseribime:** Susanoo’s daughter and Ōkuninushi’s wife in the *Kojiki*. Her mother is not securely identified there. [EA]
- **Isotakeru/Itakeru**, **Ōyatsuhime**, and **Tsumatsuhime:** forestry-associated children in *Nihon shoki* traditions. [EA/RV]
- **Ōtoshi** and **Ukanomitama:** children through Kamu-Ōichihime in the *Kojiki*. [EA]
- **The five male deities and three Munakata goddesses** arise through the *ukehi* with Amaterasu. Their parentage is contested inside the narrative because each deity is generated from the other sibling’s possessions; Amaterasu and Susanoo argue ownership differently. [EA]
- **Yahashira-no-Mikogami**, the “eight child deities,” are worshiped collectively with Susanoo and Kushinadahime at modern Yasaka Shrine. The shrine grouping is a cultic systematization and should not be assumed to reproduce one ancient genealogy. [LC]

### Every documented divine interaction in the core and named derivative corpus

The following list separates actual narrative contact from mere pantheon membership.

- **Izanagi — father/producer, assigner, and banisher.** Izanagi creates Susanoo from his nose, gives him the sea, and expels him when he refuses to govern and cries for his mother. *Kojiki*, 712. **[EA]**
- **Izanami — mother sought but not directly encountered.** Susanoo cries to go to the land of his dead mother; in the *Nihon shoki* she is also his co-parent with Izanagi. No surviving early text narrates a meeting between Susanoo and Izanami. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA; absence of interaction noted]**
- **Amaterasu — sister, opponent, oath partner, victim, and recipient of Kusanagi.** Susanoo ascends to bid farewell; she arms herself; they conduct the *ukehi*; he claims victory and ravages her domain; she hides in the rock cave; after Orochi’s death he sends her the sword from its tail. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720, with differing details. **[EA]**
- **Tsukuyomi — co-sibling only.** The three are generated or appointed together in early cosmology, but no *Kojiki* or *Nihon shoki* episode records a direct Susanoo–Tsukuyomi meeting, combat, or conversation. **[EA; documented noninteraction]**
- **The collective heavenly gods, Yaoyorozu-no-kami — judges and punishers.** They impose extensive offerings/fines, remove nails or hair in variant versions, and expel Susanoo. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**
- **Ame-no-Koyane and Futodama — ritual actors in the aftermath of his offense.** They participate in restoring Amaterasu and in purification traditions later connected to Susanoo’s crimes; direct dialogue with him is not preserved in the core account. *Kojiki*/*Nihon shoki*; elaborated in *Kogo shūi*. **[EA/LC]**
- **The five male *ukehi* gods—Ame-no-Oshihomimi, Ame-no-Hohi, Amatsuhikone, Ikutsuhikone, and Kumanokusubi.** Generated when Amaterasu chews Susanoo’s sword; possession and parentage are disputed by the siblings. *Kojiki* 712; variants in *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**
- **The three Munakata goddesses—Tagorihime/Takirishime, Ichikishimahime, and Tagitsuhime.** Generated when Susanoo chews Amaterasu’s jewels; assigned to Amaterasu or Susanoo according to differing ownership logic and textual order. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**
- **Ame-no-Uzume — no direct meeting securely narrated.** She restores Amaterasu after the cave withdrawal caused by Susanoo, but Susanoo is already outside that immediate performance scene in the narrative’s focus. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA; indirect causal relation]**
- **Ōgetsuhime — host and victim.** She produces food from bodily orifices; Susanoo interprets this as pollution and kills her; food species emerge from her corpse. *Kojiki* 712. The *Nihon shoki* transfers the cognate food-goddess episode to Tsukuyomi and Ukemochi, demonstrating that the Susanoo version is not universal. **[EA; canonical conflict]**
- **Kamimusubi — disposer of Ōgetsuhime’s remains.** Kamimusubi takes the seeds generated from the corpse; the text does not narrate a direct conversation with Susanoo. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA; indirect]**
- **Ōyamatsumi — affinal ancestor.** Kushinadahime’s parents are children of this mountain deity; Susanoo also marries Ōyamatsumi’s daughter Kamu-Ōichihime. No major direct encounter is narrated. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**
- **Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi — local kami, petitioners, and parents-in-law.** Susanoo questions them, negotiates marriage to their daughter, directs the brewing of sake, and appoints Ashinazuchi master of the Suga palace. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**
- **Kushinadahime/Ki-Inadahime — rescued goddess and spouse.** Susanoo transforms her into a comb in some versions, carries her in his hair during the fight, restores/marries her, and establishes a palace with her. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**
- **Yamata-no-Orochi — adversary addressed as an awesome deity/monster.** Susanoo deceives it with sake, kills it, and extracts a divine sword from its tail. *Kojiki* 712; multiple *Nihon shoki* variants, 720. **[EA]**
- **Isotakeru — son and companion.** Accompanies Susanoo to Soshimori in Silla; receives or distributes tree species. *Nihon shoki* variant, 720. **[EA/RV]**
- **Ōyatsuhime and Tsumatsuhime — daughters and forestry agents.** Receive responsibility for spreading useful trees with Isotakeru. *Nihon shoki*, 720. **[EA/RV]**
- **Ōkuninushi/Ōnamuchi — descendant or son, guest, victim of trials, and eventual heir.** Susanoo subjects him to snakes, centipedes, fire, and a hair-cleaning ordeal; Ōkuninushi escapes with Suseribime and Susanoo’s sword, bow, and koto; Susanoo calls after him, conferring names and instructing him to rule. *Kojiki* 712. Genealogical distance differs in the *Nihon shoki*. **[EA]**
- **Suseribime — daughter and accomplice against her father.** She marries Ōkuninushi, supplies protective scarves, helps him survive Susanoo’s tests, and escapes with him. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA]**
- **Gozu Tennō — identification, not an early meeting.** Medieval texts merge or equate the two; they do not normally stage Susanoo and Gozu Tennō as independent gods meeting one another. **[SI/MA]**
- **Mutō Tennō — identification/self-identification.** In the *Bingo fudoki* fragment preserved by *Shaku Nihongi*, Mutō declares himself Susanoo; later traditions vary between identity and manifestation. **[EA? through MA transmission/SI]**
- **Somin Shōrai — human host rather than god.** Included because of cultic importance: Mutō/Susanoo rewards his hospitality with epidemic protection and destroys the inhospitable household. *Bingo fudoki* fragment, preserved in *Shaku Nihongi*. **[RV/SI]**
- **Onamuchi/Daikoku and other Buddhist-combinatory figures — learned equations.** Gozu Tennō could absorb traits of Onamuchi and Buddhist or continental plague powers. These are doctrinal identifications, not episodes in the eighth-century narrative. **[SI/MA]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Birth, failed rule, and longing for the mother

**Earliest:** *Kojiki*, 712. **[EA]**

After escaping Yomi, Izanagi purifies himself. Amaterasu comes from his left eye, Tsukuyomi from his right, and Susanoo from his nose. Izanagi assigns the sea to Susanoo, but the deity does not govern. He weeps until his beard reaches his chest; mountains wither and rivers dry, and malevolent gods multiply. Asked why, he says he wishes to go to his mother’s land, Ne-no-Katasukuni. Izanagi angrily expels him.

The *Nihon shoki* changes both biology and administration. Its main account makes all three children products of Izanagi and Izanami, calls Susanoo fierce and given to cruelty, and assigns realms differently across variants. One version says he should rule the world below. These are incompatible cosmologies, not details to be harmonized.

Norinaga later explained Susanoo’s violence by pollution lingering at Izanagi’s nose. The *Kojiki* does not articulate that causal theory. [LS]

### Ascent, oath, and devastation of heaven

**Earliest:** *Kojiki*, 712; parallel versions in *Nihon shoki*, 720. **[EA]**

Susanoo announces that he will visit Amaterasu before departing. His ascent shakes mountains and rivers. Amaterasu assumes invasion, arms herself, and confronts him. They agree to prove intentions through an *ukehi*, a ritual oath whose result is manifested in generated children.

Amaterasu chews Susanoo’s sword and emits female deities; Susanoo chews Amaterasu’s jewel strands and emits male deities. The siblings dispute what this proves. In the *Kojiki*, Susanoo claims victory because the daughters generated from his possession were pure. The *Nihon shoki* variants alter the objects, sequence, children, and inference.

After claiming victory, Susanoo:

- destroys field boundaries;
- fills irrigation ditches;
- defecates in the hall of first fruits;
- flays a heavenly piebald horse backward;
- throws it into the sacred weaving hall;
- causes a weaving maiden to injure her genitals with a shuttle and die in the *Kojiki*; variants differ.

Amaterasu initially excuses some acts but finally withdraws into the rock cave, darkening the worlds. Although the cave-restoration drama centers on other gods, Susanoo’s aggression is its immediate cause. The assembly imposes a vast penalty and expels him.

The offenses later became templates for ritual categories of *amatsu-tsumi*, “heavenly transgressions.” The institutional relationship between myth and purification liturgy is clear; assuming that the myth literally records the historical invention of the rites is not. [EA/LS]

### Ōgetsuhime and the origin of food

**Earliest:** *Kojiki*, 712. **[EA]**

Hungry after expulsion, Susanoo asks Ōgetsuhime for food. She produces it from nose, mouth, and rectum. Believing she has polluted his meal, he kills her. Silkworms, rice, millet, beans, wheat, and soybeans arise from her body; Kamimusubi gathers them.

This is not a simple beneficent “gift of agriculture”: food becomes available through homicide and corpse transformation. The *Nihon shoki*’s analogous episode assigns the killing of Ukemochi to Tsukuyomi, after which Amaterasu separates from him. The divergence suggests circulating food-origin myths attached to different divine actors. [EA/RV]

### Descent, Orochi, and Kusanagi

**Earliest:** *Kojiki*, 712, followed by multiple *Nihon shoki* versions, 720. **[EA]**

At Torikami near the headwaters of the Hi River, Susanoo sees chopsticks floating downstream and finds Ashinazuchi and Tenazuchi lamenting with their last daughter, Kushinadahime. Yamata-no-Orochi has eaten seven daughters and will consume the eighth. It has eight heads and tails, red eyes, vegetation on its back, a body extending over eight valleys and peaks, and a blood-inflamed belly.

Susanoo identifies himself as Amaterasu’s brother and demands or negotiates the daughter’s hand. He transforms Kushinadahime into a comb and places her in his hair. He orders eightfold-brewed sake, eight enclosures, and a vat at each opening. Orochi drinks with every head and sleeps. Susanoo cuts it apart with his sword; the Hi River runs red. His blade strikes another sword in the tail. Recognizing it as extraordinary, he sends it to Amaterasu. It becomes Kusanagi, later part of imperial-regalia tradition.

Important variants:

- A *Nihon shoki* version places the encounter in Aki rather than Izumo. **[RV]**
- The girl may not yet be born; Susanoo protects the pregnant mother and kills the serpent at birth. **[RV]**
- The drink may be poisonous rather than merely strong. **[RV]**
- Names for Susanoo’s weapon include Orochi-no-Aramasa and Ame-no-Habakiri; its reported shrine custody varies. **[RV/LC]**
- Kusanagi’s custody is associated with Atsuta; later Yamato-Takeru traditions explain its grass-cutting name. **[LC]**
- The Silla variant puts Soshimori before the Izumo fight. **[RV]**

Interpretations of Orochi as a flooding river, eight tributaries, a metallurgical polity, or a sacrificial cult are modern reconstructions. The annual consumption of daughters is explicit narrative content; actual historical human sacrifice at the Hi River is not independently demonstrated. Kokugakuin’s Masahiro Taniguchi reads the girls as mythic sacrifices to a river god, but this remains interpretation rather than archaeological proof. [MSR]

### Suga, marriage, and first poetry

After the victory Susanoo searches for a residence and declares himself refreshed at Suga. He builds a palace and sings:

> 八雲立つ　出雲八重垣　妻籠みに  
> 八重垣作る　その八重垣を  
> *Yakumo tatsu / Izumo yaegaki / tsumagomi ni / yaegaki tsukuru / sono yaegaki o*

Approximately: “Eightfold clouds rise; at Izumo I build an eightfold fence, an eightfold fence to enclose my wife—ah, that eightfold fence.”

The verse is early as textual material in the chronicles, but attribution to a supernatural singer is part of the myth. Later poetic tradition made it the first thirty-one-syllable *waka* and Susanoo the founder of Japanese poetry. That literary status is later canonization. [EA/LC]

### Soshimori in Silla and the tree cycle

**Source:** one *Nihon shoki* variant, 720. **[EA/RV]**

Susanoo and his son Isotakeru descend to **Soshimori** 曾尸茂梨 in **Shiragi/Silla** 新羅. Susanoo says he does not wish to live there, makes a boat of clay, crosses east, and reaches the upper Hi River in Izumo. A related passage says that because the land ruled by his son should possess ships, he generates useful trees from bodily hair and assigns their functions: cedar and camphor for ships, cypress for palaces, and other species for coffins, with his children spreading them.

What the source establishes is that an eighth-century Japanese compiler preserved a tradition linking Susanoo, Silla, seafaring, trees, and Izumo. It does not establish:

- that Susanoo was a historical Korean;
- that Soshimori has been securely located;
- that the name certainly means “ox-head mountain”;
- that the myth records one identifiable migration;
- that ancient Korea was subordinate to Japan.

David Weiss shows that the episode became politically charged cultural memory, particularly under empire. Colonial-era writers used Susanoo’s Korean connection both to claim kinship and to subordinate Korea within an imperial genealogy. Such applications are modern political constructions. [LS/MI]

### Ōkuninushi in Ne-no-Katasukuni

**Source:** *Kojiki*, 712. **[EA]**

Pursued by his brothers, Ōkuninushi goes to Susanoo’s realm and meets Suseribime. They marry immediately. Susanoo subjects him to a chamber of snakes, then centipedes and wasps; Suseribime supplies protective scarves. Susanoo fires arrows into a field and burns it around him; a mouse reveals a refuge and retrieves the arrow. Finally, Ōkuninushi must clean Susanoo’s hair while vermin crawl through it. With Suseribime’s help he appears to bite them while spitting out red clay and nuts.

When Susanoo sleeps, Ōkuninushi ties his hair to rafters, blocks the door with a boulder, and escapes with Suseribime, Susanoo’s sword, bow, arrows, and sacred koto. The koto strikes a tree and awakens Susanoo. At Yomotsu Hirasaka, Susanoo calls after the fugitive, telling him to use the weapons against his brothers, take the names Ōkuninushi and Utsushikunitama, establish his palace, and make Suseribime his chief wife.

This is simultaneously persecution, initiation, theft, marriage flight, and transfer of sovereignty. Calling it merely a father blessing his son erases the hostility and genealogical uncertainty.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Izumo and related centers

#### Susa Jinja, Shimane

The *Izumo fudoki*’s Susa passage is the major early textual anchor: Susanoo approves the land and leaves his spirit there. The present shrine regards him as principal deity. Continuity of place-name and cult is significant, but the dates and form of any pre-eighth-century sanctuary are not securely reconstructable. [EA/RV]

#### Kumano Taisha, Shimane

The shrine’s deity is traditionally **Kumano Ōkami Kushimikenu-no-Mikoto**, widely identified with Susanoo. The *Izumo fudoki* gives Kumano high status, but equation of every Kumano name with the complete court-chronicle Susanoo is a historical process, not self-evident identity. [EA/RV/LC]

#### Yaegaki and Suga shrines

Both localize the marriage and palace traditions. They are important cultic geographies and pilgrimage sites, but the present buildings and institutional histories do not prove that the myth describes their literal foundation. [RV/LC]

#### Hiromine and Tsushima

Hiromine Shrine in Harima and Tsushima Shrine in Owari became major Gozu-Tennō/Tennō cult centers, spreading plague-averting rites and branch shrines. Competing shrine traditions claim precedence in transmitting Gozu Tennō to Gion. These should be treated as institutional claims, not resolved by a single foundation legend. [SI/MA]

### Gion-sha/Yasaka Shrine

Yasaka’s own records preserve two foundation traditions:

1. in 656, **Irishi**, described as an emissary or immigrant from Korea, brought the spirit of Susanoo associated with Silla’s Ox-Head Mountain;  
2. in 876, the Buddhist monk Ennyo/Enjō established a hall and enshrined the local protective deity.

The shrine itself acknowledges multiple theories. The 656 story is a shrine tradition recorded later, not contemporary seventh-century documentation. The 876 foundation sits better within the historically visible emergence of Kyoto’s Gion cult.

The Gion festival’s conventional starting point is the **869 goryō-e**, performed amid epidemic at Shinsen’en with sixty-six halberds representing the provinces. Its organization, sponsorship, and meaning changed repeatedly:

- approximately 900–1200: court and temple-linked epidemic pacification;
- 1200–1467: urban and medieval transformation;
- after the Ōnin War: interruption and revival;
- 1500–1871: consolidation through town associations and merchant wealth;
- 1871–1942: Meiji crisis, renaming, and modern reinvention;
- from 1947: postwar revival.

At present Yasaka’s central sanctuary enshrines Susanoo, Kushiinadahime, and Yahashira-no-Mikogami. Three *mikoshi* leave on July 17, remain at the *otabisho*, and return July 24. This configuration is the contemporary shrine’s authoritative practice, not proof of an unchanged ancient triad.

### Rituals

- **Gion-e/Gion Matsuri:** processions, halberds and floats, *mikoshi*, urban sponsorship, and epidemic-averting worship. [MA to present]
- **Chinowa-kuguri:** passage through a reed ring for purification and protection, linked to Somin Shōrai. [SI/F]
- **Somin talismans:** inscriptions declaring the bearer a descendant of Somin Shōrai. [SI/MA/F]
- **Offerings:** sake is prominent in retellings and dramatic performances because of Orochi; ordinary shrine offerings follow wider shrine practice rather than an exclusively Susanoo ritual code.
- **Kagura:** the Orochi cycle is central to Iwami kagura and other regional dramatic repertoires. These performances preserve and continually reshape the narrative; current choreography is not a fossilized eighth-century rite. [F/modern]
- **Prayer:** contemporary petitions commonly concern protection from disaster and disease, family safety, marriage, and prosperity. [LC]
- **Purification:** Susanoo’s heavenly offenses supply a mythic paradigm for disorder, while *Engishiki* purification liturgies institutionalize removal of offenses. [LC/LS]

### Sacrifice, violence, and coercion

The sources describe Orochi devouring one daughter each year. They do not document an operating historical temple priesthood conducting human sacrifice. River-sacrifice interpretations are plausible comparative readings, not established cult history.

The Gion/Somin tradition is explicitly violent: the deity annihilates an inhospitable household while sparing marked descendants. Medieval Gozu-Tennō texts can portray disease as deliberate divine punishment. This violence is not sanitized by calling the deity merely protective.

Meiji *shinbutsu bunri* and the accompanying *haibutsu kishaku* movement forced institutional separation. Buddhist names, images, monks, texts, and ritual objects were expelled from many shrine complexes; numerous objects and buildings were destroyed, sold, or reassigned. The replacement of Gozu Tennō with Susanoo at Gion-related shrines was therefore not an innocent terminological modernization but part of state-backed restructuring and, in places, anti-Buddhist violence. [documented modern history]

No credible evidence links Susanoo cults to “temple prostitution” as an institution. Gion’s later geiko culture is historically adjacent to Yasaka and the festival but should not be projected backward as cultic sexual service.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 712: unrecoverable formation

Names, stories, and cults probably circulated orally and regionally before compilation, but there is no securely dated pre-712 inscription naming Susanoo. Attempts to reconstruct a singular “original Susanoo”—storm, Korean immigrant, shaman, metalworking hero, river conqueror, or Susa village chief—remain hypotheses. [MSR]

### 712–733: court compilation and regional alternatives

The *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* incorporate Susanoo into an imperial cosmology centered on Amaterasu but preserve his disruptive autonomy and Izumo authority. The *Izumo fudoki* provides a markedly local profile: place-making, settlement, descendants, and shrines rather than a full court myth. The difference is evidence of plural traditions and different genres.

### Ninth–tenth centuries: ritual classification

*Kogo shūi* and *Engishiki* connect mythic offenses, clan ritual claims, court purification, and registered shrines. Susanoo’s narrative becomes a resource for explaining ritual order. [LC/LS]

### Heian period: epidemic religion and Gion

Epidemics and beliefs about wrathful spirits fostered *goryō-e*. Gion’s deity emerged within a Buddhist-temple and urban ritual complex, eventually centered on Gozu Tennō and related figures. The 869 epidemic rite is historically pivotal; the precise institutional path from that rite to the later Gion festival remains more complicated than a single foundation story.

### Medieval period: Gozu Tennō, Mutō, and wild divinity

Susanoo was identified with Gozu Tennō, a deity with shifting Indian, Chinese, Korean, and Japanese origin accounts. Gozu Tennō became a quintessential ambivalent plague power: wrathful, dangerous, and therefore protective when properly accommodated. The Somin story supplied talismans and ritual ancestry. Buddhist *honji-suijaku* and related combinatory models made “kami” and “Buddha” identities relational rather than mutually exclusive. [SI/MA]

### Early modern period: popular expansion and Kokugaku reinterpretation

Tennō, Gion, Tsushima, and Susanoo cults spread through branch shrines, talismans, village festivals, and epidemic practice. At the same time, Kokugaku philologists attempted to recover ancient Japanese meanings from the classical texts. Norinaga’s *Kojiki-den* made the *Kojiki* newly authoritative; Atsutane’s followers expanded nativist spirit theology. These movements prepared—but did not mechanically predetermine—Meiji religious policy.

### 1868–1945: separation, nationalization, and empire

The Meiji government ordered kami–Buddha separation. Gion-sha became Yasaka Shrine, Gozu Tennō was replaced in official nomenclature by Susanoo, Buddhist personnel and objects were removed, and shrine mythologies were rewritten as purified ancient tradition. Kyōsai’s and Yoshitoshi’s heroic Susanoo imagery belongs to this environment.

The government promoted emperor-centered teaching and eventually administered shrines as a supposedly nonreligious national cult. Susanoo was secondary to Amaterasu and imperial descent but remained useful as:

- a national heroic dragon-slayer;
- an ancestor/culture hero linking Japan and Korea;
- a vehicle for theories that colonial Korea shared or occupied a subordinate place in Japan’s divine genealogy.

Scholarly disputes over whether Susanoo was a storm god, culture hero, or foreign deity became entangled with theories of race and national origins. Hirafuji Kikuko identifies Takayama Chogyū’s 1899 comparison of Susanoo with Indra as a catalyst for modern Japanese mythology studies. David Weiss documents later imperial uses of the Korea connection. [MSR/modern political appropriation]

### 1945 to present

The Allied Shinto Directive of 15 December 1945 ended state sponsorship and required separation of shrines from government. Jinja Honchō was formed in February 1946 as a private religious umbrella organization. Yasaka and other Susanoo shrines continued as religious corporations; Gion Matsuri revived after wartime interruption.

Contemporary worship is not merely an archaeological revival: Yasaka, Susa, Kumano, Tsushima, Hiromine, Yaegaki, and many local shrines maintain living ritual communities. At the same time, the pre-Meiji Gozu-Tennō layer has undergone antiquarian and devotional recovery. Claims that present practice is entirely unchanged from antiquity, or alternatively that it is wholly newly invented, both exceed the evidence.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and psychology

The “storm god” model was strengthened through comparison with Indo-European thunder or dragon-slayer myths—Indra and Vṛtra, Thor and the serpent, Zeus and Typhon. Similarity is real at a structural level, but direct borrowing has not been established. Early comparative work often treated resemblance as proof of common racial origin; current scholarship is more cautious. [MSR]

Jungian readings make Susanoo an archetype of the shadow, rebellious son, destructive unconscious, or initiatory hero. These are twentieth-century psychological interpretations. They are not native ancient doctrines. [MI as alleged antiquity; MSR as modern interpretation]

### Occultism and neopaganism

No significant Renaissance Hermetic, Golden Dawn, or early Theosophical Susanoo cult is documented. Western esoteric systems concentrated on Greco-Egyptian, Jewish, Christian, Indian, and selected Buddhist materials. Modern internet occult and eclectic pagan practices sometimes invoke Susanoo as a storm, sea, warrior, or protection deity, but these are small, decentralized, and normally derivative of translations, anime, games, or contemporary shrine information.

Claims that Susanoo possessed an ancient planetary seal, fixed Western magical correspondence, canonical sigil, tarot card, or chakra are modern inventions unless accompanied by a specific dated Japanese ritual source. Contemporary personal devotion should not be confused with historical reconstruction.

### Literature, visual art, theater, and tourism

- Meiji prints made the Orochi victory a scene of national history.
- Iwami kagura transformed the episode into a spectacular, tourism-linked performance with serpents of multiple articulated bodies.
- Children’s books and school retellings usually compress the heavenly offenses and emphasize the heroic rescue.
- Regional tourism markets Izumo as Susanoo and Orochi country; mythic geography becomes cultural heritage and economic resource.
- Modern fiction often treats Orochi as a dragon, although the early term and description more directly indicate a gigantic serpent.

### Manga, anime, and games

- In **Masashi Kishimoto’s *Naruto***, “Susanoo” is a gigantic armored ocular technique, alongside techniques named Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, Izanagi, and Izanami. This is a modern fictional reuse, not a traditional iconographic form. Its confrontation with an eight-headed Orochi-form character deliberately echoes the myth. [MI]
- The **Megami Tensei/Shin Megami Tensei** franchise repeatedly turns Susanoo into a summonable combat entity within a global demon taxonomy. That classification is game design, not a statement that Shinto historically classified him as a demon. [MI]
- Numerous games and anime select one facet—storm warrior, sword god, rebellious brother, dragon slayer—or use “Susanoo” simply as the name of a weapon or attack. Such works have materially reinforced the global image of an armored thunder warrior.
- Recent manga including *Record of Ragnarok*, and games such as *Smite*, *Onmyoji*, and others, freely redesign genealogy, powers, weapons, and appearance. Their additions are modern fictional continuity.

### Nationalist appropriation

The most consequential appropriation was not contemporary fringe occultism but imperial historiography. Susanoo’s Silla visit was used to argue ancient Japanese entitlement, common ancestry under Japanese primacy, or Korea’s place within an imperial family. These claims transformed a textual variant into political history. Modern scholarship rejects the move from mythic itinerary to legal or ethnic title.

There is no evidence that Susanoo is a uniquely central symbol for a major present-day extremist organization. Broader emperor-centered Shinto symbolism can be used by nationalist groups, but attribution to Susanoo specifically requires case-by-case documentation.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### What kind of deity was Susanoo?

Major proposals include:

- storm god;
- sea god;
- underworld god;
- Izumo territorial deity;
- Susa place deity promoted into court myth;
- agricultural disorderer turned culture hero;
- river or flood conqueror;
- metallurgical hero;
- Korean immigrant/culture hero;
- trickster;
- ritual scapegoat;
- political representation of subordinated Izumo;
- narrative mediator between heavenly sovereignty and earthly land rule.

The texts support aspects of several but do not reduce to one. Emilia Gadeleva emphasizes the contradictory centrality of Susanoo; recent Japanese scholarship increasingly studies how each text constructs him rather than excavating one hypothetical original behind all versions.

### Etymology

The principal dispute is between:

1. **character/verb derivation:** *susa-* from raging, violent advance, or wildness;
2. **place-name derivation:** “male/lord of Susa” in Izumo;
3. **continental derivations:** Korean linguistic or royal-name connections.

The *Izumo fudoki* supplies an ancient etiological story deriving Susa from Susanoo’s declaration. Historical linguists may suspect the reverse, but the evidence cannot conclusively decide priority. Continental proposals are possible but not established.

### Court construction versus local cult

Older syntheses often imagined an original Izumo epic later absorbed by Yamato. The *Izumo fudoki*, however, lacks many supposedly quintessential “Izumo” stories found in the court chronicles. This complicates any simple claim that the court copied an intact local cycle. The dossier’s safest conclusion is compilation from heterogeneous court, clan, regional, ritual, and literary materials.

### Korea and Soshimori

The *Nihon shoki* variant is indisputable textual evidence; its historical explanation is disputed. Hypotheses include:

- memory of Korean immigration;
- seafaring and forestry transfer;
- metallurgical migration;
- a cult route;
- a learned etymology connecting Soshimori and Gozu;
- later narrative legitimation of Japanese relations with Silla.

The episode cannot establish a biography of a historical Susanoo. Colonial claims equating Susanoo with Korean founders or converting his visit into Japanese sovereignty are political mythmaking.

### Orochi interpretations

- **River/flood:** supported by setting, serpentine form, valleys, blood-red river, and agricultural bride. [MSR]
- **Human sacrifice:** the daughters are annual victims in the story, but no independent cult record proves actual sacrifice. [MSR]
- **Ironworking:** the sword in the tail and Izumo’s later metallurgical fame invite interpretation, but direct archaeological linkage to the text is absent. [MSR]
- **Political conquest:** Orochi as an older local power defeated by an intrusive ruler remains possible but unprovable.
- **Comparative dragon slayer:** structurally persuasive but does not itself demonstrate transmission from India or Indo-European tradition.

### “Shinto” as a historical category

Toshio Kuroda’s 1981 intervention rejected the picture of an autonomous, unchanged Shinto religion extending from prehistory. He argued that premodern kami worship operated substantially inside Buddhist institutions and that independent Shinto was a late construction. Subsequent scholars, including Breen and Teeuwen, have revised and nuanced rather than simply discarded the critique: ancient kami rites and institutions clearly existed, but “Shinto” as one coherent religion cannot be projected backward without qualification.

This matters especially for Susanoo because his medieval history is Gozu-Tennō Buddhist-kami combinatory religion. Writing “Buddhism later corrupted an originally pure Susanoo cult” reproduces Meiji ideology rather than medieval evidence.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Susanoo is securely documented before 712. **Unsupported.**
- Both *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* say he came from Korea. **False; the explicit story is a *Nihon shoki* variant.**
- Soshimori has been conclusively identified as a particular Korean mountain. **Unestablished.**
- Orochi proves historical annual virgin sacrifice. **Unestablished.**
- Orochi’s eight heads certainly represent eight tributaries. **Interpretation, not source statement.**
- Susanoo was always the deity of Yasaka under that name. **Historically misleading; Gozu Tennō and Gion’s Buddhist institution are central.**
- Gozu Tennō was merely another name for Susanoo from antiquity. **False.**
- Susanoo has a canonical ancient samurai appearance. **Anachronistic.**
- *Hotsuma Tsutae* and other supposed *jindai moji* works preserve pre-*Kojiki* eyewitness records. **Rejected by mainstream textual scholarship; their surviving forms are early-modern or later.**
- Fixed occult sigils, planetary correspondences, or tarot associations are ancient Shinto. **Modern invention unless specifically sourced.**

### State of the evidence

The main evidence survives in later manuscripts, printed editions, shrine archives, ritual documents, art collections, and archaeological sites whose divine identifications are sometimes retrospective. Loss is especially serious for provincial *fudoki*, many known only through fragments quoted centuries later. Medieval shrine fires, warfare, Meiji separation, anti-Buddhist destruction, and twentieth-century war all altered archives and objects.

Digital access has improved through the National Diet Library, Kokugakuin’s *Kojiki* project and *Encyclopedia of Shinto*, museum catalogues, J-STAGE, CiNii, and university repositories. Digital transcription does not eliminate manuscript or dating problems.

## 11. Sources and assessment

### Primary and critical texts

- *Kojiki* (712): Kokugakuin’s annotated digital text; Donald L. Philippi, trans., *Kojiki* (1969).
- *Nihon shoki* (720): scroll-one Chinese text; W. G. Aston, trans., *Nihongi* (1896).
- *Izumo no kuni fudoki* (733): Michiko Yamaguchi Aoki’s translation and critical scholarship.
- *Kogo shūi* (807).
- *Engishiki* (927), especially the shrine register and *norito*.
- *Shaku Nihongi* (late thirteenth century), for lost *fudoki* quotations.
- Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den*.
- Yasaka Shrine records and present liturgical self-description.

### Principal modern studies used

- Emilia Gadeleva, “Susanoo: One of the Central Gods in Japanese Mythology.”
- Toshio Kuroda, “Shinto in the History of Japanese Religion.”
- John Breen and Mark Teeuwen, *A New History of Shinto*.
- Mark Teeuwen, *Kyoto’s Gion Festival: A Social History*.
- David Weiss, *The God Susanoo and Korea in Japan’s Cultural Memory*.
- Hirafuji Kikuko, “Colonial Empire and Mythology Studies.”
- Kokugakuin University’s *Encyclopedia of Shinto* and *Kojiki* research project.
- Bernard Faure, *Rage and Ravage: Gods of Medieval Japan*, especially the separate studies of Gozu Tennō and Susanoo.

### Concluding finding

The fullest historical reconstruction is not a progression from “evil storm god” to “good hero.” It is a sequence of textual and institutional recombinations. The eighth-century record already contains mutually incompatible Susanoos: failed sea ruler, mourner for his mother, heavenly criminal, oath victor, food-goddess killer, dragon slayer, poet, spouse, forester, Korean traveler, ancestor, and underworld king. Izumo sources preserve a different local emphasis. Medieval epidemic religion fused him with Mutō and Gozu Tennō; early-modern philology reinterpreted him; Meiji policy forcibly purified him; empire politicized his Korean itinerary; postwar shrines and popular media continue to select and remake his identities. No recoverable single “original Susanoo” stands behind all of them. The variants are the documented history.
