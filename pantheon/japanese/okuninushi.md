# Ōkuninushi: Research Dossier

**Class:** Male *kami* (神), more specifically a *kunitsukami* (“deity of the land”) in the classical court mythologies; land-maker, culture hero, ruler of the terrestrial world, and—through later exegesis—lord of the unseen or spirit world. Medieval and early-modern traditions also identify him with the Buddhist deity Daikokuten.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest securely dated text naming and narrating the figure is the *Kojiki*, completed in 712, where he appears under several names, principally Ōnamuji and Ōkuninushi. The surviving manuscripts are much later: the oldest substantially complete *Kojiki* witness is the Shinpukuji manuscript of 1371–1372. The *Nihon shoki* followed in 720; the provincial *Izumo no kuni fudoki*, submitted in 733, calls the corresponding deity principally Ōnamochi and “the Great God who made all under heaven.” These are textual attestations, not proof that a single, already standardized deity had been worshipped under all these names before the eighth century. Archaeology demonstrates pre- and protohistoric ritual activity in Izumo, but no excavated pre-712 inscription securely names Ōkuninushi.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early:** eighth- to tenth-century texts or material evidence.
- **[CE] Canonical elaboration:** developments within the classical court corpus.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** provincial gazetteers, shrine traditions, or locally restricted forms.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** exegetical construction by medieval theologians, *kokugaku* scholars, or modern institutions.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** historically datable fusion with another deity.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore:** orally transmitted or ethnographically collected belief.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference rather than an explicit primary-source statement.
- **[MI] Modern invention or adaptation.**

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

**[EA]** In the *Kojiki*, the protagonist first called Ōnamuji is the youngest and abused member of a company of “eighty brothers.” He heals the hare of Inaba, is killed twice by his brothers and restored twice, travels to the root-country of Susanoo, marries Susanoo’s daughter Suseribime, returns with magical regalia, defeats his brothers, builds and rules the terrestrial realm, collaborates with Sukunabikona, and finally cedes political control to the heavenly lineage. The *Kojiki* expressly gathers several names under one protagonist.

**[EA/RV]** The *Izumo fudoki* is not simply a provincial copy of this narrative. Its Ōnamochi is “the Great God who made all under heaven” (*ame no shita tsukurashishi ōkami*), travels through and names localities, marries local goddesses, and installs children in territorial shrines. It does not narrate the *Kojiki*’s humiliating conquest of Izumo. This difference is central to the deity’s history, not an inconsistency to be harmonized away. The gazetteer was written in Sino-Japanese prose at the Izumo provincial administration under the court order of 713 and submitted in 733. [Kokugakuin’s *Fudoki* survey](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=8629) and Aoki’s English translation remain basic points of entry.

### Names

- **大国主神 — Ōkuninushi-no-kami**, Old Japanese approximately *Opokuninusi*: “Great-Land-Master,” “Master of the Great Land,” or “Great Master of the Land.” **[EA]** In the *Kojiki*, Susanoo confers this title after Ōnamuji escapes the root-country. It then becomes the narrator’s principal collective name for the terrestrial ruler. The spelling is semantic rather than securely phonetic.

- **大穴牟遅神 — Ōnamuji-no-kami** in the *Kojiki*; **大己貴神/命 — Ōanamuchi or Ōnamuchi** in the *Nihon shoki*; **大穴持命 — Ōnamochi-no-mikoto** in the *Izumo fudoki*. **[EA]** The name’s etymology is unresolved. Proposals treat *muchi* as an honorific meaning “lord” or “possessor,” connect *ana* with a place-name or “hole,” or treat the spellings largely as phonograms. Chamberlain’s “Great-Hole-Possessor,” based on the mouse-hole episode, is speculative and circular. Kokugakuin appropriately describes Ōnamuchi as “usually considered” another name of Ōkuninushi rather than claiming that identity is linguistically self-evident. The *Nihon shoki* main text makes him Susanoo’s son; the *Kojiki* and a *Nihon shoki* variant make him a sixth-generation descendant. [Kokugakuin, “Ōnamuchi”](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9168).

- **葦原色許男神 / 葦原醜男 — Ashihara-shikoo**, “Ugly/Strong Man of the Reed Plain.” **[EA]** The element *shiko* can express ugly, rough, formidable, or martial force; “ugly man” alone misses its heroic valence.

- **八千矛神 — Yachihoko-no-kami**, “Deity of Eight-Thousand Spears.” **[EA]** Used in the *Kojiki*’s courtship-song cycle concerning Nunakawahime of Koshi. “Eight thousand” is an intensifier, not a literal inventory.

- **宇都志国玉神 / 顕国玉神 — Utsushikunitama-no-kami**, approximately “Manifest Land-Spirit” or “Spirit of the Visible Land.” **[EA/CE]** Susanoo gives this title together with Ōkuninushi in the *Kojiki*. It later supported interpretations of the deity as the spiritual counterpart of terrestrial sovereignty.

- **国作大己貴命 — Kunitsukuri Ōanamuchi-no-mikoto**, “Ōanamuchi, Maker of the Land.” **[EA]** A title in *Nihon shoki* traditions emphasizing land formation.

- **大物主神 — Ōmononushi-no-kami**, “Great Master of Things/Spirits.” **[CE, disputed identity]** One *Nihon shoki* account treats Ōmononushi as Ōnamuchi’s *saki-mitama* and *kushi-mitama*, his fortunate and wondrous soul-components. The *Kojiki*, however, presents a deity arriving from over the sea who demands worship on Mount Mimoro/Miwa before land-making can be completed. Whether this is Ōkuninushi’s own spirit, an autonomous Miwa deity absorbed into his cycle, or a deliberately ambiguous equation must remain open.

- **大國主大神 — Ōkuninushi-no-Ōkami**, “Great Deity Ōkuninushi.” **[LS/continuous cult]** Standard modern shrine usage.

- **幽冥主宰大神 — Kakurigoto/Kakuriyo shiroshimesu Ōkami**, “Great Deity governing the unseen world.” **[LS]** Built from a *Nihon shoki* variant in which Ōanamuchi asks to govern “hidden matters.” Medieval Yoshida theology, Motoori Norinaga, Hirata Atsutane, and the modern Izumo organizations made this a systematic cosmic office. It should not be projected unchanged into the earliest narrative.

- **Daikoku / Daikokuten (大黒・大黒天).** **[SI/MA]** Buddhist Mahākāla, whose Chinese name means “Great Black,” became homophonically associated with the characters 大国, “Great Land,” in Ōkuninushi. The identity is medieval, not Vedic, Buddhist, or eighth-century Japanese doctrine. [Kokugakuin, “Daikokuten”](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9943).

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

**[EA]** The *Kojiki* was presented by Ō no Yasumaro in 712. Its preface claims that Hieda no Are had memorized traditions standardized under Emperor Tenmu and that Yasumaro wrote them down at Empress Genmei’s order. This explains the compilation’s own authority claim; it does not independently date each myth.

The *Kojiki*’s text mixes literary Chinese, phonographic characters, and syntactic devices intended to represent Japanese. Philippi’s 1968 translation is preferable to Chamberlain’s Victorian translation for linguistic study, although the latter remains openly accessible. Kokugakuin now supplies a searchable scholarly English translation and studies: [Kokugakuin *Kojiki* project](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/kojiki-en/).

**[EA]** The *Nihon shoki* was completed in 720. Written predominantly in literary Chinese, it preserves a main narrative and numerous explicitly marked “alternate writings.” These variants are independent evidence, not editorial noise. Aston’s 1896 English translation is dated but still useful and openly available: [Aston, *Nihongi*, Part I](https://sacred-texts.com/shi/nihon0.htm).

**[EA/RV]** The *Izumo fudoki* was submitted in 733. Unlike the mostly fragmentary *fudoki* of other provinces, it survives nearly complete, though in later copies. Aoki’s 1971 *Izumo Fudoki* was the first full English translation: [bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Izumo_Fudoki.html?id=T4RxAAAAMAAJ).

There is no securely identified, inscribed pre-712 image of Ōkuninushi. Early Izumo ritual deposits or monumental remains cannot automatically be assigned to him merely because later texts locate his cult there.

### Principal corpus

1. **The *Kojiki* (712; Japanese represented through mixed Chinese writing). [EA]**  
   Principal source for the hare of Inaba; persecution and resurrection; flight to the root-country; Suseribime; Susanoo’s trials; the Yachihoko courtship songs; partnership with Sukunabikona; Ōmononushi’s appearance; genealogy; and the *kuni-yuzuri*. The open Chamberlain text begins the hare narrative in its complete edition: [*Kojiki*](https://sacred-texts.com/shi/kojiki.htm).

2. **The *Nihon shoki* (720; literary Chinese). [EA/CE]**  
   Its central land-making narrative foregrounds Ōanamuchi and Sukunabikona, supplies medical, agricultural, and apotropaic functions, and preserves mutually incompatible accounts of descent, land transfer, the heavenly envoys, and the building of the shrine. It reports in one variant that Ōnamuchi had 181 children.

3. **Provincial *fudoki*, especially Izumo and Harima (compiled after the 713 order; Izumo submitted 733). [EA/RV]**  
   Izumo’s Ōnamochi names places, marries locally, establishes children, and declares territorial authority. Harima’s Ōnamuchi/Great Deity of Iwa contests territory with Ame-no-Hiboko and undertakes comic or etiological feats with Sukunabikona. The surviving *fudoki* are official provincial reports, but their legends preserve local selections rather than a unified national theology. [University of Tokyo overview](https://www.u-tokyo.ac.jp/biblioplaza/en/K_00039.html).

4. **The *Izumo no kuni no miyatsuko no kan’yōgoto*, the congratulatory address of the governor-priest of Izumo. [EA/CE]**  
   Preserved among the *Engishiki* ritual texts, it recasts Izumo deities as guardians of the Yamato sovereign: Ōnamochi remains at Kizuki while Ajisukitakahikone, Kotoshironushi, and Kayanarumi are installed around Yamato. It is valuable precisely because it ritually negotiates Izumo’s subordination and continuing power. [Text and translation](https://norito.sumeragi-iyasaka.com/en/norito/izumo-kuni-no-miyatsuko-kanyogoto/).

5. **The *Engishiki* (procedures compiled 905–927, presented 927; oldest near-complete witnesses medieval). [CE]**  
   Books 8–10 preserve *norito*, festival regulations, and the register of court-recognized shrines. The *kan’yōgoto* incorporates Ōnamochi; the shrine register documents Kizuki and related cult institutions. The compilation contains twenty-seven formal prayers but is not a narrative mythology.

6. **The *Man’yōshū* (poems mostly seventh–eighth centuries; compilation finalized around the later eighth century). [EA]**  
   It is important for contemporary poetic language, Izumo, and divine geography but contains no second full Ōkuninushi cycle comparable to the *Kojiki*. The Yachihoko dialogue is in the *Kojiki*, not a lost section of the *Man’yōshū*.

7. **The *Kogo shūi* (807; literary Chinese). [CE]**  
   Imbe no Hironari’s institutional memorandum preserves ritual traditions omitted from the two chronicles. Its direct contribution to Ōkuninushi’s mythology is limited; it is more important for the politics of court ritual offices. Claims that it supplies an independent full biography of Ōkuninushi are unsupported.

8. **Shrine records of Kizuki/Izumo, Miwa, Usa, Ise, and medieval temple-shrine complexes. [MA/LS]**  
   These document changing ritual jurisdiction, landholding, rebuilding, pilgrimage, Buddhist institutions, and theories of the enshrined deity. “Izumo tradition” is not a single archive: Senge and Kitajima priestly houses, Buddhist institutions, court documents, and local tales sometimes disagreed.

9. **The *Konjaku monogatari-shū* (early twelfth century). [MA]**  
   Its Buddhist tales illuminate the religious environment in which kami were read through karmic and Buddhist categories. No major independent Ōkuninushi narrative securely comparable to its fox, tengu, or spirit tales has been established.

10. **Medieval theological texts and shrine-origin narratives. [LS/MA]**  
    Yoshida Shintō interpreted the division of visible and hidden rule through a Buddhist-derived visible/esoteric conceptual pair. Medieval Kizuki traditions could identify the central deity with Susanoo or Gozu Tennō rather than maintaining an unbroken, exclusive Ōkuninushi identity.

11. **Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den* (44 fascicles; begun after 1763, completed 1798; first five published 1790, full publication 1822). [LS]**  
    Norinaga’s philological achievement restored the *Kojiki*’s linguistic centrality, but his supposedly pure reading also used *Nihon shoki* variants to construct a covenant in which Ninigi’s line ruled visible affairs and Ōkuninushi ruled hidden affairs. [Kokugakuin, “Kojikiden”](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=8543).

12. **Hirata Atsutane’s writings on spirits and the unseen world, early nineteenth century. [LS]**  
    Hirata expanded Ōkuninushi’s hidden realm into a systematic eschatology. This was decisive for later Izumo theology but is not simply the transparent meaning of the 712 text.

13. **Yanagita Kunio, *Tōno monogatari* (1910) and later folklore studies. [F/MSR]**  
    Yanagita’s work established methods for recording local supernatural testimony. *Tōno monogatari* is not a primary Ōkuninushi source; its significance is methodological and comparative. It should not be cited as evidence that Tōno’s mountain spirits were local forms of Ōkuninushi without independent testimony.

14. **Toriyama Sekien’s illustrated yōkai catalogues (1776–1784). [MA]**  
    These are foundational for later yōkai iconography but do not supply an authoritative Ōkuninushi cycle. Treating every Sekien supernatural figure as part of Ōkuninushi’s retinue is modern over-systematization.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**[EA] Land-making and territorial rule.** Ōkuninushi/Ōnamuchi establishes or orders the “Central Land of Reed Plains.” The verb conventionally translated “create” can mean form, cultivate, complete, or govern; the texts do not describe creation *ex nihilo*.

**[EA] Medicine and healing.** In the hare story he prescribes washing in fresh water and rolling in cattail pollen. The *Nihon shoki* credits Ōnamuchi and Sukunabikona with methods for curing illness and controlling damage by birds, beasts, and insects. *Fudoki* traditions connect the pair to medicines and hot springs. [Kokugakuin, “The Little Partner of Okuninushi”](https://www.kokugakuin.ac.jp/en/article/142393).

**[EA/RV] Agriculture, settlement, and territorial naming.** The *fudoki* show the pair carrying or planting rice and explain fields, hills, springs, and villages through their actions.

**[EA] Kingship and force.** Susanoo’s living sword, living bow-and-arrows, and heavenly-speaking lute enable Ōnamuji to overcome the eighty brothers. His gentleness toward the hare should not erase the martial title Yachihoko or his coercive consolidation of rule.

**[LS/MA] Death and the unseen world.** The earliest texts connect him with Ne-no-katasukuni and withdrawal into hidden divine affairs but do not give one consistent geography of the afterlife. Systematic rule over every dead soul is a medieval and especially early-modern construction.

**[MA–modern] Matchmaking and relational bonds (*en-musubi*).** The cult draws on his many marriages and the tradition that the gods meet at Izumo to determine human relationships. This is now Izumo Taisha’s most visible public function, but it is not explicitly his principal office in the *Kojiki*. The official modern shrine presentation emphasizes relationships and attracts millions of visitors: [Japan Tourism Agency overview](https://www.mlit.go.jp/tagengo-db/common/001565870.pdf).

**[SI] Wealth, food, and commercial fortune.** These belong primarily to Daikokuten and were transferred to Ōkuninushi through syncretism.

### Appearance and recognition

**[EA]** No early narrative fixes Ōkuninushi’s body, color, clothing, or facial type. The *Kojiki* depicts him narratively as a male traveler burdened with his brothers’ baggage, a lover, ruler, and wielder of sword and bow.

**[MA]** Kami were not normally represented by anthropomorphic cult statues in the earliest shrine record. Anthropomorphic *shinzō* developed under Buddhist influence; Kokugakuin notes that surviving kami images belong to the Buddhist-era transformation of shrines. Consequently, an undated “ancient statue of Ōkuninushi” requires inscriptional or provenance evidence.

**[MA/SI]** Daikokuten’s sack, rice bales, mallet, cap, rat, smiling face, and abundant body are not early Ōkuninushi attributes. Their transfer is a diagnostic sign of syncretism. Earlier esoteric Daikokuten could be dark, wrathful, multi-armed, skull-bearing, or weapon-bearing.

**[Early modern–modern]** Printed and painted narrative scenes made Ōkuninushi recognizable by context: carrying a sack before the eighty brothers, tending the white hare, fleeing Susanoo, working with the tiny Sukunabikona, negotiating at Inasa beach, or standing before Izumo’s monumental shrine. The hare became his most portable modern emblem.

**No securely attested ancient color, number, weekday, or celestial body belongs exclusively to Ōkuninushi.** The rat, mallet, wealth sack, rice bales, and the calendrical day of the rat derive from Daikokuten practice. Modern lists assigning black, the planet Saturn, or particular crystals to Ōkuninushi generally conflate him with Mahākāla, Chinese correspondences, or contemporary occult systems.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations

### Competing genealogies

**[EA: *Kojiki*]** Ōkuninushi is a sixth-generation descendant of Susanoo through a chain beginning with Yashimajinumi and ending with Ame-no-fuyukinu. Suseribime is therefore a remote ancestress’s daughter rather than his immediate sister-generation relative.

**[EA: *Nihon shoki* main text]** Ōanamuchi is the son of Susanoo and Kushinadahime.

**[EA: *Nihon shoki* alternate writing]** He is Susanoo’s sixth-generation descendant, agreeing broadly with the *Kojiki*.

**[RV: *Izumo fudoki*]** The text assumes relationships with Susanoo’s and Kamimusubi’s families but does not reproduce the *Kojiki*’s single linear genealogy.

### Consorts and children

- **Yakamihime:** bears Kimata-no-kami, also called Mii-no-kami. She later withdraws out of fear of Suseribime. **[*Kojiki*, EA]**
- **Suseribime:** principal wife in the root-country cycle; no child is securely named from this union in the *Kojiki*. **[*Kojiki*, EA]**
- **Nunakawahime:** woman of Koshi courted through the Yachihoko song exchange. Later Suwa traditions sometimes make her Takeminakata’s mother, but the *Kojiki* does not explicitly do so. **[*Kojiki*, EA; later RV]**
- **Takiribime/Tagorihime of Munakata:** mother of Ajisukitakahikone and Takahime/Shitateruhime. **[*Kojiki*, EA]**
- **Kamuyatatehime:** mother of Kotoshironushi. **[*Kojiki*, EA]**
- **Totori-no-kami:** mother of Torinarumi. **[*Kojiki*, EA]**
- **Takeminakata:** called Ōkuninushi’s son when he abruptly enters the *Kojiki*’s land-transfer episode, although he is absent from the preceding genealogy. **[EA, textual inconsistency]**
- **181 children:** a *Nihon shoki* variant’s total, not a recoverable list. **[CE]**

### Every documented divine interaction

The following list covers named divine beings who interact directly with Ōkuninushi/Ōnamuchi in the principal early narrative corpus. Collective groups such as the “eighty brothers” are included where the source withholds individual names.

- **Yasogami, the “eighty brother-gods”** — Force Ōnamuji to carry baggage, deceive the hare, murder him twice, and are eventually expelled or subdued by him. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA]**
- **Yakamihime** — Chooses Ōnamuji over his brothers, predicts his success, bears Kimata/Mii, then retreats from Suseribime. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA]**
- **The hare of Inaba** — A speaking numinous animal, but not explicitly titled a kami in the passage; Ōnamuji heals it and receives its prophecy. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA; classification uncertain]**
- **Kisagaihime** — At Kamimusubi’s order scrapes shell substance used to restore Ōnamuji after his fiery death. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA]**
- **Umugihime** — Mixes the remedy with maternal milk and helps revive Ōnamuji. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA]**
- **Kamimusubi** — Sends the shell goddesses to resurrect him; later identifies the tiny Sukunabikona as her child in the *Kojiki*. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA]**
- **Ōkuninushi’s mother** — Appeals to Kamimusubi and directs her son to seek Susanoo. Her personal name is not given in the episode. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA]**
- **Suseribime** — Falls in love with him, protects him in the snake and insect chambers, helps him escape, pursues and reconciles with him, and becomes principal wife. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA]**
- **Susanoo** — Tests and tries to kill Ōnamuji; has his hair tied to the rafters; is robbed of sword, bow, arrows, and lute; finally recognizes the marriage and grants the names Ōkuninushi and Utsushikunitama. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA]**
- **A field mouse** — Reveals a refuge during Susanoo’s fire ordeal and retrieves an arrow. It speaks but is not securely classified as a named deity. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA; classification uncertain]**
- **Nunakawahime** — Exchanges courtship songs with Yachihoko/Ōkuninushi and accepts his visit. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA]**
- **Sukunabikona** — Arrives in a tiny boat, bites Ōkuninushi’s cheek, is identified through Kamimusubi or Takamimusubi, collaborates in completing the land, healing, agriculture, and magic, then departs to Tokoyo. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720; Harima and other *fudoki*, eighth century. **[EA with source variants]**
- **Takamimusubi** — In the *Nihon shoki*, identifies Sukunabikona as his child and assigns him to aid Ōnamuchi. *Nihon shoki* 720. **[CE variant]**
- **Ōmononushi** — Appears from over the sea and requires installation on Mount Mimoro/Miwa to complete land-making; another version calls him Ōnamuchi’s fortunate and wondrous soul. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA/CE, disputed identity]**
- **Amaterasu** — Claims the terrestrial realm for her descendants and participates in sending envoys. She and Ōkuninushi do not hold a direct face-to-face conversation in the principal texts. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA; indirect interaction]**
- **Ame-no-oshihomimi** — Initially designated to descend and rule but reports the land turbulent. No direct meeting with Ōkuninushi occurs. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA; indirect]**
- **Ame-no-hohi** — Sent to pacify the land; ingratiates himself with Ōkuninushi and fails to report for three years. *Kojiki* 712; variant arrangements in *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA/CE]**
- **Ame-no-wakahiko** — Sent as another envoy; marries Ōkuninushi’s daughter Shitateruhime and fails his mission. No direct conversation with Ōkuninushi survives. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA; affinity and indirect interaction]**
- **Shitateruhime/Takahime** — Daughter of Ōkuninushi; wife of Ame-no-wakahiko; mourns him at his funeral. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA]**
- **Ajisukitakahikone** — Son of Ōkuninushi; appears at Ame-no-wakahiko’s funeral, is mistaken for the dead god, and destroys the mourning house in anger. There is no surviving direct conversation with Ōkuninushi. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA; genealogical, no direct scene]**
- **Takemikazuchi** — Arrives at Inasa, plants or inverts his sword, and demands surrender; Ōkuninushi refers the decision to his sons and eventually agrees conditionally. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA]**
- **Ame-no-torifune** — Accompanies Takemikazuchi and fetches Kotoshironushi. *Kojiki* 712. **[EA]**
- **Futsunushi** — Principal military envoy confronting Ōnamuchi in the *Nihon shoki*; Takemikazuchi accompanies him in some versions. *Nihon shoki* 720. **[CE variant]**
- **Kotoshironushi** — Son consulted about surrender; consents, counsels obedience, tips over his boat, and disappears behind a green brush-fence. *Kojiki* 712; significantly different *Nihon shoki* versions, 720. **[EA/CE]**
- **Takeminakata** — Son who rejects surrender, challenges Takemikazuchi, is defeated, flees to Lake Suwa, and submits. *Kojiki* 712. He is absent from the *Nihon shoki* transfer account and from the earlier *Kojiki* genealogy. **[EA, isolated canonical variant]**
- **Ninigi** — Receives the right to descend and rule following the transfer, but does not personally meet Ōkuninushi in the principal narratives. *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720. **[EA; indirect]**
- **Ame-no-Hohi’s descendants/Izumo *kuni no miyatsuko*** — Human-divine lineage charged with serving Ōkuninushi; the relationship is ritually asserted rather than narrated as an ordinary meeting. *Kan’yōgoto*, preserved in *Engishiki*, tenth century. **[CE/LS]**
- **Ame-no-Hiboko** — Contests territory or place-making with Ōnamuchi/Great Deity of Iwa in Harima traditions. *Harima fudoki*, early eighth century. **[RV]**
- **Daikokuten/Mahākāla** — Not a narrative meeting but an eventual theological and iconographic identity. Medieval onward. **[SI]**
- **Ebisu, often identified with Kotoshironushi** — Paired with Daikoku in early-modern popular religion and commerce; this is not an early father-son scene involving an entity called Ebisu. **[MA/SI]**

No primary source establishes interactions between Ōkuninushi and the yōkai catalogued by Toriyama Sekien merely because both became subjects of later folklore.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The white hare of Inaba

**[EA: *Kojiki*, 712]** The eighty brother-gods travel to seek Yakamihime’s hand, making Ōnamuji carry their baggage. A flayed hare lies at Cape Keta. The brothers maliciously tell it to bathe in salt water and dry in the wind, intensifying its pain. Ōnamuji asks what happened. The hare says it deceived the *wani* of the sea into forming a bridge from Oki Island by pretending to count their numbers; the last *wani* stripped its skin when the trick was revealed. Ōnamuji prescribes fresh-water washing and rolling in cattail pollen. Restored, the hare declares that Yakamihime will choose him.

“White hare” is conventional, but the *Kojiki* writes a term that can mean bare or naked hare; its whiteness became fixed through reception. *Wani* may mean shark, crocodile, or an archaic sea monster. Translating it as “crocodile” is not certain zoology.

**[MSR]** Comparative lunar-initiation interpretations—hare as moon, skinning as lunar death and renewal—are scholarly hypotheses, not meanings stated by the text. Klaus Antoni developed such an interpretation but acknowledged its comparative character. [Antoni’s study](https://dl.ndl.go.jp/view/download/digidepo_10208691_po_1172.pdf?alternativeNo=&contentNo=1).

### Deaths and resurrections

**[EA: *Kojiki*]** Enraged by Yakamihime’s choice, the brothers heat a rock shaped like a boar and roll it down a mountain. Ōnamuji catches it and burns to death. His mother petitions Kamimusubi, who dispatches Kisagaihime and Umugihime; shell scrapings and maternal milk restore him. The brothers next crush him in a split tree. His mother frees and revives him, then sends him away.

The cycle establishes repeated death and return, medicinal competence, maternal and female divine intervention, and the hero’s movement between worlds. It is not evidence of an actual human-sacrifice rite.

### Root-country trials and Suseribime

**[EA: *Kojiki*]** Ōnamuji enters Ne-no-katasukuni, where Suseribime immediately recognizes him as splendid and marries him. Susanoo calls him Ashihara-shikoo and imposes trials: a snake chamber; a chamber of centipedes and bees; an arrow-retrieval ordeal in a burning field; and delousing Susanoo’s hair, which is full of centipedes. Suseribime supplies protective scarves; the mouse saves him from the fire; Ōnamuji deceives Susanoo with nuts and red clay while biting the vermin.

As Susanoo sleeps, Ōnamuji ties his hair to the rafters, blocks the door with a boulder, and flees with Suseribime, the living sword, living bow and arrows, and heavenly-speaking lute. The lute strikes a tree and awakens Susanoo. At the slope marking the boundary of the root-country, Susanoo tells him to defeat his brothers, become Ōkuninushi and Utsushikunitama, and make Suseribime his chief wife.

**Variant problem:** Ne-no-katasukuni is not straightforwardly identical to the polluted Yomi visited by Izanagi. Later theology often merged or ordered the two. The early sources leave their geography and ontology unstable.

### Yachihoko’s courtships

**[EA: *Kojiki*]** Under the name Yachihoko, the god travels to Koshi to court Nunakawahime. Their antiphonal songs employ doors, birds, swords, garments, and sexual anticipation. A second song exchange dramatizes Suseribime’s jealousy and the god’s proposed departure; she answers with a cup of sake and a claim to enduring marriage.

The songs preserve a deity who is erotic, mobile, and politically polygynous. Modern presentations centered exclusively on monogamous matchmaking suppress this element.

### Making the land with Sukunabikona

**[EA]** A tiny being approaches in a boat made from a plant pod, wearing a wren-skin garment. None of the assembled gods recognizes him. A scarecrow-like deity, Kuebiko, identifies him, and Kamimusubi in the *Kojiki*—Takamimusubi in the *Nihon shoki*—declares him a child who slipped through the divine fingers. He and Ōkuninushi work together to form and order the land.

**[EA/RV]** The *Nihon shoki* assigns the pair medicine, treatment of disease, and spells against harmful creatures. *Fudoki* variants place them at hot springs, rice cultivation, and comic territorial contests. Harima records the grotesque etiological contest in which Ōnamuchi must retain his excrement while Sukunabikona carries clay; both eventually drop their burdens, producing a place-feature. These traditions resist later sanitization.

Sukunabikona ultimately crosses to Tokoyo, leaving Ōkuninushi unable to finish alone. A deity from over the sea—identified with Ōmononushi or Ōkuninushi’s own wondrous spirit—offers completion if worshipped at Miwa.

### The transfer of the land (*kuni-yuzuri*)

**[EA: *Kojiki*]** Amaterasu and Takamimusubi determine that the terrestrial realm should belong to Amaterasu’s descendants. Ame-no-hohi is sent but attaches himself to Ōkuninushi; Ame-no-wakahiko is sent with heavenly weapons but marries Shitateruhime and is killed after eight years of silence. Takemikazuchi and Ame-no-torifune then descend to Inasa beach. Takemikazuchi sits on the point of an upturned sword and demands the land.

Ōkuninushi refers the demand to Kotoshironushi, who accepts and disappears. Takeminakata challenges Takemikazuchi to a strength contest, finds the latter’s arm transform first into ice and then a sword-blade, is defeated, and flees to Suwa. With both sons submitted, Ōkuninushi agrees, asking for a heavenly-scale palace rooted deep and roofed high. He then “hides.”

**[CE: *Nihon shoki* variants]** Futsunushi may lead the mission; Takemikazuchi’s role changes; Takeminakata is absent; Ōnamuchi asks to govern hidden divine affairs while the heavenly descendant governs public affairs. The shrine’s builder and the political terms vary.

**[RV: *Izumo fudoki*]** Ōnamochi declares that he yields lands except Izumo and will remain there as a protective boundary. There is no scene of military humiliation matching the *Kojiki*. This is evidence of a different provincial ideological formulation.

**[MSR]** The widespread theory that the myth encodes Yamato’s historical conquest of an Izumo kingdom is plausible as political interpretation but is not demonstrated by an inscription or datable battle record. Alternatives read the narrative as a ritual pacification formula, a court incorporation of powerful cults, or a literary charter explaining why an imperial dynasty rules land shaped by other gods. Kokugakuin summarizes both conquest and ritual-pacification interpretations: [Ōkuninushi in the *Kojiki*](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/shinmei/okuninushinokami/).

### Izumo’s divine assembly

**[MA/F]** The tenth lunar month is commonly called *Kannazuki*, “month without gods,” elsewhere, but *Kamiarizuki*, “month with gods,” in Izumo. The gods travel to Izumo, are received at Inasa beach, and hold assemblies at Kizuki and Sada. By the Edo period, printed and popular accounts prominently said they determined marriages under Ōkuninushi’s leadership.

The belief is not narrated in the *Kojiki* or *Nihon shoki*. Medieval shrine and calendrical traditions developed it; Urabe/Yoshida theology helped theorize divine absence. Its contemporary festival form is a living shrine tradition, not evidence that the full matchmaking assembly existed in 712. The distinction is visible in the [University of Vienna’s historical discussion](https://religion-in-japan.univie.ac.at/Handbuch/Bauten/Bekannte_Schreine/Izumo).

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Izumo Taisha/Kizuki Ōyashiro

**[EA textual]** The *Kojiki* connects Ōkuninushi’s withdrawal with a palace of massive pillars and elevated crossbeams. The *Izumo fudoki* records Kizuki’s great shrine and cultic district. The *Engishiki* lists the shrine within the state ritual system.

**[Archaeological]** Excavations uncovered ritual layers from the Early Kofun through early-modern periods and, in 2000, a column made by binding three enormous cedar logs. Radiocarbon dating and documentary comparison associate it probably with the 1248 rebuilding. This supports a medieval high-rise sanctuary but does not prove every detail of modern 48-meter reconstructions. Documentary sources record six collapses between the late Heian and Kamakura periods. [Japanese Archaeological Association report](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/nihonkokogaku1994/8/11/8_11_153/_article/-char/en).

The current principal sanctuary is about 24 meters high and dates substantially to the 1744 rebuilding; it is designated a National Treasure. The deity faces west inside a structure whose entrance faces south. Direct public sight of the cult-object is restricted.

### Priestly houses

**[EA–continuous institutional history]** The Izumo *kuni no miyatsuko*, claiming descent from Ame-no-hohi, performed the court congratulatory address and governed ritual at Kizuki. In medieval and modern history authority divided between the Senge and Kitajima houses. Genealogical claims are themselves documented traditions; they are not independently verified biological descent from a god.

### Offerings and festivals

- **Regular offerings:** rice, sake, fish, seaweed, vegetables, salt, water, cloth, and branches; forms changed across court, shrine, and household settings.
- **Kami-mukae and Kamiari rites:** welcome the arriving gods at Inasa beach and conduct secluded deliberative rites during the tenth lunar month. **[MA/F, living practice]**
- **Great Shrine Festival:** modern annual observances include imperial offerings, priestly liturgy, dance, and public worship.
- **Marriage and relational prayer:** now dominant in pilgrimage, amulets, votive plaques, and weddings. **[early-modern–modern]**
- **Healing and medicine:** textual rather than a continuously documented specialized medical priesthood.
- **Divination and oracles:** associated generally with Izumo ritual specialists, but no evidence makes every Japanese divinatory rite an Ōkuninushi practice.

### Other important centers

- **Miwa/Ōmiwa Shrine, Nara:** mountain cult of Ōmononushi; linked to Ōkuninushi through the land-completion episode and soul-theory, but its deity should not always be collapsed into him.
- **Izumo Daijingū, Tanba:** later traditions claim precedence or a transfer of the deity to Kizuki; the claim is sectarian and not established by a surviving contemporary 709 document.
- **Iwa Shrine, Harima:** worship of Iwa-no-Ōkami, identified with Ōnamuchi in regional tradition.
- **Jishu Shrine, Kyoto:** modern popular association with Ōkuninushi and matchmaking; layered over a Buddhist-temple setting at Kiyomizu.
- **Numerous branch shrines and Izumo Taishakyō missions:** products of early-modern *oshi* networks and modern institutional expansion.

### Violence, sacrifice, prostitution, and coercion

A dedicated search found **no secure evidence that Ōkuninushi’s institutional cult practiced human sacrifice or “temple prostitution.”** Death and revival in his myth, violent land-pacification, and legends about offerings cannot be converted into ritual history without evidence. Animal and food offerings are documented within Japanese shrine practice, but no distinctive Ōkuninushi human-sacrifice institution is established.

Religious coercion enters the record principally through state reorganization: the Meiji separation of kami and buddhas dismantled syncretic institutions, reassigned objects and clergy, and marginalized Buddhist readings. The available evidence does not establish an Ōkuninushi cult conducting forced conversion.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 712: reconstructable background

**[MSR]** Izumo possessed major Yayoi- and Kofun-period political, metallurgical, mortuary, and ritual networks. Archaeology establishes powerful regional communities, not the name Ōkuninushi or a “King Ōkuninushi.” Claims that he was a deified historical monarch remain conjectural. Research increasingly stresses infrastructure and networks rather than a single conquered “Izumo empire”: [*The Infrastructure of the Gods*](https://japanreview.jp/issues/29/2803/).

### 712–733: court and provincial textualization

**[EA]** The *Kojiki*, *Nihon shoki*, and *Izumo fudoki* preserve competing arrangements. The court texts subordinate terrestrial sovereignty to the heavenly imperial line; the provincial gazetteer retains an expansive local creator and territorial parent. The *kan’yōgoto* ritualizes compromise by placing Izumo gods around Yamato as protectors.

### Ninth–tenth centuries: incorporation into state ritual

**[CE]** The court ranks shrines, receives Izumo’s ritual submissions, and codifies Kizuki within the *Engishiki* system. Ōkuninushi is neither erased nor sovereign: his power is administratively incorporated.

### Heian–medieval period: Buddhist combinations

**[SI/MA]** Shrine-temple complexes, Buddhist rites, *honji suijaku* reasoning, and esoteric deity systems transform the cult. Kizuki’s enshrined identity could be interpreted through Susanoo, Gozu Tennō, or Buddhist figures. Daikokuten and Ōkuninushi converge because 大国 and 大黒 share the reading *Daikoku*, while Daikokuten’s wealth and food functions answer popular cultic needs.

**[Material history]** The shrine was repeatedly rebuilt and collapsed. The 1248 structure is the best archaeological candidate for the recovered triple-log pillar.

### Fifteenth–seventeenth centuries: hidden-world theology and shrine restructuring

**[LS]** Yoshida Kanetomo’s theology reads Ōnamuchi as lord of hidden matters in a system comparable to Buddhist exoteric/esoteric organization. Late-sixteenth- and early-seventeenth-century Kizuki rebuilding removed or transferred Buddhist structures unusually early, but this was a local historical separation, not evidence of an eternally “pure Shinto.”

### Edo period: pilgrimage, Daikoku, and matchmaking

**[MA/F]** Traveling shrine agents, confraternities, printed guides, legends of the gods’ assembly, and Daikoku-Ebisu imagery popularized Izumo. Ōkuninushi’s image became warmer, wealth-bearing, and marital. The current sanctuary was rebuilt in 1744.

Motoori Norinaga’s *Kojiki-den* elevated the *Kojiki* and stabilized readings of names. Hirata Atsutane expanded the hidden-world doctrine, making Ōkuninushi central to death, spirits, and national cosmology.

### 1868–1945: separation, doctrinal dispute, and State Shinto

**[Modern documented history]** The Meiji government’s separation edicts violently disrupted many shrine-temple complexes. Kizuki Ōyashiro was officially renamed Izumo Taisha. Senge Takatomi organized the Izumo Taisha Keishinkō in 1873 and built a nationwide propagation movement.

In the 1880–1881 pantheon controversy, Senge argued that Ōkuninushi, as ruler of the unseen world, should be worshipped with the creation gods and Amaterasu in the central Shinto institution. The Ise faction prevailed with imperial intervention. Because state-administered shrine priests were restricted from doctrinal propagation, Senge separated his movement institutionally, founded Shintō Taisha-ha in 1882, and became the first head of what was renamed Izumo Ōyashirokyō in 1884. [Kokugakuin history](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9896).

This is not merely “Ōkuninushi became a State Shinto god.” He was included in imperial mythology yet excluded from the highest official propagandistic pantheon advocated by the Izumo faction. Yijiang Zhong therefore calls him one of modern Shinto’s “vanquished gods.” [Zhong, *The Origin of Modern Shinto in Japan*](https://www.bloomsbury.com/us/origin-of-modern-shinto-in-japan-9781474271097/).

The *kuni-yuzuri* could serve imperial education as a model in which legitimate local authority submits to Amaterasu’s descendant. Colonial textbooks circulated the hare story as national mythology. Such political use is modern and does not show the story was composed for twentieth-century imperialism.

### Since 1945

**[Modern documented history]** The Shinto Directive ended state sponsorship and administrative control. Izumo Taisha became a religious corporation associated with Jinja Honchō, while Izumo Ōyashirokyō continued as a separate sectarian organization centered on Ōkuninushi. The Kitajima institution continues as Izumo-kyō. Pilgrimage, marriage prayer, festivals, tourism, branch organizations, and overseas missions remain active.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Religious reception

**[Living tradition]** Shrine worship of Ōkuninushi is continuous in the limited historical sense that ritual institutions survived successive transformations; it does not mean unchanged doctrine since prehistory. Modern Izumo organizations teach that he ceded visible government but continues governing the unseen world and human bonds. Their own historical account dates Senge’s organization from 1873: [Izumo Ōyashirokyō](https://www.izumooyashirokyo.or.jp/200_naritachi.html).

**[MI]** Some Western neopagan and online occult practices detach Ōkuninushi from shrine ritual and place him in universal “deity work,” tarot, crystal, planetary, or archetypal systems. No evidence was found for a large, historically continuous Western Ōkuninushi reconstructionist movement. Personal contemporary worship is real as modern practice; claims that its eclectic correspondences are ancient are not.

No documented Golden Dawn, Renaissance Hermetic, or classical grimoire tradition includes Ōkuninushi. Lists that assign him a Western planetary seal or Kabbalistic sphere are modern inventions.

### Literature, images, animation, and games

- **Schoolbooks and children’s literature:** The white hare became the most common child-oriented episode, often removing the brothers’ murderous violence and Ōkuninushi’s sexual relationships. **[MI adaptation]**
- **Meiji and later public sculpture:** Images at Izumo commonly show the god compassionately addressing the hare or meeting Sukunabikona; these are retrospective narrative monuments, not copies of ancient cult statues.
- ***Noragami* manga and anime:** Adachitoka’s series depicts Ōkuninushi/Daikokuten as a wealthy, politically powerful god capable of monstrous transformation. This deliberately combines historical names with original fantasy. **[MI]**
- ***Shin Megami Tensei* and related games:** Ōkuninushi appears as a summonable mythic combatant within an encyclopedic demon system. Statistics, elemental affinities, and battle skills are game design, not traditional attributes. [Official series portal](https://shin-megamitensei.jp/).
- **Other manga, games, novels, and tourism media:** frequently make him a handsome youth, benevolent healer, national ruler, romantic matchmaker, or Daikoku-like wealth god. These select different historical strata rather than reproducing one ancient original.

### Commercial and political use

The deity’s name and hare imagery market tourism, marriage services, confectionery, souvenirs, railway campaigns, and regional identity. Commercialization is not necessarily desacralization; shrine pilgrimage and retail have long coexisted.

No substantial modern extremist movement uniquely centered on Ōkuninushi was identified. His clearest political appropriation remains the imperial-national reading of *kuni-yuzuri* and the counter-history in which Izumo represents an alternative, conquered, or suppressed Japan. Both are modern political deployments of ancient texts.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Ōkuninushi one god or a literary composite?

The early sources themselves assemble Ōnamuji, Yachihoko, Ashihara-shikoo, Utsushikunitama, Ōkuninushi, the Harima Iwa deity, and sometimes Ōmononushi. **[MSR]** Many scholars therefore see the classical figure as a court-period synthesis of local land, ruler, agricultural, healing, and Miwa/Izumo deities. The contrary traditional position treats the names as transparent aspects of one primordial being. Only the first is a historical hypothesis; the second is a theological harmonization.

Hirohata Sukeo argued against the once-common reduction of Ōnamuchi to a deified Izumo monarch, noting that the cycle’s wandering lover and culture-hero features do not fit a simple royal biography. [“The Making of Ohnamuchi’s Myth”](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/minkennewseries/39/3/39_KJ00002396866/_article/-char/en).

### Was *kuni-yuzuri* a record of conquest?

- **Conquest model:** remembers Yamato absorption of an Izumo polity.
- **Cult-integration model:** explains why dangerous or powerful terrestrial gods became protectors of imperial rule.
- **Ritual model:** develops from pacification language used before royal descent and enthronement.
- **Literary-political model:** creates a legitimate imperial order precisely by acknowledging a prior land-maker who voluntarily, if under military pressure, authorizes succession.

No model is proven by a contemporary battle inscription. Archaeology shows Izumo’s power and long-distance connections, not the narrative’s literal events.

### Was Ōkuninushi always the deity of Izumo Taisha?

The eighth-century sources connect Ōnamochi/Ōkuninushi to Kizuki, but medieval records also foreground Susanoo and syncretic Gozu Tennō. The present exclusive identification is historically strong but not perfectly continuous. A research report on Kizuki notes that the enshrined identity was genuinely debated and that Susanoo was often named in medieval discourse: [Tsukuba research report](https://tsukuba.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/43706/files/Kakenhi_15520057.pdf).

### How old is the gods’ assembly and matchmaking office?

The assembly is absent from the two earliest chronicles. Medieval calendrical and shrine traditions attest divine travel; Edo-period circulation makes marital deliberation especially prominent. The modern statement that Ōkuninushi has been “god of matchmaking since antiquity” compresses several stages.

### Kuroda and the category “Shinto”

Kuroda Toshio challenged the story of Shinto as a timeless, autonomous indigenous religion existing unchanged before Buddhism. For most of premodern history, kami cults operated within court, Buddhist, local, and shrine-temple systems. Breen and Teeuwen likewise treat “Shinto” as repeatedly constructed and institutionalized rather than as a fixed essence. This framework is particularly important for Ōkuninushi because his history runs through court mythology, provincial cult, Buddhist Daikoku, *kokugaku*, State Shinto conflict, sectarian religion, and postwar shrine practice. [Breen and Teeuwen, *A New History of Shinto*](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/book/10.1002/9781444317190).

### Norinaga and Atsutane

Norinaga’s philology recovered Old Japanese readings and treated the *Kojiki* as foundational, but modern research shows that his interpretation also combined it with *Nihon shoki* variants. Atsutane transformed Norinaga’s textual revival into a more systematic religious cosmology, especially concerning spirits and Ōkuninushi’s unseen realm. The result profoundly shaped Senge Takatomi and modern Izumo religion.

### Folklore studies

Hori Ichirō and Yanagita’s successors shifted attention from court texts to pilgrimage, possession, local gods, household rites, and popular practice. Their work is essential for recognizing that villagers did not necessarily organize divine identities according to court genealogies. Michael Dylan Foster’s work on yōkai similarly warns that illustrated catalogues and modern media continually remake supposedly ancient categories. Neither approach supports reclassifying Ōkuninushi himself as a yōkai.

### State of the evidence

- No autograph *Kojiki*, *Nihon shoki*, *Izumo fudoki*, or *Engishiki* survives.
- The oldest complete narrative witnesses are centuries later than composition.
- The *Izumo fudoki* is unusually complete; most provincial gazetteers survive only in fragments or quotations.
- Shrine archives suffered fire, warfare, rebuilding, institutional separation, and dispersal.
- The 2000 discovery of the triple-log pillar materially changed discussion of medieval Izumo architecture.
- The deity’s earliest iconography is much less secure than his textual record.
- Claims of a pre-712 inscription naming Ōkuninushi, an excavated royal tomb belonging to him, a continuous 48-meter shrine from prehistory, ancient human sacrifice, or an ancient Western occult correspondence remain unestablished.
