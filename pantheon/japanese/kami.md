# The Kami (as a class): Research Dossier

**Class:** A plural and open-ended class of divine, numinous, ancestral, natural, tutelary, dangerous, and apotheosized beings or powers. Individual kami may be classified as gods, goddesses, spirits, ancestors, culture heroes, sovereigns, animals, landscape features, calamities, or collective divinities. *Kami* is not coterminous with “Shinto god,” and neither *yōkai*, Buddhist beings, nor ghosts constitute wholly separate, impermeable classes.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The Japanese word *kami* is securely attested in the early eighth-century written corpus, above all the *Kojiki* (古事記, presented in 712), the *Nihon shoki* (日本書紀, 720), the surviving provincial *fudoki* commissioned in 713, and eighth-century poems later collected in the *Man’yōshū*. Earlier archaeology documents ritual practices and objects but cannot securely identify their recipients by the unattested name *kami*. Chinese accounts of the archipelago, such as the third-century *Wei zhi*, report ritual specialists, divination, mourning, and political sacrality, but do not document the Old Japanese lexeme.

**Scope warning:** “The kami” is a category rather than one divine personality. It has no single genealogy, iconography, cult, or finite narrative corpus. The early chronicles alone name hundreds of kami; regional records, shrine archives, medieval Buddhist literature, folklore, and modern enshrinements add thousands more. Accordingly, the interaction register below is exhaustive only for the principal class-defining episodes and divine networks of the foundational mythological corpus. A literally exhaustive list of every interaction involving every locally named kami would require a shrine-by-shrine and manuscript-by-manuscript database rather than a single dossier.

**Evidentiary labels used below:**

- **[EA]** securely attested early material, principally before 800.
- **[LC]** later canonical or institutional elaboration.
- **[RV]** regional or shrine-specific variant.
- **[LS]** learned or priestly systematization.
- **[SI]** syncretistic identification.
- **[MA]** medieval or early-modern accretion.
- **[F]** folklore or oral-traditional record.
- **[MSR]** modern scholarly reconstruction.
- **[MI]** modern invention or demonstrably modern reformulation.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Kami as a category

The standard Japanese noun is **kami** (神; Old Japanese conventionally reconstructed as *kamï/kamwi* in different scholarly systems). Its Sino-Japanese counterparts include **shin** or **jin**, as in *shintō* 神道, *jinja* 神社, and *jinmyō* 神名. Honorific forms include **kami-sama** 神様; exalted titles include **ōkami** 大神, “great kami,” and **ōmikami** 大御神, “great august kami.” **Mikoto** 命 or 尊, “august one,” commonly follows divine or heroic names but is not itself a synonym restricted to kami.

Translation as “god” is serviceable but incomplete. Some kami are fully personified sovereign deities; others are the sea, a mountain, thunder, pestilence, a clan ancestor, an imperial dead person, a strangely formed object, or a localized efficacy known principally through ritual. “Spirit” is likewise too narrow, because early texts can treat the mountain or sea itself—not merely a separable spirit within it—as kami.

**[LS, eighteenth century] Motoori Norinaga’s definition.** In *Kojiki-den* 3, Motoori rejects simple etymological definitions and describes as kami:

> the kami of heaven and earth appearing in the ancient records, the spirits enshrined in shrines, and also human beings, birds, beasts, plants, seas, mountains, and other things possessing extraordinary and awe-provoking qualities.

His “extraordinary” includes evil, uncanny, or destructive powers as well as noble and beneficial ones. “Anything whatsoever which was outside the ordinary” is a useful compression of Motoori, not a complete literal rendering. His definition is an Edo-period philological theology derived inductively from classical usage, not a verbatim prehistoric creed.

### Etymology

**[MSR: unresolved.]** No proposed derivation has commanded consensus.

- Derivation from **kami** 上, “above,” was influential in medieval and early-modern exegesis. Historical phonology distinguishes the relevant Old Japanese vowels and undermines a simple identity.
- Connections with Ainu **kamuy** have repeatedly been proposed. Resemblance and long contact make comparison legitimate, but the direction of borrowing or common ancestry has not been demonstrated securely.
- Older proposals from words meaning “hidden,” “mirror,” “fire,” “bite,” “hair,” or “superior” are speculative.
- Motoori’s refusal to ground the category in a single etymology remains methodologically safer than treating one pun as linguistic history.

The Chinese graph 神 did not create the native word, but its semantic range—deity, spirit, numinous efficacy—helped shape written discourse. Buddhist authors could call the Buddha a foreign or neighboring kami, showing that early usage was not an impermeable ethnic taxonomy.

### “Eight million kami”

**Yaoyorozu no kami** 八百万神 literally uses “eight hundred myriads,” but idiomatically means “myriad,” “countless,” or “all the multitudinous kami,” not a census of exactly 8,000,000. **[EA]** Its earliest textual occurrence is in the *Kojiki*’s Heavenly Rock-Cave episode, where the myriad kami assemble at the Tranquil River of Heaven. Related expressions—*yaso yorozu no kami*, “eighty myriads of kami,” and “eighty various kami”—occur in the *Nihon shoki* and *Man’yōshū*. The modern slogan “there are eight million Shinto gods” turns an indefinite ancient numerical idiom into a falsely precise statistic.

### Principal classifications

These taxonomies overlap and are often later abstractions:

- **Amatsukami** 天津神: “heavenly kami,” associated with Takamanohara and the descent of the heavenly grandchild. **[EA]**
- **Kunitsukami** 国津神: “landly” or terrestrial kami. The term does not mean that all were enemies of the heavenly kami. **[EA]**
- **Ujigami** 氏神: originally clan-associated tutelaries; later commonly territorial community kami. **[EA→LC]**
- **Ubusunagami** 産土神 and **chinju** 鎮守: birth-place and territorial/protective kami; frequently assimilated to local *ujigami*. **[LC/RV]**
- **Yama-no-kami**, **ta-no-kami**, **umi-no-kami**, **mizu-no-kami**: mountain, rice-field, sea, and water kami; often seasonal or locally gendered. **[F/RV]**
- **Sorei** 祖霊 and ancestral kami: family, lineage, or politically constructed ancestors. The dead do not automatically become equivalent kami in one uniform doctrine. **[LC/RV]**
- **Goryō** 御霊 and **onryō** 怨霊: powerful or angry dead whose calamity-producing force may be pacified through worship. Sugawara no Michizane’s transformation into Tenjin is the classic historical case. **[LC/MA]**
- **Araburu kami** 荒ぶる神: unruly or violent kami requiring conquest, negotiation, or pacification. **[EA]**
- **Tatari-gami** 祟り神: kami manifested through affliction or retribution. **[EA→F]**
- **Marebito** and visiting kami: periodically arriving beings, systematized especially in modern folklore studies. **[F/MSR]**
- **Gongen** 権現: “provisional manifestation,” normally a kami interpreted as a Buddhist manifestation. **[SI/MA]**
- **Myōjin** 明神: “manifest/illustrious deity,” a title widespread in medieval combinatory cults and subsequently purged from many official shrine names in the Meiji separation. **[SI/MA]**
- **Arahitogami** 現人神: a kami manifest as a living person; applied in court and later imperial discourse, but its meanings changed substantially before and during modern nationalism. **[EA→MI institutional reformulation]**

### Kami, yōkai, ghosts, Buddhas, and demons

The boundaries are historical rather than ontological constants. A feared apparition can become a pacified kami; an apotheosized human may be treated as both spirit and deity; foxes may be messengers of Inari rather than Inari themselves; tengu can appear as Buddhist adversaries, protectors, ghosts, or local mountain powers. Toriyama Sekien’s illustrated *yōkai* catalogues of 1776, 1779, 1781, and 1784 organized supernatural beings for an urban print audience, sometimes transforming puns, utensils, older monsters, and local traditions into apparently taxonomic creatures. They are therefore early-modern visual-literary evidence, not transparent catalogues of an unchanged ancient pantheon.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Before written attestation

Jōmon and Yayoi ritual deposits, figurines, bells, mirrors, weapons, agricultural rites, and special treatment of mountains or settlements demonstrate prehistoric religious activity. **[MSR]** Calling their recipients “kami” is retrospective: no surviving inscription links those objects to the word. Likewise, the *Wei zhi* account of Himiko in third-century Wa reports a female ruler employing ritual or “the way of demons/spirits,” but it does not attest Japanese *kami* terminology.

### What the earliest corpus establishes

The early texts securely establish that:

1. **Kami were plural, differentiated, and relational.** They had names, ranks, kinships, places, wills, and cults.
2. **The category included natural phenomena and human or ancestral figures**, but not under one uniform theory.
3. **Kami could be beneficial, dangerous, defeated, deceived, negotiated with, transferred, or ritually pacified.**
4. **Political rule and divine genealogy were already intertwined** in the eighth-century court compilations.
5. The corpus does **not** prove that its cosmology was believed uniformly throughout the islands, nor that its narratives survive unchanged from prehistoric times.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

No universal divine portfolio belongs to “the kami.” Collectively they encompass:

- generative forces, birth, death, decomposition, and purification;
- sun, moon, storm, wind, thunder, fire, water, sea, mountains, rocks, trees, and roads;
- rice, grain, fisheries, hunting, craft production, weaving, metalwork, hearth, and commerce;
- clan, household, village, province, nation, and imperial legitimacy;
- plague, vengeance, war, boundary defense, safe travel, childbirth, learning, and fortune.

The frequently repeated claim that Shinto is simply “nature worship” is an incomplete **[MSR]** abstraction. Nature-associated kami are central, but so are political ancestors, occupational gods, deified humans, disease gods, textual mythic personalities, and medieval Buddhist manifestations.

### Presence and manifestation

Kami are often approached through a **yorishiro** 依代, a medium or locus of descent, and a **shintai** 神体, “kami-body,” such as a mirror, sword, jewel, stone, mountain, sacred tree, or other object. A shintai is not necessarily a portrait. Sacred sites may be marked by **himorogi**, **iwasaka**, **iwakura**, ropes, fences, or gates. The standard modern shrine complex—*honden*, *haiden*, offering hall, dance stage, office, purification basin, and *torii*—is historically developed rather than a timeless prehistoric template. Permanent shrine buildings were not universal in early ritual.

### Powers and limitations

Kami may cause or avert harvest failure, disease, storm, infertility, military defeat, and pollution; send dreams or oracles; possess mediums; accept transfer or division of their spirit; and grant efficacy through amulets. But texts also impose limitations: kami can be surprised, injured, deceived, expelled, defeated, or dependent upon offerings. Some must deliberate collectively. Others are bound to particular land, lineage, shrine, or ritual conditions.

**Mitama theory.** **Aramitama** 荒御魂, a rough or active aspect, and **nigimitama** 和御魂, a harmonious aspect, are attested distinctions; later systems add **sakimitama** 幸御魂 and **kushimitama** 奇御魂. Treating every kami as necessarily divided into four independent “souls” is a **[MI]** overgeneralization from particular textual and theological schemes.

### Iconography

**[EA]** Early kami worship was not centered on anthropomorphic portrait statues. No securely identified pre-Buddhist image provides a universal appearance for kami.

**[SI/MA]** Wooden **shinzō**—anthropomorphic kami statues—appeared from the late Nara period under Buddhist artistic influence and proliferated in the Heian period. Important early examples include the ninth-century male and female kami statues at Matsunoo/Matsuo Taisha. Kami may appear as court aristocrats, monks, children, fierce protectors, women, old men, or paired figures. Hachiman imagery became particularly influential.

Medieval **miya-mandara** and **suijaku mandara** depict shrine landscapes, kami figures, and their Buddhist originals. Kasuga, Hie-Sannō, Kumano, and Hachiman iconographies are products of combinatory religion, not merely Buddhist decoration laid over an otherwise unchanged Shinto core.

Modern “standard portraits” of Amaterasu, Susanoo, or the creation gods often derive from Edo print culture, Meiji didactic art, twentieth-century illustration, manga, anime, or games. They are **[MA/MI]**, not recoverable ancient likenesses.

There is no securely attested universal sacred color, number, animal, weekday, or planet for the entire class. White ritual clothing, mirrors, *sakaki*, rice, salt, water, paper streamers, ropes, and *torii* are widespread in shrine practice, but their universality and meanings are historically variable.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Why there is no single genealogy

The *Kojiki* begins with solitary heavenly kami including Ame-no-Minakanushi, Takamimusubi, and Kamimusubi, followed by successive generations culminating in Izanagi and Izanami. The *Nihon shoki* gives several alternative openings and orders. Izanagi’s purification produces Amaterasu, Tsukuyomi, and Susanoo in the *Kojiki*; *Nihon shoki* variants give different modes and sequences of generation. Provincial *fudoki* preserve genealogies independent of, or only partially reconciled with, court mythology.

The heavenly/earthly distinction became politically important through the myth that Amaterasu’s descendant received authority to rule. It was never a complete empirical classification of every local kami. Clan compilers connected their ancestral kami to the court pantheon, while medieval and early-modern houses constructed further lineages.

### Human beings, ancestors, and emperors

Early chronicles apply *kami* language to extraordinary sovereigns and ancestral beings. Later shrines enshrined named emperors, courtiers, warriors, scholars, founders, and war dead. This is not one automatic process:

- Sugawara no Michizane became Tenjin through a historically documented cult of placation and scholarly patronage.
- Tokugawa Ieyasu was posthumously enshrined as Tōshō Daigongen through early-seventeenth-century political ritual.
- Emperor Meiji became the principal enshrined kami of Meiji Jingū after the shrine’s completion in 1920.
- Yasukuni enshrines the war dead collectively as kami; it is not their cemetery.

### Kami–Buddha combinations

Buddhism entered elite Japanese politics in the sixth century. Initial narratives sometimes treated the Buddha as an outside kami. Over subsequent centuries, Buddhist temples adopted local tutelaries, shrines received Buddhist ritual specialists and scriptures, and kami were interpreted as:

1. beings suffering within saṃsāra and needing Buddhist liberation;
2. protectors of the Dharma;
3. local manifestations of Buddhas and bodhisattvas.

The mature **honji suijaku** 本地垂迹 paradigm described Buddhas as “original grounds” and kami as compassionate local “traces.” The phrase is documented by the early ninth century, but systematic shrine-specific equations developed unevenly. Amaterasu could be related to Dainichi Nyorai; Hachiman to Amida or a bodhisattva identity; Sannō kami to Tendai divinities; Kumano kami to Buddhas and Pure Lands. There was no single universal concordance table.

**Reverse honji-suijaku** and Yoshida Shintō could assert that kami were primordial and Buddhas their manifestations. Such systems are **[LS/MA]**, not evidence that an autonomous, doctrinally unified Shinto had always existed.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Creation and the death of Izanami

**[EA]** In the *Kojiki*, the heavenly kami command Izanagi and Izanami to solidify the drifting land. Their first marriage rite fails after Izanami speaks first; after correction, they generate islands and kami. The episode encodes gendered ritual hierarchy, but whether that hierarchy reflects ancient marriage generally or the compilers’ ideology cannot be assumed.

Izanami dies giving birth to Kagutsuchi. From her body and Izanagi’s killing of the fire kami arise further deities. Izanagi travels to Yomi, sees Izanami’s decomposing body, flees, and seals the boundary. Their exchange establishes death’s daily toll and compensating births. His purification afterward generates numerous kami, including the three august children.

The *Nihon shoki* supplies alternate creation sequences, divine orders, and birth mechanisms. **Absence finding:** there is no single “official Shinto Genesis” shared unchanged by every source.

### Amaterasu, Susanoo, and the Heavenly Rock-Cave

Susanoo’s ascent prompts an oath-contest with Amaterasu. Each chews or transforms the other’s possessions to produce divine children; both claim vindication under differing logics. Susanoo then destroys fields, pollutes sacred space, and throws a flayed heavenly horse into the weaving hall. Amaterasu hides in a cave, removing light.

The myriad kami respond through collective ritual: divination, mirror and jewel production, liturgical speech, offerings, laughter, Ame-no-Uzume’s exposing dance, and Tajikarao’s strength. Amaterasu emerges after seeing her reflection; a rope prevents her return. The myth provides precedents for ritual specialists, regalia, sacred performance, and restoration after cosmic disorder.

Differences between *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* include the victim’s injury, sequence, degree of Susanoo’s culpability, and divine roles. Later interpretations that make the episode purely an eclipse myth, seasonal agricultural drama, shamanic initiation, or psychological allegory are **[MSR]** hypotheses, not statements made by the texts.

### Susanoo and Yamata-no-Orochi

After expulsion, Susanoo encounters a couple whose daughters have been consumed by an eight-forked serpent. He promises to save Kushinadahime, transforms or conceals her, prepares repeated vats of strong drink, and kills the intoxicated serpent. In its tail he finds the sword later called Kusanagi and presents it to Amaterasu.

**[EA]** The episode establishes Susanoo as both heavenly transgressor and terrestrial culture hero. Hydraulic, agricultural, metallurgical, and political readings are **[MSR]**; none has displaced the others conclusively.

### Ōkuninushi and the making of the land

The *Kojiki* narrates the White Hare episode, persecution by Ōkuninushi’s brothers, repeated deaths and revivals, ordeals imposed by Susanoo, marriage to Suseribime, and consolidation of power. Ōkuninushi later collaborates with the dwarf-like Sukunabikona and receives assistance or completion from Ōmononushi.

The *Izumo fudoki* preserves a different regional landscape mythology, notably the land-pulling story in which land is drawn from distant places to enlarge Izumo. It does not merely repeat the *Kojiki*’s Ōkuninushi biography. **[RV]** “Izumo mythology” therefore denotes multiple textual strata and political settings, not one lost book copied imperfectly by the court.

### Transfer of the land and heavenly descent

Heavenly envoys first fail to secure the terrestrial realm. Takemikazuchi eventually confronts Ōkuninushi; Kotoshironushi submits, while Takeminakata resists and is defeated. Ōkuninushi yields governing authority and receives a distinguished dwelling. Ninigi descends with regalia and ritual ancestors.

**[EA/political myth]** The cycle legitimates Yamato sovereignty by portraying terrestrial authority as divinely transferred. It also preserves negotiation and resistance rather than simple primordial ownership. Izumo and Suwa cults developed their own perspectives; reading the court account as neutral history is unwarranted.

### Hoori, Hoderi, and the sea palace

Hoori loses Hoderi’s fishhook, descends to the sea deity’s palace, marries Toyotamabime, recovers the hook, and receives tide-controlling jewels. He subordinates his brother. Toyotamabime gives birth on land but returns to the sea after Hoori violates her prohibition against watching.

**[EA]** The story links the imperial genealogy to sea powers while explaining separation between human and marine realms. Comparative “lost fishhook” studies identify parallels across maritime Asia, but direct lines of borrowing remain debated.

### Vengeful dead and apotheosis

**[LC/MA]** Historical calamities were sometimes interpreted as the work of aggrieved dead. Sugawara no Michizane, exiled in 901 and dead in 903, was associated after death with lightning and political disaster; placatory rites and the Kitano cult transformed him into Tenjin, later patron of learning. This is one of the clearest cases in which documentary history shows a human becoming a major kami through political memory, fear, ritual, and institutional expansion.

### Folkloric cycles

Seasonally alternating mountain and field kami, household kami, boundary gods, dangerous water beings, animal messengers, and visiting divinities appear in local practice and later ethnography. Yanagita, Hori Ichirō, Orikuchi Shinobu, and successors created influential comparative categories from this material. Their typologies are **[MSR]**, even where the underlying customs are well documented.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Shrines and sacred places

A **jinja** is an institution for kami worship, also called *yashiro*, *miya*, *mori*, or *hokora* in differing contexts. Early worship could occur at temporary sites, mountains, rocks, trees, springs, or ritual enclosures without a permanent sanctuary. Shrine architecture developed in interaction with court patronage, Buddhist construction, local wealth, pilgrimage, and periodic rebuilding.

Major institutions include:

- **Ise Jingū:** centered on Amaterasu and Toyouke; closely tied to imperial ritual. Its historical institutions and rebuilding cycle are documented, but claims extending every feature unchanged into remote prehistory are later sacral historiography.
- **Izumo Taisha:** associated principally with Ōkuninushi and the land-transfer complex; archaeological and textual evidence confirms exceptional antiquity and political importance, though reconstructions of gigantic ancient buildings remain debated in scale.
- **Usa Hachiman:** major center for Hachiman, court oracles, Buddhist protection, and later warrior patronage.
- **Iwashimizu Hachiman:** founded near Kyoto in the ninth century and integrated into court protection.
- **Kasuga:** Fujiwara tutelary complex closely linked to Kōfukuji.
- **Hie/Sannō:** protective kami of Mount Hiei and Enryakuji.
- **Kumano:** shrine-temple pilgrimage network interpreted through kami, Buddhas, Pure Lands, asceticism, and healing.
- **Kitano:** center of the pacified Michizane/Tenjin cult.
- **Suwa:** cult of Takeminakata and local powers, with ritual histories not reducible to the *Kojiki* episode.
- **Fushimi Inari:** agricultural and commercial cult with highly variable identities and strong medieval Buddhist associations.

The *Engishiki*’s register documents a countrywide court-recognized system by the tenth century. Its listed **shikinaisha** were privileged within the state liturgy; omission does not prove that an unlisted local cult did not exist.

### Priesthoods and religious personnel

Ancient offices included the **Jingikan**, court ritual lineages such as Nakatomi and Inbe, provincial officials, shrine hereditary houses, female mediums, and temporary community functionaries. **Kannushi** and **shinshoku** are broad priestly terms; **negi**, **gūji**, and related titles denote particular ranks or offices.

**Miko** historically included spirit mediums, oracle-givers, ritual dancers, itinerant religious women, and modern shrine attendants. The modern image of an unmarried young woman in standardized red-and-white clothing is institutionally narrowed. Some itinerant miko became entertainers or sex workers after losing stable patronage. That evidence does **not** establish a general institution of “Shinto temple prostitution”; shrines are not temples, and ritual sexuality, itinerant prostitution, and temple-based commercial prostitution must not be collapsed into one claim.

### Ritual pattern

A typical shrine rite may include purification, invitation or acknowledgment of the kami, offerings, *norito*, music or *kagura*, bows and claps, communal participation, and dismissal or conclusion. Offerings include rice, sake, salt, water, fish, vegetables, fruit, cloth, branches, and symbolic valuables. Exact practice varies by shrine and period.

The *Engishiki norito* document rites for harvest, wind, fire, palace protection, travel, enthronement, Ise, and the Great Purification. They praise the kami, state ritual precedent, enumerate offerings, and request protection; they are not systematic creeds.

Divination, dream oracles, spirit possession, pilgrimage, amulets, votive plaques, sacred lots, and transfer of divided spirit are all documented. Modern claims that Shinto has “no belief, only ritual” overcorrect: doctrine was often decentralized, but narratives, cosmologies, ethical expectations, and institutional teachings plainly existed.

### Sacrifice and contested material

Animal and food offerings are textually secure. The early chronicles also preserve narratives of human beings offered or endangered to appease river, serpent, or other powers. **Hitobashira**, “human pillar,” appears in legends of people buried in embankments, bridges, castles, or other construction works. Later records describe sacrificed persons becoming local kami, such as the eighteenth-century account of Kita/Okita Myōjin.

The evidentiary levels must remain separate:

- **[EA textual attestation]:** chronicles narrate human-sacrifice proposals or substitutions.
- **[F/MA]:** many castle and bridge stories are legends first recorded long after the alleged event.
- **[Archaeological inference]:** figurines or effigies beneath structures may be interpreted as substitutes, but do not by themselves prove an earlier living victim.
- **Finding:** Japan had traditions imagining and sometimes reporting human sacrifice, but popular lists treating every *hitobashira* legend as an excavated historical killing are unsound.

### Economics and politics

Shrines accumulated land, labor rights, tolls, estate income, pilgrim offerings, craft and market connections, and court or warrior patronage. Shrine-temple complexes were economic and sometimes military powers. Medieval monks, shrine priests, estate managers, warrior houses, and local communities competed over the same sacred institutions. “Shinto shrine versus Buddhist temple” is frequently an anachronistic division for this period.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Prehistory to the sixth century

**[MSR]** Archaeology shows diverse ritual cultures, but cannot establish a unitary “prehistoric Shinto.” Clan cults, agricultural rites, political sacrality, Korean and Chinese contacts, and regional practices contributed to later kami worship. Any claim of an unchanged Shinto religion extending intact from Jōmon times is unprovable.

### Sixth to eighth centuries: court formation

Buddhism, continental ritual technologies, writing, law, and state centralization changed the ways kami were classified and served. The *Kojiki*, *Nihon shoki*, and *fudoki* did not merely preserve myths; they selected and reorganized them within Yamato state-building. Court rites distinguished heavenly and terrestrial kami and connected sovereignty to Amaterasu’s line.

### Ninth to twelfth centuries: institutional and Buddhist integration

Court rankings, official offerings, shrine registers, temple–shrine relationships, and protective kami cults expanded. Hachiman became a Buddhist protector and bodhisattva. Kami were read within karmic and salvific frameworks. The *Engishiki* fixed an administrative snapshot in 927/967, while local practices remained more extensive.

### Twelfth to sixteenth centuries: combinatory systems

Honji-suijaku, shrine mandalas, pilgrimage, esoteric initiations, oracles, and temple-shrine estates flourished. Kasuga–Kōfukuji, Hie–Enryakuji, and Kumano exemplify institutions in which “Shinto” and “Buddhism” cannot be cleanly separated. Medieval theologians developed specialized lineages—Watarai/Ise, Sannō, Miwa, and others.

Yoshida Kanetomo’s fifteenth-century system claimed an encompassing and primordial Shinto. **[LS/MA]** Its importance supports Kuroda’s point that a self-conscious, translocal Shinto tradition was historically constructed; it also shows that such construction began before Meiji.

### Seventeenth to mid-nineteenth centuries

Tokugawa rule maintained shrine-temple administration while Confucian, Buddhist, and nativist thinkers reinterpreted kami. Yoshikawa and Suika Shintō joined kami theology to Neo-Confucian ethics. Kokugaku scholars recovered early vocabulary through philology but also recast it for their own age.

Motoori emphasized the *Kojiki*, divine creation, imperial descent, and affective receptivity to ancient language. Hirata Atsutane developed a more systematic spirit world and activist national teaching. These movements influenced Restoration ideology, but neither should be mistaken for an unchanged oral survival from 712.

### 1868–1945: separation and state system

In 1868 the new government issued separation orders removing Buddhist titles, images, clergy, and institutions from shrines. The policy of **shinbutsu bunri** triggered or enabled **haibutsu kishaku**, during which temples and images were destroyed, lands confiscated, and monks forced to laicize in many areas. The central orders mandated separation, not universally the destruction of Buddhism, but local officials and activists often crossed that line.

In 1871 the government abolished hereditary control of many priestly posts, instituted shrine ranks, and declared shrines ritual foundations of the state. A Shrine Bureau followed in 1900; from 1906 shrine consolidation eliminated or merged many small local shrines.

“State Shinto” remains debated:

- **Conventional interpretation:** from Meiji through 1945 the state funded, ranked, supervised, and used shrines and imperial ritual for national integration, education, mobilization, and empire.
- **Contemporary official theory:** shrine rites were nonreligious national ceremonies; constitutionally protected “religions” existed separately.
- **Shimazono and others:** the emperor-centered system extended beyond the shrine bureaucracy into education, households, moral instruction, and public ritual.
- **Caution:** the system changed repeatedly between 1868 and 1945; it was not one stable church.

Colonial governments erected shrines in Taiwan, Korea, Karafuto, Manchuria, Micronesia, and elsewhere. Especially during wartime mobilization, colonial subjects could be compelled or heavily pressured to attend rites presented as patriotic rather than religious. This was political deployment of kami worship within empire, not evidence of voluntary acceptance everywhere.

### 1945 to the present

The SCAP Shinto Directive of 15 December 1945 terminated state sponsorship and control of Shrine Shinto. The 1947 Constitution guaranteed religious freedom and separated religion and state. In February 1946, most shrines organized within the private religious corporation **Jinja Honchō**, the Association of Shinto Shrines.

Jinja Honchō reports roughly 80,000 affiliated or existing shrines in Japan, but it is not a pope-like authority over all kami traditions. Independent shrines, sectarian Shinto groups, new religions, Buddhist temples with kami cults, household worship, local festivals, and unaffiliated practitioners remain.

The postwar emperor is constitutionally a symbol of the state. The 1946 imperial rescript’s famous treatment of imperial divinity did not erase every ritual or descent tradition, and historians dispute the common English shorthand that Hirohito simply “renounced being a god.”

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Folklore, ethnology, and the yōkai boom

Yanagita Kunio’s *Tōno monogatari* and later folklore studies made rural narratives central to modern conceptions of Japanese identity. Hori Ichirō developed influential accounts of folk religion, sacred visitors, shamanism, and the relation of local practice to institutional religion. Michael Dylan Foster’s work traces how *yōkai* became objects of scholarship, entertainment, tourism, collecting, and commercial branding.

The modern popular continuum connecting kami, *yōkai*, ghosts, and “nature spirits” draws on real historical permeability, but franchises often erase differences between a shrine’s kami, Buddhist protector, local monster, and invented character.

### Literature, art, film, manga, and games

Kami imagery has been reshaped by:

- Edo illustrated books and theatrical traditions;
- Meiji mythological paintings and imperial education;
- Lafcadio Hearn’s English-language presentations of Japanese supernatural traditions;
- Miyazaki Hayao’s *Princess Mononoke* and *Spirited Away*, which use invented and reconfigured kami-like beings rather than reproducing a canonical pantheon;
- manga, anime, *Pokémon*, *Ōkami*, *Shin Megami Tensei*, *Persona*, *Touhou*, and related media.

These works materially shape global expectations that kami are environmental “spirits” or fantasy species. Such portrayals are **[modern reception]**, even where they quote classical names.

### Western esotericism and neopaganism

There is no strong evidence that “the kami” as a class had a major independent role in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, or early European ceremonial magic. Claims that Golden Dawn adepts transmitted ancient kami invocations are **[MI]** unless a specific modern text is produced.

From the late twentieth century, individual occultists, New Age writers, animists, and neopagans incorporated kami into eclectic practice. Internet communities now debate whether non-Japanese practitioners may maintain *kamidana*, obtain shrine amulets, or combine shrine worship with witchcraft. These are documented modern transnational developments, not ancient Shinto lineages. Scholarship on global and digital Shinto shows actual overseas shrine communities and household practices; this should not be conflated with every online “work with a kami” claim.

Jungian descriptions of kami as archetypes are likewise **[MI interpretive tradition]**. They may be psychologically meaningful to their users but do not date earlier than modern depth psychology.

### Nationalism and extremist appropriation

Imperial descent, shrine worship, and apotheosis of war dead were used in state ideology and colonial mobilization. Postwar right-wing groups continue to treat shrine practice, especially Yasukuni, as a foundation of national restoration.

Yasukuni began as Tokyo Shōkonsha in 1869 and enshrines those designated as having died in imperial service. In 1978 its priesthood secretly enshrined fourteen convicted Class-A war criminals; public disclosure followed in 1979. Prime-ministerial visits and offerings remain contested because of constitutional church–state questions, colonial memory, and the shrine museum’s war narrative. The site also includes Koreans, Taiwanese, Christians, and Buddhists whose families have objected to involuntary enshrinement.

This political record does not make all contemporary kami worship extremist. Conversely, describing shrine rites as merely private spirituality would conceal their documented use in nationalism and empire.

### Commercialization

Shrine tourism, character merchandise, branded amulets, anime collaborations, “power spots,” regional mascots, wedding packages, and digital divination are established parts of modern religious economies. Commercialization is not wholly new—premodern pilgrimage and print culture were already commercial—but the media scale and global market are modern.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Shinto ancient or invented?

Toshio Kuroda’s 1981 “Shinto in the History of Japanese Religion” challenged the model of an autonomous indigenous religion continuing unchanged from prehistory. He argued that premodern *shintō* often denoted kami, kami-related matters, or a component inside the Buddhist **kenmitsu** order; a distinct, independent religion was substantially a late-medieval and modern construction.

This thesis transformed the field but is not the last word:

- **Kuroda’s evidence:** shifting meanings of *shintō*; pervasive temple-shrine institutions; Buddhist production of kami doctrine; lack of a centralized, independent church.
- **Counterqualification:** identifiable premodern practices of kami worship, court ritual, shrine institutions, local myth, and non-Buddhist lineages undeniably existed.
- **Breen and Teeuwen’s synthesis:** neither an eternal, self-identical Shinto nor a purely Meiji fabrication. Particular traditions, sites, myths, and rites were repeatedly assembled into new configurations called Shinto.
- **Current responsible formulation:** kami worship is ancient; “Shinto” as a unified religion with timeless essence is historically constructed; construction occurred in several phases, especially medieval system-building, Edo nativism, and Meiji administration.

### “Animism”

Calling kami belief animism may describe attribution of agency to mountains, animals, plants, objects, and phenomena. It becomes misleading when it implies primitive irrationality, uniformity, or a doctrine that every object automatically contains a miniature spirit. The sources instead show selective recognition, ritual manifestation, named relationships, and politically organized cults.

### Etymology and category

No secure etymology of *kami* settles what kami “really are.” Historical semantics must be reconstructed from usage. The category changed whenever Buddhist, Confucian, nativist, imperial, ethnological, or global discourse redescribed it.

### Textual reliability

- The *Kojiki* is dated 712, but its oldest complete manuscript is from 1371–72.
- The *Nihon shoki* has earlier fragmentary witnesses and preserves explicit variants.
- *Fudoki* survival is uneven; only Izumo is substantially complete.
- *Engishiki* is administrative and selective.
- Shrine origin legends often survive only in late manuscripts.
- Folklore collections are mediated by collectors, editors, informants, and print genres.

A narrative may be early as a surviving text without being a factual record of prehistoric events. Conversely, late recording does not prove recent invention; it merely limits how early the tradition can be demonstrated.

### Discredited or overstated theories still circulating

- **“Yaoyorozu means exactly eight million gods.”** False literalization.
- **“Every natural object is automatically a kami.”** Overgeneralization.
- **“Shinto has remained unchanged since the Jōmon period.”** Unsupported.
- **“Kami means ‘above’.”** Traditional pun contradicted by historical phonology.
- **“Kami and Ainu kamuy are certainly cognate.”** Possible contact relationship, not demonstrated certainty.
- **“Shinto was purely aniconic until 1868.”** False; medieval *shinzō* and mandalas survive.
- **“Buddhism merely contaminated an originally pure Shinto.”** A Meiji/nativist polemic contradicted by centuries of constitutive interaction.
- **“All yōkai are kami,” or “kami and yōkai are entirely separate biological species.”** Both impose rigid modern taxonomy.
- **“Human sacrifice at every famous bridge or castle is archaeologically proven.”** False.
- **“Miko were an institution of sacred temple prostitutes.”** Unsupported generalization from heterogeneous ritual and itinerant occupations.
- **“The emperor’s 1946 declaration ended belief in kami or abolished imperial rites.”** False.
- **“The kami have ancient correspondences in Western ceremonial magic.”** Modern invention unless supported by a dated transmission document.

### State of the evidence

Primary materials survive across the Imperial Household archives, National Archives of Japan, National Diet Library, Shōsōin, National Museum collections, shrine and temple archives, university collections, and local museums. Fires, war, iconoclasm, periodic rebuilding, shrine-temple separation, administrative consolidation, and manuscript decay destroyed or displaced a substantial part of the record.

Important modern resources include the National Diet Library’s digital collections, the Japanese Historical Text Initiative, Kokugakuin University’s *Encyclopedia of Shinto*, museum catalogues, critical Japanese editions, archaeological reports, and shrine-specific document collections. Kokugakuin’s encyclopedia is exceptionally useful but represents scholarship produced within an institution historically connected to Shinto studies; it should be read alongside critical historians such as Kuroda, Hardacre, Grapard, Teeuwen, Rambelli, Breen, and Shimazono.

## 10. Chronology

- **Jōmon–Yayoi periods:** Ritual objects and sites attest diverse prehistoric religious practices; no written evidence securely names *kami*. **[MSR]**
- **Third century CE:** Chinese *Wei zhi* describes ritual-political authority in Wa; it does not attest the Japanese word *kami*. **[external early evidence]**
- **Sixth century:** Buddhism becomes a major court issue; chronicles frame it partly through the vocabulary of foreign kami. **[retrospective chronicle evidence]**
- **Late seventh–early eighth centuries:** Court ritual and written myth are reorganized under the centralized state.
- **712:** *Kojiki* presented to Empress Genmei. Earliest surviving integrated record using *kami* throughout. **[EA]**
- **713:** Provinces ordered to compile *fudoki*. **[EA administrative record]**
- **c.715–733:** Surviving Harima, Hitachi, Izumo, Hizen, and Bungo *fudoki* composed. **[EA/RV]**
- **720:** *Nihon shoki* completed, preserving multiple divine variants. **[EA]**
- **Eighth century:** *Man’yōshū* poetry documents kami in courtly, local, and travel contexts. **[EA]**
- **Late eighth–ninth centuries:** Anthropomorphic wooden kami images appear under strong Buddhist artistic influence. **[SI]**
- **807:** *Kogo shūi* submitted, preserving Inbe ritual claims. **[LC]**
- **Early ninth century:** Honji-suijaku terminology begins to appear; Hachiman’s Buddhist identity develops. **[SI]**
- **859:** Iwashimizu Hachiman’s cult established near Kyoto. **[LC/SI]**
- **905–927:** *Engishiki* compiled. **[LC]**
- **967:** *Engishiki* promulgated; its shrine register becomes a major record of official cults.
- **Eleventh–twelfth centuries:** Mature honji-suijaku systems, shrine mandalas, pilgrimage networks, and Buddhist kami narratives proliferate. **[SI/MA]**
- **Early twelfth century:** *Konjaku monogatari-shū* compiled. **[MA]**
- **Thirteenth–fourteenth centuries:** Major shrine *engi*, oracle collections, and illustrated scrolls codify local sacred histories.
- **1371–72:** Shinpukuji manuscript of the *Kojiki*, the oldest complete surviving witness, copied by Buddhist monks.
- **Fifteenth century:** Yoshida Kanetomo develops Yoshida Shintō as an encompassing learned system. **[LS/MA]**
- **1603–1617:** Tokugawa regime established; Ieyasu is apotheosized as Tōshō Daigongen. **[political apotheosis]**
- **Seventeenth century:** Confucian-Shinto systems, including Yoshikawa and Suika Shintō, develop. **[LS]**
- **1764–1798:** Motoori composes *Kojiki-den*. **[LS]**
- **1776–1784:** Toriyama Sekien publishes four illustrated *yōkai* catalogues. **[MA]**
- **Early nineteenth century:** Hirata Atsutane elaborates a systematic spirit world and Restoration Shinto. **[LS]**
- **1868:** Government orders separation of kami and Buddhas; local anti-Buddhist violence and destruction follow in many regions.
- **1871:** Modern shrine ranking system instituted; hereditary priestly privileges curtailed; shrines declared state ritual foundations.
- **1889–1890:** Meiji Constitution and Imperial Rescript on Education consolidate the modern emperor-centered order.
- **1900:** Shrine Bureau created.
- **1906–1912:** Large-scale shrine mergers suppress or absorb many local institutions.
- **1910:** Yanagita publishes *Tōno monogatari*. **[F/MSR]**
- **1920:** Meiji Jingū completed, enshrining Emperor Meiji and Empress Shōken.
- **1937:** Ministry of Education publishes *Kokutai no hongi*, systematizing imperial-national teaching. **[MI state ideology]**
- **1930s–1945:** Shrine attendance and imperial rites intensify across Japan’s empire; colonial coercion is documented.
- **15 December 1945:** SCAP issues the Shinto Directive, ending state sponsorship and control.
- **January 1946:** Imperial rescript rejects certain modern claims about the emperor–people relationship; later shorthand calls this a “renunciation of divinity,” though its language and implications are debated.
- **February 1946:** Jinja Honchō founded as a private religious organization representing most shrines.
- **3 May 1947:** Postwar Constitution takes effect, guaranteeing religious freedom and church–state separation.
- **1968/69:** Donald Philippi publishes his annotated English *Kojiki*.
- **1978:** Yasukuni secretly enshrines fourteen convicted Class-A war criminals.
- **1981:** Kuroda Toshio’s critique of Shinto as a timeless independent religion appears in English.
- **2000s–present:** Digital archives, global shrine communities, online household practice, anime/game reception, and transnational debates expand the category’s reach.
- **Present:** Approximately 80,000 shrines operate in Japan according to Jinja Honchō’s public account; kami worship also continues outside its authority in local, Buddhist, new-religious, household, and overseas contexts.
