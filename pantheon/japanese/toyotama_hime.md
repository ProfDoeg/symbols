# Toyotama-hime: Research Dossier

**Class:** female *kami*—a sea-god’s daughter, divine princess, wife and ancestress in the imperial genealogy; later worshipped especially as a sea, water, childbirth, and safe-delivery deity. She is not classified as a *yōkai* in the early sources, although modern popular catalogues sometimes place her among supernatural shape-shifters.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the *Kojiki*, completed in 712 CE, under the forms 豊玉毘売 and 豊玉毘売命, *Toyotama-bime/Toyotama-bime no Mikoto*. Its surviving textual witnesses are much later: the oldest substantially complete manuscript is the Shinpukuji copy of 1371–1372. The independently compiled *Nihon shoki* of 720 attests 豊玉姫, *Toyotama-hime*, and preserves several variant recensions of her story. The earliest secure cultic evidence is later: names corresponding to her occur in provincial material preserved as a *Yamashiro fudoki* fragment and in two Awa Province shrine names recorded by the *Engishiki* shrine register, completed in 927.

---

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Toyotama-hime is the daughter of the sea deity conventionally called Ōwatatsumi, Watatsumi, or Toyotama-hiko; wife of Hoori/Hikohohodemi, the “Luck of the Mountain”; mother of Ugayafukiaezu; elder sister of Tamayori-hime; and, through Ugayafukiaezu, paternal grandmother of the legendary first emperor Jimmu. Because Ugayafukiaezu marries Tamayori-hime, Toyotama-hime is also Jimmu’s maternal aunt. That double sea-god connection belongs to the dynastic genealogy constructed in the eighth-century chronicles, not to independently datable history about an actual imperial family.

### Documented forms

- **豊玉毘売**, *Toyotama-bime*: principal *Kojiki* spelling, 712.
- **豊玉毘売命**, *Toyotama-bime no Mikoto*: “Toyotama Princess, the August One,” *Kojiki*.
- **豊玉姫 / 豊玉姫命**, *Toyotama-hime / Toyotama-hime no Mikoto*: *Nihon shoki*, 720; also *Kogo shūi*, 807, and later shrine usage.
- **和多都弥豊玉比売命**, *Watatsumi Toyotama-hime no Mikoto*: form preserved in a *Yamashiro no kuni fudoki* fragment, literally joining her to the sea deity/name Watatsumi.
- **Toyo-tama-bime**, **Toyo-tama-hime**, **Toyotamabime**, and **Toyotamahime**: scholarly romanizations. Philippi’s *Kojiki* uses reconstructed Old Japanese-style spellings; Aston’s *Nihongi* uses nineteenth-century romanization.

### Etymology

No ancient source supplies an etymology. The name is transparent only in part:

- **toyo** 豊: “abundant,” “rich,” “luxuriant,” or an honorific intensifier.
- **tama** 玉: “jewel,” “precious stone,” “pearl,” or—under a different semantic analysis—*tama* as soul/spirit or numinous potency.
- **hime/bime** 姫・毘売: princess or high-born/divine woman. The voiced *bime* is the compound form.

Modern Japanese scholarship consequently offers several reconstructions:

1. **“Abundant-jewel princess” or “princess of abundant pearls.”** This connects the name with the sea’s treasure, the jewel bowl in the courtship scene, and the tide-governing jewels possessed by the sea god.
2. **A sacred maiden in whom a *tama* or divine spirit lodges.** This interprets the name in relation to *Tamayori-hime*, whose name can mean a woman upon whom a spirit “approaches” or “depends.”
3. **A title praising extraordinary beauty.** This is reflected in shrine explanations that her face was “like a jewel.”
4. **A priestess associated with marine jewels, pearls, or stone beads.** This is a modern scholarly reconstruction, not a title stated in the chronicles.

Kokugakuin’s critical mythological database presents these as competing interpretations rather than a settled translation. “Pearl Princess” is convenient but not certain.

### Titles and later equations

The chronicles do not give Toyotama-hime a portfolio-style title such as “goddess of childbirth.” Her modern domains derive from narrative and shrine practice.

- **Sea deity / water deity:** an inferred and cultic classification based on descent from Watatsumi and residence in the sea palace.
- **Childbirth and safe-delivery deity:** a later cultic development, especially prominent at shrines bearing her name.
- **Dragon princess / daughter of the Dragon King:** a later retelling shaped by the *Nihon shoki*’s dragon reading and by the mature Buddhist and East Asian “Dragon Palace” complex.
- **Otohime:** modern popular writing sometimes treats Toyotama-hime as the prototype of, or equivalent to, the Dragon Palace princess in the Urashima tradition. No early source identifies her by that name.
- **Ryūjin’s daughter:** common modern wording. The early texts call her father a sea god; treating “Ryūjin” as his fixed personal name is later systematization.
- **Azumi ancestress:** sometimes asserted online, but the *Kojiki* directly derives the Azumi line from Utsushi-hikana-saku, a child/descendant of the sea god, not from Toyotama-hime herself. A direct Toyotama-hime–Azumi genealogy should therefore be labeled conjectural.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The *Kojiki* (古事記), 712

**Status:** securely attested early canonical material.

Compiled by Ō no Yasumaro from court genealogical and narrative traditions and presented in 712, the *Kojiki* is the earliest surviving work to name Toyotama-hime. It is written in a mixed sinographic register used to represent Japanese narrative and verse. Its relevant episodes occur near the end of the Age of the Gods.

The text says that:

1. Hoori reaches the sea god’s realm while seeking his brother’s lost hook.
2. A maid of Toyotama-hime comes to the well with a jeweled vessel.
3. Hoori places a jewel from his necklace in his mouth and spits it into the vessel; it adheres.
4. Toyotama-hime sees him, and the two are mutually attracted.
5. Her father recognizes Hoori as a descendant of the heavenly deities, receives him ceremonially, and gives Toyotama-hime to him.
6. The couple remains together for three years.
7. Hoori returns to land after the sea god recovers the hook and gives him the tide-raising and tide-lowering jewels.
8. Toyotama-hime later comes to the shore to give birth, saying that a heavenly deity’s child must not be born in the sea.
9. A birth hut is roofed with cormorant feathers, but labor begins before it is finished.
10. She warns Hoori that beings of another country give birth in their native form and asks him not to look.
11. Hoori spies upon her and sees her as an **八尋和邇**, *yahiro-wani*, crawling and writhing.
12. Ashamed, she leaves their son, closes the sea boundary, and returns beneath the sea.
13. Still loving Hoori, she sends her sister Tamayori-hime to rear the child and sends a song; Hoori answers with another song.

The transmitted wording is explicit:

> 化八尋和迩而、匍匐委蛇  
> “She transformed into an eight-fathom *wani* and crawled/writhed.”

The text does not call the creature a modern zoological crocodile, shark, or dragon. Those are translations of *wani*. Philippi’s annotated English translation remains fundamental; “crocodile” reflects one historical lexical choice, while many modern Japanese editions favor “shark.” “Large marine creature” or “sea-monster” preserves the uncertainty better.

The original 712 manuscript is lost. The principal complete medieval witness is the Shinpukuji manuscript copied in 1371–1372, so the work’s compilation date and the date of its oldest full material witness must not be conflated.

### The *Nihon shoki* (日本書紀), 720

**Status:** securely attested early canonical material with explicitly preserved variants.

Written largely in Literary Sinitic, the *Nihon shoki* gives a court-sponsored version and then several “one writing says” alternatives. This structure is indispensable: it demonstrates that no single form of the story was uncontested in 720.

- **Main account:** Toyotama-hime changes into a **dragon** during childbirth. Hikohohodemi sees her and she is ashamed. She returns to the sea but sends Tamayori-hime to care for the child.
- **Variant accounts:** describe her as an **eight-fathom great *wani*** or **great bear-*wani***; vary how Hikohohodemi provides light or peers inside; and vary the precise wording of the separation and the infant’s abandonment or care.
- Some versions emphasize that the sea road is closed; others emphasize Toyotama-hime’s shame, continued affection, and dispatch of her sister.

The main text’s 龍, “dragon,” is not sufficient to overwrite the *Kojiki*’s *wani*. A recent Japanese study argues that the *Nihon shoki* editors deliberately chose the prestigious Chinese imperial symbol of the dragon to present Hikohohodemi’s descendants as rulers comparable to the Chinese emperor. This is a plausible literary-political interpretation, not a fact stated by the chronicle.

Aston’s 1896 translation remains freely accessible and useful, but its zoological vocabulary and notes require comparison with newer Japanese editions.

### *Kogo shūi* (古語拾遺), 807

**Status:** later canonical/learned court elaboration.

Compiled by Inbe no Hironari, the *Kogo shūi* retells court mythology partly to defend Inbe ritual prerogatives. It names Toyotama-hime in the Hikohohodemi material but does not preserve an independent biography. Its importance lies in showing continued court transmission in the early ninth century.

### *Sendai kuji hongi* / *Kujiki*

**Status:** early-medieval learned systematization of uncertain date and authority.

The received work, generally placed in the ninth or tenth century despite its claim to great antiquity, includes Toyotama-hime in its imperial-descent genealogy. It cannot be used as evidence predating the *Kojiki* or *Nihon shoki*. Claims that it is an ancient source independent of both chronicles remain unestablished.

### Provincial *fudoki*

**Status:** early provincial tradition, but surviving only fragmentarily in this case.

A lost *Yamashiro fudoki* passage preserved in quotation names **Watatsumi Toyotama-hime no Mikoto**. Because the original gazetteer is lost and the passage survives through later quotation, its wording is evidence for an early provincial cultic or genealogical association but not a complete independent narrative.

No extant *Izumo fudoki* episode tells Toyotama-hime’s marriage and childbirth story. General references to *fudoki* animal-wife or water-deity traditions are comparative material, not direct attestations of her.

### *Engishiki* and norito

**Status:** secure tenth-century cultic register; absence from the surviving standard norito corpus.

The *Engishiki*, completed in 927 and promulgated in the tenth century, lists:

- **和多津美豊玉比売神社**, *Watatsumi Toyotama-hime Jinja*, in Nafata/Nakata District, Awa Province.
- **天石別豊玉比売神社**, *Ame-no-Iwawake Toyotama-hime Jinja*, likewise in Awa.

These entries demonstrate named institutional cults by the early tenth century. They do not describe the shrines’ icons, rites, foundation dates, or narratives.

Toyotama-hime is not a speaking or receiving deity in the standard *Engishiki* norito usually translated as the classical liturgical corpus. Modern claims that there is an ancient “Toyotama-hime norito” require a specific manuscript or shrine text; none was established in this research.

### *Man’yōshū*

**Status:** no secure direct named appearance established.

The eighth-century anthology contains maritime, dragon-palace, jewel, and Watatsumi imagery, as well as poems about rejuvenating water, but no securely identified poem naming Toyotama-hime was located. Such imagery cannot automatically be assigned to her.

### Medieval narrative and yōkai literature

No independent Toyotama-hime tale in the *Konjaku monogatari shū* was established. Its animal-spouse and supernatural-birth tales are comparative material.

Toriyama Sekien’s eighteenth-century illustrated yōkai catalogues do not provide a canonical entry for Toyotama-hime. Classifying her as one of Sekien’s yōkai is a modern conflation.

### Early-modern commentary

Motoori Norinaga’s monumental *Kojiki-den* (古事記伝, composed and published in stages, 1760s–1798) philologically restored the prestige of the *Kojiki* and treated its divine-age narrative as an authoritative record of the ancient Japanese way. His readings strongly influenced modern pronunciation, exegesis, and shrine discourse.

Hirata Atsutane’s nineteenth-century Restoration Shinto system expanded the reality and cosmic organization of the kami world. Toyotama-hime was incorporated into this broader nativist sacred history, but no distinctive Atsutane cult or major new Toyotama myth was established.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Early attributes

The early chronicles establish only a limited set:

- residence in the sea god’s palace;
- divine beauty;
- affinity with jewels and marine wealth through her name and courtship setting;
- ability or necessity to assume her “original-country” form in childbirth;
- passage between sea and land;
- motherhood of a dynastic ancestor;
- power to close the sea boundary after the taboo is broken;
- continued affection expressed through a messenger and song.

She does not personally wield the tide-raising and tide-lowering jewels in the *Kojiki*: her father gives them to Hoori. Modern art sometimes transfers the jewels to her because of her name and marine identity.

### *Wani*, crocodile, shark, or dragon

The zoological question cannot be resolved from the word alone.

- **Early attested:** *Kojiki* 八尋和邇; *Nihon shoki* variants with 大鰐 and 大熊鰐.
- **Early alternate redaction:** *Nihon shoki* main text 龍, dragon.
- **Modern translation:** crocodile, alligator, shark, sea monster, or sea dragon.
- **Scholarly possibilities:** a remembered crocodilian, shark, fabulous marine animal, clan/totemic emblem, metaphor for an unfamiliar birthing posture, or narrative marker of an otherworld being.
- **Not established:** that the tale proves a crocodile cult, a historical crocodile population in eighth-century Japan, or a particular clan’s zoological totem.

The expression *yahiro*, “eight fathoms,” is an epic measure of enormity, not a reliable body length.

### Childbirth imagery

The unfinished cormorant-feather birth hut explains the son’s long name, conventionally rendered “He of the Brave Shore, whose Cormorant-Feather Thatching was Unfinished.” Later shrines derive safe-childbirth benefits from Toyotama-hime’s delivery. The claim that she experienced an easy birth is shrine interpretation; the chronicle instead depicts urgent labor, an unfinished hut, exposure, separation, and abandonment of the original plan to raise the child across both worlds.

Scholars have interpreted the taboo through:

- childbirth pollution;
- protection and seclusion of mother and infant;
- the prohibition against seeing an otherworld spouse’s true form;
- a clash between local and foreign birthing customs;
- the boundary between sea and land;
- dynastic incorporation of a maritime people followed by narrative separation.

None has displaced the others.

### Iconography

No securely identified ancient image of Toyotama-hime survives. Early kami worship ordinarily did not require anthropomorphic cult statues, and no eighth-century object bears an uncontested inscription identifying her.

Later visual forms include:

- a courtly Japanese princess;
- a woman accompanied by waves, jewels, a dragon, shark, or crocodilian creature;
- the childbirth scene, with Hoori peering into the hut;
- a full dragon in the birth hut, following the *Nihon shoki* main text and later Dragon Palace imagery.

A well-documented example is Yōshū/Toyohara Chikanobu’s 1886 woodblock print, usually titled “Princess Toyotama, Daughter of the Dragon King.” It depicts the birth revelation in dragon form. This is important Meiji reception, not evidence for ancient iconography.

There are no early canonical colors, weekday, number, planet, weapon, or plant assigned to her. Modern lists that supply such correspondences are recent devotional or occult invention unless they identify a shrine-specific practice.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

| Relation | Being | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| Father | Watatsumi/Ōwatatsumi; also called Toyotama-hiko in later systematization | *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720 |
| Sister | Tamayori-hime | *Kojiki*; *Nihon shoki* variants |
| Husband | Hoori, also Hikohohodemi, Yamasachi-hiko, “Luck of the Mountain” | *Kojiki*; *Nihon shoki* |
| Son | Ugayafukiaezu | *Kojiki*; *Nihon shoki* |
| Grandson | Kamu-Yamato-Iware-biko, later Jimmu, through Ugayafukiaezu and Tamayori-hime | dynastic genealogy in *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* |
| Husband’s father | Ninigi | chronicle genealogy; no direct meeting with Toyotama-hime |
| Husband’s brother | Hoderi/Umisachi-hiko | chronicle genealogy; no direct interaction with Toyotama-hime |
| Possible wider sea-god kin | Utsushi-hikana-saku and figures ancestral to maritime clans | genealogical inference; not consistently called her siblings |

### Every documented named divine interaction

- **Hoori / Hikohohodemi:** courtship, marriage, three-year cohabitation in the sea palace, separation after he violates her childbirth taboo, and exchange of songs. *Kojiki* (712); *Nihon shoki* main text and variants (720). **Tier: securely attested early/canonical, with variant redactions.**
- **Watatsumi / the Sea God:** she informs or is presented by her father; he recognizes Hoori and arranges the marriage. *Kojiki* (712); *Nihon shoki* (720). **Tier: securely attested early/canonical.**
- **Tamayori-hime:** Toyotama-hime brings or later sends her sister to care for Ugayafukiaezu and entrusts her with a song/message for Hoori. *Kojiki* (712); *Nihon shoki* variants (720). **Tier: securely attested early/canonical; details vary.**
- **Ugayafukiaezu:** she gives birth to him, names or participates in naming him in some recensions, leaves him on land, and arranges his care. *Kojiki* (712); *Nihon shoki* (720). **Tier: securely attested early/canonical.**
- **Jimmu / Kamu-Yamato-Iware-biko:** genealogical relationship only; no meeting or action between them is narrated. *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki*. **Tier: canonical genealogy; no interaction.**
- **Ninigi:** daughter-in-law relationship through Hoori; no documented meeting. **Absence of interaction.**
- **Hoderi/Umisachi-hiko:** brother-in-law relationship; he motivates Hoori’s journey by demanding his lost hook, but does not meet Toyotama-hime in the surviving early narratives. **Absence of direct interaction.**
- **Shiotsuchi/Shiotsutsuji:** he directs Hoori toward the sea palace, but no direct exchange with Toyotama-hime is recorded. **Absence of direct interaction.**
- **Amaterasu:** distant position within the constructed heavenly genealogy; no direct interaction. **Absence of interaction.**
- **Ryūjin or Otohime as separate named beings:** no early encounter or equation is narrated. Treating Toyotama-hime as “Otohime, daughter of Ryūjin” is later harmonization.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The lost fishhook and marriage in the sea palace

**Earliest source:** *Kojiki*, 712; parallel and variants in *Nihon shoki*, 720.

Hoderi, master of the sea’s luck, and Hoori, master of the mountain’s luck, exchange tools. Hoori loses Hoderi’s fishhook. Even after making many replacement hooks, he cannot satisfy his brother. Shiotsuchi places Hoori in a boat and sends him to the sea god’s palace.

At the palace well, Toyotama-hime’s maid discovers Hoori after his jewel adheres to her water vessel. Toyotama-hime comes out, sees him, and reports him to her father. The sea god recognizes him as the heavenly descendant, hosts him on layers of marine-animal skins and silk, and arranges the marriage. Hoori remains three years.

Toyotama-hime notices his sighing and informs her father. The sea god summons the fishes, discovers the missing hook, and instructs Hoori how to return it with a curse. He also gives Hoori the tide jewels with which to subdue Hoderi.

The marriage therefore joins the heavenly line to the sea realm while also giving Hoori the means to defeat his brother. Toyotama-hime participates in the discovery of Hoori’s distress, but the recovery of the hook and gift of the tide jewels are her father’s acts.

**Development:** Scholars commonly distinguish the international “lost fishhook” story from the animal-wife or forbidden-sight childbirth episode. Kokugakuin’s survey notes a theory that the marriage/birth genealogy was inserted into an older Hoori–Hoderi tale to connect the imperial line with a sea deity. This is reconstruction from narrative seams, not manuscript proof of a surviving earlier version.

### The forbidden-sight childbirth

**Earliest source:** *Kojiki*, 712; several *Nihon shoki* redactions, 720.

Toyotama-hime, pregnant after Hoori’s departure, comes to the seashore. She states that the offspring of a heavenly deity should not be born in the sea. Hoori constructs a birth hut roofed with cormorant feathers, but labor overtakes her before it is complete.

She explains that inhabitants of another land assume their homeland form when giving birth and orders Hoori not to look. He looks anyway. In the *Kojiki* she is an eight-fathom *wani*, crawling and writhing; in the *Nihon shoki* main account she becomes a dragon; alternative accounts use great *wani* forms. Hoori recoils. Toyotama-hime realizes she has been seen and is overcome by shame.

She had intended to keep the sea route open so that she could raise the child while passing between the two worlds. Instead she closes the sea boundary and returns. Continued love remains: she sends Tamayori-hime as nurse and conveys a poem praising the red jewels on the string while declaring Hoori himself more splendid. Hoori replies with a poem remembering the woman with whom he slept.

The story is therefore not simply a punishment of male curiosity or a revelation that the wife was secretly a monster. The text presents her nonhuman form as the lawful form of her native realm, identifies Hoori as the taboo breaker, and lets Toyotama-hime retain agency by closing the boundary.

### The “Melusine motif”

**Status:** modern comparative classification.

The tale belongs to an internationally distributed class of supernatural-spouse narratives: a man marries a woman from another realm, accepts a prohibition against seeing her under specified conditions, violates it, discovers an animal or monstrous form, and loses her; their descendants nevertheless prosper. European scholarship often calls this the “Melusine” type after the medieval French serpent-woman Mélusine.

The comparison is structurally useful but historically limited:

- The Toyotama episode is attested in 712 and 720, centuries before the familiar literary Mélusine texts.
- “Melusine motif” does not demonstrate transmission from medieval France to ancient Japan.
- Both may independently express a widely distributed forbidden-sight or animal-spouse pattern.
- A direct common origin remains unproved.
- Kokugakuin reports the further hypothesis that the motif was joined to the lost-fishhook story by court compilers to create an imperial sea-god genealogy. That remains a source-critical model rather than an attested editorial memorandum.

### Etiological functions

The narrative explains:

- Ugayafukiaezu’s name through the unfinished cormorant-feather roof;
- why sea and land are now separated;
- why the imperial line contains sea-divine ancestry;
- Tamayori-hime’s role as nurse and later mother of Jimmu;
- the endurance of emotional connection even after cosmic separation.

Proposed functions involving ancient exogamy, maritime-clan incorporation, birthing postures, water rituals, or female ritual authority are modern reconstructions with differing levels of plausibility.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Tenth-century evidence

The two Awa shrine names in the *Engishiki* are the strongest early institutional evidence. The register proves that shrines bearing Toyotama-hime’s name were recognized within the court ritual-administrative system by 927. It does not establish their legendary foundation dates or that their present successors occupy precisely the same sites.

### Toyotama-hime Shrine, Chiran, Kagoshima

The modern shrine at Chiran, Minamikyūshū, enshrines Toyotama-hime as principal deity alongside Hikohohodemi, Toyotama-hiko/Watatsumi, and Tamayori-hime.

Its own record says:

- Toyotama-hime and Tamayori-hime departed from the Ei/Kaimon area to rule Chiran and Kawanabe.
- Because the sisters took different routes or misunderstood their assigned destinations, Toyotama-hime came to Chiran.
- She governed and cared for the people, died there, and was enshrined by grateful residents.
- The shrine burned in the Tenshō era, 1573–1591.
- Shimazu Tadamitsu donated the present site in 1610.
- Rebuilding is traditionally recorded in the Ōei, Genki, 1610, and 1671 periods.
- The present sanctuary dates from 1939.
- It was formerly ranked a prefectural shrine under the modern shrine-ranking system.

These are shrine traditions and institutional records, not episodes found in the *Kojiki* or *Nihon shoki*. The local “death” and burial mound attributed to Toyotama-hime directly differ from the chronicles, in which she returns alive to the sea realm.

Present cult concerns include:

- safe childbirth;
- birth of a beautiful child;
- maritime safety and abundant fishing;
- agricultural prosperity;
- seasonal purification;
- local kagura and the water-powered puppet spectacle.

The shrine reports annual observances including spring rites, a June 5 rice-planting festival with a *Ta-no-kami* dance, summer *rokugatsudō* observances on July 9–10, and an autumn festival. Its water-powered puppet tradition existed by the late Edo period, lapsed during wartime, and was revived in 1979. This is local performing-arts history, not an ancient rite described in the chronicles.

The shrine also preserves an Indonesian-style *keris*. A 2026 study investigates its manufacture, date, and route of transmission. It should not be treated as evidence for an ancient Indonesian origin of Toyotama-hime’s cult.

### Udo Jingū, Miyazaki

Udo Jingū’s principal deity is Ugayafukiaezu, not Toyotama-hime, but the shrine identifies its coastal cave as the birth site. The cave’s “breast rock” and associated sweets express later maternal and infant-nurture traditions. The institution was historically a syncretic mountain and cave complex in which Buddhist and kami practice were intertwined; it received patronage from the Itō lords in the Edo period and became formally “Shinto” through Meiji-period separation.

The location is a powerful material localization of the myth, but the eighth-century chronicles do not name Udo Cave as the birthplace.

### Aoshima Shrine, Miyazaki

Aoshima Shrine worships Hikohohodemi, Toyotama-hime, and Shiotsuchi. Its present configuration localizes the return from the sea palace and treats all three as a cultic group. The co-enshrinement does not mean that the early texts narrate a meeting between Toyotama-hime and Shiotsuchi.

### Kagoshima-area shrines and competing identities

South Kyushu contains numerous localizations of the descent, sea-palace, childbirth, and Jimmu cycles. Their deity lists changed across time. Hirakiki/Hirasaki Shrine near Mount Kaimon, for example, has been associated in different records with Watatsumi, Sarutahiko, Kuni-no-Tokotachi, Toyotama-hime, Konohanasakuya-hime, and Amaterasu. Early-Meiji assignment of Toyotama-hime there is evidence for modern restructuring, not proof that she was its unchanged original deity.

Kagoshima Jingū and Kirishima Jingū place her within wider Hikohohodemi and imperial-descent cults, usually as a subsidiary or genealogically related deity. Claims that any one southern shrine is the uniquely authentic site of her marriage or childbirth are mutually competing sacred geographies.

### Other local cults

Shrines bearing her name or including her occur in Tokushima, Saga, Chiba, Kyoto, Miyazaki, Kagoshima, Tsushima, and elsewhere. Local benefits include water, fishing, navigation, marriage, fertility, childbirth, nursing, beautiful skin, and protection from aquatic animals.

At Ureshino in Saga, a Toyotama-hime shrine is associated with a sacred or protective catfish and, in modern tourism, beautiful-skin benefits connected to the hot springs. These are regional cult and commercial developments, not elements of the early myth.

### Ritual limits of the evidence

No documented Toyotama-hime cult involving human sacrifice, temple prostitution, ritual violence, or institutionalized sexual rites was found. Searches combining her name with sacrifice, *hitobashira*, persecution, and related terms produced no credible primary evidence. General practices attributed to “ancient Shinto” cannot be assigned to her without a local record.

## 7. Historical evolution of the cult and figure

### Before 712: reconstructable background, not direct attestation

The sea-palace, lost-hook, marine animal, taboo-wife, and divine-ancestor components probably circulated orally before the court chronicles. Maritime peoples and routes between Kyushu, the Inland Sea, the Korean peninsula, and the southern islands formed the cultural environment in which such stories were intelligible. No inscription, securely identified image, or datable pre-712 object names Toyotama-hime.

### 712–720: court canonization

The *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* place Toyotama-hime inside the divine ancestry of the Yamato ruling house. Their political importance lies less in a personal cult than in joining the heavenly descendant to the sea realm and furnishing Jimmu with doubly maritime ancestry.

The chronicles already disagree about her birthing form. The *Nihon shoki* itself preserves alternatives rather than suppressing them.

### Eighth–tenth centuries: textual repetition and institutional cult

The *Kogo shūi*, provincial fragments, the received *Kujiki*, and the *Engishiki* show that the name continued in court mythography and shrine geography. The Awa shrine entries constitute the clearest documentary cult evidence.

### Medieval period: shrine–temple complexes and Dragon Palace imagery

Toyotama-hime’s marine realm could be interpreted within Buddhist cosmology and dragon-king traditions. Sea gods, nāgas, dragon kings, Benzaiten, Kannon, and local water deities existed in overlapping cultic worlds, but no universal or exclusive *honji-suijaku* equation for Toyotama-hime has been established.

The sea palace increasingly resembled the Buddhist and East Asian **Ryūgū**, the Dragon Palace. This helps explain later dragon imagery and association with Otohime, but it is not evidence that the *Kojiki* already told the later Urashima story.

### Early modern period: philology, pilgrimage, and local elaboration

Local shrine legends expanded the sparse chronicle biography. In Chiran, Toyotama-hime became a civilizing local ruler who died and was buried in the district. At Udo, the cave, breast rock, and maternal cult provided tangible access to the birth story.

Motoori Norinaga’s *Kojiki-den* made close reading of the *Kojiki* central to National Learning. This increased the cultural authority of the *wani* version and of the divine genealogy while embedding it in a theory of an indigenous Japanese antiquity.

### 1868–1945: separation, shrine ranking, and imperial ideology

Beginning in March 1868, the Meiji state ordered the separation of kami and buddhas. Former shrine-temple complexes were reorganized, Buddhist images and terminology removed or reassigned, and official deity lists standardized. The process was coercive in many places and sometimes accompanied by destruction of Buddhist property.

Toyotama-hime’s dynastic position made her compatible with imperial genealogical education. Shrines associated with Hoori, Ugayafukiaezu, and Jimmu became stations in a sacred national geography. The Chiran shrine received prefectural rank and its present sanctuary was built in 1939, during the State Shinto period. Chikanobu’s 1886 print likewise belongs to a Meiji visual culture that popularized episodes of imperial-age mythology.

It would nevertheless be excessive to claim that Toyotama-hime herself was a central State Shinto deity comparable to Amaterasu, Jimmu, or the war dead. Her role was supporting and genealogical.

### 1945–present

The Allied-era disestablishment of State Shinto ended government administration of shrines as nonreligious state rites. Shrines entered the postwar religious-corporation system, many under the Association of Shinto Shrines, while local festivals and pilgrimage continued.

Toyotama-hime remains actively worshipped, especially for safe childbirth, children, water, fishing, navigation, and local welfare. This is institutional continuity through transformed legal and ritual systems, not an unchanged survival from prehistoric Japan.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Folklore and comparative study

Yanagita Kunio’s folklore movement and later scholars including Hori Ichirō encouraged reading chronicle myths alongside living regional narratives, spirit beliefs, and sacred geography. Yanagita’s *Tōno monogatari* does not supply an ancient Toyotama-hime episode; using it as direct evidence for her would be erroneous.

The classification of the tale as a “Melusine-type” supernatural-wife story is modern folkloristics. Japanese tale indices also connect it with the “lost fishhook” cycle. These systems identify morphology, not historical identity or direct borrowing.

### Modern Shinto historiography

The common description of Toyotama-hime as a “Shinto goddess” is serviceable for present practice but historically broad. Kuroda Toshio’s influential critique argued that “Shinto” as an autonomous, continuous religion distinct from Buddhism was substantially a modern construction; medieval kami cults belonged to a Buddhist–kami system. Breen and Teeuwen similarly emphasize institutional transformation rather than timeless continuity. Accordingly, medieval Toyotama worship should not automatically be imagined as modern shrine Shinto projected backward.

### Visual art

Chikanobu’s 1886 print transformed the birthing *wani* into a dramatic dragon, presenting Toyotama-hime as a beautiful courtly princess with a spectacular nonhuman revelation. This image has materially shaped internet and illustrated-book representations.

No securely documented Toyotama-hime entry occurs in Toriyama Sekien’s canonical yōkai catalogues. Modern “yōkai encyclopedia” treatment reflects the expansion of *yōkai* as a commercial umbrella for nearly all Japanese supernatural beings.

### Science

The fossil crocodylian genus **Toyotamaphimeia** was established by Ryōsuke Aoki in 1983 for the Japanese Pleistocene species now called *Toyotamaphimeia machikanensis*. The name deliberately invokes Toyotama-hime’s crocodilian interpretation. It is a modern scientific commemoration, not archaeological confirmation that the ancient word *wani* meant this fossil animal.

### Popular culture

Toyotama-derived names and characters occur in manga, anime, games, novels, tourism, and devotional art. Examples include a character named Toyotama in *Sekirei* and numerous “dragon princess” or sea-goddess adaptations. Similar names alone do not prove faithful adaptation of the chronicle deity.

Modern commercial images often add:

- a trident;
- fixed blue or turquoise colors;
- mermaid anatomy;
- ownership of tide jewels;
- rulership of all dragons;
- “divine feminine” or Jungian shadow symbolism;
- astrological correspondences;
- a universal beauty or skin-care portfolio.

These are modern creative or commercial constructions unless tied to a documented shrine tradition.

### Occult and neopagan claims

No substantial Toyotama-hime tradition was found in Renaissance Hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, classical Western grimoires, or pre-twentieth-century European occultism. Modern invocations in eclectic paganism, goddess spirituality, chaos magic, and online deity-work communities are contemporary adaptations.

No reliable statistics isolate Toyotama-hime worshippers from general shrine attendance. Claims of an ancient secret Toyotama mystery cult, unbroken hereditary witchcraft, or a historically fixed initiation system were not substantiated.

### Political appropriation

Her principal political use has been genealogical: the chronicles incorporate sea-divine ancestry into the legitimacy of the imperial line; Meiji and wartime education presented that genealogy within national history and morality. No distinctive contemporary extremist cult centered on Toyotama-hime was established.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### What is *wani*?

The oldest and most persistent dispute concerns 和邇/鰐.

- Aston and other early translators often used “crocodile” or “sea dragon.”
- Many Japanese interpreters favor shark on lexical and maritime grounds.
- Other scholars retain *wani* because its fabulous and zoological senses overlap.
- The existence of modern fossil crocodilians in Japan does not determine an eighth-century literary word.
- The *Nihon shoki*’s deliberate juxtaposition of dragon and *wani* shows that its compilers could distinguish but also associate the categories.

### Was the birth story original to the lost-hook tale?

One model treats the lost hook, visit to the underwater realm, tide jewels, marriage, and taboo childbirth as a single inherited story. Another finds compositional seams: the lost-hook tale can end with Hoori’s return and victory, while the marriage and childbirth extend it into an imperial genealogy. Kokugakuin notes the proposal that the Toyotama–Ugayafukiaezu generation was inserted between Hoori and Tamayori-hime to construct a fuller royal descent.

There is no surviving pre-redaction manuscript that settles the question.

### One goddess or two?

The chronicles call Toyotama-hime and Tamayori-hime sisters. Some modern scholars, including arguments discussed by Toshio Akima, see them as differentiated forms of a single water-maiden or priestess figure: the first bears and withdraws; the second receives, nurses, and continues the line. This structural hypothesis does not erase the textual fact that both chronicles distinguish them as separate named beings.

### Totemism and maritime peoples

Older comparativist scholarship sometimes treated Toyotama-hime’s *wani* form as proof of a crocodile-, shark-, or sea-animal-totem clan incorporated into Yamato genealogy. Critics note that “totemism” is often imported too mechanically and that no independent evidence identifies such a Toyotama clan organization. Maritime-clan context is plausible; a specific totemic institution is unproved.

### Birth posture and pollution

A minority interpretation suggests that “crawling/writhing like a *wani*” described an unfamiliar recumbent birth posture, contrasted with a Japanese squatting posture. Other readings emphasize birth pollution or protective seclusion. The chronicle itself says that she assumed her native form; it does not explain the scene sociologically.

### Dragon ideology

The *Nihon shoki* main text’s dragon may reflect Chinese literary and imperial symbolism. A 2024 study argues that presenting Toyotama-hime as a dragon endowed the future imperial line with a mark of sovereignty intelligible within a Sinitic political world. That argument fits the chronicle’s Literary Sinitic form but remains an interpretation of editorial intention.

### “Shinto” and continuity

Kuroda’s critique and later institutional histories warn against treating every kami narrative as evidence of a separate prehistoric religion called Shinto. Toyotama-hime’s record instead passes through:

1. court mythography;
2. provincial cults;
3. medieval Buddhist–kami institutions;
4. early-modern local and nativist interpretations;
5. Meiji separation and state ranking;
6. postwar voluntary shrine religion.

### State of the evidence

- Original eighth-century chronicle manuscripts are lost.
- The *Kojiki* depends on medieval manuscript witnesses, chiefly the 1371–1372 Shinpukuji manuscript.
- Early *Nihon shoki* fragments survive, but the complete textual tradition also rests on later copying.
- The relevant *Yamashiro fudoki* survives only in quotation.
- The *Engishiki* preserves shrine names but almost no local ritual detail.
- Shrine foundation stories are frequently first documented centuries after their claimed events.
- No ancient securely labeled image of Toyotama-hime has been identified.

### Negative findings from the dedicated contested-material pass

No credible evidence was found for:

- human sacrifice to Toyotama-hime;
- temple prostitution;
- a Toyotama-hime-specific cult war or persecution;
- a fixed Buddhist *honji* universally equated with her;
- an ancient occult initiation;
- appearance in Toriyama Sekien’s standard yōkai catalogues;
- a direct appearance in *Tōno monogatari*;
- a named poem in the *Man’yōshū*;
- a distinct canonical *Engishiki* norito addressed to her;
- direct equation with Otohime in the eighth-century texts;
- an unbroken prehistoric-to-modern cult with unchanged doctrine.

These are findings about the presently located record, not proof that no unedited local document can exist.
