# Tenjin (Sugawara no Michizane): Research Dossier

**Class:** Deified human; *goryō* 御霊 (“august” or dangerous dead spirit); *onryō* 怨霊 (resentful spirit); thunder, fire, plague, agricultural, literary, and tutelary kami; in medieval Buddhist systems, a Dharma-protecting *deva* and manifestation of Eleven-Headed Kannon.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Sugawara no Michizane is securely documented as a historical court scholar in ninth-century court records and in his own surviving writings. His posthumous agency is first reported retrospectively in the *Nihon kiryaku* 日本紀略 under 923, when popular opinion attributed Crown Prince Yasuakira’s death to “the spirit-soul of Master Sugawara”; the chronicle itself was compiled in the eleventh century from earlier court records. The earliest approximately contemporary text to present the dead Michizane explicitly as a celestial being is the account conventionally called *Dōken shōnin meido-ki* 道賢上人冥途記 or *Nichizō yume-ki* 日蔵夢記, set in 941 and preserved in the late-eleventh-century *Fusō ryakki*. There Michizane appears as **Nihon Dajō Itokuten** 日本太政威徳天, a formidable heavenly ruler. A cult at his Dazaifu grave is traditionally dated to 905; a shrine building there is recorded by shrine tradition for 919. The Kitano cult is securely established by the middle tenth century, conventionally founded in 947.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** contemporary or near-contemporary documentary, literary, architectural, or cultic evidence.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** a later source that became authoritative within Tenjin worship.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a local shrine, narrative, or ritual tradition not safely generalized.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** theological, antiquarian, courtly, or priestly ordering of earlier material.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** a historically datable equation of Tenjin with another religious figure or complex.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore:** orally transmitted or locally embedded legend, even when later committed to writing.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction.**
- **[MI] Modern invention or modern reframing.**

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Tenjin is not a prehistoric kami described in the *Kojiki* or *Nihon shoki*. He is the posthumously transformed identity of **Sugawara no Michizane** 菅原道真 (845–903), a court scholar, poet, official, and minister exiled to Dazaifu in 901. The historical sequence is unusually visible: condemned official; dangerous dead spirit blamed for elite deaths and public calamity; celestial avenger and thunder god; imperially sponsored tutelary deity; Buddhist protector and Kannon manifestation; patron of poetry and scholarship; finally the pre-eminent Japanese god of examinations and educational success.

### Principal names

- **Sugawara no Michizane** 菅原道真: historical personal name. *Sugawara* is the clan name, the particle *no* marking affiliation, and *Michizane* the personal name. “Tenjin” is not his birth name.
- **Kankō** 菅公, “Lord Sugawara”: reverential abbreviation widely used by shrines and later worshippers.
- **Kan Shōjō / Kan Jōshō** 菅丞相, “Chancellor Sugawara”: literary and theatrical title, especially familiar through *Sugawara denju tenarai kagami*.
- **Kanke** 菅家, “the Sugawara house”: an authorial designation attached to his writings and later to the corpus surrounding him.
- **Tenjin** 天神, literally “heavenly deity/deities.” Before and apart from Michizane, the word could designate celestial kami collectively. Its use for Michizane therefore reflects an appropriation and narrowing of an older category, not the transparent derivation of a unique personal name. [EA/LS]
- **Tenjin-sama** 天神様, the honorific popular form.
- **Tenman Tenjin** 天満天神 and **Tenman Daijizai Tenjin** 天満大自在天神: mature divine titles. Kitano’s institutional chronology assigns the imperial bestowal of *Tenman Daijizai Tenjin* to 987. *Daijizai-ten* 大自在天 is also the Japanese Buddhist name of Maheśvara/Śiva; the title consequently carried Buddhist-cosmological resonance, but this does not establish a simple identity “Tenjin = Śiva.” [SI/LS]
- **Tenman Daijizai Tenjin-gū** 天満大自在天神宮: cultic title of the shrine and deity.
- **Nihon Dajō Itokuten** 日本太政威徳天, approximately “Majestic-Deva of Japan’s Great Government”: the title given to Michizane in the 941 otherworld narrative. Variant transcriptions include *Nihon Dajō Ittokuten*. [EA, as a tenth-century narrative preserved later]
- **Karai Tenjin** 火雷天神, “Fire-Thunder Tenjin,” and related forms such as **Kaminari Tenjin** 雷天神: titles expressing his thunder and fire aspect. They belong to the fusion of Michizane’s angry spirit with older Kitano thunder worship. [LC/SI]
- **Tenman-gū no Tenjin**, “the Tenjin of the Tenman shrine,” and shrine-local forms such as Kitano Tenjin, Dazaifu Tenjin, Osaka Tenjin, and Yushima Tenjin.
- **Bunten / god of letters**, **gakumon no kami** 学問の神 (“kami of learning”), **shodō no kami** 書道の神 (“kami of calligraphy”), and patron of *kanshi*, *waka*, and linked verse: functional descriptions that became titles. The 986 prayer by Yoshishige no Yasutane calls him **“ancestor of the literary way and lord of the realm of poetry”** (*bundō no so, shikyō no shu* 文道之祖、詩境之主). This is an important early sign that the benevolent literary god was already emerging within a century of Michizane’s death. [EA]
- **Tōtō Tenjin** 渡唐天神, “Tenjin Who Crossed to Tang/China”: the medieval Zen form in which Tenjin travels miraculously to China and receives instruction or transmission from the Chan master Wuzhun Shifan. The story is impossible as ordinary chronology—Wuzhun lived 1177–1249—but functions as a deliberately transhistorical religious legend. [MA/SI]
- **Tenjin no honji** 天神の本地: “the original ground of Tenjin,” both a theological expression and the title of later medieval vernacular narratives recasting Michizane’s earthly life as a divine-incarnation story. [MA]

### Classification shifts

1. **903–c. 930:** deceased, disgraced courtier whose spirit might retaliate. [EA]
2. **By 923 in retrospective court historiography:** named resentful spirit causing princely death. [EA, source compiled later]
3. **941 narrative:** celestial ruler with enormous supernatural retinue and power over calamity. [EA/LC]
4. **Mid-tenth century:** enshrined *goryō* and tutelary kami at Kitano and Dazaifu. [EA]
5. **Late tenth–eleventh centuries:** court-protecting and literature-governing Tenjin. [EA/LS]
6. **Medieval period:** Buddhist Dharma protector, Kannon manifestation, thunder/fire/plague/agricultural and martial deity. [SI/MA]
7. **Early modern period:** nationwide patron of poetry, calligraphy, schools, neighborhood confraternities, and popular theatrical culture. [MA]
8. **Modern period:** predominantly “god of learning” and examinations, though the frightening thunder-god layer remains visible in art and legend. [MI as emphasis, not as wholesale invention]

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Historical substrate

Michizane’s earthly career is unusually well documented for a deified person. His writings survive principally in:

- **Kanke bunsō** 菅家文草, a collection of Chinese-language prose and poetry compiled in Michizane’s lifetime. [EA]
- **Kanke kōshū** 菅家後集, poems associated especially with exile at Dazaifu. [EA, although textual transmission requires critical treatment]
- Court histories and administrative records concerning his appointments, embassy policy, promotion, accusation, exile, death, and posthumous restoration.
- Poetry anthologies preserving works attributed to him.

Robert Borgen’s critical study remains the most substantial English reconstruction of the historical Michizane and devotes a separate chapter to his transformation into Tenjin. Borgen emphasizes the ambiguity of later terminology: documents may call the same figure “Michizane” or “Tenjin” without implying two distinct entities.

### The first posthumous records

#### The *Nihon kiryaku* notice under 923

The chronicle says that when Crown Prince Yasuakira died, “the whole world said” that the event was the work of Master Sugawara’s spirit acting from old resentment. The wording records a circulating attribution rather than an official finding. The same annalistic sequence records the cancellation of the 901 condemnation, restoration of Michizane’s former office of Minister of the Right, and award of senior second rank. [EA]

This is evidence that by the time represented in the record, political restoration and fear of the dead man’s resentment were linked. It is not contemporary proof that every subsequent disaster was immediately attributed to him.

#### The Seiryōden lightning strike, 930

On 26 June 930 in the traditional calendar, lightning struck the Seiryōden palace building, killing and injuring courtiers. The *Nihon kiryaku* and *Fusō ryakki* record the catastrophe. Crucially, their notices do **not** explicitly say that Michizane caused it. The familiar statement “Michizane’s ghost struck the palace” is the interpretation consolidated by subsequent Tenjin literature, not the wording of the basic annalistic notice. [EA for the lightning; LC for attribution]

The later *Kitano Tenjin engi* explains the strike as the work of **Karai Kaki Dokuō** 火雷火気毒王, “Fire-Thunder-Fire-Energy Poison King,” third among Tenjin’s 168,000 attendants. Thus even the mature legend does not always depict Michizane personally throwing the bolt; it can assign the act to his divine retinue. [LC]

#### *Dōken shōnin meido-ki* / *Nichizō yume-ki*, episode dated 941

This otherworld-journey narrative says that the ascetic Dōken, later called Nichizō, died or entered a deathlike state at Mount Kinpu and returned after touring the other world. Guided by divine beings and meeting Zaō Bosatsu, he encounters the dead Michizane as Nihon Dajō Itokuten. Tenjin appears as a sovereign with an immense retinue, explains the roots of Japan’s calamities, and is implicated in Emperor Daigo’s punishment in hell. [EA/LC]

The text survives through later compilations, notably the *Fusō ryakki*, so “941” is the date of its narrated revelation and probable early formation, not the date of an extant autograph manuscript. The narrative is nevertheless earlier than the surviving Kitano painted legends and already presents a fully celestial Michizane.

### Shrine-foundation records

Several texts transmit competing foundation traditions:

- **Kitano engi / Kitano founding record:** foregrounds the female medium **Tajihi no Ayako** 多治比文子, who received a revelation in 942 and worshipped Michizane in a small household shrine before the cult moved to Kitano in 947. [LC, containing plausible tenth-century institutional memory]
- **Saichin ki-mon** 最鎮記文 and related traditions: foreground the child Tarōmaru, son of the Hira shrine priest Miwa no Yoshitane, and the monk Saichin; in this lineage Ayako may be absent. The difference probably reflects rival institutional claims over the shrine’s foundation and administration. [RV/LS]
- **Tenmangū takusen-ki** 天満宮託宣記: a collection of Tenjin oracles, important for the authority claimed through spirit possession. [LC]
- **Dazaifu traditions:** say that Umasake no Yasuyuki placed a shrine over Michizane’s grave in 905 and that a larger structure replaced it in 919. These dates are institutionally traditional; the present Dazaifu main sanctuary dates from 1591. [LC with architectural confirmation for the later building]

### *Kitano Tenjin engi*

The *Kitano Tenjin engi* 北野天神縁起 is the central canonical narrative complex.

- A prose text existed by **1194**.
- Kenkyū-era and Kenpō-era textual recensions survive without illustrations.
- The celebrated **Jōkyū version**, dated about **1219**, is an eight-scroll painted handscroll set at Kitano Tenmangū; an unfinished ninth scroll is also associated with the project.
- Its initiation or calligraphy has been attributed to the Tendai abbot Jien, but shrine Buddhist clerics are also proposed as the effective producers. Attribution is disputed, not settled.
- More than thirty recensions survive. They divide into textual lineages and frequently add local foundation legends or miracles.
- Important examples include the Kōan version (1278 according to Kitano and museum cataloguing), Tsuda version (1298), Matsuzaki/Hōfu version (1311), Egara version (1319), and later Mitsunobu and Edo-period copies.

The cycle recounts Michizane’s life, accusation, exile, death, vengeance, revelations, the establishment of Kitano, otherworld punishment, and miracles. The Jōkyū paintings supplied the enduring images of Tenjin as a red, horned thunder deity amid black clouds and of terrified courtiers fleeing palace lightning. [LC]

### Later textual and visual corpus

- **Honchō monzui** 本朝文粋, eleventh-century anthology: preserves courtly prayers and writings important to the transition from feared spirit to patron of letters. [EA]
- **Fusō ryakki** 扶桑略記, late eleventh century: preserves the Dōken/Nichizō otherworld episode and annalistic material. [EA/LC]
- **Konjaku monogatari-shū**, early twelfth century: belongs to the broader world of karmic and supernatural tale literature in which dangerous dead, divine punishment, and Buddhist causality made the Tenjin story intelligible. It is not, however, the principal primary biography of Tenjin. [Context]
- **Tenjin kōshiki** 天神講式, medieval liturgical texts used in ritual praise; a dated example survives from 1356, with other copies from the fifteenth century. [MA/SI]
- **Tenjin no honji**, medieval *otogi-zōshi*: adapts the Kitano narrative into a vernacular divine-life story, increasing *waka* and reducing the classical Chinese poetry characteristic of learned Michizane traditions. [MA]
- **Tōtō Tenjin texts and paintings:** Zen-centered narratives and icons, especially from the later medieval period. [MA/SI]
- **Sugawara denju tenarai kagami**, written by Takeda Izumo II, Miyoshi Shōraku, and Namiki Sōsuke; first performed as puppet theatre at Osaka’s Takemoto-za in 1746 and shortly afterward in kabuki. It substitutes “Kan Shōjō” for Michizane and freely dramatizes historical and divine material. It is a major source for popular memory, not evidence for tenth-century events. [MA]
- **Early-modern shrine chronicles, guidebooks, prints, devotional images, poetry offerings, and Tenjin confraternity records:** evidence for popular expansion and iconographic standardization. [MA]
- **State and postwar shrine publications:** evidence for modern institutional self-presentation, not neutral transcripts of medieval tradition. [MI/modern documentary evidence]

### Sources named in the general Japanese pantheon brief but not primary Tenjin texts

The *Kojiki* (712), *Nihon shoki* (720), provincial *Fudoki*, *Man’yōshū*, *Kogo shūi* (807), and the *Engishiki* (927) all precede or stand too close to Michizane’s death to contain the developed Michizane–Tenjin cult. They are indispensable for earlier Japanese categories of kami, thunder, ritual, and court religion, but they do not attest Tenjin as the deified Michizane. Philippi’s *Kojiki* and Aston’s *Nihongi* translations therefore provide comparative background only.

Toriyama Sekien’s yōkai catalogues, Motoori Norinaga’s *Kojiki-den*, Hirata Atsutane’s writings, and Yanagita Kunio’s *Tōno monogatari* likewise do not constitute the primary Tenjin corpus. They belong respectively to early-modern classification, *kokugaku*, modern folklore formation, and the historiography of Japanese religion. Treating every famous Japanese religious book as evidence for every kami would manufacture a false antiquity.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains in historical sequence

1. **Vengeance and redress:** disasters were attributed to the spirit of a wronged minister. The injustice of his exile later made Tenjin a divine redresser of false accusations. [EA/LC]
2. **Thunder and fire:** palace lightning and Kitano’s older thunder associations made this one of his earliest supernatural domains. [LC/SI]
3. **Plague and calamity:** as a *goryō*, Tenjin participated in the wider logic by which angry elite dead caused epidemic disease, drought, and political disorder unless ritually pacified. [EA/LS]
4. **Protection of the capital and court:** after appeasement, the destructive outsider became a guardian of the very polity believed to have wronged him. Kitano entered the elite shrine system and received imperial patronage. [EA]
5. **Rain and agriculture:** the thunder god’s command of weather was reinterpreted positively as control of rain, crops, and fertility. This function followed from the thunder complex rather than from Michizane’s human profession. [SI/MA]
6. **Chinese poetry, *waka*, calligraphy, linked verse, and literary culture:** based on Michizane’s documented learning and poetic achievement. The 986 prayer supplies early evidence; medieval poetry offerings made it institutionally central. [EA/MA]
7. **Scholarship and examinations:** the contemporary defining domain. Students pray for admission and examination success, buy amulets, and leave wooden *ema* 絵馬 bearing written petitions. This is an extension of the literary god into mass education and modern competitive examinations. [MA/MI]
8. **Children, sincerity, protection from misfortune, health, and longevity:** promoted by particular modern shrines and not uniformly primary across the cult. [RV/MI]
9. **Martial protection:** medieval materials sometimes treat Tenjin as a martial and state-protecting deity; this is secondary to the vengeance and court-guardian complex. [MA]

### Principal visual forms

#### Court scholar

Tenjin appears as a grave seated or standing court noble in formal robes, often holding a **shaku** 笏, the flat ceremonial baton. He may wear a black court cap and sit frontally on a dais. Plum branches, calligraphy implements, or an ox may identify him. This became the standard benevolent image of the scholar-god. [LC/MA]

#### Thunder demon or wrathful deity

In the Jōkyū *Kitano Tenjin engi*, he or his power appears as a red or dark-red horned thunder being, moving through black clouds while lightning and fire strike the palace. This is not a marginal “yōkai” corruption but an authoritative medieval shrine image of Tenjin’s punitive form. [LC]

#### Tōtō Tenjin

The “China-crossing” Tenjin is generally shown as a scholar or Zen-associated figure, sometimes in Chinese-style dress and carrying or accompanied by a plum branch. The icon makes Tenjin a patron and transmitter of transnational literary and Zen culture. [MA/SI]

### Emblems and sacred associations

- **Plum blossom, ume** 梅: the dominant plant emblem. Michizane’s own poetry and remembered love of plum blossoms ground the association. [EA/LC]
- **Tobi-ume** 飛梅, the “flying plum”: the tree supposedly uprooted itself and flew from Kyoto to Dazaifu because it longed for its master. This is shrine legend, not a contemporary report. [F/LC]
- **Pine and cherry:** in one familiar exile legend, the pine follows but stops en route, while the cherry withers in grief. Versions vary. [F/MA]
- **Ox:** Tenjin shrines commonly display recumbent ox statues, which worshippers rub for healing or intelligence. Explanations include Michizane’s birth in an ox year, death on an ox day, affection for cattle, an ox halting his funeral cart and thereby choosing his grave, or the older Chinese-influenced cult described as involving ox sacrifice. No single explanation is demonstrably original. [LC/F/LS]
- **Umebachi** 梅鉢, “plum-bowl” crest: emblem of Tenjin shrines, with local variants such as the Kaga umebachi. [MA]
- **Writing brush, books, scrolls, inkstone, and calligraphy:** attributes of the learned god. [MA]
- **Lightning, thundercloud, and fire:** signs of the wrathful form. [LC]
- **The twenty-fifth day:** Michizane’s death is traditionally dated to the twenty-fifth day of the second month; shrine observances consequently cluster on the twenty-fifth, especially monthly markets and plum festivals. [LC/MA]
- **Color and number:** red belongs to the wrathful painted body; black clouds to the thunder epiphany. Claims that Tenjin has one universally sacred modern color or numerological system cannot be securely established.

### Powers and limits

Tenjin’s texts grant him power to cause or command lightning, fire, disease, political deaths, drought, and other calamities; to deliver oracles through mediums; punish slanderers; protect the state; inspire learning and poetry; and grant successful petitions. His limitations are cultic rather than metaphysical: ritual recognition, restoration of rank, shrine establishment, Buddhist rites, and offerings redirect wrath into protection. This structure—injury, uncontrolled posthumous force, recognition, pacification, beneficial guardianship—is characteristic of *goryō* cult, though Tenjin’s literary specialization is distinctive.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Human family transformed into cultic relations

Michizane was born into the scholarly Sugawara lineage, son of **Sugawara no Koreyoshi**. Medieval legends sometimes replace ordinary birth with a miraculous appearance as a child in his father’s garden, assimilating him to Buddhist sacred-biography patterns. [EA for historical family; LC for miraculous birth]

At Kitano, the principal sanctuary enshrines:

- **Sugawara no Michizane**
- **Chūjō-dono** 中将殿, identified by the shrine as Michizane’s son, commonly associated with Sugawara no Takami
- **Kichijō-nyo** 吉祥女, identified by the shrine as Michizane’s principal wife

These are shrine classifications, and Kichijō-nyo should not be silently equated with the Buddhist goddess Kisshōten merely because the names look similar. Kitano also enshrines Michizane’s mother at Tomouji-sha. [LC/RV]

### Divine hierarchy

In the 941 otherworld narrative, Michizane has become a mighty heavenly sovereign with an immense retinue. Later Kitano texts give him 168,000 attendants. In Buddhist interpretation he becomes both a *deva* protecting the Dharma and the local manifestation of a bodhisattva. In court ritual he becomes a guardian of the capital and one of the elite shrines supported by the polity. These are different hierarchical systems, not a single consistent pantheon.

### Every documented divine interaction

Because sources use “interaction,” “manifestation,” and “retainer” differently, equations are separated from narrated encounters.

- **Zaō Bosatsu / Zaō Gongen** 蔵王菩薩・蔵王権現 — Dōken’s guide or interlocutor announces or mediates the encounter with Michizane as Nihon Dajō Itokuten in the *Dōken shōnin meido-ki*, episode dated 941, preserved in *Fusō ryakki*. **[EA/LC: narrated divine encounter]**
- **Shukongōjin** 執金剛神, Vajra-bearing protector — guides Dōken through the other world toward encounters including Tenjin’s court; *Dōken shōnin meido-ki*, 941 narrative. **[EA/LC: shared narrative sequence]**
- **The Twenty-Eight Attendants** 二十八部衆 — encountered in Dōken’s otherworld journey before the appearance of Tenjin; same source. They are not individually shown conversing with Tenjin. **[EA/LC: joint narrative, direct interaction unestablished]**
- **Karai Kaki Dokuō** 火雷火気毒王 — third divine messenger among Tenjin’s 168,000 retainers; causes the Seiryōden lightning strike on Tenjin’s behalf in *Kitano Tenjin engi*. **[LC: subordinate-agent interaction]**
- **Fire-Thunder deity of Kitano** 火雷神 — Michizane’s spirit was historically fused with the pre-existing thunder complex at Kitano. This is a cultic conflation rather than a surviving myth in which the two meet. **[SI/MSR]**
- **Eleven-Headed Kannon** 十一面観音 — identified as Tenjin’s *honji*, or original Buddhist ground. The relationship structures many *Kitano Tenjin engi* recensions; some scroll cycles use thirty-three episodes by analogy with Kannon’s thirty-three saving forms. **[SI/LS]**
- **Maheśvara / Daijizai-ten** 大自在天 — Tenjin’s title *Tenman Daijizai Tenjin* invokes the Buddhist title “Great Sovereign Deva.” The titulary affinity is documented, but a myth of Tenjin meeting Śiva is not. **[SI/LS; no narrated interaction]**
- **Brahmā, Indra, or other major Buddhist gods:** no securely identified, discrete Tenjin episode was established in the sources consulted. Generic celestial retinues should not be converted into named meetings.
- **Amaterasu, Susanoo, Ōkuninushi, Inari, Hachiman, and the classical *Kojiki/Nihon shoki* kami:** no early mythic encounter with Tenjin is attested. Later co-enshrinement or modern fictional meetings do not establish traditional interaction.
- **Daigo Tennō’s posthumous spirit:** the 941 otherworld narrative places the dead emperor in infernal punishment connected with Michizane’s grievance. Daigo is a deceased emperor, not independently a kami in that text; the episode is therefore a posthumous human-spirit relation, not strictly a god–god meeting. **[EA/LC]**

### Absence as a finding

Tenjin has no securely attested divine parents, divine consort, divine offspring, mount, or sibling group comparable to the genealogies of the *Kojiki*. His cult contains enshrined human relatives and Buddhist equivalences, but later visual or popular adjacency must not be converted into ancient genealogy.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. Exile, death, and the making of an angry spirit

**Early historical core:** In 901 Michizane was removed from office and sent to Dazaifu as *Dazai gon-no-sochi*, an effective exile. Court tradition blamed accusations associated with Fujiwara no Tokihira, including an alleged plot concerning succession. Michizane died at Dazaifu in 903. [EA]

**Legendary elaboration:** Later narratives make Michizane the wholly innocent victim of Tokihira’s slander and present his exile as a moral crime that destabilizes heaven and earth. His human lament becomes the motivation for divine vengeance. [LC]

**Development:** The sharp moral opposition—perfectly loyal scholar versus treacherous Fujiwara villain—is clearer in shrine legend and theatre than in the politically complex surviving court record. Borgen’s reconstruction cautions against treating the complete exonerating legend as a contemporary transcript.

### B. Calamities and the palace lightning

After Michizane’s death, Tokihira died in 909; Crown Prince Yasuakira died in 923; other politically significant deaths and disasters followed. The *Nihon kiryaku* reports that Yasuakira’s death was popularly said to be the work of Sugawara’s resentful spirit. In 930 lightning struck the Seiryōden, killing courtiers including Fujiwara no Kiyotsura. [EA]

The later canonical story unites these otherwise separate events into one chain of revenge. The Jōkyū scrolls show a wrathful thunder deity descending amid clouds and panic. Some versions assign the lightning to Karai Kaki Dokuō, Tenjin’s messenger. [LC]

**Critical distinction:** the lightning is historical; its attribution to Michizane is a historical belief whose clearest narrative form is later. The annalistic lightning notice itself does not name him.

### C. Court restoration and appeasement

In 923 the state revoked the exile decree, restored Michizane’s office, and awarded posthumous rank. Later, he received further promotions: senior first rank, Minister of the Left, and eventually Chancellor/Great Minister of State in 993. [EA]

The cult reads these measures as appeasement. Politically, they also restored the honor of the Sugawara lineage and reinscribed a dangerous victim into the court order. The deity’s “promotion” parallels the dead official’s bureaucratic promotion.

### D. Dōken’s journey to the other world

In the narrative set in 941, the ascetic Dōken dies or enters trance on Mount Kinpu, tours the afterlife, encounters Buddhist protectors and Zaō, and meets Michizane as Nihon Dajō Itokuten. Tenjin rules from a magnificent stronghold with a vast divine following. Emperor Daigo suffers for the injustice done to Michizane. The narrative explains national calamity in Buddhist karmic and political terms and recommends ritual remediation. [EA/LC]

This is the earliest full mythic cosmology of Tenjin. It already moves beyond a wandering ghost: he is a heavenly monarch whose wrath and salvation operate on a national scale.

### E. The oracles of Ayako and Tarōmaru; foundation of Kitano

One account says that in 942 Tenjin possessed or spoke through **Tajihi no Ayako**, a girl or female medium of Kyoto’s western wards, asking to be worshipped at the Right Guard’s Riding Ground at Kitano. Unable initially to construct a great sanctuary, she made a small shrine near her home. [LC]

A second revelation came through **Tarōmaru**, the young son of the Hira shrine priest **Miwa no Yoshitane**. With the cooperation of the monk Saichin and others, the spirit was installed at Kitano in 947. [LC]

Competing foundation documents distribute authority differently. One centers the low-status female medium; another marginalizes or omits her and privileges male priestly and monastic founders. The discrepancy is evidence of institutional contest, not an error to be harmonized. [RV/LS]

Fujiwara no Morosuke sponsored enlargement of the shrine in 959. This is an important political reversal: the Fujiwara house, associated in legend with Michizane’s destruction, became a patron of his cult.

### F. The flying plum

On leaving Kyoto, Michizane is said to have addressed his beloved plum tree:

> When the east wind blows, send me your fragrance, plum blossoms; do not forget spring because your master is absent.

The poem is transmitted as Michizane’s, but the story that the tree physically flew to Dazaifu is later legend. The *Tobi-ume* becomes proof of loyal attachment between cultivated nature and the exiled scholar. [EA for poetic tradition; F/LC for flight]

Variants add a pine that follows only partway and a cherry that withers. These personified-tree episodes explain the botanical landscape of Tenjin shrines and dramatize loyalty, abandonment, and exile. [F]

### G. The ox chooses the grave

According to Dazaifu tradition, the ox drawing Michizane’s funeral cart stopped and refused to proceed. The attendants took this as a sign and buried him where the animal halted; Dazaifu Tenmangū stands over that grave. [LC/F]

Other explanations of the ox association invoke birth or death calendrics, Michizane’s compassion toward cattle, or inherited thunder/agricultural symbolism. These are competing etiologies. The resting ox is an established icon; its single original explanation is not recoverable.

### H. The pomegranate and the monk Son’i

In a dramatic medieval episode, Michizane’s ghost confronts the cleric **Son’i**. He spits out a pomegranate seed that bursts into flame; Son’i extinguishes it with a water-sprinkling mudra. The spirit then manifests as a dark-red horned thunder deity and fills the palace with black smoke. [LC]

The episode does not simply oppose “Shinto god” and Buddhist monk. It dramatizes contest and accommodation within a shared ritual world: Buddhist power can check divine fire, while Tenjin is subsequently incorporated as a protector within Buddhist cosmology.

### I. Tenjin as Eleven-Headed Kannon

Medieval theology interpreted Tenjin as the local *suijaku* or trace-manifestation of **Jūichimen Kannon**. Some illustrated legends are organized into thirty-three sections, echoing Kannon’s thirty-three saving forms. [SI/LS]

This identification transforms a vengeful ghost into a compassionate savior without erasing the wrathful form; wrath becomes an expedient means. The equation was neither prehistoric nor inevitable. It arose within the medieval shrine-temple complex and was materially expressed in icons, sutras, halls, relic devotion, and clerical ritual.

### J. Tōtō Tenjin

Tenjin travels to China in a single night and seeks instruction from the Southern Song Chan master **Wuzhun Shifan**, sometimes on the advice of the Japanese Zen master **Enni**. Wuzhun recognizes or instructs him and grants a robe or sign of transmission. [MA/SI]

The impossible chronology is part of the genre’s point: the Japanese god of literature transcends time and geography to join the lineage of Chinese civilization and Zen. The legend strengthened Tenjin’s authority among medieval literati and Zen communities.

### K. *Sugawara denju tenarai kagami*

The 1746 puppet play, soon adapted to kabuki, dramatizes Michizane’s fall as the story of Kan Shōjō, his calligraphic transmission, loyal retainers, threatened heirs, substitutions, sacrifice, and supernatural vengeance. Famous sections include *Kurumabiki* and *Terakoya*. [MA]

Its emotional center—loyalty tested through the sacrifice of children and retainers—belongs to early-modern theatre. These episodes must not be cited as Heian evidence, but the play profoundly shaped the public Tenjin: wronged master, calligraphic genius, righteous avenger, and focus of costly loyalty.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Dazaifu Tenmangū

Dazaifu is both grave and shrine. Institutional tradition dates a small shrine over the grave to 905, erected by Umasake no Yasuyuki, and a larger sanctuary to 919. The present main sanctuary was reconstructed in 1591. Warfare, fire, and rebuilding repeatedly altered the complex. [LC/EA for surviving building]

The shrine identifies itself as a principal center of Tenjin worship and reports more than 12,000 affiliated or dedicated Tenjin shrines nationwide. It receives millions of visitors, particularly students. These institutional numbers are valuable evidence of modern scale but should be understood as shrine-reported figures.

Important practices include:

- prayer for academic achievement and examination success;
- purchase of amulets;
- written *ema* petitions;
- plum offerings and appreciation;
- rubbing ox statues;
- the **Jinkō-shiki** procession from the shrine to Enoki-sha, traditionally founded in 1101 by Ōe no Masafusa;
- **Oni-sube**, a fire and demon-expulsion rite;
- **Kyokusui-no-en**, a water-course poetry gathering.

The annual procession ritually revisits Michizane’s exile and temporary residence while praying for peace and agricultural abundance. [LC/MA]

### Kitano Tenmangū

Kitano’s cult formed around oracles in 942 and 946/947 and the installation of 947. Fujiwara no Morosuke enlarged the buildings in 959. By 987 Tenjin had received the title Tenman Daijizai Tenjin and an imperially sponsored Kitano festival. Kitano entered the official shrine hierarchy, became one of the Twenty-Two Shrines by the late eleventh century, and received an imperial visit in 1004. [EA]

The present main sanctuary is a major example of the architectural form later called *gongen-zukuri*. The shrine’s treasures include:

- the Jōkyū *Kitano Tenjin engi emaki*;
- later illustrated recensions;
- sutras and reliquaries;
- medieval and early-modern sacred images;
- poetry and linked-verse offerings;
- shrine archives and clerical diaries.

Before 1868 Kitano was a Buddhist–kami institution. Tendai clerics and shrine monks participated in governance and ritual; Manshu-in exercised the office of *bettō*; Buddhist halls, pagodas, bells, sutras, relics, and Kannon images stood within the precinct. Calling this simply a “Shinto shrine” without qualification imposes the post-Meiji settlement backward. [EA/MSR]

### Osaka Tenmangū and Tenjin Matsuri

Osaka tradition dates the shrine’s establishment to 949 and the origin of its festival to 951. The **Tenjin Matsuri** developed into a major urban festival featuring:

- *hokonagashi*, the casting or floating of a sacred halberd to determine a ritual place;
- land procession;
- transfer of the divine spirit to a portable shrine;
- river procession;
- fire, lanterns, music, theatre, and modern fireworks.

Boat forms expanded in the early modern commercial city; festival-supporting *kō* confraternities and elaborate welcoming figures flourished from the eighteenth century. The festival suffered interruptions during late-Tokugawa conflict and the world wars; its boat procession resumed in 1949 and changed route in 1953 because of subsidence and bridge clearance. [LC/MA/modern documentary history]

### Other centers and expansion

Prominent shrines include Hōfu Tenmangū, Egara Tenjinsha at Kamakura, Yushima Tenmangū and Kameido Tenjinsha in Edo/Tokyo, and numerous local Sugawara, Tenjin, and Tenmangū shrines. Claims about a fixed set of “Three Great Tenjin Shrines” vary because there is no universally accepted third member. [RV]

The spread was facilitated by:

- branch enshrinement and divided spirits;
- local foundation legends modeled on the Kitano cycle;
- patronage by courtiers, warriors, urban guilds, and schools;
- Tenjin confraternities;
- illustrated-scroll recitation and display;
- shrine networks and pilgrimage;
- poetry, calligraphy, and educational institutions.

### Priests, monks, and shrine government

Medieval and early-modern Kitano was administered by a layered shrine-temple establishment. The Tendai-associated *bettō* supervised the complex; shrine monks performed rites; Sugawara descendants occupied hereditary ritual offices. This institutional Buddhism was not ornamental. It helped create the canonical Tenjin through doctrine, narrative, art, relic cult, and liturgy. [EA]

The Meiji government’s prohibition of hereditary priestly offices and reclassification of shrines disrupted these structures. At Kitano, the effect of the 1871 ban on hereditary shrine offices and subsequent personnel changes was substantial.

### Offerings, prayer, and material devotion

Historically documented practices include:

- court offerings and imperial messengers;
- Buddhist sutra copying and deposition;
- relic veneration;
- *kōshiki* praise liturgies;
- poetry and linked-verse offerings;
- processions and portable shrines;
- prayers for rain, protection, redress, and learning;
- amulets and written petitions;
- *ema*, including modern examination requests;
- offerings of plum-related foods and local specialties;
- monthly worship on the twenty-fifth.

No credible evidence located in this research demonstrates that Michizane’s Tenjin cult practiced human sacrifice or temple prostitution. One Kokugakuin summary notes that a Chinese deity involved in the syncretic formation of Tenman Tenjin received sacrificed oxen. That is evidence invoked in reconstructing the ox/thunder complex, not proof of human sacrifice at Kitano or Dazaifu.

### Cult violence and coercion

The cult’s foundational stories are explicitly violent: the god kills or punishes through disease, fire, lightning, and political catastrophe. Medieval institutions also possessed land, wealth, armed connections, and political influence. However, no source consulted establishes a distinctive Tenjin priesthood systematically conducting coercive conversion or human sacrifice. The major documented suppression affecting the cult was instead the nineteenth-century dismantling of its Buddhist components.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### 845–903: the scholar and official

Michizane’s historical learning, Chinese poetry, service under Emperor Uda, rapid promotion, and dramatic fall supplied the raw material for deification. Nothing securely shows that he was worshipped as a god while alive. [EA]

### 903–930: grave cult, rumor, and political restoration

A grave shrine at Dazaifu was established according to traditions beginning in 905. Elite deaths and disasters were attributed to Michizane’s grievance. His official rehabilitation in 923 shows that the court considered the political memory dangerous or important enough to reverse. The 930 lightning strike became the decisive visual and narrative event only through later interpretation. [EA/LC]

### 930s–950s: formation of the celestial and Kitano Tenjin

The 941 otherworld narrative makes Michizane a heavenly sovereign. Mediumistic revelations in the 940s produced competing claims about who founded Kitano. Installation in 947 and Fujiwara sponsorship in 959 established an institutional cult in the capital. [EA/LC]

This phase is not adequately described as a merely spontaneous folk cult or purely top-down court invention. It joined popular rumor, spirit-medium revelation, monastic narrative, Sugawara lineage interests, Fujiwara patronage, and imperial anxiety.

### Late tenth–eleventh centuries: from enemy to guardian

The 986 description “ancestor of the literary way, lord of poetry” demonstrates early literary patronage. Imperial titulature in 987, inclusion among officially supported shrines, posthumous promotion in 993, imperial pilgrimage in 1004, and inclusion among the Twenty-Two Shrines show full absorption into the state-protecting order. [EA]

The transformation did not erase wrath. Beneficial power was the controlled form of the same terrifying efficacy.

### Twelfth–fourteenth centuries: canonical narrative and Buddhist systematization

The prose *Kitano Tenjin engi* took shape by 1194; the Jōkyū handscrolls followed around 1219. Numerous recensions spread through regional Tenjin shrines. [LC]

During this period:

- Tenjin was mapped into *honji-suijaku* theology;
- Eleven-Headed Kannon became his dominant *honji*;
- liturgies, sutra offerings, relic worship, and shrine-monks institutionalized the cult;
- local shrines inserted their own miracles into the canonical narrative;
- Tenjin became a deity of poetry, rain, agriculture, protection, and justice.

The cult cannot be separated cleanly into “Shinto” core and “Buddhist additions.” The medieval Tenjin known from the richest evidence was produced through their interaction.

### Fifteenth–sixteenth centuries: Zen, art, regionalization

Tōtō Tenjin iconography integrated him with Zen and Chinese literary authority. Painted legends circulated widely. Civil warfare damaged some institutions; Dazaifu declined in periods of conflict and recovered in the late sixteenth century. Its present main sanctuary dates to 1591. [MA]

Toyotomi Hideyoshi’s great Kitano tea gathering of 1587 connected the site with tea culture and urban spectacle.

### Seventeenth–mid-nineteenth centuries: popular education and urban culture

Under Tokugawa rule, Tenjin became a pervasive patron of calligraphy, poetry, neighborhood associations, schooling, and urban festivals. Tenjin confraternities supported rites; children and learners encountered the god through temple-school culture and calligraphic exemplars. This relationship helped prepare the modern exam god but should not be reduced to a simple claim that every *terakoya* worshipped Tenjin. [MA]

The 1746 *Sugawara denju tenarai kagami* fixed the wronged scholar and calligraphy master in popular theatre. Prints, illustrated books, pilgrimage guides, and festival material multiplied his recognizable forms.

### 1868–1945: separation, national shrine administration, and State Shinto

Meiji decrees separating kami and buddhas dismantled the institutional form in which Tenjin had been worshipped for centuries. At Kitano:

- Buddhist halls, bells, pagodas, and images were removed or transferred;
- Eleven-Headed Kannon imagery and Buddhist implements were displaced;
- sacred relics were transferred;
- shrine monks lost their roles;
- hereditary and Buddhist administrative structures were reorganized.

This was not the “restoration” of an unchanged ancient Tenjin. It produced a newly purified shrine identity by suppressing the visible Buddhist half of the historical cult. [EA/MSR]

Under the modern shrine system, Tenjin could be presented as a loyal imperial subject, exemplary scholar, and national moral figure. That emphasis softened the politically embarrassing fact that his cult began in fear of an emperor and court punished by a wronged subordinate. [MI/modern political reframing]

No evidence located establishes a major, organized extremist movement uniquely centered on Tenjin. Broader imperial-nationalist uses of shrines and loyal-subject education affected his representation, but sensational claims of a specific Tenjin extremist cult would require evidence not found here.

### 1945–present: postwar shrine religion and examination culture

The postwar constitution ended state shrine administration; Kitano, Dazaifu, Osaka, Yushima, and other shrines continued within the postwar religious-corporation system. Their contemporary public identity emphasizes learning, culture, arts, sincerity, and community festival.

The examination cult is now the most visible form:

- students write desired schools or scores on *ema*;
- families buy academic-success amulets;
- shrines hold blessing rites;
- plum season overlaps the entrance-examination calendar;
- successful students return thanks or dispose of old talismans ritually.

This is continuous with Tenjin’s literary patronage but also reflects modern mass schooling, standardized examinations, and tourism. It is therefore both inherited religion and modern adaptation.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature, theatre, and visual art

The most influential popular reinvention predates modernity narrowly defined: *Sugawara denju tenarai kagami* remains a major bunraku and kabuki work. Its *Terakoya* scene, involving substitution and child sacrifice in loyalty to Kan Shōjō’s household, shaped emotional memory more than archival court history. Modern productions continue this reception.

Museum exhibitions increasingly present Tenjin not simply as an exam god but as a case study in apotheosis, medieval narrative art, Buddhist–kami combination, and political memory. The Kyoto National Museum’s 2026 “Kitano Tenjin” exhibition explicitly traces the movement from *goryō* to fire, thunder, plague, martial, agricultural, literary, and capital-protecting deity.

### School and commercial culture

Tenjin’s name and plum crest appear on:

- stationery, examination pencils, sweets, souvenirs, and charms;
- tutorial schools and educational services;
- local festivals and tourism campaigns;
- products marketed seasonally to candidates.

The idea that a written *ema* functions like a supernatural examination application is a modern vernacular framing of an older votive technology. The practice itself is real and institutionally supported; claims of guaranteed results are devotional or commercial speech, not historical evidence.

### Manga, anime, and games

Modern fiction freely detaches Tenjin from shrine theology. Examples include portrayals in which he is a bureaucratic senior god, lightning user, scholarly patron, or member of a comic pantheon. *Noragami*, for example, turns Tenjin into a character who interacts with invented or radically reworked gods and spirit-servants. Such encounters are **modern fictional inventions** and must not be entered into the traditional god–god interaction record.

Popular claims connecting unrelated fictional figures named with similar characters—such as *Tenjin* 天神 and *Tengen* 天元—are wordplay or fan theory unless a creator documents the connection.

### Occult, esoteric, neopagan, and New Age material

The contested-material search did not establish a historically significant Western occult Tenjin current comparable to Golden Dawn treatment of Egyptian or Greco-Roman gods. Tenjin appears occasionally in modern magical, anime-derived, or eclectic polytheist lists, but these are small-scale and generally lack institutional continuity with Japanese shrine practice. Claims that an ancient Tenjin grimoire, tantric cult independent of Japanese Buddhism, or global “neopagan tradition” exists should be treated as **unestablished or modern invention**.

The medieval cult was genuinely esoteric in the Japanese Buddhist sense: ritual mudras, Dharma-protector theology, Kannon identification, relics, sutras, and visionary journeys are documented. Calling this “occultism” in the modern Western sense obscures the historical institutions.

### Psychological interpretation

Modern readers sometimes cast Tenjin as an archetype of the “rejected intellectual,” the return of repressed injustice, or the conversion of rage into cultural authority. Such readings can be illuminating as modern interpretation, but they are not recovered Heian doctrine and were not found as a historically central Jungian Tenjin school. [MI]

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Michizane really believed to cause the 930 lightning?

The event is recorded, but the basic annalistic notices do not name Michizane as its cause. Later shrine literature does. Scholarship must therefore distinguish:

- the 930 meteorological and political disaster; [EA]
- early rumor surrounding Michizane’s resentment; [EA]
- later canonical attribution of the strike to Tenjin or his messenger. [LC]

The popular compressed claim is historically meaningful as received tradition but textually late in its explicit form.

### When did the cult begin?

Possible benchmarks differ:

- 905 grave shrine at Dazaifu, according to shrine tradition;
- 919 larger Dazaifu sanctuary;
- 923 recorded public attribution of princely death;
- 941 otherworld narrative;
- 942 Ayako’s oracle;
- 947 Kitano installation;
- 959 aristocratic enlargement;
- 987 imperial title and festival.

“Foundation” means different things: grave commemoration, belief in an active spirit, local shrine, capital institution, or state-recognized deity. Scholars consequently periodize the emergence differently.

### Was the cult popular, aristocratic, Buddhist, or state-made?

All four elements are documented. Mediums and neighborhood worship appear in foundation traditions; Sugawara descendants preserved memory; monks supplied cosmology and liturgy; Fujiwara and imperial sponsorship created an elite institution. Monocausal accounts are inadequate.

### Who founded Kitano?

The Ayako-centered and Saichin/Miwa-centered records differ. Scholarship interprets this as reflecting competition within the early institution. The low-status female medium may preserve a popular foundation strand later subordinated by male clerical administration, but the exact original sequence cannot be reconstructed with certainty.

### What does *Tenjin* mean here?

“Tenjin” initially meant heavenly deity or deities and was not Michizane’s exclusive proper name. The Michizane cult appropriated the designation through his heavenly and thunder identity. The popular etymology “ten = sky, jin = god, therefore lightning god” is linguistically possible at the character level but historically insufficient: it does not explain the institutional fusion of *goryō*, local thunder cult, Chinese ritual concepts, Buddhist *deva*, and court titulature.

### Was Tenjin originally a thunder god?

Michizane was a dead courtier before becoming Tenjin. Yet the cult formed at a place with pre-existing thunder associations and absorbed a thunder-deity complex. It is therefore misleading both to say that the historical Michizane “was always the thunder god” and to treat lightning as an irrelevant late ornament. Thunder was constitutive of the tenth-century transformation. [MSR]

### Buddhist identity

Eleven-Headed Kannon is the best-attested medieval *honji*, but medieval correspondences were neither universally exclusive nor equivalent to Western-style identity statements. A kami could be a trace of a bodhisattva, a Dharma protector, a wrathful deity, and a dead human without the system demanding one reduction.

The title *Daijizai-ten* evokes Maheśvara but does not prove that worshippers simply replaced Śiva with Michizane. Likewise, the Kannon identification does not make every Tenjin image a Kannon icon.

### The “Shinto” problem

Kuroda Toshio’s critique of “Shinto” as an autonomous, continuous religion is especially pertinent. The richest medieval Tenjin evidence comes from a shrine-temple complex operated by monks and shrine specialists, grounded in Buddhist cosmology. Breen and Teeuwen’s historical approach similarly cautions against projecting the post-Meiji shrine system onto early Japan.

Tenjin can properly be called a Shinto kami in contemporary classification, provided that this does not erase the Buddhist institutions that formed and maintained his medieval cult.

### Sources and textual loss

No autograph of the 941 vision survives. The account is mediated by later compilations. The earliest prose *Kitano Tenjin engi* precedes its best-known painted recension. Painted copies are scattered among Kitano Tenmangū, the Tokyo National Museum and other collections, Hōfu, Egara-related collections, and museums abroad. Some recensions are incomplete; the Jōkyū series itself includes an unfinished or underdrawn ninth scroll.

Kitano’s archives, shrine diaries, ritual documents, artworks, sutras, and architectural records preserve an unusually rich institutional record. Meiji separation, fire, war, rebuilding, dispersal, and reclassification altered the material archive.

### Important modern scholarship

- **Robert Borgen, *Sugawara no Michizane and the Early Heian Court* (1986):** fundamental English historical study; separates the courtier from accumulated legend while treating the cult seriously.
- **Jonathan Stockdale, “Spirits in Exile”:** interprets Michizane within exile and *goryō* traditions.
- **Kokugakuin University, *Encyclopedia of Shinto*:** valuable for shrine terminology, texts, and the composite formation of Tenjin; institutional and category choices should still be read critically.
- **Art-historical research on the Jōkyū scroll:** analyzes patronage, Jien’s possible role, narrative politics, Buddhist imagery, format, and copy lineages.
- **Research on Kitano architecture and relic worship:** demonstrates how Buddhist sacred objects shaped the supposedly “Shinto” sanctuary.
- **Breen and Teeuwen:** place shrines and “Shinto” in changing institutional contexts.
- **Kuroda Toshio:** challenges the notion of a timeless autonomous Shinto tradition.
- **Hori Ichirō and later folklore scholarship:** useful for spirit belief, pilgrimage, and popular religion, but broad folklore categories must not substitute for dating Tenjin-specific evidence.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- That the *Kojiki* or *Nihon shoki* names Sugawara no Michizane as Tenjin: impossible; both predate him.
- That the 930 chronicle explicitly says Michizane personally threw the lightning bolt: it does not.
- That the flying plum is a documented botanical event: shrine legend.
- That Tenjin was an ancient, unchanged god of school examinations: the educational emphasis developed historically.
- That Tenjin is simply Śiva or simply Kannon: reduction of a contextual medieval correspondence into timeless identity.
- That shrine worship was purely “Shinto” until Buddhism contaminated it: contradicted by the medieval institutional record.
- That there is a securely documented ancient human-sacrifice cult of Tenjin: no supporting evidence found.
- That modern manga meetings with Inari, Ebisu, Ōkuninushi, or fictional gods preserve old myths: modern fiction.
- That a large continuous Western occult or neopagan Tenjin order exists: not established.

## 11. Sources

### Primary and near-primary source map

- *Kanke bunsō* and *Kanke kōshū*: Michizane’s Chinese writings and exile poetry.
- *Nihon kiryaku*: retrospective annals preserving the 923 attribution to Sugawara’s spirit, restoration, and the 930 lightning event.
- *Dōken shōnin meido-ki* / *Nichizō yume-ki*, preserved through *Fusō ryakki*: earliest developed celestial Tenjin narrative.
- Kitano founding records, *Saichin ki-mon*, and *Tenmangū takusen-ki*: competing oracle and foundation traditions.
- *Honchō monzui*: prayers and literary formulations documenting benevolent and literary Tenjin.
- *Kitano Tenjin engi* recensions: principal medieval narrative and visual corpus.
- *Tenjin kōshiki*: ritual praise.
- *Tenjin no honji*: vernacular incarnation narrative.
- Tōtō Tenjin texts and icons: Zen-literary reinvention.
- Shrine archives, diaries, sutras, relic records, poetry offerings, architecture, and festival documents at Kitano and Dazaifu.
- *Sugawara denju tenarai kagami*: early-modern theatrical reception rather than Heian biography.
