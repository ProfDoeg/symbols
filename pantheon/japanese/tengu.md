# The Tengu: Research Dossier

**Class:** A historically changing **collective class** of Japanese supernatural beings. Depending on period and source, tengu are classified as celestial portents, mountain demons or spirits, enemies of Buddhism, manifestations of pride, ghosts of arrogant clerics or rulers, yamabushi-like mountain powers, tutelary beings, *yōkai*, or quasi-divine attendants. “The Tengu” is therefore not one god with a stable genealogy.

**Earliest secure Japanese attestation:** The characters **天狗** occur in the *Nihon shoki* 日本書紀, completed in 720, under the annal for **637 CE**. A noisy luminous object crosses the sky; the monk Min 旻, who had studied on the continent, identifies it as a *tengū*, glossed in one manuscript tradition as **amatsu-kitsune**, “heavenly fox.” This is a Chinese-derived celestial omen, not yet a bird-man, long-nosed mountain spirit, yamabushi, or martial-arts teacher.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early material**
- **[LE] Later canonical elaboration**
- **[RV] Regional or local variant**
- **[LS] Learned or priestly systematization**
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification**
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion**
- **[F] Folklore or orally transmitted legend**
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction**
- **[MI] Modern invention or modern popular retelling**
- **[NE] Negative evidence: absent from the source under discussion**

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### The changing referent

The graph compound **天狗** is Chinese:

- 天, *tiān* in Chinese, *ten* or *ama/ame* in Japanese: “heaven,” “celestial.”
- 狗, *gǒu* in Chinese, *ku/inu* in Japanese: “dog.”
- Chinese: **天狗, Tiāngǒu**, “Heavenly Dog.”
- Sino-Japanese: **天狗, tengu**; older forms include *tengū*. The manuscript gloss **あまつきつね, amatsu-kitsune**, “heavenly fox,” reflects Japanese interpretation rather than the compound’s literal Chinese sense. The *Nihon kokugo daijiten* records that gloss for the 637 passage and also later astronomical uses of *tengu* or *tenku* ([Kotobank](https://kotobank.jp/word/%E5%A4%A9%E7%8B%97-102256)).

The name’s history must not be mistaken for continuous identity:

1. **Chinese animal and astral omen [EA/Chinese background].** The received *Shanhai jing* 山海經 describes a white-headed beast shaped like a *li* 貍, called *tiāngǒu*, whose presence wards off calamity. Chinese astral literature separately made *Tiāngǒu* a noisy meteor-like portent of warfare. The tenth-century *Taiping yulan*, preserving earlier astronomical material, describes it as a great rushing star, fiery, thunderous, and ominous of armies, political upheaval, and bloodshed.
2. **Japanese celestial omen [EA].** That astral meaning explains the *Nihon shoki* notice.
3. **Heian mountain deceiver and Buddhist antagonist [LE].** By the eleventh and early twelfth centuries *tengu* denotes beings that inhabit remote terrain, make illusions, possess people, impersonate Buddhas, abduct or humiliate monks, and obstruct Buddhist practice.
4. **Medieval “tengu-way” and proud cleric [LE/LS].** *Tengudō* 天狗道, “the realm or path of tengu,” becomes an undesirable postmortem state especially associated with pride, heterodox power, and attachment to fame. Tengu are incorporated into Buddhist accounts of **māra** and hindrances to liberation.
5. **Bird-bodied and yamabushi-like mountain being [MA/SI].** Medieval pictures show raptor- or crow-like beings, often with human bodies and wings. Association with mountain ascetics gradually supplies the *tokin* cap, ritual robes, staff, and mountain habitat.
6. **Long-nosed red-faced figure [MA].** The now-standard anthropomorphic type becomes increasingly prominent in late-medieval and early-modern art. It should not be back-projected into the 637 notice or every *Konjaku* story.
7. **Yōkai and quasi-kami [F/modern classification].** Modern catalogues commonly call tengu *yōkai*, but particular mountains and institutions may treat a named tengu as guardian, attendant, *gongen*, or spiritually efficacious local power.

### Named types and titles

- **Daitengu 大天狗**, “great tengu”: high-ranking or powerful mountain lords. The systematic rank is comparatively late.
- **Kotengu 小天狗**, “lesser tengu”: subordinate tengu, frequently birdlike.
- **Karasu-tengu 烏天狗**, “crow tengu”: human-bodied, beaked, winged type. “Crow” is conventional; many images resemble kites or other raptors rather than zoological crows.
- **Hanataka-tengu 鼻高天狗**, “high-nosed tengu”: red-faced, long-nosed anthropomorphic type.
- **Konoha-tengu 木葉天狗**, “leaf tengu,” and **koppa-tengu 木っ端天狗**, “wood-chip/insignificant tengu”: variable folklore and catalogue terms. Modern English references sometimes wrongly treat *konoha-tengu* as a simple synonym of *daitengu*.
- **Tengu-sama 天狗様**, honorific “Lord Tengu”: local devotional or respectful speech.
- **Tengudō 天狗道**: the tengu realm or condition, not a separate being.
- **Tengubi 天狗火**, “tengu fire,” and **tengu-daoshi 天狗倒し**, “tengu tree-felling”: names for anomalous lights and mountain sounds attributed in folklore to tengu.

### Named mountain tengu

The best-known names belong to medieval and early-modern geographical rankings rather than to one ancient pantheon:

- **Atago Tarōbō 愛宕太郎坊**, Mount Atago, Kyoto.
- **Hira Jirōbō 比良次郎坊**, Hira mountains.
- **Kurama Sōjōbō 鞍馬山僧正坊**, Mount Kurama.
- **Akiba Sanjakubō 秋葉山三尺坊**, Mount Akiba.
- **Sagami Ōyama Hōkibō 相模大山伯耆坊**, Mount Ōyama.
- **Hikosan Buzenbō 英彦山豊前坊**, Mount Hiko.
- **Shiramine Sagamibō 白峯相模坊**, often associated retrospectively with the spirit of retired emperor Sutoku.

The ordering of these figures into “great tengu” lists is **[LS/MA]**, not an early canonical genealogy.

### Disputed equations

- **Garuda / Karura 迦楼羅 [MSR/SI disputed].** Bird-headed Buddhist iconography offers a plausible visual reservoir, but no early source says simply “tengu are Garuda.” Moreover, Garuda is normally a Buddhist protector, while medieval tengu frequently oppose Buddhism.
- **Sarutahiko 猿田彦 [MSR/modern equation].** His conspicuous nose in later iconography encourages comparison with long-nosed tengu. Neither the *Kojiki* nor *Nihon shoki* calls him a tengu.
- **Amanozako 天逆毎 [LS/MA].** Early-modern works made this fierce being, supposedly generated from Susanoo’s violent breath or vomit, an ancestor of tengu. The relevant recensions and derivations are late and textually insecure; this is not evidence for an ancient Shinto tengu genealogy.
- **Yamabushi [SI].** Tengu and mountain ascetics became visually and conceptually intertwined, but a yamabushi was a human practitioner of Shugendō, not definitionally a tengu.
- **Foreigners or Jews [MI/discredited].** Claims that tengu noses, caps, or ritual dress preserve memories of Jews or other foreign ethnic groups have no accepted textual, archaeological, or linguistic demonstration. Superficial comparison of the yamabushi *tokin* with *tefillin* is not historical evidence.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The 637 portent

The *Nihon shoki*, book 23, records under Empress Suiko’s successor Jomei, year 9, second month, that a great star moved from east to west with a sound like thunder. People called it a meteor; the continental-trained monk **Min** said: “It is not a shooting star; it is a heavenly dog.” The omen precedes political or military disturbance in the chronicle’s narrative environment.

What this establishes:

- The graph compound 天狗 was intelligible in early eighth-century Japan as a Chinese-derived omen term.
- A learned monk is made the interpreter.
- The text connects it with a loud celestial phenomenon.

What it does **not** establish:

- A cult, shrine, priesthood, sacrifice, mountain habitat, wings, beak, long nose, fan, yamabushi costume, or martial teaching.
- That a Japanese folk being already possessed all later tengu characteristics.
- That the event in 637 was recorded contemporaneously. The surviving statement belongs to a work compiled in 720.

### Early court and ritual corpora

- ***Kojiki* 古事記, 712 [NE].** No securely identifiable tengu appears. Philippi’s translation therefore supplies no early tengu dossier. Sarutahiko is not called a tengu.
- ***Nihon shoki* 日本書紀, 720 [EA].** Contains the sole foundational celestial-omen notice.
- ***Fudoki* 風土記, eighth century [NE].** The surviving provincial gazetteers contain abundant local gods and uncanny geography, but no secure corpus establishing the later tengu class.
- ***Man’yōshū* 万葉集, principally eighth century [NE].** No foundational tengu myth or cult.
- ***Kogo shūi* 古語拾遺, 807 [NE].** No tengu genealogy.
- ***Engishiki* 延喜式, compiled 927, including *norito* [NE].** No tengu shrine category, state offering schedule, or tengu liturgy. This is decisive negative evidence against calling tengu an ancient state-cult kami.
- **Ise, Izumo, and Usa early shrine traditions [NE].** They do not furnish an early tengu cult comparable to their established kami traditions.

The absence matters: the medieval tengu is not a major figure of the early myth-historical or state-ritual canon.

### Heian and medieval narrative corpus

#### *Utsuho monogatari* and court literature

Late-Heian literature contains mountain marvels and beings called tengu, helping bridge the astral term and the later mountain deceiver. Exact dating and textual identity remain less secure than the *Nihon shoki* notice.

#### *Konjaku monogatarishū* 今昔物語集, early twelfth century

Book 20 supplies the first major tengu dossier. The collection is conventionally dated to the early twelfth century; its compiler is unknown. Important episodes include:

- **20:1 [LE].** An Indian tengu hears a Buddhist verse carried over the sea, follows it to Mount Hiei intending to stop it, but is defeated or converted by Buddhist power.
- **20:2 [LE].** The Chinese tengu **Chirayōju 智羅永寿**—whose name has variant romanizations—comes to Japan after overcoming Chinese clerics. Japanese monks defeat him.
- **20:3 [LE].** A tengu produces a radiant false Buddha in a persimmon tree. The statesman Minamoto no Hikaru waits for the illusion’s limit; it collapses into a bird, commonly translated as a kite or buzzard, which is killed.
- **20:4 and related tales [LE].** Tengu abduct, deceive, or humiliate monks and exploit spiritual vanity.
- **20:6 [LE].** A tengu appears as a woman and confronts or confesses before a holy monk.
- **20:9–10 [LE].** Clerics pursue *gejutsu*, illicit or heterodox techniques associated with tengu rather than legitimate Buddhist attainment.

The repeated structure is not merely anti-clerical. Tengu threaten Buddhist institutions, but their defeat ultimately validates Buddhist authority. Komine Kazuaki and Haruko Wakabayashi have emphasized this legitimating function. A modern study summarizes the recurring pattern: Indian and Chinese tengu reach Japan, challenge its clergy, and fail ([Kimbrough](https://nirc.nanzan-u.ac.jp/journal/6/article/1353/pdf/download)).

#### *Tengu zōshi* 天狗草紙, 1296

The **Seven Tengu Scrolls**, dated by a colophon to E仁 4 (1296), satirize pride and corruption among monks and institutions associated with Kōfukuji, Tōdaiji, Enryakuji, Onjōji, Tōji and other centers, as well as yamabushi and newer movements. The scrolls compare conceited religious specialists to tengu; the concluding material moves toward their awakening and salvation.

The Tokyo National Museum scrolls are now divided among collections. The official e-Museum description identifies them as caricatures of arrogant and immoral monks, though some surviving temple-origin sections contain tengu only as tiny background presences ([e-Museum](https://emuseum.nich.go.jp/detail?content_base_id=100262&content_part_id=0&content_pict_id=0&langId=en)). The dated preface in the Kōfukuji material supports a date around 1296 ([Cultural Heritage Online](https://online.bunka.go.jp/heritages/detail/218360/1)).

The “seven tengu” here should not be reduced to seven individual monsters. The configuration maps religious institutions and clerical categories into an argument over authority. Wakabayashi’s monograph accordingly reads the scrolls as rhetoric about evil and Buddhist legitimacy, not simply a monster picture book ([Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/hawaii-scholarship-online/book/18491)).

#### *Zegaibō emaki* 是害房絵巻

This early-fourteenth-century illustrated cycle elaborates the Chinese challenger of *Konjaku* 20:2. Zegaibō comes to Japan boasting that he has subdued Chinese Buddhism, challenges Mount Hiei, is defeated, and is nursed before returning home chastened. It turns a short *setsuwa* pattern into a visually expansive contest between foreign tengu and Japanese Buddhist power.

#### *Taiheiki*, *Genpei jōsuiki*, and tengu political discourse

Fourteenth-century war chronicles extend the category to defeated rulers, resentful spirits, and clerics whose pride threatens the polity. “Tengu” can function as both ontology and political accusation: an opponent’s supernatural power is real within the narrative but spiritually corrupt.

#### Noh: *Kurama tengu* 鞍馬天狗

The fifteenth-century Noh play supplies the influential Yoshitsune connection in developed form. A mountain ascetic reveals himself as the Great Tengu of Kurama and promises the child Ushiwakamaru—later Minamoto no Yoshitsune—martial assistance against the Taira. The drama, rather than a contemporaneous biography, is the secure source for the familiar tengu-master episode.

#### *Gikeiki* 義経記, Muromachi-period

The *Gikeiki* is central to Yoshitsune’s legendary childhood, but the fully familiar scene in which Sōjōbō and crow tengu explicitly teach him swordsmanship is not simply present in the form repeatedly asserted by modern summaries. The precise Kurama-tengu pedagogy developed through Noh, illustrated narrative, local tradition, and later retelling. “The *Gikeiki* says Sōjōbō taught Yoshitsune swordsmanship” is therefore at best imprecise and often **[MI]**.

### Early-modern catalogues and testimony

- **Toriyama Sekien, *Gazu hyakki yagyō*, 1776 [MA].** Sekien presents a large birdlike tengu rather than the now-universal red long-nosed man. The Tokyo University of the Arts catalogue confirms the 1776 Edo edition ([Geidai Library](https://jmapps.ne.jp/geidailib/det.html?data_id=11348)); the National Diet Library notes that Sekien’s first volume was followed in 1779, 1781, and 1784 and became a major source for later artists ([NDL](https://www.ndl.go.jp/en/imagebank/column/sekienyokai)).
- **Early-modern encyclopedic genealogy [LS].** *Wakan sansai zue* and the 1754 *Tengu meigi-kō* connect Amanozako and related figures with tengu. These are scholarly myth-making of their own period, not recovered prehistoric doctrine.
- **Hirata Atsutane, *Senkyō ibun* 仙境異聞, completed 1822 [LS/testimony].** Atsutane interviewed Torakichi, a boy who claimed contact with a supernatural mountain master. Atsutane sought knowledge of an indigenous otherworld and strategically distinguished elevated *sanjin* or immortals from vulgar “tengu.” Modern historians treat the work as a remarkable pseudo-ethnography and record of ritual performance, not independent proof of a supernatural realm ([Hansen](https://nirc.nanzan-u.ac.jp/journal/6/article/1307/pdf/download); [Encyclopedia of Shinto](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=8809)).
- **Yanagita Kunio, *Tōno monogatari*, 1910 [F/folkloristics].** Yanagita recorded living northern Japanese accounts of mountain beings, including tengu traditions. The work is foundational folklore collection but also a shaped literary presentation of oral testimony, not an untouched transcript of antiquity.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and actions

No single domain covers the corpus.

- **Astral portent [EA]:** loud meteor or portent of rebellion and warfare.
- **Mountains and wilderness [LE/F]:** inhabitants or rulers of difficult peaks, forests, ravines, and giant trees.
- **Buddhist obstruction [LE]:** manufacture false Buddhas, interrupt preaching, abduct monks, tempt ascetics, and grant spiritually dangerous powers.
- **Pride [LE/LS]:** embodiment or postmortem consequence of clerical arrogance.
- **Possession and illness [LE/F]:** tengu may possess humans, especially in medieval explanatory discourse.
- **Wind, flight, and sudden displacement [MA/F]:** storms, blasts, aerial travel, abduction, and returning victims far away.
- **Martial knowledge [MA/F]:** swordsmanship, strategy, secret techniques, especially in Kurama and martial-lineage legends.
- **Protection [SI/F]:** guardian of a mountain, temple, shrine, village, or fire-protection cult.
- **Trickery [LE/F]:** illusions, imitation of sacred phenomena, misleading travelers.
- **Punishment [F]:** discipline of boastful, disrespectful, or impious people.

### Forms

1. **Celestial light [EA].** No body.
2. **Bird or raptor [LE].** The false Buddha of *Konjaku* collapses into a bird, revealing an early bird association.
3. **Human-bird hybrid [MA].** Wings, claws or talons, beak, clerical garments.
4. **Yamabushi form [MA/SI].** Human or hybrid body wearing *suzukake* robes, pom-pom sash, small polygonal *tokin*, and high clogs.
5. **Long-nosed human [MA].** Red face and exaggerated nose. The nose can encode pride—“raising one’s nose”—but derivation solely from that metaphor is not proven.
6. **Invisible or human-appearing being [F].** Folklore need not provide monstrous anatomy.

The earliest securely dated extensive tengu imagery is the late-thirteenth-century *Tengu zōshi*. Standard reference works distinguish the beaked *karasu-tengu* and anthropomorphic long-nosed type, while also warning through the images themselves that medieval forms were more varied ([JAANUS](https://www.aisf.or.jp/~jaanus/deta/t/tengu.htm)).

### Equipment and emblems

- **Feather fan, hauchiwa 羽団扇 [MA/F]:** controls or symbolizes wind; now perhaps the most characteristic attribute after the nose.
- **Sword [MA/F]:** martial knowledge and the Kurama tradition.
- **Shakujō 錫杖 [SI]:** ringed Buddhist staff.
- **Tokin 頭襟 and yamabushi robes [SI].**
- **One-toothed geta 一本歯下駄 [MA/F]:** precarious mountain mobility and ascetic costume.
- **Rope, staff, scroll, or fan [MA]:** variable by school and artist.
- **Wings and beak [MA]:** iconographic bridge between bird and anthropomorphic forms.

No securely ancient source assigns tengu a fixed sacred color, number, weekday, planet, plant, sacrificial animal, or universal festival. Red, seven, crow, and cedar occur prominently in later imagery or local groupings but are not pan-historical correspondences.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### Genealogy

The early and central tradition has **no single tengu parentage**. Tengu may be:

- a class existing independently;
- transformations or rebirths of proud monks;
- resentful spirits of rulers;
- mountain beings;
- retainers under great tengu;
- local manifestations attached to syncretic cults.

The Amanozako–Susanoo genealogy is an early-modern learned construction. Likewise, ranking every major mountain tengu beneath Atago Tarōbō is a later systematization, not an early scripture.

### Documented interactions with named divine or supernatural beings

Because “tengu” is a collective, the list distinguishes explicit narrative encounters from mere equations.

- **Buddhas collectively —** An Indian tengu follows a Buddhist verse to Mount Hiei intending to suppress it but is converted or defeated by Buddhist teaching; *Konjaku monogatarishū* 20:1, early twelfth century. **[LE]**
- **A false Buddha —** A tengu creates a radiant counterfeit Buddha in a persimmon tree; the illusion collapses into a bird; *Konjaku* 20:3, early twelfth century. The Buddha is an impersonation, not a separate deity meeting the tengu. **[LE]**
- **Māra / Tenma 天魔 —** Medieval Buddhist discourse places tengu among the powers of *māra* or treats them as agents/forms of obstruction. This is a doctrinal classification rather than one universally narrated meeting. **[LS/SI]**
- **Shōgun Jizō 勝軍地蔵 —** At Mount Atago, tengu belief, mountain asceticism, fire protection, and the cult of Shōgun Jizō became institutionally associated. Anne-Marie Bouchy documents their coexistence and layering; no early myth makes Jizō the biological parent of tengu ([Bouchy](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-asian-studies/article/abs/cult-of-mount-atago-and-the-atago-confraternities/37D2656C37D11DFCF32ECC92A2BCCC31)). **[SI]**
- **Kagutsuchi 火之迦具土 —** Modern Atago Shrine treats tengu as attendants of the fire deity enshrined there; this reflects the post-separation shrine formulation, not the medieval origin of tengu ([Atago Shrine](https://atago.org/english.html)). **[SI/modern institutional tradition]**
- **Susanoo 須佐之男 —** Early-modern genealogy makes Amanozako emerge from Susanoo’s violent substance and then makes her ancestral to tengu. No such interaction occurs in the 712 *Kojiki* tengu corpus, because there is no such corpus. **[LS/MA]**
- **Amanozako 天逆毎 —** Declared an ancestor or progenitor of tengu in early-modern learned writings. **[LS/MA]**
- **Sarutahiko 猿田彦 —** Modern comparative identification based mainly on the nose and liminal guidance. No securely attested premodern interaction. **[MSR/MI]**
- **Karura / Garuda 迦楼羅 —** Proposed iconographic relationship; no secure narrative encounter establishes identity. **[MSR]**
- **En no Gyōja 役行者 —** Later Shugendō traditions place tengu and other mountain beings within the supernatural landscape mastered or pacified by the ascetic founder, but versions are local and late. **[F/SI]**

### Named human or transformed-spirit interactions

These are not god–god relations, but they are central and often mistaken for genealogy:

- **Minamoto no Hikaru —** Exposes the false-Buddha illusion; *Konjaku* 20:3. **[LE]**
- **Minamoto no Yoshitsune/Ushiwakamaru —** Kurama’s Great Tengu befriends, trains, or promises military aid to him; *Kurama tengu*, fifteenth century, with later visual and folk elaboration. **[MA/F]**
- **Retired emperor Sutoku —** Later medieval legend represents his vengeful postmortem power in tengu-like or demonic terms and links him to Shiramine. The equation “Sutoku = Sagamibō” is not uniform across early sources. **[MA/RV]**
- **Torakichi —** Claims apprenticeship under a mountain superhuman; recorded by Hirata Atsutane in 1820–22. **[LS/testimony]**

**Absence finding:** No early scripture supplies a stable set of tengu parents, consorts, children, or siblings. Lists presenting such a family as “Shinto canon” are late synthesis or invention.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The Heavenly Dog portent

**Earliest source:** *Nihon shoki*, 720, annal for 637.  
**Story:** A great luminous body crosses the sky with a thunderous sound. Competing observers call it a meteor; Min corrects them: it is a heavenly dog.  
**Development:** This follows Chinese omen science. Chinese sources distinguish the auspicious animal of one *Shanhai jing* passage from the destructive astral dog of astronomical literature; later popular summaries often blend them.  
**Function:** Court historiography and political portent.  
**Tier:** **[EA]**

### The international challenge to Japanese Buddhism

**Earliest full source:** *Konjaku*, early twelfth century.  
**Story:** Tengu from India or China attempt to overcome Japanese Buddhism. Chirayōju/Zegaibō boasts of previous victories but is defeated by Mount Hiei’s powers.  
**Variants:** The illustrated *Zegaibō emaki* expands the journey, combat, defeat, medical care, humiliation, and departure.  
**Function:** The exotic challenger magnifies the efficacy of Japanese Buddhism. The narrative permits satire and anxiety while restoring institutional supremacy.  
**Tier:** **[LE]**, expanded **[MA]**

### False Buddha and clerical delusion

**Source:** *Konjaku* 20:3 and related tales.  
**Story:** A luminous Buddha appears in a tree and draws crowds. Minamoto no Hikaru recognizes that genuine Buddha activity should not take this theatrical form. When the tengu’s illusion expires, a bird falls and is killed.  
**Meaning:** Discernment separates Buddhist truth from wonder-working display. The tengu’s power is genuine within the story but limited and counterfeit.  
**Tier:** **[LE]**

### Proud monks become tengu

**Sources:** Medieval *setsuwa*, doctrinal discourse, *Tengu zōshi*, and war tales.  
**Story complex:** Clerics with learning or supernatural power but without humility or correct aspiration cannot easily be classified as ordinary damned sinners. Pride traps them in *tengudō*, where they retain power and religious knowledge but remain obstructed.  
**Variants:** Some tengu repent and attain salvation; others remain hostile or politically dangerous. The 1296 scrolls end not with eternal demonization but movement toward awakening.  
**Function:** Critique rivals, police institutional boundaries, explain uncanny power, and warn against clerical pride.  
**Tier:** **[LE/LS]**

### Yoshitsune and the Tengu of Kurama

**Earliest secure developed witness:** Noh *Kurama tengu*, fifteenth century.  
**Story:** Temple children visit Mount Kurama during cherry-blossom season. The socially isolated Ushiwakamaru treats an unfamiliar yamabushi courteously. The stranger reveals himself as Kurama’s Great Tengu, recounts martial precedents, and promises to teach or support the boy so he can defeat the Taira and vindicate the Genji.  
**Development:** Later pictures and popular tales turn the encounter into vivid nightly fencing lessons with Sōjōbō and ranks of crow tengu. This precise image is not securely a twelfth-century tradition and should not be credited wholesale to the *Gikeiki*.  
**Function:** Gives the otherwise isolated child a supernatural patron and explains his extraordinary military skill.  
**Tier:** Noh **[MA]**; sword-school elaborations **[F/MI]**

### Great tengu of sacred mountains

**Sources:** Shrine-temple records, pilgrimage literature, gazetteers, tale collections, and oral tradition.  
**Story complex:** Each major mountain has a powerful named tengu and retinue. Atago Tarōbō, Kurama Sōjōbō, Akiba Sanjakubō, Hira Jirōbō, and others protect or terrorize their territories.  
**Development:** Geographical lists become increasingly systematic in the early-modern period. Local cult histories differ; they should not be harmonized into one command structure.  
**Tier:** **[RV/LS/F]**

### Tengu abduction and secret knowledge

**Sources:** Medieval tales through nineteenth- and twentieth-century folklore.  
**Story:** A child, monk, or traveler disappears into the mountains, experiences a supernatural realm, acquires clairvoyance, martial or ritual knowledge, then returns altered.  
**Hirata variant:** Torakichi reports journeys and teachings from a mountain master; Atsutane interrogates him about cosmology, medicine, ritual, flight, and social organization.  
**Modern transformation:** UFO and “alien abduction” analogies are interpretive overlays, not historical meanings of the original accounts.  
**Tier:** **[F/LS]**, alien analogy **[MI]**

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No centralized tengu cult

There is no evidence for an ancient nationwide tengu priesthood, state shrine rank, canonical offering schedule, or tengu festival in the *Engishiki*. Tengu practice is embedded instead in mountain religion, Buddhist temples, Shugendō, localized shrine-temple complexes, fire-protection cults, confraternities, votive customs, performance, and placatory rites.

### Mount Atago

Mount Atago northwest of Kyoto became a layered sacred complex involving ancestor and mountain religion, Shōgun Jizō, fire protection, yamabushi, and tengu. **Atago Tarōbō** was understood as the mountain’s great tengu. Atago confraternities (*kō*) spread widely and survive in modified form. Before Meiji, resident yamabushi helped organize the cult; the 1868 separation of kami and Buddhas and later anti-Buddhist measures dispersed the ascetic institution and destroyed or displaced documentation. Bouchy emphasizes that Atago is not purely “Shinto” or “Buddhist” but historically composite.

Modern Atago Shrine identifies Kagutsuchi as principal deity and calls tengu attendants of the Atago gods. This is living institutional tradition, but its categorical arrangement reflects modern shrine history.

### Mount Akiba

Akiba worship joined fire protection, mountain asceticism, temple and shrine institutions, and **Sanjakubō**, frequently represented as a tengu or gongen. In the Edo period Akiba became, alongside Atago, a major fire-prevention cult. A Hamamatsu municipal history notes that Akiba Shrine and the temple worshipping Sanjakubō coexisted on the mountain until the separation policy; the temple was abolished and later re-established ([Hamamatsu](https://www.city.hamamatsu.shizuoka.jp/documents/100276/021203simin_2_3.pdf)).

The post-1868 institution recast the shrine’s deity as Kagutsuchi, while Sanjakubō’s Buddhist cult continued elsewhere. Treating one present institution as the sole timeless “original” erases this contested history.

### Kurama

Kuramadera’s mountain landscape generated the Kurama-tengu cycle. The tradition is literary, performative, topographical, and commercial as well as devotional. Sōjōbō is not securely an ancient enshrined kami with a continuous priesthood; he is the named lord of an accretive mountain legend.

### Dōryōson and Saijōji

At Daiyūzan Saijōji, the transformed monk Dōryō became a protector often shown in tengu-like form. The cult demonstrates the permeability between deceased ascetic, guardian spirit, temple protector, and tengu iconography. It is a specific institutional tradition, not evidence that all tengu are deified monks.

### Mount Hiko, Ōmine, and Shugendō

At mountain training centers, tengu could represent dangerous local powers, guardians, predecessors, or beings to be placated. Ritual texts refer to offerings for mountain guardians and tengu. Such rites do not necessarily mean worship of tengu as supreme gods; propitiation, hospitality, command, and veneration overlap in Japanese ritual vocabulary.

### Offerings and votives

Documented or locally reported forms include:

- plaques and images of tengu;
- prayers for fire protection or safety in mountain travel;
- offerings at subsidiary halls or mountain stations;
- ritual performances with tengu masks;
- processions in which a tengu clears or leads the route;
- placatory rites for potentially disruptive mountain powers;
- confraternity pilgrimage and distribution of fire-protection talismans.

### Violence and sacrifice

The dedicated searches found **no securely documented institution of human sacrifice to tengu**. Stories in which a monster or “strange god” demands a human victim belong to broader local legend complexes and cannot be reassigned to tengu without an explicit source. Claims of historical tengu human sacrifice should therefore be labeled **unestablished**.

There is, however, documented institutional violence in the broader history: sectarian rivalry, political weaponization of demon labels, Meiji destruction or confiscation of Buddhist property, forced disbanding of Shugendō organizations, and loss of records. These are historical effects involving tengu-associated institutions, not “cult violence committed by tengu worshippers.”

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Chinese antecedents to 720

Chinese *Tiāngǒu* already had multiple referents: animal, meteor, military omen, and later eclipse-related heavenly dog. Japan borrowed the written term through continental learning. Direct linear descent from one Chinese beast into every Japanese form is not demonstrable.

### 720–ca. 1000: an omen term with a documentary gap

After the *Nihon shoki* celestial notice, there is no continuous securely documented cult or narrative tradition connecting the astral dog to later mountain beings. The gap is central. The later Japanese tengu may combine the prestigious imported name with indigenous mountain fears, fox-like deception, bird imagery, Buddhist demonology, and ascetic culture. That is a historical reconstruction, not a recorded act of fusion.

### Eleventh–twelfth centuries: mountain deceiver and Buddhist adversary

Court literature and *setsuwa* establish beings called tengu as illusionists and opponents of monks. Their powers are impressive but subordinate to legitimate Buddhism.

### Thirteenth–fourteenth centuries: clerical pride and institutional satire

*Tengu zōshi* makes the connection between tengu and corrupt or conceited religious authority explicit. Tengu discourse becomes a way to map conflicts among temples, sects, ascetics, rulers, and reformers.

### Fourteenth–sixteenth centuries: political demon, mountain lord, martial teacher

War tales and Noh connect tengu with defeated sovereigns, vengeful spirits, political disorder, sacred mountains, and Yoshitsune. Yamabushi iconography becomes increasingly prominent. Named mountain tengu develop recognizable territories.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: classification and commercialization

Printed encyclopedias, illustrated catalogues, urban entertainment, netsuke, masks, and woodblock prints stabilize visual types. Sekien’s 1776 tengu remains markedly avian, demonstrating that the long-nosed form did not erase alternatives. Early-modern scholars manufacture genealogies and rank lists.

### 1820s: Nativist supernatural ethnography

Hirata Atsutane used Torakichi’s testimony to investigate a purported native otherworld and advance *kokugaku* religious claims. The text simultaneously records belief, shapes it through leading questions, and repackages tengu discourse for elite intellectual and ritual networks.

### 1868–1945: separation, suppression, and State Shinto

The 1868 kami–Buddha separation orders and the destructive *haibutsu kishaku* movement fractured the shrine-temple complexes in which tengu traditions flourished. In 1872 the government abolished Shugendō as an independent organization and required practitioners to affiliate with approved Shingon or Tendai institutions; a modern historical study describes the order as effectively dismantling Shugendō ([J-STAGE](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/rsjars/92/2/92_131/_article/-char/ja/)).

Tengu were not themselves a central object of State Shinto doctrine. Rather, institutions associated with them were reclassified: Buddhist images and titles were removed, gongen renamed, and fire cults assigned classical kami such as Kagutsuchi. Some folklore and festival forms persisted outside official theology.

Kuroda Toshio’s critique is essential here: treating “Shinto” as an unchanged indigenous religion existing in isolation from Buddhism distorts the combinatory medieval system. Tengu are an especially poor fit for a timeless, bounded “Shinto pantheon.”

### Post-1945 to present

Postwar religious freedom allowed Shugendō organizations, temples, and local festivals to revive or reorganize. Tengu remain active in:

- temple and shrine legend;
- mountain pilgrimage;
- mask dances and festivals;
- fire-protection and guardian traditions;
- folk narrative;
- tourism, municipal branding, mascots, toys, and souvenirs;
- yōkai scholarship and museums;
- manga, anime, games, and international fantasy.

Continuity is real in particular institutions, but there is no evidence for one unbroken pan-Japanese “Church of the Tengu.”

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Folkloristics

Yanagita and later folklorists converted locally narrated tengu encounters into objects of national folklore study. This preserved testimony but also reclassified varied local beings under analytical categories such as *yōkai*, mountain god, or anomalous experience. Yanagita’s *Tōno monogatari* is both evidence for early-twentieth-century narration and a literary intervention.

Hori Ichirō’s work on Japanese folk religion and mountain belief placed such traditions within patterns of sacred geography, spirit possession, itinerant specialists, and the relation between institutional and popular religion.

### Visual canon and the yōkai boom

Sekien’s catalogues made supernatural beings collectible as named images. Later ukiyo-e artists such as Kuniyoshi and Yoshitoshi elaborated tengu as comic, martial, grotesque, erotic, or heroic figures. The British Museum preserves, for example, a handscroll in which long-nosed tengu confer in a cryptomeria tree ([British Museum](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_1945-0714-0-1)).

Michael Dylan Foster argues that modern yōkai culture emerges through the interaction of belief, taxonomy, entertainment, commodities, scholarship, manga, film, and games rather than through simple survival of ancient belief ([Foster](https://academic.oup.com/california-scholarship-online/book/23281)).

### Literature, theatre, film, manga, and games

Materially influential examples include:

- Noh *Kurama tengu*, continually performed and adapted.
- Ukiyo-e Yoshitsune-and-tengu scenes, which visually fixed martial instruction.
- Shigeru Mizuki’s postwar yōkai manga and reference works, which reintroduced encyclopedic monster culture to mass audiences.
- *GeGeGe no Kitarō* and related franchises.
- The tengu mask worn by Sakonji Urokodaki in *Demon Slayer*, which internationalized the red long-nosed image.
- *Dead or Alive 2*, *Nioh*, *Genshin Impact*, *Touhou Project*, *Pokémon*, and many other games that make tengu a species, boss, warrior, woman, bureaucrat, or fantasy race.

These works are evidence for modern reception, not sources for medieval belief. Female tengu “races,” standardized elemental powers, RPG rank systems, and romantic tengu societies are generally **[MI]**, even when assembled from older motifs.

### Occult, New Age, and neopagan uses

No evidence establishes a major historic Western occult tengu current comparable to ceremonial-magic traditions surrounding angels or Greco-Egyptian gods. Contemporary occult and neopagan websites sometimes present tengu as elemental air spirits, astral teachers, patrons of martial artists, or deities accepting standardized offerings. Unless tied to a documented Japanese lineage, these are modern eclectic constructions.

Calling modern Japanese tengu observance “neopagan reconstructionism” is usually misleading. Living shrine, temple, Shugendō, and folk practices have different institutional histories. Conversely, an individual Western practitioner’s modern tengu devotion should not be backdated as ancient Shinto liturgy.

### Political appropriation

Medieval authors could classify hostile clerics or rulers as tengu, turning demonology into institutional rhetoric. Atsutane used supernatural testimony within a nativist program that privileged supposedly indigenous knowledge over Chinese and Buddhist traditions. Later nationalism celebrated Atsutane selectively, but tengu never became a central State Shinto state deity.

Claims that tengu prove ancient Israelite settlement in Japan, encode a racial group, or preserve Jewish ritual costume are modern pseudo-history. They lack dated transmission evidence and often reproduce antisemitic caricature.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Origin problem

There are three principal explanatory models:

1. **Chinese derivation:** Japanese tengu descend from the Chinese *Tiāngǒu*.
2. **Composite formation:** the imported name and astral learning combined with Japanese mountain beings, bird symbolism, fox-like deception, Buddhist *māra* doctrine, and ascetic traditions.
3. **Primarily Buddhist-medieval formation:** the recognizable Japanese tengu arises within Buddhist narrative and institutional controversy, while the 637 “heavenly dog” is mainly an etymological predecessor.

The evidence favors a historically composite formation, with an important discontinuity between the astral omen and medieval being. A single “original tengu” cannot presently be recovered.

### Why birds and long noses?

Proposals include:

- transformation of the dog’s muzzle into a beak and then a nose;
- influence from Karura/Garuda masks;
- raptors as mountain and sky beings;
- yamabushi iconography;
- gigaku masks with prominent noses;
- symbolism of pride;
- Sarutahiko comparison.

None alone explains the entire record. The safest account is convergent visual development.

### Buddhist enemy or Buddhist guardian?

Both are documented, at different times and institutions. *Konjaku* and *Tengu zōshi* make tengu dangerous embodiments of illusion and pride, yet also make their defeat, repentance, or conversion serve Buddhism. Atago, Akiba, Saijōji, and Shugendō contexts can elevate tengu-like figures as protectors. “Demon became god” is useful shorthand but too linear.

### “Shinto deity” or yōkai?

Neither label is timeless. Tengu are absent from early state ritual lists, central to medieval Buddhist imagination, entangled with Shugendō, and later included in folk-kami and yōkai classifications. Breen and Teeuwen’s historical approach to Shinto and Kuroda’s critique of an ancient autonomous Shinto both counsel against placing tengu unproblematically inside a fixed “Shinto pantheon.”

### Major modern scholarship

- M. W. de Visser’s early twentieth-century “The Tengu” gathered a large source corpus but sometimes used diffusionist speculation too freely. Its value is documentary, not final.
- Komine Kazuaki emphasized how tengu narratives ultimately authorize Buddhism.
- Haruko Wakabayashi’s *The Seven Tengu Scrolls* analyzes evil, clerical institutions, visual rhetoric, *māra*, and legitimacy.
- Anne-Marie Bouchy reconstructed the mixed Atago cult and confraternities.
- Michael Dylan Foster located tengu within the production and commodification of *yōkai*.
- Wilburn Hansen analyzed Atsutane’s construction and ritual use of Torakichi’s otherworld testimony.
- Japanese art historians have reconstructed the dispersed *Tengu zōshi* sequence, hands, textual relations, and dating; older foundational research remains accessible through the Tokyo National Research Institute for Cultural Properties ([Umezu](https://tobunken.repo.nii.ac.jp/record/7643/files/074_1_Umedu_Redacted.pdf)).

### State of the evidence

- The *Nihon shoki* survives in manuscript traditions much later than its compilation, requiring philological care.
- *Konjaku* lacks a known author and complete autograph; modern critical editions reconstruct its text.
- *Tengu zōshi* is physically dispersed among the Tokyo National Museum, Nezu Museum, and other holdings; some sections survive as copies or fragments.
- Shrine-temple separation and *haibutsu kishaku* destroyed or displaced documents and ritual objects.
- Folklore collections preserve testimony filtered through collector, editor, publication market, and national disciplinary agendas.

## 11. Conclusions

The reconstructable history is not the biography of one heavenly dog who gradually changed shape. It is the reuse of one written name for several partially connected phenomena.

The secure sequence begins with a Chinese-style astral portent in a Japanese chronicle. After a substantial documentary gap, medieval Buddhist literature gives the name to mountain-dwelling illusionists whose attacks ultimately confirm Buddhist authority. Thirteenth- and fourteenth-century institutions turn tengu into images of pride, rival clerics, political danger, and potentially redeemable evil. Mountain religion and Shugendō supply landscape, costume, hierarchy, and local guardians. Noh and later legend make Kurama’s tengu Yoshitsune’s supernatural patron. Early-modern print culture stabilizes birdlike and long-nosed visual forms while scholars invent genealogies. Meiji religious policy dismantles the syncretic institutions in which many named tengu operated. Folklorists, artists, temples, shrines, tourism, and popular media then preserve and remake the figure.

Accordingly:

- The Chinese “heavenly dog” is a genuine linguistic and conceptual antecedent, but not a complete prototype of the Japanese bird-man.
- The long nose, red face, feather fan, yamabushi clothing, and martial instruction are later developments.
- Tengu are not securely attested as ancient kami in the *Kojiki*, *Engishiki*, or early shrine liturgies.
- “Demon,” “spirit,” “monster,” “fallen monk,” “guardian,” “gongen,” and “yōkai” are all historically attested classifications, but none applies uniformly.
- No ancient canonical tengu genealogy, universal cult, human-sacrifice institution, occult correspondence system, or continuous pan-Japanese priesthood can be established.
- The disagreements and category shifts are not noise around a stable being; they are the documented history of tengu.
