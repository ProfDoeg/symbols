# Ryūjin (Watatsumi): Research Dossier

**Class:** *kami* (神, divine being/spirit), specifically a sea deity; in later Buddhist-Shinto and folkloric synthesis, a dragon deity or dragon king (*ryūjin* 龍神, *ryūō* 龍王). “Ryūjin,” “Ōwatatsumi,” the three Watatsumi kami, Buddhist nāga kings, and every local Japanese dragon god are not originally one securely attested individual. Their partial equation is itself a medieval and early-modern historical development.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the *Kojiki* (古事記), completed in 712 CE, in which (1) Ōwatatsumi-no-kami 大綿津見神 is born during Izanagi and Izanami’s generation of the world; (2) three Watatsumi kami arise from Izanagi’s ablutions; and (3) an unnamed or generically designated Sea-Deity, conventionally identified as Watatsumi, rules the marine realm, receives Hoori, gives him Toyotama-hime in marriage, recovers the lost fishhook, and supplies the tide-flowing and tide-ebbing jewels. The *Nihon shoki* (日本書紀, 720) independently preserves several variants. Neither chronicle presents the familiar modern composite straightforwardly under the personal name “Ryūjin.”

## Evidentiary labels used

- **[Early—secure]** Text or cult designation attested in an eighth-century or earlier source.
- **[Early variant]** Variant embedded in an early source, especially a *Nihon shoki* “alternative writing.”
- **[Heian canonical/ritual]** Attested in ninth–tenth-century court, shrine, or Buddhist materials.
- **[Medieval accretion]** Developed in syncretic Buddhist, shrine, literary, or artistic traditions after the ancient chronicles.
- **[Regional tradition]** A geographically bounded shrine or folklore claim, not automatically evidence for an ancient national cult.
- **[Early-modern systematization]** Edo-period scholarship, art, popular religion, or folklore.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** Scholarly inference from the surviving evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A recent claim presented as ancient without evidence in the ancient or medieval corpus.
- **[Not established]** A claim for which no adequate dated evidence was found.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### The historically layered figure

The earliest record contains several entities or divine designations that later interpreters often merge:

1. **Ōwatatsumi-no-kami** 大綿津見神, “Great Watatsumi Deity,” produced by Izanagi and Izanami in the *Kojiki* cosmogony. **[Early—secure]**
2. **Sokotsu-watatsumi-no-kami** 底津綿津見神, **Nakatsu-watatsumi-no-kami** 中津綿津見神, and **Uwatsu-watatsumi-no-kami** 上津綿津見神, the Bottom-, Middle-, and Upper-Watatsumi deities born when Izanagi washes at different depths after returning from Yomi. The *Kojiki* associates them with the Azumi/Adzumi maritime lineage. **[Early—secure]**
3. The **Sea-Deity/Sea-God** (*wata-tsumi*, *umi no kami*; written 海神), ruler of the marine realm in the Hoori narrative. Later commentators commonly identify him with Ōwatatsumi or call him Toyotama-hiko, but the exact relation between this ruler and the three purification-born Watatsumi kami is not resolved by the *Kojiki*. **[Early—secure; identity disputed]**
4. **Toyotama-hiko** 豊玉彦, “Rich-Jewel Prince,” the Sea-God’s name in one *Nihon shoki* variant. **[Early variant]**
5. **Ryūjin** 龍神, “Dragon Deity,” and **Ryūō** 龍王, “Dragon King,” categories and titles produced by the meeting of indigenous water/serpent cults, Chinese dragon imagery, and Buddhist nāga-king traditions. Their routine equation with the chronicle Sea-God becomes clearly visible in medieval narrative and art. **[Syncretistic identification]**

A modern scholarly reference from Kokugakuin lists the chronicle forms 綿津見, 綿津見大神, 海神, 少童命, 海童, and 豊玉彦, as well as numerous *Man’yōshū*, *Fudoki*, genealogical, and *Engishiki* occurrences. It also stresses that scholars disagree over whether the Hoori-cycle Sea-God reflects an Azumi clan tradition, a Hayato narrative adapted by the Azumi, or a court construction designed to incorporate maritime authority into imperial genealogy. [Kokugakuin’s Watatsumi database](https://kojiki.kokugakuin.ac.jp/shinmei/watatsuminokami/).

### Watatsumi: spelling and etymology

**Watatsumi / Wadatsumi** is written:

- 綿津見, the familiar *Kojiki* spelling;
- 海神, a semantic spelling meaning “sea deity,” read *watatsumi*, *umi no kami*, or in Sino-Japanese contexts *kaijin*;
- 海童 or 少童, graphically “sea child/youth,” used in *Nihon shoki* and later records;
- 海若, a Chinese literary designation for a sea divinity, found in *Fudoki* and poetry;
- 和多都美, 和多都民 and other phonographic spellings in the *Man’yōshū*;
- 綿積 and related variants in lineage records.

The usual internal analysis is **wata + tsu + mi**: *wata* “sea,” *tsu* an Old Japanese genitive particle, and *mi* a divine or numinous being—approximately “spirit/lord of the sea.” The precise origin of *wata* is disputed. A comparison with Korean *pada/bada*, “sea,” has often been proposed but is not established as certain. The spellings 綿 “cotton” and 見 “see” are phonographic and should not be translated literally as “cotton harbor see.” The explanation “sea child” derives from later interpretation of 海童/少童; the chronicles never describe Watatsumi as a child. **[Modern linguistic reconstruction; disputed]**

### Ryūjin and Ryūō

- **龍神 Ryūjin:** “dragon deity.” It is a class-title as readily as a proper name.
- **龍王 Ryūō:** “dragon king,” the East Asian rendering of Sanskrit *nāgarāja*, mediated through Chinese Buddhist 龍王.
- **海龍王 Kairyūō:** “Sea Dragon King.”
- **八大龍王 Hachidai Ryūō:** the Eight Great Dragon Kings of Buddhist scripture.
- **娑竭羅龍王 / 沙竭羅龍王 Sāgara/Shagara Ryūō:** the Ocean Dragon King, whose Sanskrit name *Sāgara* means “ocean.”
- **竜宮様 / 龍宮様 Ryūgū-sama:** “Lord/Deity of the Dragon Palace,” a folk and devotional title.
- **豊玉彦命 Toyotama-hiko-no-mikoto:** a name increasingly used in shrine tradition for the ruler of the sea palace.
- **大海神 Ō-watatsumi / Ō-umi-no-kami / Daikai-jin:** “Great Sea Deity,” with reading dependent on context.

The tendency to write as though “Ryūjin” were the unchanged personal name of the Sea-God in 712 is historically misleading. The late-twelfth-century *Hikohohodemi no Mikoto emaki* is especially valuable because its text systematically changes the older “Sea-God” into “Dragon King” and the marine palace into the “Dragon Palace.” That is direct evidence for medieval transformation rather than proof that the two expressions were always identical. [David Weiss, “Visits to the Palace of the Sea God in Ancient and Medieval Japan”](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/15/3/350).

### Classification through time

- **Eighth century:** marine *kami*, ruler of fish and tide; or a triad of purification-born marine kami. **[Early—secure]**
- **Ninth–twelfth centuries:** sea kami increasingly interpreted in a Buddhist cosmology populated by nāgas and dragon kings. **[Heian/medieval syncretism]**
- **Medieval–Edo periods:** king of a jeweled underwater court; dragon, serpentine deity, or Chinese-looking human sovereign; giver or possessor of jewels; rain and water power. **[Medieval and early-modern accretion]**
- **Modern shrine discourse:** ancestral maritime kami, protector of seafarers, fishing, purification, safe childbirth, and sometimes traffic safety. **[Modern institutional continuation]**
- **Modern popular mythology:** a single “Japanese dragon god of the sea” named Ryūjin who owns Ryūgū-jō and controls all tides. This is a convenient composite, not the form in which the earliest evidence survives. **[Modern synthesis]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The *Kojiki* (712)

The *Kojiki*, written in a mixture of Literary Chinese and phonographic Old Japanese, was presented to the court in 712. The earliest surviving complete manuscript is much later—the Shinpukuji manuscript of 1371–72—so the received text stands at the end of a long manuscript transmission.

The work contains three relevant complexes:

1. **Cosmogonic birth:** Izanagi and Izanami produce Ōwatatsumi-no-kami among the divine powers of the natural world. **[Early—secure]**
2. **Izanagi’s purification:** after escaping Yomi, Izanagi bathes. Washing at the bottom, middle, and surface produces the three Watatsumi deities and, in parallel, the three Sumiyoshi deities. The Watatsumi triad is identified as the deities worshipped by the Azumi lineage, descended from Utsushi-hi-kana-saku. **[Early—secure]**
3. **Hoori at the Sea-Deity’s palace:** Shiotsuchi-no-oji sends Hoori to the marine realm. Toyotama-hime encounters him at the well by her father’s palace. The Sea-Deity recognizes Hoori’s celestial ancestry, hosts him, marries him to Toyotama-hime, finds Hoderi’s hook in a fish’s mouth, and gives Hoori the tide-flowing and tide-ebbing jewels. He then orders a one-fathom *wani* to carry Hoori home. [*Kojiki*, “The Palace of the Ocean-Possessor”](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-kojiki-2/shell/section-xl-the-palace-of-the-ocean-possessor).

What the text does **not** say is equally important: it does not call the Sea-Deity “Ryūjin”; it does not clearly describe him as a dragon; it does not name his dwelling Ryūgū-jō; and it does not describe the later red-and-white coral palace. Toyotama-hime’s nonhuman delivery form is described through the problematic term *wani*—often “crocodile,” “shark,” or a fabulous marine creature in translation—not as uncomplicated proof that her father was already imagined as the standard Chinese dragon.

### The *Nihon shoki* (720)

The court chronicle *Nihon shoki*, composed principally in Literary Chinese, preserves a main Hoori account and several alternative writings. Its variants are indispensable because they resist a single canonical reconstruction.

The main version sends Hiko-hoho-demi to a magnificent Sea-God’s palace. The Sea-God assembles the fishes, finds the hook in the “Red Woman” fish’s mouth, gives his daughter Toyotama-hime to Hoori, and eventually provides the flowing- and ebbing-tide jewels. [Aston’s *Nihongi*, Book II](https://sacred-texts.com/shi/nihon0.htm).

Significant variants include:

- the Sea-God named **Toyotama-hiko**;
- differing means of descent—a basket or boat made by Shiotsutsu-no-oji;
- alternative instructions in which wind and waves, not the two jewels alone, subdue the elder brother;
- differing treatments of Toyotama-hime’s pregnancy and departure;
- variant identities of the creature transporting Hoori;
- a version in which Hoori’s actions exercise unusually explicit power over Hoderi’s state of mind and body.

The *Nihon shoki* also makes the imperial genealogy explicit: Toyotama-hime’s son Ugayafukiaezu marries her sister Tamayori-hime; their offspring include the future Emperor Jimmu. Thus marine divine ancestry is installed within the genealogy of the ruling house. **[Early political-theological construction]**

### *Fudoki*

The provincial gazetteers ordered in 713 survive unevenly. Relevant materials include sea-deity terms and the Urashima fragment of the *Tango no kuni fudoki*, preserved only through the medieval *Shaku Nihongi*. Because the original Tango manuscript is lost, the Urashima text is ancient in attribution but textually mediated.

The *Tango fudoki* Urashima story sends Ura-no-Shimako with a supernatural turtle-woman to an otherworld; early versions do not simply reproduce the familiar modern underwater coral Ryūgū-jō ruled by “Ryūjin.” Terms and geography vary between the marine divine realm, Tokoyo, and Penglai/Hōrai imagery. **[Early related otherworld narrative, not an early Ryūjin episode]**

### *Man’yōshū*

The eighth-century poetry anthology uses forms including 綿津海, 海若, 海神, 和多都美, and 和多都民能可味能美許等. Frequently *watatsumi* means the sea itself by metonymy, or the sea’s divine ruler, rather than a narratively individualized dragon king.

Takahashi no Mushimaro’s Urashima poem, conventionally *Man’yōshū* 9.1740, develops the vanished-time and opened-container themes. It is evidence for the early Urashima complex, but not for the later identification of Urashima’s host as a named Ryūjin with Toyotama-hime’s genealogy.

A poem associated with Shika/Chika Island invokes “the august deity of Shika,” relevant to the maritime worship later represented at Shikaumi Shrine. Modern shrine publicity connects this to the Watatsumi triad, but the poem alone does not supply the complete later institutional theology. [Fukuoka’s official account of Shikaumi Shrine](https://www.crossroadfukuoka.jp/spot/12517).

### *Kogo Shūi* (807)

The *Kogo Shūi*, composed by Inbe no Hironari, preserves ritual-clan traditions omitted or minimized by the court chronicles. It knows tide jewels in its ritual and antiquarian apparatus, but it is not an independent ancient biography of a deity named Ryūjin. A modern reader should not infer from its jewel lore that every magical jewel in Japanese ritual belonged to one dragon king. [Critical notes to the English *Kogo Shūi*](https://sacred-texts.com/shi/kgsh/kgsh5.htm).

### *Engishiki* and the *norito* (compiled 927)

The *Engishiki* preserves the court’s administrative and liturgical system, including the shrine register (*Jinmyōchō*) and twenty-seven model *norito*. Its register names numerous sea/Watatsumi shrines, including:

- Ōwatatsumi/Daikai Shrine in Settsu;
- Kaijin shrines in Tajima, Oki, Harima, Kii, and Iki;
- Watatsumi shrines on Tsushima;
- Watatsumi-no-miko Shrine;
- Watatsumi-Toyotama-hime Shrine.

This is powerful evidence for geographically distributed institutional cults bearing sea-deity names by the early tenth century. It is not proof that every listed shrine worshipped a uniform dragon-shaped Ryūjin.

No standard *Engishiki* *norito* is a “Ryūjin norito” in the form widely circulated on modern spiritual websites. Claims that a popular modern “Ryūjin norito” is an ancient *Engishiki* liturgy should be rejected unless an exact fascicle and text can be supplied. **[Modern invention/misattribution]**

### Buddhist scriptures and ritual literature

The crucial imported source-family is Buddhist nāga lore:

- The *Lotus Sūtra*, especially Kumārajīva’s Chinese translation of 406, lists eight dragon kings: Nanda, Upananda, Sāgara, Vāsuki, Takṣaka, Anavatapta, Manasvin, and Utpalaka. [Chapter 1 translation](https://nichiren.info/buddhism/lotussutra/text/chap01.html).
- Chapter 12 locates Mañjuśrī in Sāgara’s ocean palace and makes Sāgara father of the eight-year-old dragon girl who offers a jewel and attains buddhahood. [Columbia primary-source translation](https://afe.easia.columbia.edu/main_pop/ps/ps_china-lotus-sutra-dragon-king.htm).
- Buddhist cosmological texts describe jeweled ocean dragon palaces and dragon kings controlling rain.
- Esoteric rainmaking texts invoke assemblies of dragon kings and deploy mandalas, dhāraṇī, offerings, and images.

These sources supplied a ready vocabulary—dragon king, ocean palace, jewel, rain power, Buddhist protector—through which the older Japanese Sea-God could be reread. They are sources for the later synthesis, not sources showing that the *Kojiki* itself originally meant Sāgara.

### Court histories and rain rites

Imperial histories document Buddhist rainmaking at Shinsen’en. Later tradition credits Kūkai with summoning Zennyo Ryūō in 824; the historically firmer institutional record for dragon-king rain rites becomes visible later in the ninth century, including an 875 rite. Zennyo Ryūō’s characteristic image is a Chinese official bearing a jewel, with a serpent tail emerging from his robes; a twelfth-century painting is preserved at Kongōbuji. [JAANUS, “Zennyo ryūō”](https://www.aisf.or.jp/~jaanus/deta/z/zennyoryuuou.htm).

Zennyo Ryūō, Sāgara, and Watatsumi should therefore be related but not collapsed. They participate in overlapping Japanese water-divinity systems.

### Medieval narrative corpus

Important materials include:

- **The late-twelfth-century *Hikohohodemi no Mikoto emaki*.** Its original is lost, but later copies preserve a text attributed to Fujiwara no Norinaga. It explicitly substitutes “Dragon King” and “Dragon Palace” for the older Sea-God terminology and adorns the palace with Buddhist seven-jewel imagery and Chinese architecture. **[Medieval syncretistic recasting]**
- ***Konjaku monogatari-shū***, early twelfth century. Its Buddhist miracle tales repeatedly use dragon kings as donors, protectors, adversaries, recipients of scripture, and rulers beneath water. These belong to the wider ryūō complex; not every unnamed dragon king is demonstrably Watatsumi.
- ***Heike monogatari*, *Taiheiki*, setsuwa,* temple legends, and shrine-origin narratives.** These expand dragon-palace voyages and divine-jewel stories.
- ***Taishokkan* and Tamatori traditions.** A dragon king steals or holds a precious jewel sent to the Fujiwara; the diver Tamatori retrieves it and dies from the wound used to conceal it. **[Medieval legend, later elaborated in Noh, illustrated books, and ukiyo-e]**
- ***Tawara Tōda* traditions.** Fujiwara Hidesato aids a dragon king or dragon woman against the giant centipede of Mount Mikami and receives inexhaustible treasures. The *Taiheiki* contains an early form; later *otogi-zōshi* amplify it. This is a Dragon King story, not securely an episode of the chronicle Watatsumi.
- **Urashima *otogi-zōshi*.** Medieval versions introduce the rescued turtle, marriage, Ryūgū, and a longer temporal gap; the familiar child’s-tale form is later still.

### Edo scholarship and folklore collection

- **Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den*** (1798 completion): philological reconstruction of the *Kojiki*, influential in fixing readings and distinguishing native vocabulary from Chinese representation. His readings affected shrine nomenclature, including the modern reading *Watatsumi* for 海神社. **[Early-modern learned systematization]**
- **Hirata Atsutane:** expanded Kokugaku theology and the systematic divine world. His reconstructions are evidence for late-Edo religious thought, not transparent survivals of prehistoric belief.
- **Toriyama Sekien’s yōkai catalogues** (1776–84): important for the visualization and classification of aquatic monsters such as *mizuchi* and *ayakashi*. Sekien is not an early witness for the *Kojiki* Sea-God, and his catalogues do not warrant treating Ryūjin as simply another yōkai.
- **Yanagita Kunio:** interpreted Ryūgū, Tokoyo, visiting deities, island otherworlds, and Urashima through comparative Japanese folklore. His theories—particularly southern-island analogies—are modern scholarly constructions, not ancient testimony.
- **Hori Ichirō:** analyzed folk religion, sacred geography, wandering religious specialists, and the interaction of institutional and local practice. His work is especially useful against an exclusively textual “official Shinto” model.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains securely attested early

**Marine sovereignty.** The Sea-Deity assembles large and small fishes and compels them to answer concerning the lost hook. **[Early—secure]**

**Tides and water.** He possesses or controls the tide-flowing and tide-ebbing jewels and says that Hoori’s choice of high or low fields will succeed because “I rule the water.” **[Early—secure]**

**Storm and wave power.** A *Nihon shoki* variant has the Sea-God promise to raise offshore and shore winds against Hoderi. **[Early variant]**

**Dynastic alliance.** By marrying Toyotama-hime to Hoori, the Sea-God joins the marine realm to the heavenly-descended line. **[Early—secure]**

**Transport between worlds.** The Sea-God commands marine creatures to escort Hoori. **[Early—secure]**

### Later domains

- Rainfall, wells, rivers, irrigation, and drought relief. **[Buddhist and regional syncretism]**
- Maritime safety, ship protection, fishing, abundant catches, and control of dangerous seas. **[Cultic development]**
- Purification, through the three Watatsumi born during Izanagi’s ablution. **[Early myth; later ritual interpretation]**
- Safe childbirth, especially through Toyotama-hime cults. **[Regional shrine development]**
- Medicine or healing. Frequently asserted in popular summaries, but no early Watatsumi text located in this research establishes a general office as “god of medicine.” **[Late/local or not established]**
- Protection of Japan and creation of the “divine wind” against Mongol invasions. No secure medieval source found here specifically makes Watatsumi/Ryūjin the sole author of the 1274 or 1281 storms. **[Popular modern attribution; not established]**

### Tide jewels

The two objects are:

- **潮満珠 / 潮盈珠** *shiomitsu-tama*, “tide-filling/flowing jewel”;
- **潮干珠 / 潮乾珠** *shiohiru-tama*, “tide-ebbing/drying jewel”;
- later Sino-Japanese forms **満珠 manju** and **干珠 kanju**.

In the Hoori myth, they are tactical instruments for drowning and rescuing Hoderi. They establish Hoori’s superiority and submission of the elder line. They should be distinguished from the Buddhist **如意宝珠 nyoi-hōju**, the wish-fulfilling *cintāmaṇi*, although medieval iconography and exegesis brought the jewel complexes together.

The *Nihon shoki* says Empress Jingū found a *nyoi* pearl in the sea and describes divinely altered tides during her campaign cycle. The fully developed claim that the Dragon King deliberately loaned Jingū both tide jewels through Azumi no Isora is later, appearing in medieval shrine and military legend rather than as a simple statement of the 720 text. **[Early motifs; medieval synthesis]**

### Palace

The ancient palace is variously described as:

- shining like fish scales in the *Kojiki*;
- fortified, towered, imposing, and gleaming in the *Nihon shoki* variants;
- reached through descent into or across the sea.

The familiar **Ryūgū-jō** 龍宮城—an explicitly “Dragon Palace Castle” beneath the sea, constructed of red and white coral, changing with the seasons, and inhabited by Otohime and dancing fish—is a composite formed from Buddhist jeweled-palace imagery, Chinese dragon courts, medieval picture scrolls, *otogi-zōshi*, theater, illustrated books, school texts, and modern children’s culture. The coral specification is not in the *Kojiki*. **[Medieval-to-modern accretion]**

### Form and iconography

No securely identified eighth-century image of “Watatsumi” survives that can serve as a fixed ancient iconographic type.

Later forms include:

1. **Chinese-style human king:** crown or official’s cap, flowing robes, jewel in hand, sometimes standing among waves. **[Medieval/Edo]**
2. **Anthropomorphic ruler with serpentine disclosure:** exemplified by Zennyo Ryūō images, human above and snake-tailed below. **[Buddhist iconography]**
3. **Full East Asian dragon:** horned, scaled, whiskered, clawed, cloud- or wave-borne, often pursuing or holding a flaming jewel. **[Chinese-Buddhist-derived visual language]**
4. **Invisible sovereign represented by palace, waves, jewels, fish, *wani*, turtle, or attendants.**
5. **Dragon King with aquatic retinue:** fish, octopus, jellyfish, turtle, and marine monsters. The specific “boneless jellyfish” servant tale is folklore and not part of the ancient Hoori corpus.

The late-twelfth-century *Hikohohodemi* scroll is the turning point in the documented visualization of the older Sea-God as Dragon King, although the original is lost and extant copies require caution.

Utagawa Kuniyoshi’s 1847–48 triptych shows Tamatori fleeing through the waves with the pearl while the Dragon King and marine retainers pursue her; the palace appears behind them. [British Museum object record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/A_2008-3037-20201). Such Edo prints powerfully shaped the modern image of Ryūjin as a monstrous dragon commanding sea creatures.

### Animals, objects, colors, numbers

- **Fish:** subjects of the Sea-God; one carries Hoderi’s hook. **[Early—secure]**
- ***Wani*:** vehicle and marine form associated with the Sea-God’s family; exact zoological translation disputed. **[Early—secure; interpretation disputed]**
- **Turtle:** central to medieval and modern Urashima, not to Watatsumi’s earliest Hoori episode. **[Medieval accretion]**
- **Snake/dragon:** characteristic of Buddhist and later Japanese water-god imagery. **[Syncretistic development]**
- **Jewel/pearl:** tide jewels, Buddhist *cintāmaṇi*, dragon pearl, and stolen Fujiwara treasure are historically related in reception but textually distinct.
- **Three:** bottom, middle, and upper Watatsumi triad. **[Early—secure]**
- **Eight:** Eight Great Dragon Kings in Buddhist scripture. **[Buddhist canonical]**
- **Black and white horses:** documented in Japanese rainmaking traditions at water shrines, but not exclusive attributes of Watatsumi.
- No ancient canonical color, weekday, planet, or plant exclusive to Watatsumi has been established. Modern lists assigning “blue,” Monday, the Moon, crystals, or zodiac signs are generally esoteric correspondences of recent origin. **[Modern invention unless locally sourced]**

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing genealogical arrangements

#### Ōwatatsumi in the cosmogony

**Parents/producers:** Izanagi and Izanami.  
**Source:** *Kojiki*, 712.  
**Tier:** **[Early—secure]**

The text says the divine pair “gave birth” to Ōwatatsumi during world-generation. It does not explicitly identify this cosmogonic deity with all later entities called Watatsumi.

#### The three Watatsumi kami

**Producer:** Izanagi alone, through purification after Yomi.  
**Members:** Sokotsu-, Nakatsu-, and Uwatsu-watatsumi.  
**Associated lineage:** Azumi no Muraji, descended from Utsushi-hi-kana-saku.  
**Source:** *Kojiki*, 712; parallel *Nihon shoki* material, 720.  
**Tier:** **[Early—secure]**

These are not said to be daughters or sons of Ōwatatsumi. They may be a functional triad rather than one being divided into three.

#### The palace ruler’s family

**Name:** Sea-God; in one *Nihon shoki* variant Toyotama-hiko.  
**Daughters:** Toyotama-hime and Tamayori-hime.  
**Son-in-law:** Hoori/Hiko-hoho-demi.  
**Grandson:** Ugayafukiaezu.  
**Further dynastic relation:** Tamayori-hime and Ugayafukiaezu produce four sons, including the future Jimmu.  
**Sources:** *Kojiki* 712; *Nihon shoki* 720.  
**Tier:** **[Early—secure, with variant naming]**

The modern formula “Ryūjin’s daughter Otohime married Hoori” conflates separate traditions. **Oto-hime** can function as “younger princess” and is attached to Toyotama-hime in some retellings; in modern Urashima stories, Otohime is usually the ruler’s daughter or the turtle-bride. The early Urashima texts do not establish that she is Toyotama-hime or that Urashima’s host is the same Sea-God.

### Divine interactions: discrete catalogue

The list below includes specific attested interactions with named divine beings or divinized actors. It excludes mere membership in the same pantheon.

- **Izanagi-no-mikoto and Izanami-no-mikoto —** generate Ōwatatsumi during creation. *Kojiki*, 712. **[Early—secure]**
- **Izanagi-no-mikoto —** his ablution produces Sokotsu-, Nakatsu-, and Uwatsu-watatsumi. *Kojiki*, 712; *Nihon shoki* parallel, 720. **[Early—secure]**
- **Sokotsutsu-no-o, Nakatsutsu-no-o, Uwatsutsu-no-o —** arise in parallel with the three Watatsumi during Izanagi’s washing; the texts do not narrate a conversation or combat between the two triads. *Kojiki*, 712. **[Early—secure joint appearance; no further interaction attested]**
- **Shiotsuchi-no-oji —** directs or transports Hoori to the Sea-God’s realm; no direct dialogue between Shiotsuchi and the Sea-God is reported in the main early accounts. *Kojiki*, 712; *Nihon shoki*, 720. **[Early—secure linked action]**
- **Hoori / Hiko-hoho-demi / Yamasachi-hiko —** the Sea-God receives him, identifies his heavenly descent, hosts him, makes him his son-in-law, retrieves the hook, gives him tide jewels and strategic instructions, and sends him home. *Kojiki*, 712; *Nihon shoki*, 720. **[Early—secure]**
- **Toyotama-hime —** the Sea-God recognizes her meeting with Hoori and gives her to him in marriage; she later returns from the marine realm to give birth. *Kojiki*, 712; *Nihon shoki*, 720. **[Early—secure]**
- **Tamayori-hime —** the Sea-God’s younger daughter is sent from the marine family to rear Ugayafukiaezu; she later becomes his consort. *Kojiki*, 712; *Nihon shoki*, 720. **[Early—secure]**
- **Hoderi / Ho-no-susori / Umasachi-hiko —** the Sea-God does not ordinarily meet him face-to-face, but engineers his impoverishment and submission by instructing Hoori and supplying the jewels or storm magic. *Kojiki*, 712; *Nihon shoki*, 720. **[Early—secure indirect conflict]**
- **Ugayafukiaezu —** grandson of the Sea-God through Toyotama-hime; the texts do not record a direct meeting between them. *Kojiki*, 712; *Nihon shoki*, 720. **[Early genealogy; absence of interaction]**
- **Emperor Jimmu / Kamu-Yamato-Iware-biko —** descendant of the Sea-God through both Toyotama-hime and Tamayori-hime; no direct meeting is recorded. *Kojiki*, 712; *Nihon shoki*, 720. **[Early genealogy; absence of interaction]**
- **Sāgara Ryūō —** later Japanese thought treats the oceanic Buddhist dragon king as a counterpart or identity underlying the sea-palace sovereign. No early chronicle stages a meeting between Sāgara and Watatsumi; this is an equation, not an interaction. Buddhist scriptures and medieval Japanese exegesis. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Zennyo Ryūō —** shares dragon, jewel, water, and rain functions but has a separate Japanese Buddhist cult history. No primary text securely records Watatsumi and Zennyo meeting. **[Related cult; no interaction attested]**
- **Empress Jingū —** a legendary sovereign treated as divinely inspired and later enshrined. Medieval traditions have the marine Dragon King provide her with tide jewels through Azumi no Isora; the *Nihon shoki* supplies the campaign, divine tides, and *nyoi* pearl but not the complete later loan narrative. **[Medieval accretion built from early motifs]**
- **Azumi no Isora / Ado no Isora —** in medieval shrine and performance traditions, the barnacled maritime deity emerges from the sea and obtains or transmits the tide jewels for Jingū. This is not the Hoori episode of 712. **[Medieval regional/syncretic tradition]**
- **Kasuga deity/Kasuga Daimyōjin —** later accounts send a messenger to Sāgara’s dragon palace to obtain tide jewels. This belongs to medieval shrine-Buddhist legend and is not an early Watatsumi myth. **[Medieval accretion]**
- **Hachiman —** Jingū’s son Ōjin becomes Hachiman in later cult; sea, conquest, and tide-jewel traditions become incorporated into Hachiman-Sumiyoshi-Azumi networks. No early text records Hachiman meeting a named Ryūjin. **[Medieval cultic association; direct interaction not established]**
- **Mañjuśrī —** teaches in Sāgara’s ocean palace and reports the dragon girl’s attainment. *Lotus Sūtra*, Chinese translation 406. This concerns Sāgara, not the chronicle Watatsumi unless a later interpreter equates them. **[Buddhist canonical]**
- **Śākyamuni Buddha —** receives Sāgara’s daughter’s jewel and witnesses her attainment. *Lotus Sūtra*, chapter 12. **[Buddhist canonical; not originally a Watatsumi episode]**
- **Sāgara’s dragon daughter —** daughter of Sāgara and protagonist of the jewel-and-enlightenment episode. *Lotus Sūtra*, chapter 12. **[Buddhist canonical]**
- **Urashima/Ura-no-Shimako and Otohime —** later folklore places them in Ryūjin’s palace, but early Urashima witnesses neither securely name the ruler Ryūjin nor identify the woman as Toyotama-hime. **[Medieval/modern reception, not early interaction]**
- **Fujiwara Hidesato/Tawara Tōda and the Dragon King —** Hidesato aids a dragon sovereign against a giant centipede and is rewarded. *Taiheiki* and later *Tawara Tōda monogatari*. Identification with Watatsumi is not textually necessary. **[Medieval Dragon King legend]**
- **Tamatori and the Dragon King —** Tamatori takes the pearl from the Dragon Palace and is pursued by the king and his marine court. *Taishokkan* tradition, Noh and later art. **[Medieval accretion]**

No secure interactions were found with Amaterasu, Susanoo, Ōkuninushi, Inari, Benzaiten, or Kannon in the early Watatsumi corpus merely because all became prominent in Japanese religion. Local or syncretic temples may pair them, but pantheon proximity is not an episode.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. Birth of Ōwatatsumi

**Earliest source:** *Kojiki*, 712.  
**Tier:** **[Early—secure]**

During the generation of islands and natural powers, Izanagi and Izanami produce a Great Sea Deity. This short cosmogonic notice establishes marine divinity but supplies no dragon body, palace, jewels, or biography.

Its relation to the later palace ruler is assumed by many commentators but never explicitly explained by the text. The same narrative method produces named gods for mountains, wind, fire, and other domains, making Ōwatatsumi part of a territorial and cosmological ordering.

### B. Birth of the three Watatsumi in purification

**Earliest source:** *Kojiki*, 712; parallel in the *Nihon shoki*, 720.  
**Tier:** **[Early—secure]**

After fleeing the polluted land of Yomi, Izanagi strips and washes. At different depths of the water, six deities arise: three Watatsumi and three Tsutsunoo/Sumiyoshi gods. The *Kojiki* adds an etiological lineage notice connecting the Watatsumi triad to the Azumi maritime clan.

This establishes a cultic-political connection between purification, depth-zones of the sea, and a specialist seafaring lineage. It does not say that a single dragon was split into three bodies. Modern shrine theology that describes them as guarding the bottom, middle, and surface is a reasonable ritual interpretation grounded in their names, but not a narrated ancient administrative scheme.

### C. Hoori and the Sea-God

**Earliest sources:** *Kojiki*, 712; *Nihon shoki*, 720.  
**Tier:** **[Early—secure, with multiple early variants]**

Hoderi possesses the luck of the sea and Hoori the luck of the mountain. They exchange tools. Hoori loses Hoderi’s fishhook and cannot satisfy him even by forging many replacements. Grieving at the shore, he meets Shiotsuchi-no-oji, who gives him a vessel and directions to the Sea-God’s palace.

At the palace well, Hoori encounters a serving woman or Toyotama-hime herself. A jewel or reflected radiance signals his supernatural status. The Sea-God receives him with elaborate hospitality after recognizing the heavenly lineage of Ninigi.

The Sea-God summons every fish. One fish, absent because of a sore mouth, is examined; the hook is found. Hoori remains in the marine realm for three years and marries Toyotama-hime.

When homesickness overcomes him, the Sea-God returns the hook with a curse formula and gives him the tide-filling and tide-ebbing jewels. He instructs Hoori to choose fields opposite to Hoderi’s because divine control of water will ruin the elder brother, and to drown or release him until he submits. A *wani* carries Hoori to shore.

The myth ends not simply in reconciliation but in political subordination: Hoderi swears service and offers mimic performances. The *Nihon shoki* connects these dances to the Hayato. Scholars have therefore read the story as:

- legitimation of younger-brother rule;
- incorporation of maritime and southern peoples;
- dynastic acquisition of marine sacral authority;
- an origin account for court service and performance;
- a Japanese adaptation of widespread “lost fishhook” and marine-marriage narratives.

These interpretations are **modern reconstructions**. The texts themselves plainly foreground hierarchy, marriage, magical water control, and submission.

### D. Toyotama-hime’s childbirth

Toyotama-hime comes ashore pregnant and asks Hoori not to watch her delivery because beings of other lands give birth in their native form. He looks and sees her as a huge *wani* or marine creature. Ashamed, she abandons the child and closes the sea-road, though she sends Tamayori-hime to nurture him.

The episode establishes the irreducible otherness of the marine lineage even while it joins the imperial genealogy. Later art often changes Toyotama-hime’s *wani* form into a dragon, showing the same dragonization process affecting her father.

### E. The tide jewels and Empress Jingū

The *Nihon shoki* Jingū cycle includes a sea-found *nyoi* pearl, divinely manipulated tides, Sumiyoshi intervention, and a triumphal expedition against Silla. The account is a court myth-history, not contemporary evidence for a third-century campaign.

Medieval texts and shrine traditions bring together:

- Hoori’s tide jewels;
- Jingū’s sea pearl;
- Azumi no Isora;
- Sumiyoshi and Hachiman;
- Sāgara or an unnamed Dragon King;
- the conquest of Korea.

In the developed version, Isora descends to the Dragon Palace or approaches the Dragon King, obtains kanju and manju, and gives them to Jingū. She uses them to drown and release the opposing army. This narrative contributed to medieval and later imperial ideology, especially in times of foreign conflict. It should be cited as a medieval synthesis, not quoted as though the *Kojiki* said Ryūjin gave Jingū the jewels.

The use of Jingū’s conquest myth in modern nationalism does not demonstrate a distinctive Ryūjin extremist cult. Rather, marine deities and tide jewels could be incorporated into a wider imperial and colonial mythology. **[Documented political reception; no independent Ryūjin political movement established]**

### F. Urashima Tarō

#### Ancient layer

The *Nihon shoki* entry under Yūryaku, the *Tango fudoki* fragment, and the *Man’yōshū* poem preserve Ura-no-Shimako’s journey to an immortal or divine otherworld and his catastrophic return after centuries. The supernatural woman may be connected with a turtle; geography is variously marine, Tokoyo-like, or Penglai-like. **[Early—secure related tradition]**

#### Medieval layer

*Otogi-zōshi* versions make Urashima release a turtle, meet a beautiful woman, travel to the Dragon Palace, marry or remain with her, and receive a forbidden box. After centuries have passed on land, he opens it and suddenly ages or transforms. **[Medieval accretion]**

#### Modern standardized layer

Meiji and Taishō educational texts and children’s books stabilize:

- the benevolent fisherman;
- rescued turtle;
- underwater Ryūgū-jō;
- Princess Otohime;
- dancing sea creatures;
- three days or years below versus centuries above;
- smoke from the *tamatebako* turning him into an old man.

The modern claim “Ryūjin is Otohime’s father in the original Urashima Tarō” is therefore false if “original” means the eighth-century witnesses. **[Modern harmonization]**

### G. Tamatori and the stolen pearl

A precious jewel sent from China to the Fujiwara is lost to or seized by the Dragon King. Fujiwara no Fuhito enlists or marries an *ama* diver, Tamatori. She enters the Dragon Palace, takes the jewel, and, pursued by the king’s sea creatures, cuts her body to conceal it. She retrieves the treasure but dies.

This legend became a major Noh and visual-art subject. It fuses Buddhist jewel symbolism, Fujiwara prestige, female self-sacrifice, maritime danger, and the Dragon King’s ambivalent role as both treasure sovereign and adversary. Kuniyoshi’s prints made this among the most recognizable Ryūjin images.

### H. Tawara Tōda and the centipede

In the *Taiheiki* and later tales, a dragon ruler asks Fujiwara Hidesato to destroy the giant centipede attacking the aquatic palace. Hidesato shoots it using saliva or ritual preparation and receives inexhaustible rice, silk, a bell, and other treasures.

This cycle illustrates a recurrent Dragon Palace pattern: a human hero supplies martial power the dragon court lacks and receives otherworldly wealth. Its king is not securely named Watatsumi. **[Medieval Dragon King cycle]**

### I. The boneless jellyfish

In a widespread folktale, the Dragon King—or his queen—needs a monkey’s liver. A jellyfish is sent to fetch it, is deceived by the monkey, and is beaten until its bones are broken, explaining why jellyfish have no bones.

This is a migratory animal tale with South, Southeast, and East Asian analogues. It is not part of the ancient Hoori myth, and details vary sharply. **[Folklore]**

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Shikaumi Shrine and the Azumi

Shikaumi Shrine on Shikanoshima, Fukuoka, worships the three Watatsumi deities as powers of the lower, middle, and upper sea. Its ritual identity is connected with the ancient Azumi maritime lineage. The region controlled approaches to Hakata Bay and routes toward Korea, making maritime ritual politically and economically important.

The shrine’s modern traditions include:

- maritime safety and fishing;
- purification;
- the Yamagome festival;
- a large collection of deer antlers associated with a Jingū legend;
- identification as a principal sanctuary of the Watatsumi triad.

The *Man’yōshū* invocation of Shika’s deity is strong early literary evidence for local sacrality. Claims for a precise prehistoric foundation date are not independently verifiable. [Official Fukuoka tourism account](https://www.crossroadfukuoka.jp/spot/12517).

### Ōwatatsumi/Daikai Shrine at Sumiyoshi Taisha

Daikai Jinja, the senior subsidiary of Sumiyoshi Taisha in Osaka, worships Toyotama-hiko and Toyotama-hime. Its present main sanctuary dates to 1708 and is an Important Cultural Property. Shrine tradition calls the nearby well Tama-no-i, where Hoori supposedly immersed or deposited the tide-filling jewel. [Sumiyoshi Taisha’s official shrine record](https://sumiyoshitaisha.net/grounds/sessya.html).

This is a major example of ancient sea-deity mythology localized into physical sacred geography. The well tradition is not itself proof that an eighth-century object survives there.

### Tsushima

The *Engishiki* lists multiple Watatsumi shrines on Tsushima. The island’s position between Kyushu and Korea made marine protection, navigation, and transregional ritual crucial.

Kaijin Shrine at Mine/Kisaka later acquired Hachiman associations and was renamed Kaijin Jinja in 1871, when it entered the modern ranked-shrine system. Its changing names and deities illustrate institutional layering: maritime kami, provincial first-shrine status, Hachiman syncretism, and Meiji reclassification. [Nagasaki tourism authority](https://www.nagasaki-tabinet.com/guide/1042/).

Watatsumi Shrine at Toyotama, with torii extending into tidal water, is locally identified with the meeting of Hoori and Toyotama-hime. The identification is a regional sacred-geography tradition rather than archaeological proof of a mythic palace.

### Watatsumi/Kaijin Shrine at Tarumi, Kobe

The shrine appears in the *Engishiki* tradition but its reading and identity have shifted. Motoori Norinaga reportedly supported reading 海神社 as Watatsumi Jinja; earlier and regional readings included *Ama* or *Tarumi*. This is a useful warning that even an ancient written shrine name does not preserve one immutable pronunciation or theology.

### Mekari Shrine and ritual

Mekari Shrine at the Kanmon Strait preserves the **Mekari shinji**, performed at low tide on lunar New Year. Three priests enter the sea bearing torch, bucket, and sickle, cut wakame, and offer it for maritime safety and abundant fishing. Kitakyushu designated the harvesting action an intangible folk cultural property in 1976. [Kitakyushu cultural-property record](https://www.city.kitakyushu.lg.jp/contents/02100234.html).

Modern explanations connect the rite with Jingū receiving or using the tide jewels. The rite is real and continuing; the claim that its exact present form began with Jingū is legendary rather than documentable history.

### Other shrines and distributed worship

The *Engishiki* demonstrates that sea-deity shrines existed across the Inland Sea, Kii, Oki, Iki, Tsushima, and other maritime regions. Later Toyotama-hime and Ryūgū shrines occur widely. Their cults may center on:

- fishing and safe navigation;
- rain;
- springs and wells;
- childbirth;
- return of missing sailors;
- appeasement of drowned persons;
- local origin legends.

They cannot all be assumed to descend institutionally from one central “Church of Ryūjin.”

### Priesthood and ritual agents

No exclusive pan-Japanese “priesthood of Ryūjin” is attested. Relevant ritual agents instead include:

- Azumi lineage ritualists;
- hereditary shrine families;
- Sumiyoshi and Hachiman clergy;
- Buddhist monks performing rain rites;
- *onmyōji* performing Five Dragon rites;
- local fishermen and confraternities;
- female spirit-mediums and folk specialists in some regions.

Offerings include sea products, rice, sake, cloth, jewels or jewel substitutes, sacred dance, and at some shrines wakame. Ema and horse offerings belong particularly to rainmaking traditions.

### Human sacrifice and sexual service

A dedicated search found **no secure evidence that Watatsumi/Ryūjin possessed an institutionally documented cult of routine human sacrifice or “temple prostitution.”** Japanese bridge-, river-, and water-foundation legends sometimes involve *hitobashira* (“human pillars”), sacrificed maidens, or victims offered to water serpents, but assigning every such legend to Ryūjin is unwarranted.

Tamatori’s death is narrative self-sacrifice in a pearl-retrieval legend, not evidence for an actual cultic sacrificial rite. Likewise, marriages between women and serpent/water gods are mythic and folkloric motifs, not proof of temple sexual institutions. **[Absence of evidence is the finding]**

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 712: reconstructable background

Archaeology establishes extensive maritime exchange between the Japanese archipelago, Korea, and China, and texts later associate the Azumi and other lineages with marine ritual. Serpent-water associations probably predate writing. Nevertheless, no inscription or image securely naming Watatsumi before the eighth century has been identified. Any prehistoric “Ryūjin cult” is reconstruction. **[Not directly attested]**

### 712–759: chronicle and poetic formation

The *Kojiki*, *Nihon shoki*, *Fudoki*, and *Man’yōshū* establish:

- one Great Sea Deity;
- three purification-born Watatsumi;
- a Sea-God’s palace;
- Toyotama-hime and Tamayori-hime;
- tide jewels;
- marine ancestry in the imperial genealogy;
- localized maritime gods;
- an Urashima-like otherworld journey.

This is the early core. The ruler is a marine kami, not yet consistently the later Buddhist-style Dragon King.

### Eighth–tenth centuries: Buddhist dragon cult and court administration

Imported Buddhist scripture and ritual supplied nāga kings, jeweled palaces, wish-granting jewels, and rain control. Court rainmaking at Shinsen’en and related sites integrated dragon kings into state protection.

The *Engishiki* formalized numerous sea-god shrines within the court offering system. The result was not replacement of Watatsumi by Buddhism but overlapping institutions: court kami ritual, lineage cult, Buddhist rainmaking, and local maritime practice.

### Eleventh–thirteenth centuries: explicit fusion

The sea kami becomes openly legible as a Dragon King. The *Hikohohodemi* scroll replaces “Sea-God” with “Dragon King,” applies Buddhist seven-jewel language, and visualizes the palace through Chinese architecture. Setsuwa, temple legends, and shrine narratives expand dragon-palace journeys.

This is the strongest documentary stage for saying that Ryūjin, as the familiar composite figure, has emerged.

### Kamakura–Muromachi periods: military, shrine, and performance cycles

Jingū, Hachiman, Sumiyoshi, Azumi no Isora, Sāgara, tide jewels, and foreign conquest become tightly entangled in shrine records, war narratives, and performance. Tamatori, Tawara Tōda, and related dragon-palace legends flourish.

The cultic field is emphatically **shinbutsu-shūgō**, not a sealed autonomous “Shinto mythology.” Kuroda Toshio’s influential critique argues that much premodern kami worship operated inside the exoteric-esoteric Buddhist order rather than as an uninterrupted independent religion called Shinto. [Kuroda, “Shinto in the History of Japanese Religion”](https://religion-in-japan.univie.ac.at/k/images/0/03/Kuroda_1981.pdf).

### Edo period: popular diffusion and nativist rereading

Print, theater, pilgrimage, fishermen’s cults, illustrated encyclopedias, and local histories disseminated dragon kings widely. Artists gave Ryūjin a vivid dragon or Chinese-king body and populated his realm with sea creatures.

Kokugaku scholars, especially Motoori, sought ancient Japanese meanings beneath Chinese characters and Buddhist accretions. Their philology improved the reading of early texts but also participated in creating a purified “ancient” pantheon influential in modern Shinto.

### 1868–1945: Meiji separation and State Shinto

The Meiji state separated kami and buddhas (*shinbutsu bunri*), reorganized shrine ranks, standardized祭神 identifications, and often replaced syncretic titles with names drawn from the chronicles. Sea-god shrines were accordingly reclassified within a national shrine system; Kaijin Shrine on Tsushima, for example, received its modern name and state rank in 1871.

Watatsumi’s imperial-genealogical role made the figure compatible with national mythology, while Jingū’s Korean expedition and the tide-jewel tradition could support imperial ideology. But evidence does not show Ryūjin becoming a top-level independent State Shinto deity comparable to Amaterasu. Its cult remained distributed among local maritime shrines.

The period’s “purification” of shrine identity could suppress or obscure Buddhist dragon-king elements. This was an institutional transformation, not the recovery of an untouched pre-Buddhist state.

### 1945–present: disestablishment and plural survival

The 15 December 1945 Shinto Directive ended state sponsorship and administration. Jinja Honchō was founded in February 1946 by the decision of most participating shrines as a private religious association. [Jinja Honchō’s institutional history](https://www.jinjahoncho.or.jp/en/honcho/).

Watatsumi shrines continued as religious corporations, local cultural institutions, pilgrimage sites, and guardians of fishing and navigation. Buddhist dragon kings remained in temple ritual; folk Ryūgū traditions persisted independently of formal shrine doctrine.

There is continuous worship of named Watatsumi deities at historic shrines and continuing devotion to various ryūjin and ryūō. There is no single centralized modern Ryūjin denomination, and numerical membership specifically attributable to Ryūjin cannot be established.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature, education, and folklore

Urashima Tarō became the principal vehicle through which modern Japanese audiences imagined Ryūgū-jō. School readers and children’s books standardized a version much smoother than the ancient witnesses. The turtle, Otohime, seasonal palace, dancing fish, forbidden box, and sudden aging became nationally familiar.

Yanagita and later folklorists reinterpreted Ryūgū as one example of the otherworld beyond the sea and compared it with Tokoyo, southern-island *Nirai Kanai/Niruya*, visiting-god traditions, and ancestral realms. These comparisons are analytically productive but remain hypotheses about relationships, not recovered authorial explanations of the *Kojiki*.

### Visual arts and tattooing

Edo woodblock prints by Hokkei, Kuniyoshi, and others established recurring images:

- Ryūjin as crowned human king;
- a giant dragon among waves;
- jewel presentation;
- Tamatori fleeing marine monsters;
- palace architecture visible below the sea.

These images entered modern tattoo iconography, posters, commercial art, and fantasy illustration. Their popularity can cause Edo conventions to be misdescribed as the “ancient appearance” of Watatsumi.

### Film, manga, anime, and games

Ryūgū and Dragon King motifs recur across modern media, frequently without representing the historical deity directly. Particularly influential examples include:

- adaptations of **Urashima Tarō** in animation and children’s film;
- **One Piece**, whose Ryūgū Kingdom and Queen Otohime borrow names and marine-court imagery;
- **Dragon Ball**, where wish-granting dragon and jewel motifs belong to a wider East Asian dragon-ball complex rather than a direct adaptation of Watatsumi;
- **Genshin Impact**, whose Watatsumi Island and Sangonomiya imagery creatively combine Ryūgū, marine serpent, shrine, and fantasy motifs; its “Watatsumi Omikami” is Orobashi, not the historical Watatsumi deity;
- Japanese role-playing games and anime using “Ryūjin” as a dragon, weapon, clan, corporate name, or water spell.

These works materially shape global perceptions but are **modern creative reception**, not sources for ancient belief.

### Contemporary spiritual and neopagan use

Modern practitioners may invoke Ryūjin for emotional purification, prosperity, rain, ocean protection, transformation, or “dragon energy.” Some combine shrine language, Buddhist mantras, Reiki, Western occultism, crystals, chakras, or New Age manifestation.

No evidence was found for Ryūjin in Renaissance Hermeticism, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, classical European grimoires, or early Theosophy as a historically recognized named power. Modern claims that Ryūjin has an ancient planetary seal, fixed elemental correspondence chart, Western ceremonial sigil, or inherited “dragon current” lineage are modern occult constructions unless a datable source is produced.

Likewise, the frequently circulated **“Ryūjin norito”** is not one of the *Engishiki* liturgies and should not be marketed as an unchanged ancient official prayer. **[Modern invention or modern devotional composition]**

### Environmental reception

Marine shrines increasingly frame rites through ocean safety, fisheries, disaster memory, and environmental protection. Such applications are authentic contemporary religious developments, but environmentalist terminology should not be retrojected into the eighth-century texts.

### Nationalist and extremist appropriation

The imperial genealogy and Jingū’s conquest legend were historically available to State Shinto and colonial nationalism. Modern nationalist groups may invoke Jingū, the divine land, or shrine authority. The evidence examined does not establish a distinctive Ryūjin-centered extremist movement. Assertions that Ryūjin personally created the *kamikaze* against the Mongols or guarantees Japanese racial destiny are late ideological claims, not secure medieval doctrine.

### Commercial use

“Ryūjin” is now used for restaurants, ships, aquariums, martial-arts systems, game characters, alcohol brands, tourism, and corporate names. Commercial recurrence shows the cultural availability of “dragon god,” not necessarily devotion.

The asteroid **162173 Ryugu**, visited by JAXA’s Hayabusa2, was named after Urashima’s palace because the spacecraft would return with a sample like a treasure from Ryūgū. This is scientific-era mythic naming, not cult.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is the palace ruler identical with Ōwatatsumi and the triad?

The chronicles never supply a systematic equation. Later shrine theology and reference literature commonly unify them. Scholars remain divided:

- one approach reads the entities as manifestations of a single marine divine power;
- another treats the cosmogonic god, purification triad, and palace ruler as materials from distinct traditions combined by the compilers;
- lineage-centered interpretations connect the triad to Azumi cult but regard the palace story as a court or Hayato-derived narrative;
- others see the Hoori narrative itself as Azumi political theology.

The safest conclusion is that the received eighth-century corpus places similarly named marine powers beside one another without fully resolving them.

### What does *wani* mean?

Translations include crocodile, shark, sea-monster, and dragon-like creature. Actual crocodiles were not native to historical Japan, but the term may reflect continental or fabulous knowledge rather than a local zoological specimen. Rendering every *wani* as “dragon” harmonizes the text with later iconography; rendering it simply “shark” may understate its mythic force. **[Unresolved lexical debate]**

### Was Watatsumi originally a Korean deity?

The *wata*–Korean *bada/pada* comparison and the Azumi’s maritime connections have encouraged migration and diffusion theories. Linguistic resemblance and cross-strait exchange are relevant, but no Korean inscription identifies a deity demonstrably imported as Watatsumi. Strong claims of direct derivation exceed the evidence. **[Hypothesis, not established fact]**

### Indigenous serpent god or imported dragon king?

The binary is too simple. Japanese water and serpent traditions likely preceded extensive Buddhist dragon imagery, while Chinese and Buddhist models decisively shaped the named “Dragon King,” palace, jewel, rainmaking, and visual form. The historically documented Ryūjin is composite.

### Political meaning of the Hoori myth

Interpretations include:

- imperial incorporation of maritime power;
- legitimation of the junior line’s sovereignty;
- Azumi service at court;
- Hayato submission and court performance;
- agricultural control of water;
- rite-of-passage or otherworld-initiation structure.

The text supports hierarchy and marine alliance directly. Particular identifications with historical ethnic submission remain scholarly arguments.

### “Shinto” as an ancient continuous religion

Older scholarship frequently presented Watatsumi as one deity in an unchanged Shinto pantheon. Kuroda Toshio challenged this model, arguing that premodern kami cults generally functioned within broader Buddhist and court systems and that “Shinto” as an autonomous religion was historically constructed.

Later scholars have modified rather than simply discarded Kuroda: early kami rites, lineages, and shrines were real, but their existence does not prove an independent religion with modern boundaries. Breen and Teeuwen’s historical approach similarly emphasizes institutional change, combinatory practice, and the modern production of “Shinto.”

For Ryūjin this debate is concrete: calling the medieval Dragon King merely “a Shinto god influenced by Buddhism” understates how thoroughly Buddhist cosmology constituted his recognizable form.

### Primary evidence and losses

- The original 712 *Kojiki* manuscript is lost.
- The earliest complete *Kojiki* witness dates to the fourteenth century.
- Much *Fudoki* material survives only in quotations.
- The original late-twelfth-century *Hikohohodemi* scroll is lost; later copies preserve its text and compositions.
- Shrine archives suffered war, fire, political reorganization, and local loss.
- Oral traditions are usually documented long after their claimed beginnings.

These gaps require restraint in dating motifs.

### Discredited or weakly supported popular claims

- “Ryūjin is named in the *Kojiki* exactly as such.” **False.**
- “The *Kojiki* describes his red-and-white coral castle.” **False.**
- “Urashima Tarō visits Ryūjin and Otohime in the original eighth-century version.” **Anachronistic harmonization.**
- “Ryūjin gave Jingū the tide jewels in the *Nihon shoki* exactly as later tales describe.” **Overstatement.**
- “Every Japanese water dragon is Ryūjin.” **False generalization.**
- “Ryūjin caused the Mongol-invasion typhoons.” **Popular claim; secure source not established.**
- “The modern Ryūjin norito is an *Engishiki* prayer.” **False or unsupported.**
- “Watatsumi had a documented nationwide human-sacrifice cult.” **Unsupported.**
- “Ryūjin has an ancient Western occult sigil or planetary correspondence.” **Modern invention.**
- “Toriyama Sekien provides an ancient canonical portrait of Ryūjin.” **False; Sekien is eighteenth-century and catalogues related water beings, not an ancient Ryūjin canon.**

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Attestation or transformation |
|---|---|
| Before the eighth century | Maritime cult, serpent-water symbolism, and Azumi ritual background are archaeologically and comparatively plausible, but no securely named Watatsumi inscription has been identified. |
| 406 CE | Kumārajīva’s Chinese *Lotus Sūtra* translation supplies the influential Eight Dragon Kings and Sāgara’s ocean-palace/dragon-daughter complex. |
| 712 | *Kojiki*: Ōwatatsumi’s birth; the three Watatsumi born from Izanagi’s purification; Azumi lineage notice; Hoori’s visit to the Sea-Deity and receipt of the tide jewels. Earliest secure textual attestation. |
| 713 onward | Provincial *Fudoki* compilation; sea-deity references and the later-preserved Tango Urashima fragment. |
| 720 | *Nihon shoki*: multiple Hoori/Sea-God variants, Toyotama-hiko name, tide jewels, dynastic genealogy, Jingū’s pearl and tide motifs, brief Urashima notice. |
| Mid-eighth century | *Man’yōshū*: Watatsumi/sea-god usages, Shika deity, and the developed Urashima poem. |
| 807 | *Kogo Shūi*: ritual-clan antiquarian tradition and discussion of magical jewels; no independent named Ryūjin biography. |
| 824 | Later tradition dates Kūkai’s rainmaking encounter with Zennyo Ryūō at Shinsen’en to this year; the precise historical development requires caution. |
| 875 | Court record of Buddhist dragon-related rainmaking at Shinsen’en provides firmer evidence for institutional dragon cult. |
| 902 | Early record of Five Dragon ritual activity in the *Fusō ryakki* tradition. |
| 927 | Completion of the *Engishiki*, registering numerous sea/Watatsumi shrines and preserving official *norito*. |
| Early twelfth century | *Konjaku monogatari-shū* reflects a Buddhist narrative world rich in dragon kings and underwater palaces. |
| Late twelfth century | *Hikohohodemi no Mikoto emaki* explicitly recasts the ancient Sea-God as Dragon King and his realm as a jeweled Chinese-style Dragon Palace. |
| Thirteenth–fourteenth centuries | Jingū–Isora–tide-jewel legends, Hachiman/Sumiyoshi networks, *Taishokkan*, and military dragon-palace narratives expand. |
| Fourteenth century | *Taiheiki* preserves the Tawara Tōda/Dragon King cycle and develops dragon-rain legends. |
| Muromachi period | Urashima *otogi-zōshi* versions consolidate turtle, bride, Ryūgū, forbidden box, and long temporal displacement. |
| 1776–84 | Toriyama Sekien publishes yōkai catalogues, systematizing visual knowledge of aquatic monsters and dragon-like beings. |
| 1798 | Completion of Motoori Norinaga’s *Kojiki-den*, foundational for Kokugaku readings of Watatsumi and the ancient chronicles. |
| Early nineteenth century | Hokkei and other printmakers popularize the Sea/Dragon King and tide-jewel imagery. |
| 1840s–1850s | Kuniyoshi produces major Tamatori and Dragon King prints. |
| 1868 | Meiji Restoration begins shrine-Buddhist separation and state reorganization of kami cult. |
| 1871 | Modern shrine-ranking system; Tsushima’s major sanctuary is renamed Kaijin Shrine and incorporated into state administration. |
| Late nineteenth–early twentieth century | School texts and children’s literature standardize modern Urashima Tarō and Ryūgū-jō. |
| 1906 onward | Shrine-consolidation policies affect local sacred sites; impact on individual water shrines varies. |
| 1930s–1945 | Imperial genealogy and Jingū mythology operate within State Shinto and colonial ideology; Ryūjin remains secondary to major imperial deities. |
| 15 December 1945 | Shinto Directive terminates state sponsorship and control. |
| February 1946 | Jinja Honchō is established as a private association; many Watatsumi shrines enter the postwar system. |
| 1976 | The seaweed-cutting component of Mekari Shrine’s New Year rite is designated an intangible folk cultural property. |
| Late twentieth century | Folklore studies, museum exhibitions, manga, anime, games, tattooing, and New Age spirituality globalize the composite Ryūjin. |
| 1999 | Asteroid 162173 receives the name Ryugu, later associated publicly with the sample-return mission. |
| 2014–2020 | Hayabusa2 travels to Ryugu and returns samples, creating a new scientific reception of the Dragon Palace name. |
| 2026 | Watatsumi triad, Toyotama-hiko, and related marine kami continue to receive shrine worship; Buddhist dragon kings remain ritual figures; popular Ryūjin circulates globally as a composite Japanese sea dragon. |

## 11. Sources

### Primary texts and translations

- *Kojiki* (712). Relevant sections: divine generation; Izanagi’s purification; “Palace of the Ocean-Possessor”; Toyotama-hime’s childbirth. Philippi’s 1968 translation remains preferable for close study; Chamberlain’s older translation is freely available and useful when checked against modern editions.
- *Nihon shoki* (720), Books I–II and the Jingū cycle. Aston’s translation is antiquated but preserves the alternative writings in accessible form.
- Provincial *Fudoki*, especially the Tango Urashima fragment preserved in *Shaku Nihongi*.
- *Man’yōshū*, especially poems employing Watatsumi spellings and Takahashi no Mushimaro’s Urashima poem.
- *Kogo Shūi* (807).
- *Engishiki* (927), shrine register and *norito*.
- *Lotus Sūtra*, chapters 1 and 12, especially Kumārajīva’s Chinese recension.
- *Konjaku monogatari-shū*.
- *Hikohohodemi no Mikoto emaki* text and later copies.
- *Taiheiki* and later *Tawara Tōda monogatari*.
- *Taishokkan*, Noh, and illustrated Tamatori traditions.
- Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den*.
- Toriyama Sekien’s illustrated yōkai catalogues.
- Yanagita Kunio’s folklore collections and studies, including *Tōno monogatari* as methodological context rather than a primary Ryūjin scripture.

### Major modern scholarship and reference works

- Akima Toshio, study of the Hoori myth and political-ritual background.
- Breen, John, and Mark Teeuwen, *A New History of Shinto*.
- Foster, Michael Dylan, *The Book of Yōkai*.
- Hori Ichirō, studies of Japanese folk religion.
- Kuroda Toshio, “Shinto in the History of Japanese Religion.”
- de Visser, M. W., *The Dragon in China and Japan*—valuable as an early comparative collection, but its diffusionist assumptions and century-old scholarship require checking.
- Weiss, David, “Visits to the Palace of the Sea God in Ancient and Medieval Japan.”
- Kokugakuin University, *Encyclopedia of Shinto* and the *Kojiki* deity database.
- Modern shrine, municipal cultural-property, and museum catalogues cited above.
