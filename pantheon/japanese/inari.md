# Inari: Research Dossier

**Traditional class:** *kami* (神), “deity/divine presence,” but also a collective divine title encompassing several kami; in Buddhist settings a syncretic manifestation associated especially with Dakiniten; in regional and household religion sometimes personalized as a fox-spirit, tutelary, or named local *Inari*. Inari may be construed as male, female, androgynous, multiple, or beyond sex.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the *Kojiki* (古事記, completed 712) securely attests **Ukanomitama-no-kami** (宇迦之御魂神), later the principal kami identified with Inari, but it does not call that deity “Inari.” The earliest surviving evidence specifically for the Inari cult is the fragmentary *Yamashiro no kuni fudoki* tradition, compiled after the state ordered provincial gazetteers in 713 and preserved only in medieval quotations. It attributes the cult to the Hata clan and relates the rice-cake/swan/rice miracle. Fushimi Inari’s traditional foundation date is the first Horse day of the second month of Wadō 4, conventionally 711; that exact date belongs to later shrine tradition rather than a surviving contemporary 711 document. By 827, the spelling 稲荷 and the shrine’s dealings with Tōji are securely recorded in the court history *Ruijū kokushi*. In 927, the *Engishiki* lists three Inari shrines in Kii District, Yamashiro Province.

## Evidentiary labels

The following labels describe historical status, not religious truth:

- **[Early attested]** securely present in an ancient or early-medieval source.
- **[Later canonical elaboration]** a later formulation that became institutionally authoritative.
- **[Regional variant]** local rather than pan-Japanese.
- **[Learned systematization]** an exegetical, priestly, antiquarian, or theological synthesis.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** a historically formed equation among kami, buddhas, bodhisattvas, devas, or spirits.
- **[Medieval/early-modern accretion]** a feature first securely visible in those periods.
- **[Folklore]** orally transmitted or ethnographically recorded belief not securely projected into antiquity.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** an academic inference, explicitly distinguished from a primary-source statement.
- **[Modern invention/reworking]** a recent formulation circulated as tradition but not established in older evidence.
- **[Unestablished]** insufficient evidence for the asserted claim.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Orientation

“Inari” is not the personal name of a single consistently defined mythological character. Historically it is first a place-and-cult designation attached to Mount Inari and its shrine, then a divine title, and eventually a broad religious complex. At Fushimi Inari Taisha the title **Inari Ōkami** collectively encompasses five kami: Ukanomitama, Satahiko, Ōmiyanome, Tanaka, and Shi-no-Ōkami. Elsewhere an Inari shrine may enshrine Ukanomitama alone, another food deity, Dakiniten, a locally revealed spirit, or a distinct group of kami.

The figure’s continuity therefore lies less in a stable biography than in a transferable ritual identity: rice, food, agricultural fertility, storehouses, household welfare, commercial success, effective prayer, fox messengers, red shrine architecture, and the capacity to be divided and installed as a *bunrei* or *wakemitama* at new sites.

### Principal names

- **稲荷, Inari.** The current characters mean “rice plant” and “carry/bear,” but are partly phonetic and should not be treated as a transparent original etymology. Fushimi shrine tradition derives the word from **稲成り / 稲生り**, *ine nari*, “rice coming into being” or “rice ripening.” The *Yamashiro fudoki* fragment instead writes the name phonetically as 伊奈利 and explains it through rice sprouting where a transformed bird alighted. **[Early attested etiological tradition; etymology disputed.]**
- **稲荷神, Inari-no-kami / Inari-shin.** “Inari deity.”
- **稲荷大神, Inari Ōkami.** “Great Kami Inari,” the usual modern shrine form.
- **稲荷大明神, Inari Daimyōjin.** “Great Illustrious Deity Inari.” *Myōjin* vocabulary flourished in the Buddhist-kami religious order; it should not be retrojected unchanged into the eighth century. **[Medieval systematization.]**
- **正一位稲荷大明神, Shōichii Inari Daimyōjin.** “Senior First Rank Great Illustrious Deity Inari.” The title reflects court rank and later widespread talismanic usage. Fushimi tradition attributes authorization to an imperial visit in 1194, but its own historical discussion notes that the surviving record of that visit does not contain the alleged authorization. **[Later shrine claim; documentary gap explicitly acknowledged.]**
- **お稲荷さん, O-Inari-san / Oinari-san.** Familiar honorific, used for the deity, shrine, and sometimes—ambiguously—the fox.
- **御食津神 / 三狐神, Miketsu-no-kami.** The former means a deity of august food; the latter is a fox-associated graphic pun, “three-fox deity.” Treating this wordplay as the origin of the fox association is a later explanatory theory, not demonstrated ancient etymology. **[Learned/folk etymology.]**
- **宇迦之御魂神, Ukanomitama-no-kami** in the *Kojiki*; **倉稲魂命, Ukanomitama-no-mikoto** in a variant of the *Nihon shoki*. *Uka/uke* denotes food or sustenance and *mitama* an august spirit. Kokugakuin’s textual survey interprets the deity particularly as the spirit of harvested grain, while noting a scholarly proposal that the form visible in the court texts reflects state granaries and political control of stored rice rather than an untouched peasant cult. **[Early attested name; identification with Inari historically developed.]**
- **Uga-no-mitama / Uke-no-mitama.** Historical readings documented by the *Wamyō ruijushō* and ritual glosses.
- **Ukemochi-no-kami** (保食神) and **Ōgetsuhime** (大宜都比売神). Separate food deities in the classical texts, subsequently compared or identified with Ukanomitama/Inari. They are not simply alternate spellings of one original character. **[Learned and cultic identifications.]**
- **Toyouke-hime / Toyouke Ōmikami** (豊宇気毘売神 / 豊受大神). The food deity of Ise’s Outer Shrine, sometimes equated exegetically with Ukanomitama but institutionally distinct. **[Learned identification, not an attested mythic encounter.]**
- **Dakiniten** (荼枳尼天, also 荼吉尼天 and other spellings). Japanese esoteric-Buddhist transformation of Indic ḍākinī figures, ultimately represented as a single female deity riding a white fox and bearing a wish-granting jewel, sword, or grain. The equation with Inari is medieval and never total: Buddhist institutions may distinguish them even where popular speech calls Dakiniten “Inari.” **[Syncretistic identification.]**
- **Toyokawa Inari.** Popular name of the cult centered at the Sōtō Zen temple Myōgonji in Aichi. Its principal Buddhist object is Toyokawa Dakini Shinten, not merely a branch image of Fushimi’s Ukanomitama. **[Regional Buddhist Inari.]**
- **Saijō Inari.** Buddhist Inari tradition at Myōkyōji, Okayama, associated with Nichiren Buddhism. **[Regional Buddhist Inari.]**
- **Akomachi, Osusuki, Myōbu, Tōme.** Names or titles connected with Inari’s fox attendants in medieval and later traditions. Fushimi’s retelling of the *Inari ryūki* names a benevolent white-fox couple Osusuki and Akomachi, who ask Inari to accept them and their five children as attendants. **[Medieval legend.]**

### Sex and form

The widespread English phrase “the goddess Inari” is too narrow.

- The classical Ukanomitama is not supplied with a narrated bodily sex in the *Kojiki* genealogical sentence, although later texts and interpreters frequently treat the deity as female.
- The Fushimi cult can treat Inari as a collective of five divine “pillars.”
- Medieval Buddhist images often feminize the Inari complex through Dakiniten.
- Other images show a bearded or elderly man with rice, a youthful food goddess, an androgynous bodhisattva, or no anthropomorphic figure.
- Karen Smyers’s fieldwork found that priests and devotees could hold different conceptions without regarding the divergence as requiring resolution. **[Modern ethnographic finding.]**
- Assertions that Inari “began female and was changed to male by a Buddhist government” are not adequately supported by the early sources and should be treated as **[Unestablished/modern simplification]**.

### The fox question

Institutional Shinto ordinarily calls the fox Inari’s **眷属, kenzoku**, retainer, or **神使, shinshi**, divine messenger—not Inari itself. Fushimi explicitly rejects the equation of Inari with an ordinary or possessing fox. Popular and regional religion, however, frequently blurs deity and messenger; fox statues receive offerings, household fox spirits may be called Inari, and revealed local Inari may be experienced in fox form. Both positions are historically real.

Suggested origins include:

1. foxes’ presence around fields and granaries;
2. their predation upon rodents that threatened grain;
3. a verbal association between *miketsu* (“august food”) and “three foxes”;
4. transfer from Dakiniten’s fox mount;
5. older East Asian fox-spirit traditions;
6. Inari’s agricultural liminality between field, settlement, and mountain.

No single theory is demonstrated as the exclusive origin. **[Contested modern reconstruction.]**

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

#### *Kojiki*, 712

The *Kojiki* names Ukanomitama as the child of Susanoo and Kamu-Ōichihime, after their child Ōtoshi. That is the entire narrative appearance: no fox, shrine, rice-cake miracle, commercial patronage, or name “Inari.” Basil Hall Chamberlain’s older translation and Donald Philippi’s 1968 translation remain important English witnesses, but modern philological editions should control readings. **[Early attested.]**

This proves the court compilers knew a deity whose name means approximately “august spirit of food.” It does **not** by itself prove that every later Inari was already identified with this deity.

#### *Nihon shoki*, 720

An alternative account in the deity-birth section says that Izanagi and Izanami, when hungry, generated Ukanomitama, written 倉稲魂命. As with the *Kojiki*, the text does not call this deity Inari. The variant directly conflicts with the *Kojiki* genealogy. W. G. Aston’s 1896 translation is historically important but uses dated romanization and should be checked against Japanese editions. **[Early attested variant.]**

#### *Yamashiro no kuni fudoki* fragment, after 713

The original gazetteer is lost. A passage survives in medieval works, notably the *Nijūnisha chūshiki*. Hata no Irogu uses a rice cake as an archery target; it transforms into a white bird or swan, flies to a mountain peak, and rice grows where it lands. The place is named *Inari* from the miraculous “coming-forth of rice,” and Hata descendants maintain the cult. Later in the tale, prosperity is restored after descendants repair the offense and worship the spirit.

The fragment is early evidence for a Hata clan foundation narrative, but its surviving textual support is medieval. Its exact form in the eighth-century gazetteer cannot be independently checked. **[Early tradition in later transmission.]**

#### The year 711

Fushimi Inari gives Wadō 4 (711), second month, first Horse day, as its foundation: Hata no Irogu enshrined three divine powers on Inari Mountain’s three peaks. The date fits the early-Nara setting but is not written in a surviving contemporary foundation inscription. Some versions of the founding narrative omit the exact date. Therefore:

- foundation in the early eighth century: **probable and traditionally stable**;
- exact day in 711: **later shrine chronology, not contemporary documentation**;
- “the ten thousand torii were founded in 711”: **false conflation**. The shrine tradition dates to 711; mass torii donation is documented from the Edo period.

#### *Ruijū kokushi*, ninth century, entry for 827

This court history preserves the conflict involving wood cut from Inari Mountain for Tōji. Calamity was interpreted as Inari’s curse; the court granted the deity junior fifth rank, lower grade. This is the earliest secure occurrence identified by Fushimi of the modern characters 稲荷 and firm evidence that Inari had entered the court’s ranked religious order. **[Early attested.]**

#### *Engishiki*, compiled 905–927 and presented 927

The *Jinmyōchō* lists “Inari Shrine, three seats” in Kii District, Yamashiro. The three seats correspond to a cult already treated as plural. The *Engishiki*’s Great Palace ritual *norito* also glosses a rice spirit as *uka no mitama*, but it does not narrate a developed Inari mythology. **[Early attested institutional evidence.]**

### Principal corpus

- **Kojiki** (Japanese with Chinese characters, 712; earliest surviving manuscripts much later): genealogy of Ukanomitama.
- **Nihon shoki** (Classical Chinese, 720; later manuscripts): alternative parentage of Ukanomitama.
- **Fudoki**, especially the lost *Yamashiro no kuni fudoki* fragment (ordered 713; medieval quotation): Hata foundation and rice-cake/bird/rice origin.
- **Kogo shūi** (Classical Japanese/Chinese script, 807): important for early court ritual and food-deity context but not a full Inari narrative.
- **Ruijū kokushi** (completed 892): 827 Tōji/Inari Mountain incident and divine rank.
- **Engishiki** (927): three Inari seats and ritual terminology for grain spirit.
- **Tenryaku Jingikan kanmon** (tenth century): abbreviated Hata/founding tradition used to confirm the clan’s priestly role. **[Early-medieval institutional record.]**
- **Kagerō nikki** (late tenth century), **Makura no sōshi** (c. 1000), and writings associated with Izumi Shikibu: evidence that pilgrimage to Inari Mountain entered elite and lay devotional life. These texts attest visits and reputation, not a single systematic theology.
- **Konjaku monogatari-shū** (early twelfth century): a major repository of fox stories, possession, transformation, seduction, and Buddhist moral tales. Its foxes should not automatically be called Inari’s messengers; many are independent or dangerous supernatural animals. **[Important negative distinction.]**
- **Inari ryūki / Inariki** (attributed traditionally to Shinga, Kūkai’s disciple, but surviving form and dating are debated): establishes Inari–Tōji–Kūkai relations and contains the white-fox attendants narrative. **[Medieval text with pseudepigraphic attribution.]**
- **Ototari shingū saimon**, copied by the Shingon monk Kenna (1261–1338): a ritual origin account in which divinities rescued from a catfish promise to serve Ototari’s descendants and are identified as the Celestial Fox King. It calls Inari a manifestation of Monju. **[Medieval esoteric elaboration.]**
- **Inari ichiryū daiji**, Mt. Kōya manuscript dated 1408: a secret ritual complex involving Inari, the Celestial Fox King, and Dakiniten; preserves a variant of the Ototari narrative. **[Medieval esoteric systematization.]**
- **Keiran shūyōshū** (fourteenth century Tendai miscellany): contributes to medieval identifications and origin narratives.
- **Kada kōshiki** and related medieval liturgies: link Inari to institutions and historical patrons while framing the kami through Buddhist salvific language.
- **Inari shingyō**: Buddhist-style scripture presenting Inari as compassionately assisting beings without sufficient merit. It is a medieval or later liturgical composition, not an ancient Shinto scripture.
- **Nijūnisha chūshiki** and associated Twenty-Two Shrine commentarial works: preserve the *Yamashiro fudoki* fragment and systematize shrine traditions.
- **Inari-sha jijitsu kōshōki** and shrine petitions, especially the 1792 consultation on who could issue Senior First Rank Inari enshrinements: evidence for institutional efforts to control a decentralized cult.
- **Toriyama Sekien’s yōkai catalogues** (1776–1784): central to early-modern visual cataloguing of fox and yōkai lore, but not a canonical Inari scripture. Sekien’s fox materials document the increasingly systematized supernatural environment in which Inari’s messengers were interpreted.
- **Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den*** (1798 completed): philological Kokugaku reading of the *Kojiki*. It helped elevate classical myth as an autonomous Japanese corpus but should not be mistaken for the ancient meaning of Ukanomitama. **[Early-modern learned systematization.]**
- **Hirata Atsutane** (1776–1843): developed a more explicitly theological and spirit-centered Kokugaku. His constructions influenced modern Shinto but postdate Inari’s ancient and medieval forms.
- **Yanagita Kunio**, including “Kurainatama-kō” and wider folklore studies: reconstructed grain-spirit, field-god, mountain-god, and household-cult patterns from modern and regional evidence. These are influential comparative reconstructions, not direct records from 711.
- **Tōno monogatari** (1910): essential for Yanagita’s folklore project, but it is not a primary Inari scripture and should not be mined indiscriminately for ancient Inari doctrine.

### Significant absence

Inari does not possess a unified classical narrative cycle comparable to Amaterasu’s cave myth or Susanoo’s dragon-slaying. The classical texts attest Ukanomitama only briefly; most narrative richness arises from shrine origins, miracle tales, Buddhist liturgy, fox legend, dream revelation, and local cult.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

#### Rice, food, and grain spirit

**[Early attested core.]** Ukanomitama’s name, the founding rice miracle, and the agricultural festivals at Fushimi establish food and grain as the oldest recoverable center. The deity is associated particularly with the spiritual efficacy of harvested grain, granaries, fertility, and the annual rice cycle.

#### Clothing, shelter, and material subsistence

**[Later canonical elaboration.]** Fushimi describes Inari as feeding, clothing, and housing people. Silk production appears in foundation traditions connected with the Hata clan, renowned in later accounts for agriculture and sericulture.

#### Commercial success and industry

**[Medieval/early-modern expansion.]** Rice functioned as wealth, rent, tax, and unit of economic calculation. As urban and monetized commerce expanded, a rice and abundance deity became a patron of merchants, shopkeepers, craftsmen, manufacturers, and corporations. Edo-period “desire-fulfilling Inari” cults advertised specific benefits through dreams and miracle stories. Household and shop-lot shrines made Inari nearly ubiquitous in Edo.

#### Performing arts, actors, courtesans, and geisha

**[Early-modern urban specialization.]** Inari shrines existed in theater districts and pleasure quarters; actors worshipped backstage Inari, and prints record actors praying at theater shrines. Inari consequently became patron of performance and of people working in pleasure-quarter economies.

“Geisha” must not be used as a timeless synonym for prostitute. Edo geisha were professional entertainers, male and female at different stages of the institution’s development; licensed courtesans such as *yūjo* and *oiran* were distinct, though the occupational worlds overlapped. The evidence supports Inari devotion among actors, entertainers, and inhabitants of pleasure districts, not a universal rule that every geisha worshipped Inari.

#### Household safety, fire protection, health, fertility

**[Regional and early-modern accretions.]** Petitions included protection against fire, childbirth, recovery from disease, toothache, cough, broken bones, and venereal disease. These functions belong to the broad economy of *genze riyaku*, benefits in this world; they are not all present in the founding sources.

#### Fishing, smithing, and specialized trades

**[Regional variants.]** Coastal communities could invoke Inari for fishing; smiths and other trades adopted local Inari tutelaries. Claims that Inari was from the beginning universally “god of tea, sake, swordsmithing, fertility, industry, and foxes” flatten later specializations into an invented ancient portfolio.

### Iconography

#### Aniconic and collective forms

The primary sacred presence may be a mirror, stone, mountain site, talisman, or concealed object rather than a humanlike statue. Fushimi’s sacred geography—the three peaks and thousands of named *otsuka* stones—is as important as any portrait.

#### Anthropomorphic forms

Documented forms include:

- an elderly or bearded man bearing rice;
- a youthful woman carrying grain;
- a courtly or priestly figure;
- an androgynous bodhisattva;
- Dakiniten riding a white fox;
- local revealed figures whose forms are known only through dreams or possession.

No single image is universally canonical.

#### Fox statues

Paired foxes commonly flank Inari approaches. Their mouths may hold:

- a **granary key**, signifying custody of rice and wealth;
- a **jewel**, often read as the wish-fulfilling *hōju*;
- a **scroll**, signifying divine teaching or occult knowledge;
- a **rice stalk or sheaf**.

White foxes indicate spiritually potent attendants rather than zoological color. Red bibs are devotional additions, often associated with protection and care.

#### Vermilion and torii

Vermilion became a strong visual marker of Inari shrines. At Fushimi, donors have erected torii as prayers and thank-offerings since the Edo period. The famous **Senbon Torii** means “thousand torii,” not a precise inventory. Popular descriptions of “ten thousand torii” refer loosely to the much larger complex of gates across the mountain.

The date 711 belongs to the shrine’s traditional foundation, **not** the torii tunnels. The tunnels are products of early-modern and modern donation.

### Food offerings

Rice, sake, produce, and other ordinary shrine offerings are fundamental. Fried tofu became associated with foxes and gave its name to *inari-zushi* and kitsune udon. The assertion that foxes naturally favor fried tofu is folklore, not zoological evidence.

### Sacred time

- **Hatsu-uma**, the first Horse day of the second lunar month: commemoration of Inari’s descent/installation at Fushimi and the most characteristic Inari observance.
- Spring planting and autumn harvest rites.
- Fushimi’s Inari Festival and seasonal processions.
- Modern monthly and annual fire rites at particular institutions.

There is no securely ancient universal Inari color-number-planet system. Lists assigning Inari a planet, zodiacal rulership, crystal, tarot card, or fixed “correspondence” generally belong to **[Modern occult invention]** unless tied to a named historical ritual text.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Conflicting genealogies

#### *Kojiki* version, 712

- Father: **Susanoo-no-mikoto**
- Mother: **Kamu-Ōichihime**, daughter of Ōyamatsumi
- Sibling: **Ōtoshi-no-kami**

This is a genealogy of Ukanomitama, not explicitly of “Inari.” **[Early attested.]**

#### *Nihon shoki* variant, 720

- Parents: **Izanagi-no-mikoto and Izanami-no-mikoto**
- Circumstance: produced when the pair were hungry

This contradicts the *Kojiki*. **[Early attested variant.]**

#### Fushimi collective

The modern Fushimi main sanctuary enshrines:

1. Ukanomitama-no-Ōkami, lower shrine, central seat;
2. Satahiko-no-Ōkami, middle shrine;
3. Ōmiyanome-no-Ōkami, upper shrine;
4. Tanaka-no-Ōkami;
5. Shi-no-Ōkami.

Collectively these are Inari Ōkami. The original mountain cult had three seats; Tanaka and Shi were added to the later five-seat arrangement. The identities and etymologies of the last two are not securely recoverable. Equating Satahiko automatically with Sarutahiko or Ōmiyanome automatically with Ame-no-Uzume is a later harmonization, not established by Fushimi’s earliest evidence.

### Syncretistic identities

- **Dakiniten:** principal Buddhist fusion; never a simple replacement.
- **Ukemochi and Ōgetsuhime:** food-deity comparisons and equations.
- **Toyouke:** exegetical food-deity identification.
- **Ugajin and Uga Benzaiten:** connected through fertility, food, serpent, and wish-granting symbolism.
- **Benzaiten:** linked by the jewel, prosperity, water, and shared medieval iconographic networks.
- **Monju:** medieval ritual texts identify Inari as a provisional manifestation of the bodhisattva of wisdom.
- **Celestial Fox King:** esoteric ritual identity in medieval Inari literature.
- **Kannon and other Buddhist honji:** local and textual identifications vary; there was no single universally accepted Buddhist “original ground” for Inari.

### Every securely documented divine interaction or joint relation

The corpus contains fewer narrative deity-to-deity encounters than modern composite mythology implies. The following list separates genealogy, co-enshrinement, equation, and actual narrative action.

- **Susanoo — parentage:** Susanoo and Kamu-Ōichihime generate Ukanomitama. *Kojiki*, 712. **[Early attested genealogy.]**
- **Kamu-Ōichihime — parentage:** mother of Ukanomitama. *Kojiki*, 712. **[Early attested genealogy.]**
- **Ōtoshi — sibling relation:** named immediately before Ukanomitama as the other child of Susanoo and Kamu-Ōichihime. *Kojiki*, 712. **[Early attested genealogy.]**
- **Ōyamatsumi — grandparent relation:** Kamu-Ōichihime is Ōyamatsumi’s daughter. *Kojiki*, 712. **[Early attested genealogy.]**
- **Izanagi — alternative parentage:** with Izanami produces Ukanomitama during hunger. *Nihon shoki*, 720, variant account. **[Early attested variant.]**
- **Izanami — alternative parentage:** with Izanagi produces Ukanomitama during hunger. *Nihon shoki*, 720, variant account. **[Early attested variant.]**
- **Satahiko — joint enshrinement:** one of Fushimi’s three early/main seats and later five-seat collective. *Engishiki* establishes three seats by 927; names are supplied through later shrine tradition. **[Institutional co-presence; no early narrative interaction preserved.]**
- **Ōmiyanome — joint enshrinement:** another principal Fushimi seat. **[Institutional co-presence; no early narrative interaction preserved.]**
- **Tanaka-no-Ōkami — joint enshrinement:** auxiliary member of the fivefold Fushimi Inari. **[Later canonical elaboration; identity uncertain.]**
- **Shi-no-Ōkami — joint enshrinement:** auxiliary member of the fivefold Fushimi Inari. **[Later canonical elaboration; identity uncertain.]**
- **Dakiniten — ritual fusion/equation:** medieval esoteric traditions join Inari to Dakiniten, the fox-riding wish-granting Buddhist deity. *Inari ichiryū daiji*, 1408, and related Shingon materials. **[Syncretistic identification, not a single “meeting” episode.]**
- **Monju — manifestation relation:** Inari/Celestial Fox divinities are described as a temporary manifestation of Monju. *Ototari shingū saimon*, copied by Kenna, late thirteenth–early fourteenth century; *Inari ichiryū daiji*, 1408. **[Medieval esoteric systematization.]**
- **Celestial Fox King — identity/service relation:** rescued divinities pledge service to Ototari’s lineage and are identified as the Celestial Fox King; later ritual texts integrate this figure with Inari practice. **[Medieval esoteric narrative.]**
- **Benzaiten — iconographic and theological affinity:** both participate in medieval networks of wish-granting jewels, serpentine/fertility symbolism, and prosperity. Bernard Faure documents the affinity; no early primary text establishes a direct personal encounter. **[Syncretistic relation; interaction absent.]**
- **Ugajin — iconographic and theological affinity:** associated with Inari/Dakiniten/Benzaiten through food, fertility, serpent, and jewel complexes. **[Syncretistic relation; no discrete early encounter.]**
- **Daikokuten/Mahākāla — broader Dakiniten mythology:** Buddhist accounts make Mahākāla subdue or control dangerous ḍākinīs. This belongs to Dakiniten’s Buddhist prehistory and only indirectly enters Inari after fusion. **[Imported Buddhist narrative, not originally an Inari episode.]**

**Absence finding:** no early text records Inari conversing, fighting, marrying, seducing, deceiving, or competing with Amaterasu, Ōkuninushi, Hachiman, Sarutahiko, Ame-no-Uzume, or the Seven Lucky Gods. Co-membership in modern pantheon lists is not interaction.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### 5.1 The rice cake, white bird, and first rice on Mount Inari

**Source:** *Yamashiro no kuni fudoki* fragment, originating after 713 but preserved in medieval quotations.

Hata no Irogu, wealthy enough to use a rice cake as an archery target, shoots it. The cake becomes a white bird and flies to a mountain peak. Rice springs up where it lands; the miraculous growth supplies the name Inari. In fuller transmitted versions, Irogu’s household suffers after the sacrilegious display of food, and prosperity returns when descendants retrieve sacred wood or otherwise repair relations with the spirit.

The story makes abundance conditional upon respect for food and establishes the Hata clan’s ritual authority. Its rice-name explanation is etiological; its social function is also proprietary, legitimating Hata priestly descent. **[Early shrine-origin tradition; exact surviving wording medieval.]**

### 5.2 Descent or enshrinement on the three peaks, 711

Later shrine records specify that on the first Horse day of the second month of 711, Hata no Irogu installed three kami on Inari Mountain. The year produced abundant grain and successful silk cultivation.

This is the source of Hatsu-uma observance and Fushimi’s anniversary tradition. It is not independently recorded in a document written in 711. **[Later canonical shrine chronology.]**

### 5.3 Inari, Kūkai, and Tōji

Medieval *Inari ryūki* traditions connect Inari to Kūkai and Tōji. In one form, Kūkai meets an extraordinary old man or divine figure while traveling; the deity later comes to Tōji bearing rice and accompanied by women or children. Inari becomes the temple’s protector. Other records emphasize Tōji’s use of wood from Inari Mountain, the resulting divine anger, and the court’s appeasement of Inari in 827.

The historical core is a close institutional relationship between Fushimi and Tōji in the early Heian period. Kūkai’s personal meeting is a medieval retrospective narrative rather than contemporary biography. **[Secure early institutional relation; medieval legendary encounter.]**

### 5.4 The white-fox family becomes Inari’s attendants

In the *Inari ryūki* tradition, an aged white-fox couple living near Funaoka Hill longs to help sentient beings but cannot do so fully in animal form. With their five young they petition Inari. The deity accepts them as retainers and names the male Osusuki and the female Akomachi.

The story explains why foxes serve rather than constitute Inari. Its benevolent fox family contrasts with independent demonic or possessing foxes in setsuwa literature. **[Medieval legend.]**

### 5.5 The Ototari, catfish, and Celestial Fox King narrative

In the *Ototari shingū saimon*, Ototari rescues divinities endangered by a catfish. They vow to serve him and his descendants, crossing mountains and rivers to fulfill their desires. The text calls them the Celestial Fox King and frames them as a manifestation of Monju. A version in the 1408 *Inari ichiryū daiji* integrates the narrative into secret rites for Inari and Dakiniten.

This is not an ancient folk tale preserved unchanged. It is a medieval ritual construction that establishes efficacy, lineage, and Buddhist legitimacy. **[Medieval esoteric elaboration.]**

### 5.6 Dakiniten and the white fox

Indic *ḍākinī* could denote dangerous female flesh-eating beings as well as tantric yogic figures. In Japanese esoteric Buddhism these materials were reorganized into Dakiniten, a single powerful figure associated with knowledge of death, royal power, worldly benefits, and later a white fox. Medieval rites could be morally ambivalent or secret, precisely because the deity promised extraordinary efficacy.

The fox-riding female image then interacted with Inari’s fox complex. At Toyokawa, Dakini Shinten may carry grain rather than the sword common in other images. The result is reciprocal transformation: Dakiniten becomes more agricultural and benevolent; Inari acquires Buddhist jewel, fox-mount, esoteric, and wish-fulfilling imagery. **[Syncretistic identification.]**

Claims that the fusion proves Inari was “originally Hindu” are false. The Japanese Inari cult is documented independently; Dakiniten is one major medieval contributor to its later forms.

### 5.7 Fox transformation, possession, and revelation

*Konjaku monogatari-shū* and later collections tell of foxes appearing as women, wives, monks, or other humans; seducing, deceiving, rewarding, possessing, or frightening people. Some later cults classify benevolent foxes as Inari’s *zenko* and harmful wild foxes as *yako* or *nogitsune*.

That classification is unstable. A fox in a medieval tale is not automatically Inari. Conversely, popular possession cults could name the possessing power Inari even when priests rejected the identification. Smyers’s fieldwork shows that dream, possession, healing, and private revelation remain mechanisms for creating personalized Inari identities. **[Folklore and modern ethnography.]**

### 5.8 Wish fulfillment and miracle cults

Early-modern Inari shrines proliferated through stories of successful cures, commercial fortune, recovered status, protection from fire, and dream commands to establish shrines. These narratives often created a named local Inari whose efficacy was highly specialized. Their diversity, not a centrally standardized myth, was the engine of expansion.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Fushimi Inari Taisha

Fushimi, Kyoto, is the principal historical center and self-identified head shrine of Inari worship.

- **Traditional foundation:** 711 on Mount Inari.
- **Court recognition:** divine rank after the 827 Tōji dispute; listed as three seats in the *Engishiki* in 927; highest rank by 942 according to shrine chronology.
- **Twenty-Two Shrines:** admitted into the elite Heian shrine group, reflecting court importance.
- **Relocation:** the principal buildings moved from the summit area to the mountain’s foot in the medieval period, while the peaks remained sacred.
- **Destruction:** buildings burned in 1468 during the Ōnin War.
- **Reconstruction:** present main sanctuary completed in 1499; later embellished and protected as an Important Cultural Property.
- **Rōmon:** associated with Toyotomi Hideyoshi’s vow for his mother’s recovery; the surviving structure belongs to the late sixteenth-century rebuilding.
- **Torii:** donated by worshippers from the Edo period onward, inscribed with donor and date. They materialize reciprocal relations: a petitioner gives a gate in request or gratitude.
- **Mountain practice:** pilgrimage circuits pass named *otsuka*, small altars, fox statues, former shrine sites, and summit peaks. Many *otsuka* carry individualized names and are maintained by private devotional associations.

### “Thirty thousand shrines”

Fushimi states that Japan has **about 30,000 Inari shrines**. This is an institutional estimate, not a complete audited census. The real number depends on whether one counts only legally recognized shrines, Buddhist temples called Inari, roadside and household shrines, company roof shrines, field altars, and private *hokora*. Claims of 32,000, 40,000, or “one third of all Japanese shrines” use differing criteria.

### Other major centers

- **Toyokawa Inari / Myōgonji, Aichi:** Sōtō Zen temple centered on Toyokawa Dakini Shinten. Major example of post-Meiji Buddhist Inari survival.
- **Saijō Inari / Myōkyōji, Okayama:** Nichiren Buddhist Inari complex.
- **Kasama Inari, Ibaraki:** major eastern-Japan shrine, traditionally founded in the seventh century, though its earliest history requires the same caution applied to retrospective foundation dates.
- **Yūtoku Inari, Saga:** founded in the late seventeenth century, a major Kyushu center.
- **Ōji Inari, Tokyo:** famous for the New Year fox-fire legend and Edo pilgrimage.
- **Takekoma Inari, Miyagi:** shrine tradition attributes transfer from Fushimi to the Kōnin era, 810–824.
- Countless castle, mansion, village, fishing, theater, factory, and corporate Inari shrines represent local specialization.

### Priesthood and authority

The Hata descendants appear as hereditary ritual authorities in early and medieval foundation traditions. Later Fushimi priestly families asserted exclusive knowledge for dividing and installing properly authorized Inari spirits. A 1792 inquiry shows officials challenging unauthorized issuance of “Senior First Rank Inari” objects by outsiders. Fushimi priests insisted that true Inari was not a fox spirit and required petitioners to reject occult misconduct.

This was an attempt to regulate a cult that had already escaped centralized control. **[Documented early-modern institutional conflict.]**

### Festivals

- **Hatsu-uma:** first Horse day of the second month; pilgrimage, offerings, food, and renewal.
- **Inari-sai:** Fushimi’s major spring festival; the five kami travel in portable shrines through parish districts connected historically with southern Kyoto and Tōji.
- **Motomiya-sai:** devotees and affiliated shrines return to the head shrine.
- **Hitaki-sai/fire rites:** large numbers of prayer tablets are burned.
- Planting, harvest, business-opening, factory-safety, household, and local calendrical rites vary substantially.

### Offerings and prayer

Common offerings include rice, sake, water, salt, vegetables, fruit, candles, flags, torii, fox statues, and fried tofu. Petitionary categories include harvest, household peace, business prosperity, traffic safety, performing arts, fertility, healing, and success in personal undertakings.

Inari practice may be:

- formal shrine liturgy conducted by priests;
- Buddhist chanting and esoteric ritual;
- pilgrimage and circumambulation;
- private household or company worship;
- mediumistic revelation;
- dream-based foundation of a named local Inari;
- divination through *omikuji*;
- maintenance of an *otsuka* by a lay association.

### Economics

Inari’s expansion depended on the ability to divide and install the divine presence, the low spatial requirements of a small shrine, urban demand for practical benefits, donor-funded torii and images, pilgrimage traffic, and the formation of devotional associations. Edo merchants installed Inari on shop premises; warrior households used estate shrines; theaters maintained backstage shrines; modern companies place Inari on rooftops or industrial grounds.

### Sacrifice and contested practices

- Rice, sake, fish, vegetables, and other food offerings are extensively documented.
- Animal sacrifice is not a defining institutional feature of historical Inari worship.
- **Human sacrifice:** no reliable evidence was found that Fushimi Inari or mainstream Inari institutions practiced human sacrifice. Dakiniten texts describe dangerous beings desiring human flesh or hearts and rites aimed at controlling them, but mythic appetite is not evidence of an Inari temple conducting human sacrifice.
- **Temple prostitution:** no evidence establishes “sacred prostitution” as an Inari ritual institution. Inari’s patronage in pleasure quarters reflects location, occupation, protection, and prosperity—not temple-organized sexual rites.
- **Fox possession and coercive exorcism:** possession diagnoses and exorcistic responses are documented in folklore and Buddhist practice. They could stigmatize households reputed to own fox spirits. Such accusations should not be converted into proof of literal spirit ownership.

## 7. Historical evolution of the cult and figure

### Before the eighth century: reconstructive background

Agricultural communities worshipped field, mountain, food, and grain spirits before surviving records name Inari. Fox veneration and fear also had wider East Asian backgrounds. Connecting any particular prehistoric archaeological object to “Inari” is **[Unestablished]**.

The Hata were an immigrant-descended kin group associated with Yamashiro, agriculture, irrigation, sericulture, and court service. The idea that Inari was simply imported intact from Korea is not demonstrated. The evidence instead suggests a Yamashiro cult formed among local landscape, rice ritual, Hata lineage, and wider East Asian religious resources. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction.]**

### Early eighth century: foundation and court compilation

The traditional 711 foundation and the *Yamashiro fudoki* story establish the Hata, Mount Inari, rice, and miraculous bird. The *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* independently preserve Ukanomitama genealogies but do not explicitly equate that deity with Inari.

### Ninth–tenth centuries: court rank, Tōji, and elite shrine status

The move of the capital to Heian-kyō placed Inari near a major political and religious center. Relations with Tōji and Shingon Buddhism increased institutional reach. The 827 court response to Inari’s anger, later promotions, the *Engishiki* listing, and inclusion among elite shrines transformed a clan cult into a court-recognized protector.

The cult was not “pure Shinto” invaded later by Buddhism; its historically visible expansion occurred inside a religious order where kami and Buddhism were already intertwined.

### Eleventh–twelfth centuries: pilgrimage and popularization

Court diaries and literature attest visits to Inari Mountain. The shrine became available to women and ordinary petitioners as well as official ritual. Fox narratives flourished in setsuwa collections, though the link of each fox to Inari remained variable.

### Thirteenth–fifteenth centuries: mythology and esoteric systematization

This is the decisive period for elaborate Inari mythology. Shingon, Tendai, shrine, and regional authors produced competing *engi*, *saimon*, secret transmissions, and rites. Inari could be:

- a Japanese kami with an independent sacred mountain;
- protector of Tōji;
- manifestation of Monju;
- linked to Dakiniten;
- Celestial Fox King;
- a continental or Indian-origin power;
- patron of royal enthronement rites;
- compassionate helper of persons lacking merit.

Matthew Keller argues that coherence came from maintaining ambiguity across institutions, not from producing one orthodoxy. **[Modern scholarly synthesis grounded in medieval texts.]**

### Fifteenth–sixteenth centuries: war, destruction, and reconstruction

Fushimi’s buildings burned during the Ōnin conflict in 1468. The present main sanctuary dates to 1499. Elite patrons, including Toyotomi Hideyoshi, contributed to reconstruction and used Inari petitionary worship. Political patronage did not erase popular access.

### Edo period, 1603–1868: national proliferation

This was likely the greatest phase of diffusion:

- urban household and shop shrines multiplied;
- warriors maintained estate tutelaries;
- merchants recast rice abundance as monetary and commercial prosperity;
- theaters and pleasure quarters adopted Inari;
- healing and fire protection became prominent;
- charismatic specialists and shrine propagators spread miracle stories;
- torii donation expanded;
- local “faddish deities” appeared rapidly and could decline just as rapidly.

The decentralization produced institutional disputes over authentic installation and the difference between Inari and wild fox spirits.

### 1868–1945: separation, State Shinto, and purification of categories

The Meiji government’s **shinbutsu bunri** measures ordered the institutional separation of kami and buddhas. The associated *haibutsu kishaku* movement destroyed Buddhist images, books, buildings, and clerical livelihoods.

At Fushimi, visibly Buddhist elements were removed or reclassified. Buddhist Inari institutions survived by insisting that their principal deity was Dakiniten rather than a Shinto kami. Toyokawa Inari remained a Sōtō temple. Popular practice continued to cross official boundaries.

Fushimi was ranked a government-supported **kanpei taisha**, an imperial shrine of the highest category. Inari’s agricultural and productive meanings could fit state programs of national prosperity, but its highly personalized, commercial, and Buddhist forms never became completely controllable.

### 1945–present: disestablishment and plural continuity

Occupation reforms ended government sponsorship of State Shinto. Many shrines joined Jinja Honchō, but Fushimi Inari operates independently. Buddhist Inari centers, local shrines, household cults, companies, mediums, and private associations continue simultaneously.

Fushimi’s “about 30,000 shrines” figure signals extraordinary reach but not doctrinal unity. Contemporary devotees may understand Inari as Ukanomitama, five kami, Dakiniten, a family protector, a local fox-associated revelation, or a presence without form.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and ethnography

Karen A. Smyers’s *The Fox and the Jewel* (1999), preceded by her 1996 article “‘My Own Inari,’” is the central English-language ethnography. Its major contribution is not merely a catalogue of beliefs but the demonstration that apparently unified Inari worship contains intense personalization. Priests may emphasize institutional orthodoxy; lay practitioners may establish named *otsuka*, receive dreams, combine sects, or understand fox, snake, jewel, and gender in private ways.

The jewel represents shared institutional symbolism and concentrated power; the fox expresses ambiguity, individuality, and boundary-crossing.

### Folklore studies

Yanagita Kunio and successors placed Inari within grain-spirit, field-god, mountain-god, fox-possession, and household-religion patterns. Hori Ichirō emphasized Japanese folk religion’s practical, shamanic, and community dimensions. Michael Dylan Foster’s *The Book of Yōkai* helps distinguish the yōkai fox from an automatic Inari identity and shows how early-modern catalogues shaped modern “Japanese monster” taxonomies.

### Occult and neopagan reception

Western occult and neopagan sources often recast Inari as:

- a universal “fox goddess”;
- a gender-fluid patron of witches;
- a deity assigned fixed colors, crystals, tarot correspondences, moon phases, or planetary rulership;
- an equivalent of European trickster or fertility gods;
- a spirit available through generic “deity work.”

The gender variability and fox connection are historically grounded; most fixed correspondence tables are **[Modern invention]** unless derived from a named Japanese institution. There is no evidence that Renaissance Hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, or early Theosophy maintained a significant, historically continuous Inari cult. Contemporary online worship outside Japan exists, but its scale is not securely measured and ranges from shrine-affiliated practice to eclectic neopagan adaptation.

### Literature and visual art

Lafcadio Hearn’s *Glimpses of Unfamiliar Japan* (1894) greatly shaped Western images of fox worship, possession, and Inari statues. His descriptions are valuable eyewitness and literary evidence for Meiji-period popular religion but are not ancient testimony.

Ukiyo-e and later prints represented:

- Ōji fox fires;
- actors praying to theater Inari;
- shrine pilgrimage;
- fox weddings and transformations;
- Inari and Dakiniten as distinguishable or blended figures.

Toriyama Sekien’s catalogues helped stabilize yōkai imagery for later artists, although his foxes are not all Inari.

### Film, manga, anime, and games

Modern works repeatedly transform Inari into a fox woman, fox-eared girl, magical patron, or generalized shrine spirit. Examples include:

- *Inari, Konkon, Koi Iroha*, which personifies Uka-no-Mitama as a female shrine deity and builds a modern romantic-fantasy divine household;
- *Kakuriyo* and numerous supernatural manga/anime using fox attendants and Inari-like shrines;
- *Ghost of Tsushima*, whose foxes guide the player to “Inari shrines,” turning the messenger into a navigation mechanic;
- *Persona*, *Shin Megami Tensei*, *Pokémon*, *Naruto*, and other franchises that use kitsune imagery, though not every nine-tailed or shrine fox is Inari.

A major modern distortion is the automatic equation:

> kitsune = Inari = nine-tailed fox = fox-eared goddess.

Historically, these categories overlap but are not identical. Nine-tailed fox traditions often derive from Chinese and continental omen or demon lore; fox-wife narratives and possession traditions need not involve Inari.

### Tourism and commercialization

Fushimi’s torii tunnels have become one of Japan’s most internationally recognized visual sites. Commercial imagery frequently removes their donor inscriptions and ritual function, turning the gates into a generic sign of “mystical Japan.” Fox masks, keychains, anime designs, food branding, corporate shrines, and tourism media continue the cult’s long association with exchange and prosperity.

### Nationalism and extremism

Fushimi participated in the State Shinto shrine-ranking system, and the broader shrine world was incorporated into imperial ideology. Inari, however, was not a principal ideological war deity comparable to Yasukuni’s enshrined war dead or imperial Amaterasu discourse. No well-documented modern extremist movement centered specifically on Inari emerged from the sources surveyed. General nationalist appropriation of “Shinto” should not be attributed automatically to every Inari shrine.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is Inari an ancient “Shinto god”?

Older histories often described an unbroken indigenous Shinto religion existing since prehistory. Kuroda Toshio’s 1981 essay challenged this model, arguing that medieval kami worship functioned largely within the Buddhist *kenmitsu* system and that “Shinto” as a distinct autonomous religion was much later.

Breen and Teeuwen accept the need to distinguish ancient kami, shrines, rites, and myths from the later abstraction “Shinto,” while allowing more premodern institutional development than the strongest reading of Kuroda. Inari strongly supports the revisionist approach: its most successful medieval forms are simultaneously shrine-based, Buddhist, local, courtly, and popular.

### Is Inari Ukanomitama?

- **Institutional position:** at Fushimi, Ukanomitama is the central seat and principal ancient identity.
- **Supporting evidence:** food/grain semantics; Ukanomitama appears consistently among divergent lists of Fushimi deities; *Engishiki* ritual glosses connect *uka no mitama* with grain spirit.
- **Qualification:** neither the *Kojiki* nor *Nihon shoki* calls Ukanomitama “Inari”; Fushimi is collectively fivefold; other Inari centers worship other divine configurations.

The equation is historically powerful and probably old, but it is not a complete definition.

### Etymology

The “rice becoming/ripening” derivation is ancient as an etiological explanation but may be folk etymology. The earliest phonetic spelling 伊奈利 does not settle original morphology. The modern spelling 稲荷 is securely visible by 827; its literal “rice-carrying” appearance may have reinforced agricultural interpretation.

### Indigenous or immigrant origin?

The Hata connection is textually strong. Older scholarship sometimes made Inari a Korean or continental import; nationalist accounts made it purely indigenous. Neither extreme is proven. The cult likely emerged from Hata lineage interests, Yamashiro landscape, rice and grain ritual, and religious materials circulating through immigrant-descended communities. “Hata origin” does not equal “a complete foreign deity imported unchanged.”

### Origin of the fox

No consensus exists. Agricultural ecology, granary protection, taboo vocabulary, field/mountain liminality, Dakiniten, and Chinese-Korean fox traditions all remain relevant. The fox appears to have accumulated meanings through multiple routes.

### Gender

The evidence does not support one original gender followed by a single linear transition. Grammatical and genealogical placement, iconography, Buddhist fusion, institutional preference, and personal revelation generate different sexes and forms. “Gender-fluid Inari” is a useful modern summary only if it does not imply that ancient Japanese sources articulated contemporary gender theory.

### Primary-evidence condition

- The *Yamashiro fudoki* original is lost.
- Many Inari origin texts survive in later manuscripts, compilations, and temple archives.
- Medieval materials remain dispersed among shrine archives, Tōji, Mt. Kōya lineages, the Kanazawa Bunko/Shōmyōji collection, and printed *Shintō taikei* volumes.
- Shrine destruction in 1468 and anti-Buddhist destruction after 1868 caused losses.
- Modern critical work is still recovering manuscripts and comparing institutional recensions.

Matthew Keller’s recent work is important because it treats the diversity of medieval origin stories as evidence rather than corruption.

### Discredited or weakly supported claims still circulating

- “Inari is simply the Japanese fox goddess.” **False simplification.**
- “The fox is always Inari’s physical form.” **Contradicted by institutional teaching and independent fox lore.**
- “Fushimi’s ten thousand torii date from 711.” **False; donations proliferated from the Edo period.**
- “Inari is unquestionably female.” **Contradicted by male, multiple, and non-gendered forms.**
- “Inari is unquestionably male.” **Likewise false.**
- “Inari was imported intact from India as Dakini.” **Chronologically impossible; the cult is independently attested before the mature fusion.**
- “Every kitsune story concerns Inari.” **False.**
- “Inari worship involved temple prostitution.” **No supporting evidence found.**
- “Inari demanded historical human sacrifice.” **No supporting evidence found; confusion arises from Dakiniten demonology.**
- “The Kojiki gives the complete canonical story of Inari.” **False; it gives one sentence about Ukanomitama and never uses the name Inari.**
- “There are exactly 30,000 Inari shrines.” **Institutional estimate, not exact census.**
- “Sekien’s yōkai catalogues are ancient mythology.” **They are eighteenth-century artistic and literary systematizations.**

## 10. Chronology

- **Pre-eighth century:** field, food, mountain, granary, and fox-related beliefs provide possible background. No securely identified “Inari” evidence. **[Reconstruction.]**
- **711:** traditional foundation of the Mount Inari cult by Hata no Irogu on the first Horse day of the second month. **[Later shrine chronology.]**
- **712:** *Kojiki* names Ukanomitama as child of Susanoo and Kamu-Ōichihime. **[Secure text.]**
- **After 713:** *Yamashiro no kuni fudoki* compiled; lost fragment preserves Hata rice-cake/bird/rice origin. **[Early tradition, later quotation.]**
- **720:** *Nihon shoki* variant makes Ukanomitama child of Izanagi and Izanami during hunger. **[Secure text.]**
- **807:** *Kogo shūi* documents early court ritual and food-deity context.
- **810–824:** later traditions place early branch enshrinements and Kūkai/Inari relations in this era.
- **827:** Tōji wood-cutting incident; divine anger and junior fifth rank recorded in *Ruijū kokushi*. Earliest secure modern spelling 稲荷 identified by Fushimi.
- **892:** completion of *Ruijū kokushi*, preserving the 827 record.
- **905–927:** *Engishiki* compilation.
- **927:** *Engishiki Jinmyōchō* lists Inari Shrine’s three seats.
- **942:** Fushimi shrine chronology records elevation to senior first rank.
- **Tenth century:** *Tenryaku Jingikan kanmon* restates Hata priestly and foundation claims; court literature begins documenting pilgrimage.
- **Late tenth–eleventh centuries:** Inari Mountain appears in elite diaries and literary works.
- **Early twelfth century:** *Konjaku monogatari-shū* records numerous fox tales, not all tied to Inari.
- **1194:** imperial visit later invoked by Fushimi priests as authorization for ranked branch enshrinement; authorization absent from surviving visit record.
- **Thirteenth–fourteenth centuries:** esoteric Inari origin texts, *saimon*, Dakiniten relations, Monju manifestation theory, and Celestial Fox King rites proliferate.
- **Late thirteenth–early fourteenth century:** Kenna copies the *Ototari shingū saimon*.
- **1408:** *Inari ichiryū daiji* produced on Mt. Kōya.
- **Fifteenth century:** a large, deliberately plural medieval Inari mythology is in circulation across shrine, Shingon, Tendai, and regional networks.
- **1468:** Fushimi buildings destroyed during the Ōnin War.
- **1499:** reconstructed Fushimi main sanctuary completed.
- **Late sixteenth century:** Toyotomi Hideyoshi’s patronage and rōmon tradition.
- **1603–1868:** dramatic spread through villages, warrior estates, shops, theaters, pleasure quarters, healing cults, and local miracle shrines.
- **Edo period:** torii donation becomes a defining Fushimi practice.
- **1776–1784:** Toriyama Sekien publishes influential illustrated yōkai catalogues.
- **1792:** Fushimi priests respond to official inquiry over unauthorized Senior First Rank Inari enshrinements and distinguish the deity from fox spirits.
- **1798:** completion of Motoori Norinaga’s *Kojiki-den* strengthens Kokugaku re-reading of classical kami.
- **1868:** *shinbutsu bunri* begins; Buddhist objects and institutions are removed from or separated from shrines.
- **Late 1860s–1870s:** *haibutsu kishaku* destroys Buddhist cultural property; Inari institutions negotiate forced categorization.
- **1871–1946:** Fushimi holds the highest government-supported shrine rank.
- **1894:** Lafcadio Hearn publishes influential descriptions of Inari fox imagery and belief.
- **1910:** Yanagita’s *Tōno monogatari* helps establish modern folklore studies; his later grain-spirit work influences Inari interpretation.
- **1945–1946:** State Shinto dismantled; Fushimi loses state status and continues independently.
- **Postwar:** shrine, Buddhist, domestic, corporate, mediumistic, and popular-cultural Inari forms continue.
- **1981:** Kuroda Toshio publishes the English version of “Shinto in the History of Japanese Religion,” transforming historiography.
- **1994:** Steven Heine analyzes Sōtō Zen, Toyokawa Inari, and the tension between venerated and exorcised foxes.
- **1996:** Smyers publishes “‘My Own Inari.’”
- **1999:** Smyers publishes *The Fox and the Jewel*.
- **2010:** Breen and Teeuwen publish *A New History of Shinto*.
- **2015:** Bernard Faure’s two-volume study of medieval Japanese deities reframes Dakiniten and related fluid pantheons.
- **2025:** Matthew Keller publishes a detailed study of medieval Inari origin stories and their institutional diversity.
- **Present:** Fushimi describes itself as head shrine of roughly 30,000 Inari shrines; the cult remains active within Shinto, Buddhism, folk and private religion, corporate worship, tourism, and popular media.
