# Amaterasu: Research Dossier

**Class:** *kami* (神), conventionally “goddess,” specifically a solar and imperial-ancestral deity; in medieval Buddhist traditions also interpreted as a manifestation, counterpart, or transformed form of buddhas and esoteric deities.  
**Earliest secure attestation:** the completed *Kojiki* (古事記, 712 CE), written in a mixture of Literary Chinese and phonographically used Chinese characters. Its earliest complete surviving manuscript is the Shinpukuji copy of 1371–1372. The *Nihon shoki* (日本書紀, 720), whose early manuscript tradition is fragmentary but substantially older than the complete *Kojiki* witness, independently preserves several variant Amaterasu narratives. No securely identified prehistoric inscription or archaeological image names Amaterasu. Claims that a particular Yayoi-period solar image, Queen Himiko, or an early mirror depicts or represents her are modern reconstructions, not direct attestations.

**Evidentiary labels used below:** **[Early canonical]** eighth-century court compilation; **[Later canonical]** early court ritual or historiographical text; **[Regional variant]** locally transmitted cult or legend; **[Learned systematization]** theological or antiquarian construction; **[Syncretistic]** Buddhist–kami or cross-traditional identification; **[Medieval/early-modern accretion]** post-classical narrative; **[Folklore]** orally collected or popular tradition; **[Modern reconstruction]** scholarly hypothesis; **[Modern invention]** demonstrably modern representation.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Amaterasu is the solar ruler of Takamagahara, the “Plain of High Heaven,” in Japan’s eighth-century court mythologies; elder sibling and adversary of Susanoo; possessor or archetype of the sacred mirror; sender of the heavenly grandson Ninigi to rule the terrestrial realm; and, through Ninigi’s descendants, ancestral deity of the imperial lineage. This familiar composite is not a timeless Japanese creed. It is first visible in court-sponsored texts of 712 and 720, was ritually anchored at Ise, repeatedly reinterpreted through Buddhism and medieval shrine theology, philologically reconstructed by early-modern *kokugaku*, nationalized under the modern imperial state, and reorganized as the teaching of private religious institutions after 1945. Kokugakuin’s summary properly distinguishes the *Kojiki* birth from Izanagi’s eye from the *Nihon shoki* main account in which Izanagi and Izanami deliberately produce a ruler of the universe. ([Kokugakuin, “Amaterasu”](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9440))

### Names

- **天照大御神 — Amaterasu Ōmikami**; Old Japanese approximately *Amaterasu Opomi₁kami₂*: “the great august kami who shines in heaven.” This is the principal *Kojiki* form. *Amaterasu* is grammatically an attributive verbal expression, not necessarily an opaque personal name: *ama* (“heaven”) + *terasu* (“to shine upon/illuminate,” with the exact morphology debated). *Ō-mi-kami* combines “great,” an honorific, and *kami*.
- **天照大神 — Amaterasu Ōkami**, also read in Sino-Japanese as **Tenshō Daijin**: common in the *Nihon shoki*, medieval texts, Buddhist discourse, and later inscriptions.
- **大日孁貴 — Ōhirume no Muchi** (graphic variants include 大日霎貴): a major *Nihon shoki* name, usually parsed “Great Honored Woman of the Sun/Day.” *Hi* may mean sun/day; *me* is woman or female. *Muchi* is an honorific also found in other divine names. “Great spirit-woman” and “woman/priestess of the sun” are alternative scholarly readings, not settled ancient glosses.
- **天照大日孁尊 — Amaterasu Ōhirume no Mikoto**: expanded *Nihon shoki* variant.
- **日神 — Hi no Kami**, “Sun Kami”: generic designation in the *Nihon shoki*.
- **天照皇大神 — Amaterasu Sume-Ōmikami / Amaterasu Kōtaijin**, “heaven-shining sovereign great deity”: court and shrine title emphasizing imperial sovereignty.
- **皇大御神 / 皇大神 — Sume Ōmikami / Kōtaijin**, “Sovereign Great Deity”: especially associated with Ise’s Inner Shrine, formally **Kōtaijingū** (皇大神宮).
- **神明 — Shinmei**, “illustrious/numinous deity”: a medieval and early-modern collective or abbreviated designation for Amaterasu and Ise-derived worship. Shrines called *Shinmei-sha* commonly enshrine her.
- **撞賢木厳之御魂天疎向津媛命 — Tsukisakaki Izu no Mitama Amazakaru Mukatsuhime no Mikoto**: the elaborate name spoken in the *Nihon shoki* Jingū narrative, traditionally interpreted as Amaterasu’s *aramitama*, her forceful or active divine aspect, enshrined at Hirota. The identification should not be reversed into proof that the earliest Hirota deity was simply “Amaterasu under another name.”
- **Amaterasu no Ōkami**, **Amaterasu Ōmikami**, and Anglicized “Sun Goddess” are current forms. “Queen of the gods” is an interpretive paraphrase: the early texts give her supreme authority in Takamagahara but do not construct a systematic Graeco-Roman-style pantheon.

### Gender and classification

**[Early canonical]** The *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* present Amaterasu through female-coded names, kinship, weaving, and later interpretive conventions, but the title *Amaterasu Ōmikami* itself is not grammatically gendered. Paolo Villani emphasizes that neither the solar title nor Izanagi’s form of address alone identifies the newborn deity’s sex. ([Villani, “Reading, Translating, Sexing Early Japan”](https://www.iris.unict.it/retrieve/10d46c9d-7b79-47c5-a805-8b2813f71231/8_villani.pdf))

**[Medieval/early-modern accretion]** Some medieval theologians understood Tenshō Daijin as male, or as transcending ordinary sex, partly because Amaterasu was correlated with the male-presenting cosmic Buddha Dainichi Nyorai. Later texts and images could alternate masculine, feminine, and androgynous forms. The now-standard phrase “sun goddess” is therefore accurate for dominant classical and modern reception but hides a significant history of interpretation. ([Kokugakuin, “Amaterasu”](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=9440); [Chiba Kei, “Amaterasu and the Modern Japanese Imperial State”](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/genderhistory/2/0/2_0_5/_article))

### Contested etymologies and origin theories

1. **Transparent verbal title:** *ama-terasu*, “shining in/illuminating heaven,” is the least speculative reading.
2. **Ōhirume as “sun woman”:** philologically straightforward and consistent with the received female deity.
3. **Ōhirume as sun priestess or consort:** Orikuchi Shinobu proposed that the figure originated as the human or divine female attendant of a male solar deity. **[Modern reconstruction]** This is a comparative theory, not a statement in the early texts.
4. **Originally male solar god:** proposed from gender-neutral wording, medieval male representations, and comparisons with other solar traditions. **[Modern reconstruction]** The evidence shows historical gender variability; it does not establish a recoverable prehistoric male Amaterasu.
5. **Himiko:** Matsumae and others explored relations between solar cult, female ritual sovereignty, and the third-century queen Himiko. A few writers have gone further and identified Amaterasu with Himiko or Empress Jitō. **[Modern reconstruction]** Neither the Chinese *Wei zhi* account of Himiko nor the Japanese chronicles identify her as Amaterasu; the linguistic resemblances are insufficient to establish identity.
6. **Continental derivation:** parallels have been proposed with Korean, Chinese, Southeast Asian, Central Asian, and circumpolar sun myths. These can illuminate motifs—solar siblings, a hidden sun, ritual laughter—but no consensus derivation of Amaterasu from a named foreign deity has been demonstrated.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest securely dated evidence

The first unequivocal record is textual, not archaeological:

- **712 — *Kojiki*. [Early canonical]** Ō no Yasumaro’s preface says he wrote down and organized traditions recited by Hieda no Are under an imperial commission associated with Emperor Tenmu and Empress Genmei. Amaterasu is born from Izanagi’s left eye during purification after Yomi; rules Takamagahara; engages Susanoo in an *ukei* pledge; withdraws into the rock cave; receives Susanoo’s sword; directs the transfer of the terrestrial land; and sends Ninigi with the mirror. The text’s 712 completion is secure, but its surviving complete manuscript is medieval; it is evidence for an early eighth-century court redaction of inherited materials, not a transcript of prehistoric belief.
- **720 — *Nihon shoki*. [Early canonical]** A court chronicle in Literary Chinese, organized as a main account followed by variant “one writing says” traditions. Its plurality is crucial: Amaterasu may be born deliberately to Izanagi and Izanami; from Izanagi’s left eye during ablution; or when he holds a bronze mirror. Different variants alter the oath, field destruction, weaving-hall injury, cave episode, and heavenly descent. Aston’s translation remains useful but its Victorian language and assumptions require checking against the Japanese text.
- **c. 713 onward — provincial *Fudoki*. [Regional evidence]** Surviving or quoted gazetteers preserve local deities and place traditions. Their comparative importance lies partly in how little they say about Amaterasu: they demonstrate that the centralized Amaterasu genealogy did not erase diverse provincial divine landscapes.
- **late seventh–eighth centuries — *Man’yōshū*. [Early poetic corpus]** The anthology uses solar and imperial imagery and records Princess Ōku, associated with the Ise *saiō* institution. It is valuable evidence for court-poetic solar language, but it does not retell the full cave myth as a cult catechism.
- **807 — *Kogo Shūi*. [Later canonical/lineage apologetic]** Inbe no Hironari’s memorial preserves traditions of the Inbe ritual clan, including the cave preparations and divine craftsmen. It is explicitly interested in clan precedence and supplements the court chronicles; its additions cannot automatically be projected before 712.
- **804 — *Kōtaijingū Gishikichō*. [Institutional record]** A protocol of the Inner Shrine’s buildings, personnel, offerings, and rites; among the earliest detailed documentary witnesses to Ise as an operating institution.
- **927 — *Engishiki*. [Later canonical ritual code]** Fifty books of court regulations, including Ise administration and twenty-seven formal *norito*. The prayers link sovereign rule, harvest, offerings, purification, and heavenly deities. They document actual court ritual norms more directly than the mythic narratives do. Felicia Bock and Donald Philippi’s editions/translations remain standard points of entry; an accessible corpus identifies books 16–24 as Ise-related liturgies. ([Engishiki norito corpus](https://norito.sumeragi-iyasaka.com/en/))
- **tenth–fourteenth centuries — Ise records and shrine compilations. [Institutional/learned systematization]** These include *Jingū zatsureishū*, *Yamatohime no Mikoto Seiki*, and Watarai or Outer Shrine writings. Some preserve older regulations; others recast Ise in medieval Buddhist cosmology.
- **c. 1120 — *Konjaku monogatari-shū*. [Medieval narrative]** A vast Buddhist tale collection. It belongs to the mixed religious world in which kami appear within karmic and Buddhist narrative frameworks, but it is not a major independent source for the classical Amaterasu cycle.
- **medieval *Reikiki*, *Daijingū engi*, *Bikisho* (1324), and Ise-related secret texts. [Syncretistic]** These identify or associate Amaterasu with Dainichi Nyorai, Brahmā, Dakiniten, wish-fulfilling jewels, serpent bodies, and esoteric initiation. Such material is evidence for medieval religion, not an encoded prehistoric “original Shinto.”
- **Toriyama Sekien’s yōkai catalogues (1776–1784).** They are essential for Edo-period yōkai taxonomy but furnish no major primary Amaterasu tradition. Treating every famous Japanese supernatural figure as present in Sekien is erroneous.
- **Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den* (1798 completed; published serially). [Learned systematization]** A monumental philological commentary that privileged the *Kojiki*’s Japanese wording over the *Nihon shoki*’s Chinese rhetoric and helped establish the modern reading and authority of the myths.
- **Hirata Atsutane, early nineteenth century. [Learned systematization]** Extended *kokugaku* into a more systematic theology, spirit world, and national cosmology. His system is not a transparent survival of antiquity.
- **Yanagita Kunio, *Tōno monogatari* (1910) and folklore studies. [Folklore scholarship]** Vital for local spirits, ritual specialists, and oral tradition, but *Tōno monogatari* is not a source for a hidden canonical Amaterasu cycle. Its relative absence of Amaterasu is itself evidence against reducing Japanese vernacular religion to the imperial deity.
- **Hori Ichirō. [Modern scholarship]** Documented Japanese folk religion, sacred specialists, pilgrimage, and popular practice, helping distinguish local religion from centralized shrine theology.

### What the early evidence does not establish

It does not securely tell us:

- when a deity first called Amaterasu was worshipped before the eighth century;
- whether a prehistoric solar deity was male, female, or a paired cult;
- whether Himiko, Empress Jitō, or another ruler was the “model” for Amaterasu;
- whether the Ise mirror is materially the same object imagined by the *Kojiki*;
- whether the cave narrative records a literal eclipse;
- or whether all Japanese communities accepted the court genealogy.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain and powers

**[Early canonical]**

- **Sun and daylight:** Her seclusion causes pervasive darkness and calamity; her reappearance restores light.
- **Heavenly sovereignty:** Izanagi assigns her Takamagahara in the *Kojiki*. In the heavenly-descent cycle she exercises authority jointly or in parallel with Takamimusubi.
- **Rice and cultivated order:** Susanoo attacks her fields, irrigation works, ritual hall, and weaving operation. Court and Ise ritual later make her a giver or guarantor of rice.
- **Weaving and sacred clothing:** She oversees a heavenly weaving hall. The context is cultic production, not merely domestic textile work.
- **Imperial ancestry and authorization:** She commissions Ninigi’s descent and becomes genealogical source of the ruling house.
- **Oracular power:** In the Jingū narrative and later shrine histories she speaks through possession, dreams, or oracles.
- **Protective yet dangerous divine presence:** Later theology distinguishes peaceful *nigimitama* and forceful *aramitama*. The latter is associated particularly with Hirota.

### Powers and limitations

Amaterasu can illuminate heaven and earth, generate children through an oath involving exchanged objects, commission other deities, curse the returning arrow that kills Ame no Wakahiko, and authorize terrestrial rule. Yet she does not act as an omnipotent creator: she fears Susanoo’s approach, fortifies herself, retreats after his pollution and violence, depends upon the assembled kami to restore her, and delegates terrestrial conquest. Her authority is therefore high but relational and ritually mediated.

### Emblems and sacred objects

- **Yata no Kagami (八咫鏡), the “eight-span mirror.”** Crafted for the cave operation; in the *Kojiki* given to Ninigi with an injunction conventionally understood as treating it like Amaterasu’s own spirit or presence. The object traditionally housed at Ise is never publicly inspected. Its antiquity, material, and identity therefore cannot be independently verified.
- **Yasakani no Magatama:** Curved jewel or jewel strand, employed in the cave ritual and later counted among the regalia.
- **Kusanagi/Ame no Murakumo sword:** Taken from Yamata no Orochi by Susanoo and presented to Amaterasu; later transmitted in the descent/regalia complex.
- **Sakaki tree:** Bears mirror, jewels, and cloth offerings in the cave rite.
- **Roosters or “long-crying birds of Tokoyo”:** Made to crow before the cave; their solar association is early, but identifying every rooster as Amaterasu’s universal sacred animal overstates the text.
- **Rice, silk, sacred cloth, hemp, and food offerings:** securely tied to ritual and narrative.
- Claims that Amaterasu has a canonical weapon, zodiac sign, fixed color, magic number, or modern occult correspondence are generally **[Modern invention]** unless tied to a named contemporary system.

### Iconography

Early Amaterasu worship was primarily aniconic or object-centered: mirror, shrine, pillar, offerings, and concealed sacred presence rather than a public anthropomorphic statue. At Ise, access to the central sacred space and mirror is heavily restricted. Consequently there is no securely identified Nara-period “portrait of Amaterasu.”

**[Medieval syncretistic]** Buddhist mandalas and deity paintings could render Tenshō Daijin anthropomorphically—as courtly woman, male deity, child, monk-like figure, or through Dainichi-related imagery. Ise pilgrimage mandalas visually integrated shrines, Buddhist temples, legendary caves, pilgrims, and local topography. The supposed Ama-no-Iwato at Ise could be mapped onto an ancient tomb chamber, showing how landscape was remythologized. ([Andreeva, “The Elderly Nun, the Rain-Treasure Child…”](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/13/7/585))

**[Early-modern/modern]** Woodblock prints and paintings standardize the cave scene: Amaterasu in white or court dress, Uzume dancing, Tajikarao at the door, and the assembled kami laughing. A familiar frontal woman radiating sunbeams is largely a late pictorial convention. Meiji-era representation became politically sensitive: state pedagogy could invoke Amaterasu while avoiding overly ordinary bodily depiction. Chiba argues that regulation of her androgynous possibilities accompanied her appropriation as imperial legitimacy. ([Chiba](https://www.jstage.jst.go.jp/article/genderhistory/2/0/2_0_5/_article))

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Competing origin accounts

| Source | Origin |
|---|---|
| *Kojiki* (712) | Izanagi washes his left eye after returning from Yomi; Amaterasu is born. Tsukuyomi comes from the right eye and Susanoo from the nose. |
| *Nihon shoki* main narrative (720) | Izanagi and Izanami deliberately beget Ōhirume no Muchi, radiant enough to illuminate the universe, and send her upward to rule heaven. |
| *Nihon shoki* variant | Izanagi washes his left eye, producing Amaterasu. |
| *Nihon shoki* variant | Izanagi holds a bronze mirror in his left hand and produces Ōhirume; a right-hand mirror produces Tsukuyomi. |

Thus **Izanagi alone** is the parent in the dominant *Kojiki* account; **Izanagi and Izanami** are parents in the *Nihon shoki* main account. Presenting Izanami unconditionally as Amaterasu’s mother silently harmonizes incompatible versions.

### Children and descent

During the *ukei* with Susanoo, each deity chews and exhales divine objects belonging to the other. The logic of parentage varies with whether ownership of the “seed” or the performer’s body determines descent.

Amaterasu claims the five male kami generated from her jewels:

1. **Ame no Oshihomimi**
2. **Ame no Hohi**
3. **Amatsuhikone**
4. **Ikutsuhikone**
5. **Kumanokusubi**

Susanoo claims the three female Munakata kami generated from his sword:

1. **Tagorihime**
2. **Ichikishimahime**
3. **Tagitsuhime**

The sources differ over who “won” and why. In the *Kojiki*, Susanoo treats the production of gentle female children as proof of his purity; variants of the *Nihon shoki* use different gender tests and ownership logic. The episode is ritual generation, not sexual intercourse.

Ame no Oshihomimi marries Takuhatachijihime, daughter of Takamimusubi in standard genealogies, and fathers **Ninigi**. Ninigi fathers the line that eventually reaches **Jimmu**, the legendary first emperor. This makes Amaterasu Jimmu’s divine ancestress, but the chain is a court mythic genealogy, not independently documented biological history.

### Every specific divine interaction in the early and major later record

The following separates direct scenes from genealogical association. It is comprehensive for the named interactions central to the *Kojiki*, *Nihon shoki*, *Kogo Shūi*, Ise foundation tradition, and major medieval identifications; innumerable local shrine liturgies cannot be proven exhaustive.

- **Izanagi —** produces Amaterasu from his left eye and assigns her Takamagahara. *Kojiki* (712). **[Early canonical]**
- **Izanagi and Izanami —** deliberately beget the brilliant Ōhirume no Muchi and send her to heaven. *Nihon shoki* main account (720). **[Early canonical variant]**
- **Tsukuyomi —** sibling, assigned the night/moon realm in the classical triad. *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* (712/720). **[Early canonical]**
- **Tsukuyomi —** Amaterasu sends Tsukuyomi to Ukemochi; after Tsukuyomi kills the food deity, Amaterasu condemns him and separates day from night. *Nihon shoki* (720). **[Early canonical variant]** This episode is absent from the *Kojiki*, where Susanoo kills the food deity Ōgetsuhime. Calling Tsukuyomi Amaterasu’s husband or describing a divorce is a later harmonization unsupported by the early text.
- **Susanoo —** comes to take leave before descending to the underworld; Amaterasu arms and dresses for combat, suspecting conquest. *Kojiki*/*Nihon shoki* (712/720). **[Early canonical]**
- **Susanoo —** participates with her in the *ukei*, producing five male and three female kami. Same sources. **[Early canonical; variant outcomes]**
- **Susanoo —** destroys her fields and irrigation, pollutes the sacred hall, and throws a backward-flayed horse into the weaving hall; Amaterasu retreats. Same sources. **[Early canonical; details vary]**
- **Susanoo —** after killing Orochi, sends or presents the sword later called Kusanagi to Amaterasu. Same sources. **[Early canonical]**
- **Ame no Oshihomimi —** claimed as Amaterasu’s son in the pledge; initially chosen for descent but reports the terrestrial land in disorder and later yields the mission to Ninigi. *Kojiki*/*Nihon shoki*. **[Early canonical]**
- **Ame no Hohi —** son sent to Ōkuninushi’s realm; fails to report back, becoming attached to the terrestrial ruler. *Kojiki*/*Nihon shoki*. **[Early canonical]**
- **Amatsuhikone —** produced and claimed as her son in the pledge. *Kojiki*/*Nihon shoki*. **[Early canonical]** No separate narrative meeting survives.
- **Ikutsuhikone —** produced and claimed as her son. Same sources. **[Early canonical]** No separate narrative meeting survives.
- **Kumanokusubi —** produced and claimed as her son. Same sources. **[Early canonical]** No separate narrative meeting survives.
- **Tagorihime, Ichikishimahime, Tagitsuhime —** generated from Susanoo’s sword in the joint pledge; allocated to Susanoo by ownership logic and later worshipped as the Munakata deities. Same sources. **[Early canonical]**
- **Wakahirume —** in a *Nihon shoki* variant, this “Young Sun Woman” is weaving when Susanoo’s flayed horse causes her fatal injury; other versions have Amaterasu herself wounded or an unnamed weaving maiden killed. *Nihon shoki* (720). **[Early canonical variant]**
- **Omoikane —** devises the plan to draw Amaterasu from the cave and later advises heavenly policy. *Kojiki*/*Nihon shoki*. **[Early canonical]**
- **Ame no Koyane —** performs divination and liturgical speech before the cave; later accompanies Ninigi. Same sources. **[Early canonical]**
- **Futodama —** holds or arranges ritual offerings and participates in divination; later accompanies Ninigi. Same sources; elaborated in *Kogo Shūi* (807). **[Early canonical/later clan elaboration]**
- **Ishikoridome —** crafts the mirror used to attract Amaterasu. *Kojiki*/*Nihon shoki*; elaborated in *Kogo Shūi*. **[Early canonical]**
- **Tamanooya —** produces the jewel string used at the cave. Same sources. **[Early canonical]**
- **Ame no Uzume —** overturns a tub, becomes divinely possessed, exposes herself, dances, and causes the gods to laugh; tells Amaterasu that a deity more august than she is present. Same sources. **[Early canonical]**
- **Ame no Tajikarao —** seizes Amaterasu’s hand and draws her from the cave. Same sources. **[Early canonical]**
- **Takamimusubi —** shares or overlaps with Amaterasu’s authority in planning the pacification of the terrestrial realm and Ninigi’s descent. *Kojiki*/*Nihon shoki*. **[Early canonical]** Some textual strata foreground Takamimusubi more strongly than Amaterasu.
- **Ame no Wakahiko —** dispatched in the land-transfer mission; his bloodied arrow reaches heaven, where Amaterasu and Takamimusubi return it with an oath, killing him if treacherous. *Kojiki*/*Nihon shoki*. **[Early canonical]**
- **Takemikazuchi —** commissioned within the heavenly council to secure Ōkuninushi’s submission. Same sources. **[Early canonical]**
- **Ame no Torifune —** accompanies Takemikazuchi in the *Kojiki* land-transfer mission. *Kojiki* (712). **[Early canonical]**
- **Futsunushi —** takes the corresponding military role with Takemikazuchi in the *Nihon shoki*. *Nihon shoki* (720). **[Early canonical variant]**
- **Ōkuninushi —** yields the visible terrestrial realm after envoys sent under heavenly authority negotiate with him and his sons. *Kojiki*/*Nihon shoki*. **[Early canonical]** Amaterasu does not personally meet him in the surviving principal narratives.
- **Kotoshironushi —** accepts the heavenly demand for land transfer. Same sources. **[Early canonical]** Interaction is through Amaterasu’s envoys.
- **Takeminakata —** resists the transfer and is defeated by Takemikazuchi. *Kojiki*. **[Early canonical]** Again, no face-to-face meeting with Amaterasu.
- **Ninigi —** grandson selected for descent; receives authority and sacred objects from Amaterasu. *Kojiki*/*Nihon shoki*. **[Early canonical]**
- **Sarutahiko —** confronts or awaits the descending party; Uzume identifies him and he guides Ninigi. Same sources. **[Early canonical]** No direct Amaterasu–Sarutahiko conversation is recorded.
- **Toyouke Ōmikami —** in Ise tradition, Amaterasu requests Toyouke from Tanba to provide sacred food; the Outer Shrine is established. *Nihon shoki* gives a Yūryaku-era foundation setting, while fuller versions are later shrine tradition. **[Later institutional tradition]**
- **Yamatohime no Mikoto —** carries Amaterasu’s cult through successive sites and receives the oracle selecting Ise. *Nihon shoki* and later *Yamatohime* texts. **[Early court tradition, greatly elaborated later]**
- **Dainichi Nyorai —** Amaterasu is equated with, treated as a trace of, or paired with the cosmic Buddha Mahāvairocana. Medieval Ryōbu Shintō and Ise texts, especially from the eleventh century onward. **[Syncretistic]**
- **Brahmā/Bonten —** medieval cosmological texts place Amaterasu in Brahmā’s palace or correlate Ise with Brahmanic heaven. **[Syncretistic medieval accretion]**
- **Dakiniten —** esoteric enthronement traditions connect Dakiniten, Dainichi, fox imagery, and Amaterasu’s imperial authority; attested in texts including the *Bikisho* (1324). **[Syncretistic]**
- **Uhō Dōji —** medieval Ise mountain cult relates this rain-treasure child, astral/esoteric imagery, and Amaterasu. **[Syncretistic regional tradition]**
- **Kuni no Tokotachi —** Watarai Shintō elevated Toyouke by identifying the Outer Shrine deity with the primordial Kuni no Tokotachi, implicitly contesting Amaterasu’s supremacy. **[Medieval learned systematization]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### 5.1 Birth and investiture

**Kojiki version. [Early canonical]** Izanagi returns polluted from Yomi and performs ablution at Awagihara. Deities emerge from his discarded clothing and washing. From his left eye comes Amaterasu; from his right eye Tsukuyomi; from his nose Susanoo. Izanagi gives Amaterasu his jeweled necklace and commands her to rule Takamagahara.

**Nihon shoki main version. [Early canonical variant]** Izanagi and Izanami have already produced the islands and many deities. They deliberately produce a being brilliant enough to illuminate the “six directions” or universe and send her to heaven. The ablution birth is demoted to an alternative writing.

**Development:** The difference matters politically and theologically. The *Kojiki* integrates Amaterasu into purification and makes her the greatest of Izanagi’s three noble children; the *Nihon shoki* main text gives both creator deities parentage and presents her cosmic luminosity as intentional. Neither is demonstrably the single “original version.”

### 5.2 The pledge with Susanoo

Susanoo announces that he wishes to say farewell before joining Izanami. His ascent shakes the land. Amaterasu assumes hostile intent, binds her hair, equips herself with jewels, bow, and quivers, and stamps the earth. Susanoo proposes an *ukei*, an oath or trial whose result will reveal sincerity. They exchange objects: she chews his sword; he chews her jewel strands. Their breath produces five male and three female deities.

The texts disagree about the criterion of victory. The *Kojiki* has Susanoo claim victory because his heart produced female children; variants shift the expected sex or allocation. Modern summaries that portray this as a friendly “creation contest” omit the judicial and political stakes.

### 5.3 Susanoo’s violence and the heavenly rock cave

After claiming victory, Susanoo destroys field divisions, fills irrigation ditches, defecates in the hall of first fruits, and finally throws a horse flayed backward through the roof of the sacred weaving hall. In the *Kojiki*, a weaving maiden is startled and dies after being pierced by the shuttle; *Nihon shoki* variants make Wakahirume die or Amaterasu herself suffer injury.

Amaterasu shuts herself inside the heavenly rock chamber, sealing the door. Heaven and the central land become dark; malign spirits multiply and calamities erupt. Eight hundred myriad kami assemble by the heavenly river. Omoikane organizes a remedy:

1. roosters are made to crow;
2. Ishikoridome fashions a mirror;
3. Tamanooya fashions jewels;
4. diviners prepare ritual materials;
5. a sakaki is decorated with jewels, mirror, and cloth;
6. Ame no Koyane recites solemn words;
7. Futodama presents the offerings;
8. Uzume overturns a tub, stamps, becomes possessed, exposes breasts and genitals, and makes the gods laugh.

Amaterasu asks why they rejoice in darkness. Uzume answers that a deity greater than Amaterasu is present. When Amaterasu opens the door, the mirror confronts her with the brilliant deity. Tajikarao draws her out, and a boundary rope prevents her return. Light returns.

**Interpretation:** The story explains or authorizes elements of court ritual—mirror, jewels, offerings, divination, *norito*, sacred dance, and clan offices. Breen and Teeuwen argue that details such as the crowing birds reflect Ise ritual and that the received narrative was shaped within late seventh- and early eighth-century court culture. ([Breen and Teeuwen, rock-cave chapter](https://is.muni.cz/el/phil/podzim2019/JPN313/um/Breen__Teeuwen_A_new_history_of_Shinto_The_Rock_Cave_Myth.pdf))

Eclipse, seasonal death-and-return, shamanic seclusion, royal enthronement, and initiation have all been proposed as origins. **[Modern reconstructions]** None is established as the exclusive explanation.

### 5.4 Susanoo’s expulsion and the sword

The heavenly gods punish Susanoo with fines, hair or nails cut, and expulsion. On earth he kills Yamata no Orochi and finds a sword in its tail. He sends or presents it to Amaterasu. The weapon becomes Ame no Murakumo and later Kusanagi. This episode repairs, but does not erase, the earlier conflict.

### 5.5 Transfer of the terrestrial realm

Amaterasu and Takamimusubi declare that the Reed-Plain Central Land should be governed by their descendant. Missions fail: Ame no Hohi allies with Ōkuninushi; Ame no Wakahiko neglects his charge and is killed by his returned arrow. Takemikazuchi, with Ame no Torifune in the *Kojiki* or Futsunushi in the *Nihon shoki*, secures submission. Kotoshironushi consents; Takeminakata resists and loses. Ōkuninushi requests a grand palace and withdraws from visible rule.

**Political function:** This is not a neutral creation myth but a charter for Yamato sovereignty over other divine and human lineages. It preserves resistance inside the legitimating narrative, especially through Izumo’s gods.

### 5.6 Heavenly descent and the mirror

Ame no Oshihomimi is first selected but Ninigi ultimately descends. Amaterasu supplies sacred objects and sends ritual specialists whose descendants are linked to court clans. In the *Kojiki*, the mirror receives the command commonly translated, “Regard this mirror exactly as my spirit; reverence it as if reverencing me.” This creates a relation of presence, not merely a commemorative portrait.

The *Nihon shoki* variants differ over whether Amaterasu alone or Amaterasu with Takamimusubi issues the descent command and over the inventory of regalia. The familiar triad mirror–sword–jewel is therefore a stabilized synthesis of variant passages.

### 5.7 Separation of sun and moon

Only in the *Nihon shoki* version, Amaterasu sends Tsukuyomi to Ukemochi. Offended when the food deity produces food from her mouth and body, Tsukuyomi kills her. Amaterasu calls him wicked and refuses to see him; from then on sun and moon dwell apart. Agricultural species emerge from Ukemochi’s corpse.

This is frequently retold as Amaterasu divorcing her husband. The source calls Tsukuyomi a fellow deity and sibling in the larger genealogy; it does not securely establish a marriage.

### 5.8 Wandering and establishment at Ise

The *Nihon shoki* says Amaterasu was initially worshipped with the imperial regalia inside the palace. Emperor Sujin, fearing their power, entrusted her to Toyosukiirihime; Yamatohime later carried her through several locations until the deity chose Ise, praising its secluded and fertile setting. The traditional chronology places this in the first century BCE.

**Historical assessment:** These reign dates are legendary. Specialists variously locate Ise’s institutional formation between the fourth/fifth centuries and the decades around 700. The official shrine preserves the traditional foundation account, whereas archaeological and institutional histories emphasize the late Yamato and ritsuryō state. ([Teeuwen and Breen, *A Social History of the Ise Shrines* preview](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781474272810_A29110836/preview-9781474272810_A29110836.pdf))

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Ise Jingū

“Jingū” comprises 125 shrines centered on:

- **Kōtaijingū/Naikū**, Inner Shrine, dedicated to Amaterasu;
- **Toyouke Daijingū/Gekū**, Outer Shrine, dedicated to Toyouke, provider of sacred food.

The shrine’s official account calls Amaterasu imperial ancestress and national guardian. ([Ise Jingū](https://www.isejingu.or.jp/en/about/index.html/index.html))

The historically recoverable late seventh-century institution included:

- imperial patronage;
- a female imperial **saiō** or abstinence princess;
- hereditary priestly houses;
- controlled access and purity regulations;
- estates and tributary communities;
- textiles, rice, marine products, sake, and other offerings;
- daily food rites and calendrical festivals.

The *saiō* institution is securely visible in the late seventh century; Princess Ōku, daughter of Tenmu, is the earliest clearly historical example. It ended amid fourteenth-century political disorder.

### Shikinen Sengū

The formal periodic rebuilding is first documented under Empress Jitō in **690**. Every twenty years, adjoining eastern and western plots alternate: buildings, sacred furnishings, vestments, and treasures are renewed, and the deity is transferred to the new sanctuary. Interruptions occurred during medieval civil warfare; regular cycles resumed in the early modern period. The 62nd transfer occurred in 2013. Preparations for the 63rd culminate in 2033, involving 125 shrine buildings, more than 1,500 ritual objects, and dozens of ceremonies. ([Ise official account](https://www.isejingu.or.jp/sengu/index.html?source=post_page-----14d09d11ca94----------------------); [2025 account of the 63rd cycle](https://apnews.com/article/5828f94e07da91f2ca9a12ea777b7b96))

The often repeated statement that Ise has been rebuilt “without interruption every twenty years for 1,300 years” is false in a literal sense; warfare and modern disruption caused breaks.

### Festivals and offerings

- **Kanname-sai:** first rice offered to Amaterasu; now Ise’s most important annual festival.
- **Tsukinami-sai:** regular seasonal rites.
- **Kinensai:** prayers for harvest.
- **Niinamesai:** imperial thanksgiving for new grain.
- **Daily food offerings:** presented morning and evening through Toyouke’s service.
- **Shikinen Sengū ceremonies:** tree selection and felling, site preparation, manufacture of treasures and garments, and nocturnal transfer.
- **Daijōsai:** imperial accession harvest rite; modern State Shinto made its imperial-national meaning especially prominent, though its relation to Amaterasu is ritually more complex than a simple coronation “by the goddess.”

The Ise priesthood describes rites as prayers for imperial prosperity, world peace, and harvest. ([Ise ritual calendar](https://www.isejingu.or.jp/en/ritual/))

### Other major cult sites

- **Hirota Shrine, Hyōgo:** enshrines the *aramitama* of Amaterasu. Its foundation is narrated in the *Nihon shoki* Jingū account; the traditional 201 CE dating belongs to legendary chronology. ([Hirota Shrine](https://hirotahonsya.or.jp/pages/47/))
- **Amanoiwato Shrine, Miyazaki:** identifies a local cave with the heavenly rock chamber. **[Regional localization]** The eighth-century sources locate the event mythically in heaven and do not identify this terrestrial cave.
- **Amano Iwato sites elsewhere:** several regions claim the cave; plurality is the historical fact.
- **Shinmei shrines:** Ise-derived Amaterasu shrines spread widely, particularly through distribution of Ise *taima* amulets and pilgrimage networks.
- **Tokyo Daijingū:** founded in the Meiji period as a capital-based Ise cult center.
- **Ise-derived overseas shrines:** built in colonial and emigrant settings; in imperial colonies their worship could be state-promoted or coercively imposed.

### Pilgrimage and economics

Ise became a major pilgrimage destination beyond court control. **Onshi**, religious entrepreneurs and lodging-priests, distributed amulets, organized confraternities, hosted pilgrims, and tied distant households to Ise. Massive **okagemairi** pilgrimages occurred episodically in the early-modern period. Shrine estates, offerings, craft production, markets, and travel made Amaterasu’s cult an economic institution as well as theology. Kokugakuin notes both popular pilgrimages and widespread household enshrinement of Ise amulets. ([Kokugakuin, Ise](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/bts/detail/?id=3747))

### Sacrifice and contested abuse claims

- **Animal and food offerings:** documented, especially rice, fish, birds, sake, salt, cloth, and other produce.
- **Human sacrifice:** no reliable *Kojiki*, *Nihon shoki*, *Engishiki*, archaeological, or Ise institutional evidence demonstrates that humans were sacrificed to Amaterasu. Japanese *hitobashira* legends concern various bridges, embankments, and castles; attaching them to Amaterasu without a specific source is unwarranted.
- **Temple prostitution:** no evidence establishes institutional “sacred prostitution” in Amaterasu’s cult. Applying that comparative label to *miko*, *saigū*, or Ise personnel is unsupported.
- **Cult violence and coercion:** securely documented in the modern political use of shrine worship, forced or pressured colonial shrine attendance, and suppression or restructuring of local religious institutions. This is political-religious coercion, not evidence of ancient sacrificial killing.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 700: formation, not recoverable origin

Solar rites, mirrors, female ritualists, rice kingship, and elite ancestors existed before the texts, but none by itself names Amaterasu. A court-centered solar ancestor likely emerged through the consolidation of Yamato power. Dating proposals range from the fifth century to the reigns of Tenmu and Jitō. Matsumae argued for growth from royal clan deity to centralized imperial ancestor; later work stresses editorial and institutional construction. ([Matsumae, “Origin and Growth”](https://asianethnology.org/downloads/ae/pdf/a318.pdf))

### c. 672–800: Tenmu–Jitō state formation

After the Jinshin War of 672, Tenmu’s court intensified genealogical and ritual consolidation. Breen and Teeuwen suggest that Tenmu attributed victory to Amaterasu and that Ise rites influenced the redaction of the cave myth. Jitō’s reign supplies the first documented sengū in 690. The *Kojiki* and *Nihon shoki* then positioned Amaterasu at the origin of legitimate sovereignty.

This was not necessarily the uncontested replacement of all other gods. Takamimusubi remains prominent; Izumo traditions are incorporated through a negotiated transfer; powerful clan ancestors receive roles in the cave and descent cycles.

### Ninth–twelfth centuries: court cult and Buddhist integration

The *Engishiki* codified Ise rites and offerings. Buddhism did not simply “corrupt” an otherwise isolated Shinto cult: temples, kami worship, court rites, pilgrimage, and esoteric thought increasingly formed one religious field. Amaterasu could be treated as a protector of Buddhism, a being needing Buddhist salvation, or a manifestation of cosmic enlightenment.

### Twelfth–sixteenth centuries: medieval Ise theologies

Ryōbu Shintō paired Inner and Outer Shrines with the Womb and Diamond mandalas and correlated Amaterasu with Dainichi. Watarai Shintō, developed by Outer Shrine priests, elevated Toyouke and constructed an elaborate cosmology from shrine records, Chinese thought, and esoteric Buddhism. Yoshida Kanetomo’s fifteenth-century system ranked forms of Shinto and presented his own Yuiitsu Shintō as primordial, while describing Ise’s Amaterasu–Dainichi system as a middle form. ([“Aspects of Medieval Japanese Religion”](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/13/10/894))

The female solar ruler could now be male, androgynous, a child, a snake-bodied trace, or Dainichi’s manifestation. Those are genuine historical Amaterasu traditions—not misunderstandings to be deleted—but they are medieval, not securely ancient.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: pilgrimage and *kokugaku*

Ise pilgrimage expanded through *onshi*, confraternities, amulets, and transport networks. Meanwhile Motoori Norinaga’s *Kojiki-den* recovered Old Japanese readings and elevated the *Kojiki* as privileged access to the ancient way. Norinaga treated Amaterasu as the actual sun and supreme imperial ancestor within his theology, but his supposedly pure antiquity was itself an eighteenth-century learned reconstruction. Ueda Akinari challenged his philology and the claim that Amaterasu’s rule over Japan meant universal rule because sunlight covered the world. ([Norinaga–Akinari dispute](https://www.mdpi.com/2409-9252/5/3/32))

### Nineteenth century: new religions and imperial restructuring

In 1814, **Kurozumi Munetada** experienced what his movement describes as direct reception of a heavenly mission while worshipping the rising sun after illness. Amaterasu became the all-encompassing source of life and spiritual brightness in Kurozumikyō. The movement dates its founding to the eleventh day of the eleventh lunar month of Bunka 11; Kurozumikyō later became an officially recognized Sect Shinto organization and opened Munetada Shrine in Okayama in 1885. ([Kurozumikyō official history](https://kurozumikyo.com/about); [English teaching account](https://kurozumikyo.com/marukoto_e.html))

After 1868, the government separated kami institutions from Buddhism, dismantled many shrine-temple complexes, reorganized shrine ranks, and centered imperial rites on the claimed unbroken descent from Amaterasu. “State Shinto” is analytically disputed: narrowly it denotes state-administered shrines legally classified as nonreligious; broadly it includes the total imperial cult and national pedagogy. The term itself became standard only with the 1945 Shinto Directive. ([Kokugakuin, “State Shintō”](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/?id=8865))

### 1890–1945: nationalization and empire

Amaterasu’s descent mandate became part of **kokutai** ideology, imperial education, military loyalty, and colonial shrine policy. The state distributed Ise talismans, privileged imperial rites, and represented the emperor as descendant of Amaterasu. Colonial subjects in Korea, Taiwan, and elsewhere could face administrative and social compulsion to attend shrines or participate in rites classified by the state as patriotic rather than religious.

This does not mean every subject held identical beliefs, nor that a single “State Shinto church” existed. It means state law, education, ritual, and shrine administration made the Amaterasu genealogy politically consequential.

### 1945–1946: disestablishment and the so-called renunciation

SCAP’s **Shinto Directive**, 15 December 1945, ended state sponsorship and dissemination of State Shinto, prohibited militaristic and ultranationalistic shrine teaching in public institutions, and required religious freedom. It explicitly defined State Shinto as the state-separated, officially “nonreligious” shrine cult. ([SCAPIN-448](https://jahis.law.nagoya-u.ac.jp/scapindb/docs/scapin-448))

On 1 January 1946 Emperor Hirohito issued the rescript conventionally called the “Humanity Declaration.” It rejected the conception that the emperor was an **akitsumikami** and that the Japanese were racially superior or destined to rule the world. The Japanese text did **not explicitly renounce genealogical descent from Amaterasu**. Therefore “Hirohito renounced being descended from Amaterasu” is an inaccurate modern paraphrase. The National Archives describes the rejected ideas as the emperor being a manifest deity and Japanese ethnic superiority. ([National Archives of Japan](https://www.archives.go.jp/ayumi/kobetsu/s21_1946_01.html))

### 1946–present

Ise continued as a religious corporation closely connected to imperial household rites but no longer a state shrine. Jinja Honchō, the Association of Shinto Shrines, was established as a private umbrella body. Imperial visits, accession rites, and political advocacy continue to provoke debate over constitutional separation of religion and state. Scholarship likewise disputes whether “State Shinto” ended cleanly or whether institutional and ideological continuities remain. ([Kokugakuin, modern Shinto](https://d-museum.kokugakuin.ac.jp/eos/detail/id%3D9071); [Rethinking State Shinto](https://brill.com/view/journals/nu/66/2-3/article-p163_4.pdf))

Amaterasu is worshipped today at Ise, Shinmei shrines, household altars using Jingū *taima*, Kurozumikyō institutions, and overseas shrines. “Revival” is not quite the right word for Ise, whose institutional cult survived; it applies more readily to individualized global polytheist or neopagan devotion.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Religious movements

- **Kurozumikyō:** documented continuous movement from 1814, not a recent internet reconstruction. Its Amaterasu is an immanent life-giving sun and divine parent; Munetada’s spirit is understood as united with her.
- **Ōmoto and related new religions:** employ broad kami cosmologies in which Amaterasu is important but not always ultimate.
- **Shrine Shinto:** contemporary worship maintains Amaterasu as Ise deity and imperial ancestress without requiring a single creed among adherents.
- **Western neopaganism and eclectic polytheism:** small numbers of practitioners incorporate Amaterasu into personal pantheons. No reliable global membership count exists. Claims of an initiatory “ancient Amaterasu witch cult,” standardized chakra, tarot, crystal, planetary, or Wiccan correspondence are **[Modern invention]** unless explicitly presented as new practice.
- **Occultism:** Unlike Isis, Hecate, or Thoth, Amaterasu had no major Renaissance European hermetic reception and no fixed Golden Dawn attribution. Modern occult tables placing her under the Sun are twentieth- or twenty-first-century comparative constructions.

### Nationalism and politics

Amaterasu remains available to both religious devotion and nationalist symbolism. Modern far-right or imperial-restoration rhetoric may invoke the divine imperial lineage, Ise, or the “land of the gods.” This should not be generalized to every shrine worshipper or every Ise visitor. The modern imperial state’s use is documented; claims that the *Kojiki* itself demands twentieth-century militarism confuse text with later deployment.

### Literature, art, comics, animation, and games

- **Ōkami** (Clover Studio/Capcom, 2006): reinvents Amaterasu as a white wolf carrying a solar disk and restoring a painted world. The wolf form is **[Modern invention]**, not classical iconography, but has become one of the best-known international images of the name.
- **Naruto** manga/anime: “Amaterasu” is an inextinguishable black-flame eye technique, grouped with Tsukuyomi, Susanoo, Izanagi, and Izanami. This is a modern nominal adaptation, not a traditional power of the goddess.
- **SMITE:** presents Amaterasu as an armed warrior. Sword-and-armor imagery draws loosely on mythic regalia and her armed confrontation with Susanoo but is not a reconstruction of cult imagery.
- **Megami Tensei**, **Puzzle & Dragons**, **Fate-related media**, **Marvel and other comics**, and numerous manga/anime use the name for solar women, warriors, spells, or cosmic authorities.
- Modern depictions often give her long black hair, white-and-red shrine clothing, a radiant halo, and a sword. This composite owes more to modern national, anime, and game aesthetics than to early cult representation.
- In 2023 the Telescope Array collaboration named an exceptionally energetic cosmic-ray event the **“Amaterasu particle.”** This is commemoration by name, not religious tradition.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### From *kokugaku* to modern criticism

Motoori Norinaga’s *Kojiki-den* established philological readings that remain influential, but it also privileged the *Kojiki* as revelation of an uncorrupted ancient Japanese sensibility. Hirata Atsutane expanded this into a more universal theology. Meiji ideologues selectively appropriated these systems.

Twentieth-century scholars separated mythic narrative from literal national history. Tsuda Sōkichi’s criticism of the chronicles’ early imperial narratives provoked prosecution under the imperial state. Yanagita Kunio and Orikuchi Shinobu redirected attention toward folklore, ritual specialists, village religion, and comparative mythology, though their reconstructions of ancient patterns remain theories.

### The “Shinto” category

Kuroda Toshio’s 1981 “Shinto in the History of Japanese Religion” attacked the model of Shinto as a single, autonomous indigenous religion surviving unchanged from prehistory. He argued that medieval *shintō* discourse largely operated inside the Buddhist *kenmitsu* system and that autonomous Shinto was substantially an early-modern and modern construction. ([Kuroda PDF](https://religion-in-japan.univie.ac.at/k/images/0/03/Kuroda_1981.pdf))

Later scholars modify rather than simply accept or reject Kuroda. Breen and Teeuwen emphasize particular shrines, rites, myths, and institutions instead of assuming a continuous unitary religion. This does not make Amaterasu “invented in Meiji”; it means her very old texts and cult were repeatedly assembled into changing religious wholes.

### Origins of Ise and Amaterasu

Major positions include:

- an early royal-clan solar deity developed before centralized state formation;
- a fifth- or sixth-century Ise cult subsequently elevated;
- a late seventh-century Tenmu–Jitō construction tied to ritsuryō sovereignty;
- models linking female solar authority to shamanic queenship;
- identifications with Himiko or Jitō.

The secure floor is the late seventh-century institutional evidence and eighth-century texts. Earlier dates rely on retrospective chronicle passages, archaeology that does not name the deity, and comparative inference. The traditional Suinin-era date is cult chronology, not independently secure history.

### Gender debate

The received early narratives favor a female interpretation, but grammatical ambiguity, cross-dressing for combat, nonsexual production of heirs, and medieval male images complicate it. Claims that Amaterasu was “originally definitely male” or “always unambiguously female” both outrun the evidence. The strongest historical statement is that gender was differently marked and theologically used in different periods.

### Mirror and regalia

The Ise mirror is hidden; inspection is religiously prohibited. Reports of damage or replacement occur in later literature, but no modern material study can confirm its age. It is historically sound to say that Ise tradition identifies its sacred mirror as Yata no Kagami; it is not sound to describe a measured, photographed archaeological artifact.

### Supposed eclipse

The cave’s darkness has repeatedly been read as memory of a solar eclipse. No text dates the event, and no eclipse can be securely matched to it. Eclipse interpretation is **[Modern reconstruction]**, not recovered chronology.

### Syncretism

Older nationalist histories sometimes portrayed Buddhism as overlaying a pristine Shinto. Medieval sources instead show generative combination: Amaterasu as Dainichi’s trace, Ise mapped onto mandalas, Buddhist clergy serving shrine networks, and kami participating in Buddhist salvation. Meiji separation was therefore not restoration of an untouched ancient order but a destructive institutional reconfiguration.

### Lost and surviving evidence

- The *Kojiki* survives principally through medieval manuscripts, notably Shinpukuji.
- The *Nihon shoki* has older fragments and a rich commentary tradition; *Shaku Nihongi* preserves quotations and glosses from lost materials.
- Most provincial *Fudoki* are fragmentary or lost.
- Ise’s periodic rebuilding preserves forms and techniques while replacing material; wars, fires, secrecy, and ritual disposal have reduced the archaeological record.
- Medieval shrine-temple separation and the nineteenth-century *shinbutsu bunri* destroyed or dispersed syncretic images and records.
- Surviving materials are distributed among shrine archives, the Imperial Household Agency, the Historiographical Institute of the University of Tokyo, the National Diet Library, temple and university collections, and museums including the Jingū Chōkokan.

### Discredited or unsupported ideas still circulating

- Amaterasu is securely identical with Himiko.
- The cave myth records a datable historical eclipse.
- Tsukuyomi was unambiguously her husband and she divorced him.
- Ise’s present buildings or mirror are physically unchanged for two millennia.
- Shinto existed from prehistory as a single centralized religion with Amaterasu universally at its head.
- The 1946 declaration denied imperial descent from Amaterasu.
- Amaterasu required human sacrifice or temple prostitution.
- Modern wolf, samurai, tarot, zodiac, or crystal attributes are ancient cult facts.
- Toriyama Sekien or *Tōno monogatari* supplies an otherwise lost canonical Amaterasu biography.

## 11. Sources and assessment

### Primary texts and editions

- Ō no Yasumaro, *Kojiki* (712). Donald L. Philippi, trans., *Kojiki* (University of Tokyo Press, 1968), remains the requested standard English translation. Kokugakuin’s digital edition supplies corrected Japanese, reading text, modern translation, annotations, and English.
- *Nihon shoki* (720). W. G. Aston, trans., *Nihongi: Chronicles of Japan from the Earliest Times to A.D. 697* (1896), is public-domain and still valuable, but should be checked against modern editions.
- *Fudoki* fragments and transmitted quotations, early eighth century onward.
- *Man’yōshū*, late seventh/eighth-century poems, compiled in the eighth century.
- Inbe no Hironari, *Kogo Shūi* (807).
- *Kōtaijingū Gishikichō* (804).
- *Engishiki* (completed 927), especially books on the Department of Divinities, Ise, offerings, and *norito*.
- *Shaku Nihongi* (late thirteenth century), preserving commentary and lost quotations.
- Ise shrine texts including *Yamatohime no Mikoto Seiki*, *Jingū zatsureishū*, and Watarai writings.
- *Reikiki*, *Daijingū engi*, and *Bikisho* (1324) for medieval syncretistic Amaterasu.
- Motoori Norinaga, *Kojiki-den*.
- Hirata Atsutane, *Kodō taii* and related writings.
- Yanagita Kunio, *Tōno monogatari* and folklore studies.

### Principal modern scholarship

- Matsumae Takeshi, “Origin and Growth of the Worship of Amaterasu,” for an influential reconstruction from solar and royal cult to Ise.
- Kuroda Toshio, “Shinto in the History of Japanese Religion,” for the critique of timeless autonomous Shinto.
- John Breen and Mark Teeuwen, *A New History of Shinto*, particularly the historical decomposition of the rock-cave myth.
- Mark Teeuwen and John Breen, *A Social History of the Ise Shrines: Divine Capital*, for Ise’s institutional, economic, ritual, and political transformations.

The primary record permits a firm history beginning in 712 and an increasingly detailed institutional history from the late seventh and early eighth centuries. Before that point, Amaterasu’s formation can be reconstructed only probabilistically. Thereafter the evidence shows not one unchanged goddess but a succession of related formations: heavenly solar sovereign, Ise cult presence, imperial ancestor, Buddhist cosmic manifestation, pilgrimage deity, *kokugaku* sun, State Shinto legitimating ancestress, new-religious divine parent, postwar shrine deity, and global popular-culture character.
