# Yos (Our Grandfather Yos, Yompor Yos): Research Dossier

> **Class:** Supreme creator divinity in documented Yanesha oral theology; a remote “grandfather-generation” god rather than the immediately worshipped solar “father” divinities.  
> **Earliest secure explicit attestation:** The earliest presently traceable record naming the being is Martha Duff-Tripp/Wise’s Yanesha linguistic and ethnographic field material from the 1950s, cited by Richard Chase Smith and Fernando Santos-Granero; the first substantial, readily verifiable published treatment is Smith’s 1977 Cornell dissertation. A Franciscan report of 1716 documents Yanesha-region priests, sacrifices, and solar worship but does **not** securely name Yato’ Yos.  
> **Critical nomenclatural warning:** The normally documented name is **Yato’ Yos**, “Our Grandfather Yos/God.” **Yompor** means “Our Father” and designates the next divine generation—Yompor Ror, Yompor Rret, Yompor Yompere, and others. “Yompor Yos” is therefore not a securely documented equivalent of Yato’ Yos in the principal ethnographies and may be a cataloguing conflation of two divine ranks.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA]** securely attested early evidence.
- **[LE]** later ethnographic or canonical elaboration within Yanesha oral tradition.
- **[RV]** regional or narrator-specific variant.
- **[SYS]** scholarly or Indigenous learned systematization.
- **[SYN]** syncretistic identification or demonstrable borrowing.
- **[FOL]** folklore or oral tradition recorded without a demonstrable early textual pedigree.
- **[MSR]** modern scholarly reconstruction.
- **[MI]** modern invention, unsupported popular claim, or recent reformulation.

“Attested” below means that a narrator, observer, manuscript, or publication attests a belief. It does not turn a narrated primordial event into ordinary history.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

Yato’ Yos is the supreme creative being in the Yanesha cosmology recorded in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. He creates or animates the earth, humans, useful animals and plants, and the vital principle shared by beneficial beings. His creative antagonist is Yosoper, a classificatory brother whose imitative creations become demons, dangerous creatures, poisons, or other harmful beings. The opposition is unmistakably expressed through an Indigenous Amazonian kinship scheme, yet its names—probably from Spanish *Dios* and *Lucifer*—show extensive colonial Christian mediation. Santos-Granero consequently treats the mythology neither as untouched pre-Columbian survivorship nor as merely defective Christianity: imported names and motifs were incorporated into a distinct Yanesha theory of divine kinship, vitality, substance, and power. [Smith 1977; Santos-Granero 1991/1994, 2004, 2012.]

### Documented forms

- **Yato’ Yos / Yáto’ Yos / Ya’to Yos** — “Our Grandfather Yos/God.” *Yato’* is the inclusive reverential kin term “our grandfather”; *Yos* probably derives from Spanish *Dios*. **[LE/SYN]**
- **Nuestro Abuelo Yos / Our Grandfather Yos / Notre Grand-Père Yos** — Spanish, English, and French analytical translations used in the scholarship. They are translations, not separate divine names. **[SYS]**
- **Yos** — the shortened divine element. Smith proposed derivation from Spanish *Dios*; Santos-Granero calls this probable rather than certain. **[MSR/SYN]**
- **Yato’ Koros/Korós** — “Our Bad/Evil Grandfather,” found in Espíritu Bautista Pascual’s modern Yanesha creation account as the antagonistic brother. It occupies the position elsewhere assigned to Yosoper and should be treated as a narrator-specific alternative designation, not automatically as an additional god. **[RV]**
- **Yosoper** — the creator’s malignant classificatory brother. Smith proposed derivation from *Lucifer*. The phonological and semantic resemblance is suggestive, but no colonial text demonstrates the exact borrowing event. **[MSR/SYN]**
- **“Yompor Yos”** — requested title, but not established by the principal sources as a normal name. *Yompor* (“our father”) and *Yato’* (“our grandfather”) represent different positions in the Yanesha divine hierarchy. **[MI or unverified regional form]**

There is no attested Indigenous script for the name. Yanesha was historically oral; modern spellings employ Latin alphabets developed and standardized through linguistic, educational, and Indigenous organizational work. Variation in apostrophes and diacritics generally reflects orthography, not different gods.

### Divine class and hierarchy

Santos-Granero describes a kin-ordered sacred hierarchy:

1. **Yato’**, “our grandfather”: primordial or supreme beings, principally Yato’ Yos and his antagonist.
2. **Yompor/Yachor**, “our father/our mother”: major solar, lunar, agricultural, and culture divinities.
3. **Yemo’nasheñ**, “our brother”: junior divinities closer to humanity.

The demonic hierarchy mirrors this structure—Yosoper above the *oneñet* and *jo’* beings—but Yanesha speakers reportedly avoid inclusive kin terminology for demons. Gods are “we/our kin”; demons belong to an opposed category. This is a theological use of kinship rather than a tidy Greco-Roman-style family tree. **[LE/SYS]**

No secure interpretatio graeca or interpretatio romana exists. Equation with the Christian God is historically more complicated: the name probably incorporates *Dios*, and missionaries and later Christian Yanesha could hear the figure through Christian categories, but ethnographic Yato’ Yos has a classificatory brother, participates in a dual creation contest, and stands within an Indigenous kin hierarchy. “Yato’ Yos = the biblical God” is therefore a syncretistic identification, not a timeless equivalence.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Documentary floor

The Yanesha had no known precolonial writing system, and no inscription or ancient icon securely names Yato’ Yos. Consequently, the documentary history begins not with a temple inscription but with outsiders’ records and twentieth-century transcriptions of oral knowledge.

- **1716:** Fray Francisco de San Joseph reported Indigenous *brujos/encantadores* who worshipped and sacrificed to the Sun only seven years after establishment of Franciscan missions in Yanesha territory. This is early evidence for an organized regional solar cult, but it does **not** establish Yato’ Yos by name. **[EA]**
- **Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries:** Biedma, Amich, mission visitation records, and an Amage confession manual document peoples, missions, ritual suppression, and colonial language contact in the central Peruvian forest. The surviving Amage manuscript may derive from mission work between the 1630s and 1740s, with surviving copies probably eighteenth-century. It is crucial context for Christian lexical entry into Yanesha but, on currently accessible evidence, does not supply a secure early occurrence of *Yato’ Yos*. **[EA/context]**
- **1902:** Bailly-Maître described the Palmazú sanctuary, three sacred stones, a perpetual or carefully maintained sacred fire, offerings, prayer and dance, and a priest’s imitation of Catholic ceremonies. He did not establish that any of the stones represented Yato’ Yos. **[EA]**
- **1924:** Navarro recorded the same sanctuary and its stone beings, priest, offerings, and pilgrims, again without identifying Yato’ Yos as its installed image. **[EA]**
- **1950s:** Martha Duff-Tripp/Wise collected Yanesha language and myth materials. Santos-Granero cites a 1958 Wise version and an undated Duff-Tripp text for the creation narrative. Because some material remained unpublished or difficult to inspect, its exact archival date and wording should be kept distinct from later publication. **[EA, archival/field record]**
- **1977:** Richard Chase Smith’s *Deliverance from Chaos for a Song*, a 652-page Cornell dissertation, is the first extensive, readily identifiable scholarly synthesis of Yanesha ritual, cosmology, sacred music, priests, and the Yato’ Yos/Yosoper creation opposition. **[EA/ethnography]**
- **1991; Spanish edition 1994:** Santos-Granero’s *The Power of Love / El poder del amor* placed creation, divine hierarchy, priesthood, morality, knowledge, and sacred song in a broader account of Yanesha social life. **[LE/SYS]**
- **1998:** “Writing History into the Landscape” related the Yompor Ror cycle, shrines, pilgrimage sites, and sacred geography to remembered Yanesha movement and settlement. It mentions Yato’ Yos principally as creator of the first cosmic era. **[MSR]**
- **2002/2005 Spanish version:** “Saint Christopher in the Amazon” recorded Ayots as a son of Yato’ Yos and demonstrated structural and historical connections between Southern Arawak child-sorcery mythology and Christian hagiography. **[LE/MSR]**
- **2004:** Santos-Granero’s synthesis in the *Guía etnográfica de la Alta Amazonía* presented the three-era cosmology and explicitly discussed Christian influence on the names Yos and Yosoper. **[SYS]**
- **2008–2012:** Accounts by Yanesha intellectual Espíritu Bautista Pascual and research by Santos-Granero and Céline Valadeau added named sisters, daughters, plant transformations, and more detailed substance-and-breath creation versions. These are valuable contemporary primary oral sources, not automatically witnesses to an unchanged precolonial form. **[LE/RV]**
- **Present:** The Peruvian Ministry of Culture’s Yanesha profile repeats the Yato’ Yos–Yosoper creation account as recognized Yanesha cultural heritage. **[SYS/public heritage]**

### Oral genres

The principal corpus is oral:

- **Serrparñats:** formal narratives or “preachings” about divinities, ancestral events, and major historical transformations.
- **Coshámnats/coshamñats:** sacred music acquired through revelation and transmitted by priestly specialists; not merely songs “about mythology,” but vehicles for obtaining and distributing divine power.
- **Topographical narration:** myths tied to named rocks, rivers, hills, old temples, and pilgrimage routes.
- **Ritual performance:** prayer, dance, drinking, offerings, fire maintenance, priestly speech, and sacred music serve as primary evidence alongside verbal narratives.

There is no single Yanesha scripture, canonical codex, or fixed authoritative recension. Smith’s and Santos-Granero’s books are ethnographic records and interpretations, not Indigenous Bibles.

### Comparative corpora: relevance and limits

Weiss’s Asháninka/Campa cosmology, Shaver and Dodd’s Nomatsiguenga material, Brown and Fernández, Renard-Casevitz on Matsigenka, Gow on Piro, and Loreto/Ucayali collections illuminate a connected Arawakan and Amazonian field of transformers, solar beings, child sorcery, white predation, river spirits, and messianic expectation. They cannot be used to fill gaps in Yato’ Yos’s dossier automatically:

- Asháninka **Tasorentsi**, Nomatsiguenga creator traditions, Matsigenka narratives, or Piro cosmogony are not aliases of Yato’ Yos.
- The **Bufeo**, **Chullachaqui**, **Yacuruna**, **Pishtaco**, and Urarina spirit complexes are not documented children, aspects, or cult titles of Yato’ Yos.
- Santos-Granero found the Ayots/Poporrona’ cycle among Yanesha and several Asháninka dialect groups but not among Piro or Matsigenka groups that lacked the corresponding child-sorcery institution. That negative distribution is historically significant.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and powers

**Creation and animation.** Yato’ Yos constructs earths or cosmic levels and forms primordial persons from mud or other substances. His decisive power is breath: he blows *camuequeñets*, divine vitality, into what he forms. **[LE]**

**Source of beneficial beinghood.** Humans, edible or useful plants and animals, and sacred things are attributed to him; harmful or poisonous species and demons are attributed to Yosoper. This is a moral taxonomy of origins, not modern biological classification. **[LE/SYS]**

**Cosmic support.** In Bautista’s account the world’s four points remain supported by Yato’ Yos’s breath. This strongly continuous, sustaining role is securely attested in that modern narration but should not be projected into every earlier version. **[RV]**

**Generator of vitality.** The divine *camuequeñets* becomes the individualized vitality *yecamquem* in living beings. Santos-Granero interprets Yanesha beinghood as composite: body, vitality, and shadow are joined but analytically distinguishable. **[LE/MSR]**

**Ancestral sovereignty.** Yato’ Yos is remote and generative; Yompor Ror and other father-generation divinities act more directly in the landscape and in ritual memory. The creator’s supremacy therefore does not imply that he monopolized daily cult.

### Materials of creation: conflicting versions

- **Mud or earth plus breast milk:** the dominant published synthesis. **[LE]**
- **Excrement plus breast milk:** the Duff-Tripp version cited by Santos-Granero, who stated that he could not independently confirm the detail. **[RV/unconfirmed]**
- **Milk from his mother:** one reported kin-source. **[RV]**
- **Milk from his sister:** another. In Bautista’s modern account she is named Ñeñephnor Partsophnor Yarrona’. **[RV]**
- **Borrowed earth from Yosoper’s world:** after Yosoper appropriates the first new earth, Yato’ Yos requests material and makes the intermediate human world. **[LE]**
- **A milk-drop hardened on a toenail and animated by whistling/breath:** Bautista’s detailed version. **[RV, recorded 2008; published later]**

These accounts should not be combined into one artificial recipe.

### Form and iconography

No securely identified precolonial or historical anthropomorphic image of Yato’ Yos is documented. The sources describe actions and kinship, not a stable visual canon. No distinctive weapon, costume, animal, color, number, day, or planet is securely his.

The following must not be mislabelled as images of Yato’ Yos:

- The Palmazú stones represented **Yompor Yompere**, **Yachor Mamas**, and **Yemo’nasheñ Senyac** according to later oral identification.
- The sacred fire was called **Yompor Po’tsom**, **Yompor Camañt̃er**, or **Yompor Cant̃ell** and manifested the power of solar or locally enshrined divinities.
- The Sun is principally **Yompor Ror**, not Yato’ Yos.

Breath, milk, earth, and vitality are his principal documented symbolic substances, but they are narrative media rather than exclusive heraldic emblems.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Genealogical structure

Yato’ Yos occupies the grandfather generation. Yosoper is normally his elder or classificatory brother; some accounts call the relationship affinal. The apparent inconsistency reflects the flexible classificatory kinship terminology through which Yanesha narrators organize divine difference.

Named descendants include:

- **Ayots:** expressly called a son of Yato’ Yos in the child-sorcerer narrative.
- **Santosaprecoya:** called his daughter in Valadeau’s botanical narratives.
- **Yompor Ror:** treated in summaries as one of his sons or as a divinity whose miraculous conception Yato’ Yos initiates by sending a flower. The sources should not be flattened into a single biological genealogy.
- **Yompor Rret:** installed by Yato’ Yos as an early sun in the periodized cosmology.
- **Yompor Santo/Juan Santos Atahualpa:** in later Yanesha messianic history, an emissary or son sent by Yato’ Yos to restore immortality. This is a colonial/postcolonial sacralization of a historical rebel, not primordial evidence.
- **“The other divinities”:** some syntheses broadly make Yato’ Yos progenitor of the beneficial hierarchy, but complete named parentage is not consistently supplied.

No securely attested consort of Yato’ Yos has been established in the principal accessible sources. A woman who provides milk is variously his mother or sister, not demonstrably a wife.

### Absences

No documented episode was found in which Yato’ Yos meets, fights, marries, or bargains directly with Yachor Arrorr, Yachor Mamas, Yompor Yompere, Yemo’nasheñ Senyac, Yompor Huar, Posona’, the Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, or the Pishtaco. Shared membership in an Amazonian or Yanesha supernatural field is not an interaction.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### 5.1 Creation contest with Yosoper

**[LE, with probable colonial syncretism]**

At the beginning the divinities occupy an original level. Yato’ Yos creates another flat earth below it. His jealous classificatory brother Yosoper appropriates that earth. Yato’ Yos requests some of its substance and creates a third, intermediate world. He molds a person and animates it with his breath. Yosoper imitates him, in one version producing ten persons. Yato’ Yos curses or transforms the imitations into *oneñet* and *jo’* demonic beings. The rivalry continues as each produces plants, animals, birds, or insects: Yato’ Yos’s become useful, edible, sacred, or benevolent; Yosoper’s become harmful, poisonous, predatory, ominous, or demonic.

Smith observed that *Yos* and *Yosoper* probably reflect *Dios* and *Lucifer*. Santos-Granero agrees that the names and dual moral opposition show Christian influence but stresses the non-Christian social form: the two are classificatory brothers within an Amazonian kin hierarchy, and creation distributes shared divine vitality among humans, animals, plants, and objects.

This narrative constitutes the first of three broad mythical eras in Santos-Granero’s systematization. The three worlds created in the story coexist with a more elaborate five-level cosmos described by Smith; narrative worlds and synchronic cosmographic levels are not necessarily intended as identical enumerations.

### 5.2 Creation from milk, earth, excrement, and breath

**[RV]**

All recorded versions make animation by breath crucial, but the substrate varies. A Wise/Santos-Granero synthesis uses dirt and breast milk. Duff-Tripp reportedly supplied excrement and milk from Yato’ Yos’s mother, a detail Santos-Granero could not confirm. Bautista’s account begins with a four-pointed space sustained by divine breath. The bad brother proposes saliva; Yato’ Yos declines and asks his sister for milk. A drop falls on her right great toenail, is whistled and worked until firm, then becomes the immense earth when Yato’ Yos breathes into it. Human beings are later formed from earth and saliva and become alive only through his breath.

The milk imagery makes creation an act of shared bodily substance and nurturance, while breath confers divine subjectivity. A Genesis comparison is historically unavoidable—earth, breath, supreme creator—but “borrowed Genesis retold incorrectly” is inadequate. Milk, classificatory sibling rivalry, multiple layered earths, distributed vitality, and parallel beneficial/demonic descent belong to a specifically Yanesha construction.

### 5.3 Institution and failure of the first sun

**[LE]**

Yato’ Yos appoints Yompor Rret to illuminate creation. Rret becomes a destructive sun. During his era women give birth to lizards, monkeys, or rotten wood; sterility, darkness, catastrophe, and social killing characterize the world. The later birth and ascent of Yompor Ror and Yachor Arrorr inaugurate a restored biological and cosmic order.

Yato’ Yos recedes from the action after the initial appointment. The extensive journey, punishments, stone transformations, and ascent belong principally to Yompor Ror’s dossier, not Yato’ Yos’s.

### 5.4 Ayots and Poporrona’

**[LE/SYN]**

Ayots, a gigantic malicious son of Yato’ Yos, carries his apparently infant but actually adult son Poporrona’. Poporrona’ pesters Ayots to identify passers-by; Ayots’s utterances transform them into stones, bees, termites, or animals. After a sequence of killings and failed attacks, Poporrona’ permits himself to be bound and impaled and becomes a fruiting peach palm. Ayots is ultimately deprived of destructive freedom and made to bear the earth below.

Santos-Granero argues that the Southern Arawak cycle developed in colonial conditions and was structurally shaped by Saint Christopher traditions: giant carrier, miraculous child, attempted executions, impalement, and a miraculously flourishing palm. Kíri, an Asháninka name, may derive from *Kiristu*, “Christ.” The cycle’s distribution corresponds to populations among whom accusations of child sorcery became institutionalized; it is absent from relevant Piro and Matsigenka comparisons. Thus Ayots’s status as a son of Yato’ Yos is attested Yanesha mythology, while a claim that the whole narrative is immemorially pre-Christian is not.

### 5.5 Yompor Santo and the return of immortality

**[SYN/FOL]**

In a narrative collected by Santos-Granero, the Yanesha are losing land, population, language, dress, and sacred practice. Yato’ Yos sends his son Yompor Santo, a fair-skinned stranger who speaks Yanesha perfectly, to make the people immortal like the *mellañoteñ* spirits. Yompor Santo becomes the Indigenous sacred identity of Juan Santos Atahualpa, leader of the 1742 anticolonial rebellion.

Versions differ over whether Yompor Santo died, disappeared, was buried, or returned bodily to Yato’ Yos’s celestial world. Annual pilgrimage to a place remembered as his tomb continued into the late nineteenth century. This cycle fuses remembered colonial rebellion, Christian “Holy Father” language, Inca return traditions, and Yanesha divine emissary theology.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Was there a temple of Yato’ Yos?

No direct evidence establishes a temple image, exclusive priesthood, festival, or sacrifice specifically belonging to Yato’ Yos. The creator’s theological supremacy must not be confused with direct cult centrality. The documented temples served solar and localized father-generation divinities.

### Cornesha’ priesthood

The **cornesha’/kornesha’** combined religious, moral, and political leadership. They:

- directed temples or *puerahua*;
- mediated between people and beneficial divinities;
- maintained sacred songs and obtained revelatory songs from *mellañoteñ* spirits;
- led prayer, dance, and collective manioc-beer offerings;
- offered drink toward the dawn sun;
- used coca and tobacco but were distinguished from healing or aggressive shamans;
- could appoint war leaders while ordinarily not fighting themselves;
- gathered followers through reputation and demonstrated divine knowledge rather than through a centralized state apparatus.

Temples were also associated with smithies. Santos-Granero interprets the adoption of iron working and the integration of forges into temple complexes as evidence of Yanesha institutional adaptability, not as proof that every temple feature was ancient.

### Palmazú/Palmaso/Opanmaso pilgrimage center

Bailly-Maître’s 1902 description and Navarro’s 1924 account document the best-known sanctuary:

- a rectangular palm-thatched structure;
- three large sacred stones;
- a sacred fire made with three large logs arranged so their ends met like a star;
- pilgrims arriving from considerable distances;
- offerings, prayers, song, and dance.

Later Yanesha identification names the stones:

1. **Yompor Yompere**;
2. **Yachor Mamas**, his wife/sister, “Our Mother Manioc Beer”;
3. **Yemo’nasheñ Senyac/Santiago**, a junior relative.

These figures had been petrified by Yompor Ror. Navarro recorded offerings of manioc, maize, smoked meat and fish, coca, *chamairo*, lime, and manioc beer. Although the temple ceased functioning in the early twentieth century, offerings at the stones reportedly continued into the 1970s.

### Bailly-Maître’s “Mass”

Bailly-Maître reported that one Palmazú officiant had lived for about a year among Franciscans at Quillazú and afterward reproduced before his “idols” the external actions he had observed. The priest simulated reading from a book made of bird feathers and performed Catholic-looking gestures with apparent solemnity. **[EA/SYN]**

This evidence supports ritual mimesis and selective appropriation. It does not prove that the Yanesha mistook Catholicism for their ancestral religion, nor that Palmazú worshipped Yato’ Yos. The feather “book” translated the material authority of missionary literacy into a Yanesha ritual object.

### Sacred music

Smith argued that *coshámnats* music was central to salvation. Priests and other specialists acquired songs in altered or revelatory states, memorized them accurately, and transmitted them. Collective celebrations joined singing, dancing, mutual hospitality, and manioc beer. Their purpose was to approach divine immortality temporarily, acquire ordering power, and counter mortality and social chaos.

The theology fits Yato’ Yos as the ultimate source of vitality, but surviving descriptions more often invoke the Sun and nearer divinities. Attribution of every sacred song directly to Yato’ Yos would exceed the evidence.

### Sacrifice and difficult material

- Early eighteenth-century missionaries reported sacrifices to the Sun. The exact offerings and their connection to Yato’ Yos are not securely demonstrated.
- Palmazú offerings were plant foods, meat, fish, stimulants, lime, and beer.
- **No documented human sacrifice to Yato’ Yos was found.**
- **No evidence of “temple prostitution” was found.**
- Child-sorcery accusations led at times to violence, killing, flight, or ritual purification among Southern Arawak peoples. Yanesha cleansing rites described by Santos-Granero used tobacco emetics, fasting, vigilance, and seclusion. These are connected to the Ayots cycle but are not sacrifices to Yato’ Yos.
- Missionary descriptions using *brujo*, *ídolo*, or “superstition” are historically valuable testimony but also polemical colonial classifications.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before direct documentation

Yanesha language and culture belong to the Arawakan world while displaying longstanding Andean interaction: Quechua loans, sacred geography, pilgrimage, solar cosmology, sibling-spouse configurations, and possibly institutional influences. Exact dates for these developments cannot be reconstructed from mythology alone. Claims tying Yato’ Yos directly to archaeological cultures of Lima or Pachacamac remain hypotheses, not secure identifications.

### Seventeenth–early eighteenth centuries: mission encounter

Franciscan missions introduced sustained Christian vocabulary, saints, liturgical gestures, writing, new crops, animals, and material practices. Contact was intermittent in the seventeenth century and more sustained for roughly thirty-three years in the eighteenth. A 1716 report already found organized solar specialists and offerings. The likely derivation *Dios → Yos* and *Lucifer → Yosoper* makes this period the strongest historical setting for the recorded names, but no source records the precise moment of adoption.

### 1742 and the Juan Santos transformation

Juan Santos Atahualpa’s revolt expelled missions and colonial control from much of the central forest for decades. Yanesha memory transformed him into Yompor Santo, a divine envoy or son of Yato’ Yos. His remembered mission—to rescue Indigenous people and restore immortality—continued through pilgrimage and return expectations. This represents a demonstrable historical expansion of the pantheon’s narrative field.

### Nineteenth century: colonization and ritual continuity

Military entry, Austrian-German settlement, plantations, epidemic disease, and loss of territory drove substantial Yanesha displacement. Despite this, temples, smithies, pilgrimage, priests, and sacred songs remained active. Palmazú attracted visitors from different settlements and perhaps neighboring peoples. Its cult concerned stone and fire manifestations of nearer divinities, while Yato’ Yos remained the remote creator.

### Early twentieth century: documentation and ritual contraction

Bailly-Maître in 1902 and Navarro in 1924 observed or described Palmazú near the end of its institutional life. Their reports show neither pure continuity nor simple conversion: Catholic ceremonial forms, names such as Santiago/Cristóbal, Indigenous stones, sacred fire, pilgrimage, offerings, and priestly authority coexisted.

The Palmazú building was abandoned around the early 1920s. Other temples survived longer; Santos-Granero was shown remains of a temple abandoned after its priest’s death in the 1950s.

### Mid-to-late twentieth century

Adventist and Evangelical missions substantially changed Yanesha religious organization. Sacred expectations did not merely disappear: Santos-Granero argues that the old promise that the divinities would return and confer immortality could be reformulated through Christian preaching about God’s return and the end time.

The last traditionally active *cornesha’* is reported to have died around 1974, after which the remaining temples were abandoned. The title nevertheless survived politically: the head of the Yanesha federation FECONAYA can be styled *cornesha’*. This is institutional transformation, not proof of an unbroken temple priesthood.

### Internal war and the Shining Path period

The Shining Path/MRTA conflict, militarization, displacement, and violence profoundly affected Indigenous peoples of the central forest, particularly Asháninka communities. The requested literature documents coercion, armed resistance, sorcery fears, and “war of shadows.” No credible evidence located here shows Shining Path adopting Yato’ Yos as an ideological symbol or Yanesha people conducting violence in his name. Treating the war as a Yato’ Yos cult episode would therefore be misleading.

### Present

Yato’ Yos persists in oral narration, bilingual education, ethnographic publication, cultural revitalization, and official Peruvian heritage descriptions. Yanesha religious life today is diverse and includes Adventist, Evangelical, Catholic, Indigenous, and blended practices. The available evidence does not establish the present number of people offering a distinct cult specifically to Yato’ Yos.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Indigenous cultural revitalization

Yanesha educators and intellectuals have published creation narratives and incorporated names such as Yato’ Yos into educational materials. This is living Indigenous authorship, although a printed school text necessarily fixes one version more firmly than older oral circulation did.

### Scholarship and heritage presentation

Smith and Santos-Granero made Yato’ Yos visible beyond Yanesha communities. The Peruvian Ministry of Culture now presents the creation contest in a public ethnographic profile. Recent online “pantheon registries” extract the creator into an encyclopedia-style god entry. Such entries can be useful indexes but tend to suppress narrator differences and make a fluid oral hierarchy resemble a standardized classical pantheon.

### New Age and occult claims

The dedicated search found discussion of Western New Age appropriation of Indigenous **lucid dreaming**, including Santos-Granero’s critique of simplification and commodification. It did **not** find:

- a documented Renaissance occult reception of Yato’ Yos;
- Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or historical grimoire material naming him;
- an established neopagan Yato’ Yos movement;
- chaos-magic sigils or rites with a traceable Yanesha pedigree;
- ancient talismans, seals, zodiacal correspondences, colors, or planetary attributions.

Any contemporary website assigning such correspondences without Yanesha testimony is **[MI]**.

### Literature, film, games, and commerce

No major novel, film, comic, or commercial game appears to have materially reshaped Yato’ Yos’s international image. A modern video, *Yompor Santo’: A Yanesha Ancestor* (IBC, 2008), records Espíritu Bautista’s version of Yompor Santo returning to Yato’ Yos’s sky rather than dying. Tourism pages promote the Yompor Yompere stone sanctuary, but they sometimes simplify or alter the cast—for example, presenting “Santiago” as the petrifying actor. Those tourism narratives should not override the ethnographic variants.

### Political appropriation

Yompor Santo has clear anticolonial significance. Yato’ Yos supplies the divine authority from which the emissary comes, but no modern extremist appropriation of the creator himself was found. Claims that he is an emblem of Sendero Luminoso, ethnic supremacism, or global occult movements are unsupported.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Smith and Santos-Granero

Smith’s central contribution was to treat Yanesha sacred music and priesthood as a coherent system directed toward deliverance from mortality and chaos. His 1977 dissertation supplies much of the foundation for later descriptions of cosmology, the divine hierarchy, *cornesha’*, temples, and revealed song.

Santos-Granero expanded the analysis toward morality, knowledge, political power, landscape, ontology, materiality, colonial transformation, and messianism. His work emphasizes historical creativity: Christian names or rites can be borrowed while being placed in recognizably Yanesha relationships and values.

### Principal debates

**Is Yato’ Yos pre-Christian?**  
The recorded name probably is not: *Yos* likely comes from *Dios*. A creator concept or ancestral divine category may predate missions, but the evidence cannot reconstruct its precontact name or exact form. The defensible conclusion is a colonial-era syncretistic formation built from both Yanesha and Christian materials.

**Is Yosoper Lucifer?**  
Probably etymologically related, according to Smith and Santos-Granero, but the kinship role and creative rivalry are not simply copied Christian demonology. “Lucifer” is a plausible source, not a proven sound-by-sound derivation documented in an early bilingual text.

**How many cosmic levels exist?**  
Creation narratives commonly mention an original divine earth, an appropriated lower earth, and an intermediate human earth. Smith’s synchronic cosmology describes five planes. These need not be mutually exclusive; one is an event sequence, the other a developed spatial system. Calling one “the true number” imposes a rigidity the evidence does not require.

**What substance created the world?**  
Mud, dirt, excrement, milk, and saliva differ by narrator and collector. The stable structural element is animation through Yato’ Yos’s breath. Santos-Granero explicitly declined to confirm Duff-Tripp’s excrement detail.

**How ancient is the Ayots/Poporrona’ story?**  
Santos-Granero argues for colonial development influenced by Saint Christopher hagiography and linked to the historical rise of child-sorcery accusations. This challenges presentations of the cycle as an unchanged prehistoric myth.

**Are Yanesha temples Andean imports?**  
The priestly, pilgrimage, solar, sibling-pair, and sacred-landscape complex shows substantial Andean interaction. Yet direct derivation of the entire institution from the Inca or a specific archaeological culture has not been demonstrated. Exchange and selective incorporation are better supported than wholesale origin.

### State of the evidence

The record is uneven:

- no ancient inscriptions;
- no precontact manuscript;
- missionary notices filtered through evangelical hostility;
- early ethnographic observations that often call priests “sorcerers”;
- oral accounts collected centuries after first mission contact;
- unpublished or difficult-to-access linguistic field manuscripts;
- narrator and community variation sometimes compressed in later summaries.

The strongest method is therefore layered comparison: early mission reports establish the existence of specialists, solar worship, and suppression; twentieth-century observations establish temples and ritual forms; recorded Yanesha narratives establish the names and stories current among narrators; linguistic and structural arguments estimate—but cannot prove—the history of individual motifs.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still liable to circulate

- Yato’ Yos is securely documented in pre-Columbian inscriptions — **false**.
- Yato’ Yos is simply another spelling of Yompor Ror — **false**.
- The Palmazú stones are statues of Yato’ Yos — **unsupported and contradicted by recorded identifications**.
- “Yompor Yos” is the universally standard traditional name — **unverified**.
- Every Amazonian forest spirit in Peru belongs to his pantheon — **false conflation**.
- Yanesha creation is merely Genesis learned from missionaries — **reductive and unsupported**.
- Conversely, the recorded story is demonstrably unchanged from remote prehistory — **also unsupported**.
- Human sacrifice, sexual rites, astrology, ceremonial colors, occult seals, or neopagan orders dedicated to him — **no evidence found**.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Event in the evidentiary history |
|---|---|
| **Pre-contact, undated** | Arawakan Yanesha religious categories and long Andean-Amazonian exchange predate writing; the exact pre-Christian precursor of Yato’ Yos cannot be reconstructed securely. |
| **Seventeenth century** | Intermittent Franciscan activity introduces Christian vocabulary, saints, liturgical performance, and writing into Yanesha territory. |
| **1716** | San Joseph records Indigenous religious specialists, sacrifices, and Sun worship. This is the earliest firm documentary evidence relevant to the later institutional complex, but it does not name Yato’ Yos. |
| **1720s–1730s** | Mission registers and Amage-language materials document intensified colonial language and religious contact. |
| **1742** | Juan Santos Atahualpa’s uprising begins; later Yanesha tradition makes him Yompor Santo, emissary or son of Yato’ Yos. |
| **Late eighteenth–nineteenth centuries** | Mission withdrawal and later renewed colonization; Yompor Santo’s tomb or disappearance place becomes a pilgrimage focus. |
| **Late nineteenth century** | Palmazú and other temple-smithy centers continue amid territorial invasion and displacement. |
| **1902** | Bailly-Maître publishes a description of Palmazú’s stones, sacred fire, pilgrimage, offerings, and imitation Catholic liturgy. |
| **Early 1920s** | Palmazú temple building ceases operating. |
| **1924** | Navarro publishes a fuller account of its priest, stones, and offerings. |
| **1950s** | Wise/Duff-Tripp fieldwork records the Yato’ Yos creation tradition; Evangelical and Adventist presence expands. |
| **1958** | A Wise creation version later cited by Santos-Granero provides the earliest datable twentieth-century textual witness presently identified. |
| **1974, approximately** | Death of the last traditionally active *cornesha’*; remaining temple practice substantially contracts. |
| **1977** | Smith completes *Deliverance from Chaos for a Song*, the foundational large-scale scholarly account. |
| **1970s** | Offerings reportedly remain at the Palmazú stones; Santos-Granero conducts fieldwork and documents sacred landscapes and ruined temples. |
| **1980s–1990s** | Internal war and colonization affect the central forest; no Yato’ Yos appropriation by Shining Path is documented. |
| **1991/1994** | Santos-Granero publishes *The Power of Love / El poder del amor*. |
| **1998** | “Writing History into the Landscape” connects mythic routes, topographic transformations, and Yanesha territorial history. |
| **2002/2005** | “Saint Christopher in the Amazon” argues for colonial-hagiographic formation of the Ayots/Poporrona’ cycle. |
| **2008** | Espíritu Bautista records/presents contemporary Yanesha versions of creation and Yompor Santo’s return to Yato’ Yos. |
| **2011–2012** | Santos-Granero’s beinghood work analyzes divine breath and shared vitality; Valadeau publishes plant-divinity genealogies. |
| **2010s–2020s** | Yato’ Yos appears in bilingual education, cultural revitalization, public heritage profiles, and online reference works. |
| **2026** | No evidence establishes a centralized contemporary cult, exclusive shrine, occult movement, or fixed visual image of Yato’ Yos; the being remains documented principally through Yanesha oral heritage and ethnography. |
