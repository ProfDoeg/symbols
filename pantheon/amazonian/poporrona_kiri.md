# Poporrona' / Kíri (Quërí): Research Dossier

**Class:** Male primordial divinity or powerful mythic person; an evil pseudo-child, transformer, cannibal, and plant-progenitor. In Asháninka terminology Kíri belongs to the world of the *tasórentsi*, beings able to transform reality, although Gerald Weiss’s formal list of the greatest *tasórentsi* names Avíreri rather than Kíri. Yanesha sources call Poporrona' and Ayots explicitly “evil divinities”; Nomatsigenga classification is less securely documented in the accessible record and should not automatically be made identical to either.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Gerald Weiss recorded the River Campa/Asháninka material during fieldwork in **1960–64**. The earliest presently identifiable documentary publication is his 1969 dissertation, *The Cosmology of the Campa Indians of Eastern Peru*, followed by the paper “Campa Cosmology” in the proceedings of the XXXIX International Congress of Americanists in **1970**. There Kíri is Avíreri’s grandson, survives Avíreri’s departure, voluntarily reveals the only means by which his pursuers can kill him, and becomes the *kíri* or peach-palm. Weiss’s expanded *Campa Cosmology: The World of a Forest Tribe in South America* appeared in 1975. Poporrona' was recorded independently from the Yanesha narrators Pedro Ortiz and Espíritu Francisco in **1983**, published in Santos-Granero’s *The Power of Love* in 1991, and summarized in his 2002 article. Thus the earliest recorded name in this dossier is **Kíri**, not Poporrona' or Quërí. [Weiss’s 1970 text](https://repositorio.iep.org.pe/server/api/core/bitstreams/f3e7aad0-3db7-46f2-a682-7ea329e87fa6/content); [Santos-Granero 2002](https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/ec1a0175-8552-44f3-a358-bb24cb826716/content)

## Evidentiary key

- **[Early ethnographic attestation]** Material recorded in the earliest known field research, chiefly Weiss’s 1960–64 Asháninka work.
- **[Later ethnographic attestation]** Material recorded subsequently from identified or locally situated narrators.
- **[Regional variant]** A version belonging specifically to Yanesha, Asháninka/Ashéninka, Nomatsigenga, or Matsigenka tradition.
- **[Colonial attestation]** A claim directly present in a seventeenth- or eighteenth-century document.
- **[Folklore]** Orally transmitted material documented by collectors; this label does not mean false.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An inference proposed by a scholar rather than an explanation stated in the recorded myth.
- **[Syncretistic-identification hypothesis]** A proposed historical connection with a Christian or other non-Amazonian figure.
- **[Modern reception]** Educational, literary, artistic, political, or religious reuse after ethnographic publication.
- **[Not established]** A claim for which the searched record produced no adequate evidence.

A preliminary warning is essential: **Poporrona', Kíri, and Quërí are not simply three spellings of a single canonically fixed god**. They are corresponding figures in related but nonidentical Southern Arawak narratives. Their kinship, moral character, actions, and even separation from the older transformer vary.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

**[Regional variants]** Poporrona' is the Yanesha childlike son of the malevolent transformer Ayots and grandson of the supreme creator Yato' Yos. He appears to be a small child, cannot or does not walk, and never grows, but is actually adult and possesses the transformative and cannibalistic capacities of his father. Ayots carries him on his shoulders. Poporrona' persistently asks who each encountered person is until Ayots, irritated, names that person as an animal, stone, or other thing and thereby transforms them. Later Poporrona' acts directly: he converts his infant cousin into rotten wood, plans to flood the world and eat its inhabitants, defeats every attempt to kill him, then voluntarily prescribes his own execution and becomes the peach-palm. [Santos-Granero 2002](https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/ec1a0175-8552-44f3-a358-bb24cb826716/content)

**[Early ethnographic attestation; Asháninka regional variant]** Kíri is Avíreri’s grandson rather than son. In Weiss’s account he accompanies Avíreri and is implicated in his transformations. After Avíreri leaves for Otsitiríko, Kíri follows him “heartsick,” is overtaken by pursuers, cannot be killed by them, tells them to drive a stake through his head and body into the ground, and becomes the peach-palm. Weiss also records a variant in which Kíri and Avíreri are not distinguished and Avíreri himself becomes the palm. [Weiss 1970](https://repositorio.iep.org.pe/server/api/core/bitstreams/f3e7aad0-3db7-46f2-a682-7ea329e87fa6/content)

**[Regional variant; later ethnographic attestation]** Among the Nomatsigenga, Shaver and Dodd identify the corresponding pair as **Mábireri and Quërí** (also printed *Querí*). The accessible secondary synthesis identifies Quërí as the counterpart of Kíri, but the complete wording of the Nomatsigenga account is not machine-readable in the available scan; conclusions beyond the cited pages 92–93 should therefore remain cautious. [Shaver and Dodd, *Los Nomatsiguenga de la selva central*](https://centroderecursos.cultura.pe/sites/default/files/rb/pdf/Los%20nomatsigenga%20de%20la%20selva%20central.pdf)

### Names and linguistic forms

- **Poporrona'** — Yanesha'. The apostrophe represents a meaningful feature of the Yanesha orthography, not decorative punctuation. A published Yanesha grammar analyzes *poporrona'* as “member or progenitor of the peach-palm group,” built with the participant/progenitor suffix *-Vna'/-na'*. Accordingly, the name means approximately **“Peach-Palm Person,” “one belonging to the peach-palm kind,” or “progenitor of the peach-palm people.”** It is an indigenous botanical-personal formation, not merely a proper name without meaning. [Yanesha grammar](https://repositorio.cultura.gob.pe/bitstream/handle/CULTURA/445/Gram%C3%A1tica_del_idioma_yanesha%28amuesha%29-slp43_%20%281%29.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=3)

- **Poporr** or **poporroch/poponoch** — Yanesha terms reported for the resulting peach-palm, *Bactris gasipaes* Kunth. Orthography varies among ethnographers and editions.

- **Kíri** — Asháninka/Ashéninka. In Weiss’s record this is also the name of the peach-palm into which the person transforms. The older botanical synonym printed by Weiss is *Guilielma speciosa*; the accepted modern species is *Bactris gasipaes*.

- **Quërí / Querí** — Nomatsigenga form in Shaver and Dodd. Santos-Granero prints both spellings in English and Spanish versions. The diaeresis probably represents a linguistic transcription convention, but a full phonological analysis was not located.

- **Kiri from Kiristu?** **[Modern scholarly reconstruction; disputed by the lexical evidence]** Santos-Granero proposes that Spanish *Cristo*, pronounced or adapted by Asháninka speakers as **Kiristu**, “could have led” to abbreviated **Kíri**. His language is explicitly conjectural. The hypothesis supports his broader Child-Christ argument, but it is not an indigenous etymology reported by a narrator. Against treating it as proven stands the direct identity of *kíri* with the peach-palm in Weiss’s account and related Arawak botanical vocabulary: the narrative ends by explaining why the palm is called *kíri*. The available evidence therefore permits at least two readings:

  1. **Santos-Granero’s borrowing hypothesis:** *Cristo* → *Kiristu* → Kíri, subsequently associated with the palm.
  2. **Botanical-person interpretation:** Kíri is the eponymous peach-palm person, with the Christian resemblance secondary or coincidental.

  No historical linguistic demonstration establishing the proposed truncation *Kiristu* → *Kíri* was found. The Christian derivation is a suggestive hypothesis, not settled etymology.

### Titles and epithets

There is no securely attested litany of cult titles. Descriptive labels used by ethnographers include:

- “evil child,” “wondrous child,” or “pseudo-child”;
- “Peach-Palm Person”;
- malevolent or evil divinity;
- transformer;
- cannibal;
- son of Ayots or grandson of Avíreri;
- in comparative analysis, the Amazonian counterpart of the Child Christ.

These are descriptive scholarly glosses, not necessarily indigenous epithets.

### Classification shifts

**[Early ethnographic attestation]** Weiss defines the Asháninka *tasórentsi* as gods whose characteristic power is transformation, often by breath, and *pinkátsori* as “he who is feared” or ruler. Kíri shares transformative power but occupies a subordinate narrative position beside Avíreri.

**[Later ethnographic attestation]** Santos-Granero calls Ayots and Poporrona' unequivocally evil divinities and distinguishes them from the more ambivalent Asháninka Avíreri, who can be both destructive transformer and culture-shaping trickster.

**[Modern scholarly systematization]** “God,” “demon,” “trickster,” and “culture hero” are imperfect translations across different indigenous taxonomies. Poporrona'/Kíri is best described as a **primordial transforming person/divinity**, not as the object of a centralized “cult.”

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest record

**1960–64 field record; 1969 dissertation; 1970 publication.** Gerald Weiss conducted his River Campa fieldwork between 1960 and 1964. His 1970 paper states that it summarizes a more extensive 1969 report. It is the earliest securely datable documentary witness located for Kíri. Weiss warns that his “Campa cosmology” is a scholarly synthesis: no single individual held every detail he presents, and beliefs had different local distributions. This is important evidence against treating his account as a fixed canonical scripture. [Congress proceedings](https://repositorio.iep.org.pe/server/api/core/bitstreams/f3e7aad0-3db7-46f2-a682-7ea329e87fa6/content)

The physical support is a twentieth-century printed ethnographic paper, subsequently digitized as a PDF. It is not an inscription, indigenous manuscript, or precolonial image. The underlying source is oral testimony gathered during fieldwork.

### Principal corpus

1. **Gerald Weiss, “Campa Cosmology” (1969 dissertation; 1970 paper; expanded 1975 monograph).**  
   Language of publication: English. Source language: Asháninka testimony translated and synthesized by Weiss. Geographic focus: River Campa communities of the Ene, Perené, and Tambo rather than the more divergent Gran Pajonal Ashéninka. Role: Kíri is Avíreri’s grandson and companion, culpable in the transformer’s activity, survivor of Avíreri’s removal, self-instructor of his execution, and origin of the peach-palm. Weiss records the crucial fusion variant in which Avíreri, not a separately identified Kíri, becomes the palm. The 1975 critical ethnographic form is *Campa Cosmology*, *Anthropological Papers of the American Museum of Natural History* 52(5):217–588. [AMNH bibliographic record](https://www.amnh.org/research/anthropology/publications/purchase)

2. **Richard Chase Smith, “Deliverance from Chaos for a Song” (Cornell dissertation, 1977).**  
   Language: English; Yanesha oral materials translated by the author. Role: documents Yanesha cosmology, ritual song, Yato' Yos, Yosoper, child sorcery, and the mythic context subsequently used by Santos-Granero. It is cited for the Poporrona' complex in later ethnobotanical and historical work. Full verification of every Poporrona' passage was not possible from an openly searchable text.

3. **Fernando Santos-Granero, *The Power of Love: The Moral Use of Knowledge amongst the Amuesha of Central Peru* (1991; later Spanish edition *El poder del amor*).**  
   Language: English, later Spanish. Field language: Yanesha'. Role: first full identified Yanesha publication of the Ayots–Poporrona' cycle located in this research. Santos-Granero’s later note identifies the two 1983 narrators as **Pedro Ortiz and Espíritu Francisco**. The 1991 text includes episodes omitted from the 2002 summary, including the sequence of maize gardens and the vulture’s creation of the palm’s spines.

4. **Fernando Santos-Granero, “The Dry and the Wet: Astronomy, Agriculture and Ceremonial Life in Western Amazonia” (1992).**  
   Role: integrates Poporrona' with the Yanesha agricultural and ceremonial calendar. During his flight Poporrona' clears six maize gardens representing successive stages of the six-month growing cycle, in reverse order. The fructification of the peach-palm is coordinated with the movement of Antares and the preparation of ceremonial maize beer. This is essential evidence that Poporrona' is not solely a witchcraft exemplum: the narrative also encodes agricultural time. [Article record and excerpt](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/240981079_The_dry_and_the_wet_astronomy_agriculture_and_ceremonial_life_in_Western_Amazonia)

5. **Harold Shaver and Lois Dodd, *Los Nomatsiguenga de la selva central* (1990), pp. 92–93, 103.**  
   Language: Spanish; based on Nomatsigenga materials. Role: documents Mábireri/Quërí and Nomatsigenga beliefs concerning child witches and malignant bird instructors. The open scan establishes publication history—first edition 1990, second edition 2008—but is poorly OCRed. [Open scan](https://centroderecursos.cultura.pe/sites/default/files/rb/pdf/Los%20nomatsigenga%20de%20la%20selva%20central.pdf)

6. **France-Marie Renard-Casevitz, Matsigenka Salt Lady/Pareni study (1994).**  
   Language: French. Role: presents a Matsigenka story structurally close to the Avíreri/Kíri narrative: Pachakamui carries Iguiane; Iguiane transforms Pareni’s children; armadillo Kinteroni captures Pachakamui and nails Iguiane down; Iguiane becomes the *kamona* palm. Renard-Casevitz reportedly treats this as a “different tradition,” probably acquired through exchange with neighboring Asháninka. This is therefore a **related regional transformation**, not direct evidence that Matsigenka worshipped Kíri by that name.

7. **Fernando Santos-Granero, “Saint Christopher in the Amazon: Child Sorcery, Colonialism, and Violence among the Southern Arawak” (2002), *Ethnohistory* 49(3):507–543.**  
   Role: the principal historical argument joining the myth to Franciscan Saint Christopher imagery, epidemics, and child-witch accusations. It contains a condensed Yanesha narrative and explicitly labels its Christian-origin model as a hypothesis. [Full article](https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/ec1a0175-8552-44f3-a358-bb24cb826716/content)

8. **Santos-Granero, “San Cristóbal en la Amazonía” (Spanish version, 2005).**  
   This supplies the same argument and narrative in Spanish, making the study available in Peruvian anthropology. [Spanish text](https://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?pid=S0254-92122005000100002&script=sci_arttext)

9. **Céline Valadeau, “Catégorisation des plantes et des entités étiologiques chez les Yanesha” (2018).**  
   Language: French, with Yanesha materials. Role: independently situates Poporrona' within a Yanesha taxonomy of divine and ancestral plant origins. Poporrona' is the son of Ayots and grandson of Yato' Yos; his whole body becomes the peach-palm. [Valadeau](https://journals.openedition.org/bifea/pdf/1026)

10. **Yanesha-language secondary-school text (2024).**  
    **[Modern reception/continuing transmission]** A Peruvian Ministry of Education communication textbook includes Póporrona' in Yanesha-language educational material with a modern illustration. This is evidence of present-day pedagogical transmission, not ancient iconography. [Ministry textbook](https://repositorio.minedu.gob.pe/bitstream/handle/20.500.12799/11272/A%E2%80%99%C3%B1ema%E2%80%99trrom%20-%20Yanesha%E2%80%99.%20%20Texto%20escolar%20de%20Comunicaci%C3%B3n%20del%202%C2%B0%20Secundaria%20%E2%80%93%20Yanesha.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1)

### Negative documentary finding

**[Colonial record: absence]** None of the Franciscan sources surveyed by Santos-Granero for 1635–1742—Córdoba Salinas, Biedma, San Antonio, Rodríguez Tena, Amich, and associated mission records—names Poporrona', Kíri, or Quërí. Nor do they report the execution of children specifically accused of sorcery. They do report indigenous sorcerers and practices condemned by missionaries. This silence cannot prove that no related narrative existed, but it prevents any secure designation of Poporrona'/Kíri as a documented precontact god.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**[Regional variants]**

- transformation of primordial humans into animals, plants, stones, and substances;
- malicious provocation of an older transformer;
- cannibal predation and prospective flood destruction;
- the origin and identity of the peach-palm;
- moral instruction concerning anger and lack of self-control;
- in Yanesha interpretation, an exemplary model for the dangerous pseudo-child or child witch;
- agricultural periodicity, peach-palm fructification, maize maturation, and ceremonial beer;
- the production or explanation of landscape features through the activities of his elder companion, especially in Asháninka country.

Poporrona'/Kíri is not securely attested as the “god of” a single bounded department comparable to a classical patron deity. His identity is narrative and transformational.

### Form

- male;
- visibly an infant or small child;
- actually possesses adult capacities;
- in the Yanesha version cannot walk and is carried on Ayots’s shoulders;
- refuses or fails to grow;
- becomes an entire peach-palm after execution.

The paradox “child in appearance, adult in agency” is central. Santos-Granero calls this the model of the “pseudo-child.”

### Powers

- transformation, directly or by triggering Ayots/Avíreri’s speech;
- invulnerability or attack-redirection: arrows strike the attackers, clubs hit their wielders, and cutting weapons wound the pursuers;
- adult understanding despite infant form;
- knowledge of the only ritual or mechanical means by which he can be killed;
- postmortem vegetal transformation;
- in fuller Yanesha versions, rapid clearing and sowing of maize gardens during flight.

### Limitations

He is not absolutely immortal. He can die, but only after voluntarily disclosing a highly specific procedure. His death is also transformation rather than annihilation. The sources do not say that the resulting palm retains a speaking personality or continues to attack people.

### Emblems and associated substances

- **Peach-palm:** *Bactris gasipaes* Kunth; Weiss’s older synonym is *Guilielma speciosa*.
- **Balsa wood:** material of Poporrona'’s throne or, in a later French account, a raft and seat.
- **Hardwood stake:** driven through the head and body into the earth.
- **Peach-palm fruit:** edible and used by Asháninka people to make a fermented drink.
- **Thorns:** in one fuller Yanesha episode, a yellow-headed vulture wipes or lowers its rear onto the transformed palm; the prick and the bird’s response account for the trunk’s thorns and regional differences in fruit abundance.
- **Maize gardens:** six successive stages encode the July–December growing period in Santos-Granero’s analysis.
- **Five days:** Ayots drinks and dances for five days before his capture; Poporrona' tells his killers to return after five days. Santos-Granero compares this temporal motif with other Yanesha-Christian narratives, but no numerological cult of “five” is documented.

### Iconography

**[Not established]** No securely identified precolonial or early historic image of Poporrona', Kíri, or Quërí was found. No prescribed colors, costume, weapon, celestial symbol, or cult statue is documented.

Saint Christopher carrying the Child Christ is **comparative evidence in Santos-Granero’s hypothesis**, not an indigenous image securely identified as Poporrona'. Rubens’s 1611–14 *Saint Christopher*, a 1423 German image, and a colonial Peruvian Saint Christopher painting reproduced in the 2002 article illustrate the proposed source motif. They must not be catalogued as depictions of Poporrona'/Kíri.

The recent Yanesha schoolbook illustration is a modern pedagogical image, not evidence of traditional iconographic convention.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical variants

| Tradition | Supreme ancestor | Elder transformer | Relationship to child |
|---|---|---|---|
| Yanesha | Yato' Yos (“Our Grandfather Yos”), supreme creator | Ayots, one of Yato' Yos’s sons | Poporrona' is Ayots’s son and Yato' Yos’s grandson |
| Asháninka/Ashéninka | Not specified in the relevant Kíri passage | Avíreri | Kíri is Avíreri’s grandson |
| Nomatsigenga | Not established from accessible text | Mábireri | Quërí corresponds to Kíri; exact kin term requires checking Shaver and Dodd’s original pages |
| One Asháninka variant | — | Avíreri | Avíreri and Kíri are not distinguished; Avíreri becomes the palm |
| Matsigenka structural analogue | — | Pachakamui carrying Iguiane | Iguiane is “the Carried One,” not securely genealogized as son or grandson |

No consort, offspring, sibling, servant, or mount of Poporrona'/Kíri is securely attested. The cousins are unnamed in the Yanesha summary. The beings who pursue and execute him are collective “people” or “warriors,” not individually named gods.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine or mythic being

1. **Ayots — father/son companionship and provocation.** Poporrona' rides on Ayots’s shoulders and repeatedly asks who encountered people are; the questions anger Ayots into transforming them. Source: two Yanesha versions recorded by Santos-Granero from Pedro Ortiz and Espíritu Francisco in 1983; published 1991 and summarized 2002. **[Later ethnographic attestation; Yanesha regional variant]**

2. **Ayots — imitation and succession in violence.** Poporrona' possesses the same transformative power and cannibal appetite as his father and plans to reproduce Ayots’s river-damming flood. Same source. **[Later ethnographic attestation; Yanesha regional variant]**

3. **Avíreri — grandfather/grandson companionship.** Avíreri carries Kíri on his back during his journeys; Kíri’s questions stimulate Avíreri’s transformations. Source: Weiss’s Asháninka fieldwork, 1960–64; dissertation 1969, paper 1970, monograph 1975. **[Early ethnographic attestation; Asháninka regional variant]**

4. **Avíreri — separation and attempted reunion.** After Avíreri reaches or is confined at Otsitiríko, Kíri sets out after him “heartsick for his grandfather” but is overtaken and killed. Weiss 1970/1975. **[Early ethnographic attestation; Asháninka regional variant]**

5. **Avíreri — identity fusion.** In a second Asháninka version, Avíreri and Kíri are not distinguished; Avíreri himself becomes the *kíri* palm. Weiss 1970/1975. **[Early ethnographic attestation; variant within the Asháninka record]**

6. **Yato' Yos — descent only.** Poporrona' is Yato' Yos’s grandson through Ayots. No meeting, dialogue, command, conflict, or rescue between them is recorded in the accessible narrative. **[Genealogical relation only; absence of narrated interaction]**

7. **Mábireri — corresponding elder companion.** Quërí is paired with Mábireri in the Nomatsigenga version. The exact action sequence is cited to Shaver and Dodd 1990:92–93, but the poorly OCRed primary scan prevents a line-by-line reconstruction here. **[Regional variant; interaction securely identified only in outline]**

8. **Iguiane — structural analogue, not a direct interaction.** The Matsigenka “Carried One” occupies a role comparable to Kíri: he empowers or accompanies Pachakamui, transforms relatives, is nailed down, and becomes a palm. Kíri and Iguiane never meet in any source. **[Comparative relation only]**

9. **Child Christ — proposed historical model, not an episode.** Santos-Granero argues that Kíri/Poporrona' was modeled partly upon the Child Christ carried by Saint Christopher. No indigenous narrative records Poporrona' meeting Christ. **[Syncretistic-identification hypothesis; no narrated interaction]**

10. **Saint Christopher — proposed structural counterpart, not an episode.** Poporrona'/Kíri and Christopher share invulnerability, a seat, cranial penetration, and a palm transformation motif in Santos-Granero’s comparison. They never coexist in an indigenous story. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**

11. **Pachákama — no interaction with Kíri.** Pachákama receives Avíreri at Otsitiríko and obtains his aid holding up the earth; Kíri is absent from that encounter. **[Absence of direct interaction]**

12. **Armadillo/Etíni — no securely narrated direct interaction with Poporrona'/Kíri.** Armadillo traps Ayots/Avíreri in revenge for the transformation of his children. Poporrona' remains behind. Although executions of accused child witches in armadillo holes recall this event, no direct Poporrona'–Armadillo encounter is narrated. **[Absence of direct interaction]**

No interaction with Yosoper, Koriošpiri, the Sun, Moon, Yacuruna, Chullachaqui, Bufeo, or other widely known Amazonian beings was found.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. The Yanesha Ayots–Poporrona' cycle

**[Later ethnographic attestation; recorded 1983]**

Ayots, a gigantic and malevolent son of Yato' Yos, travels carrying Poporrona' because the child cannot walk. Poporrona' looks like a child but is actually adult. Whenever they encounter someone he demands, repeatedly, “Who is that one?” Irritated, Ayots finally says that the person is a stone, animal, bee, termite, or other being; the power of his words makes the identification real.

Ayots’s sister, married to Armadillo while animals still possessed human form, sends her son and daughter to welcome the visitors. Poporrona' repeats his questioning. Ayots transforms the children into a woolly spider monkey and an agouti. In other versions of the broader complex, the transformed children become white monkeys, termites, or bees.

The children’s parents arrange revenge. They invite Ayots to a drinking and dancing festival and keep him drinking for five days. Exhausted, he accepts Armadillo’s invitation to rest in an underground house. Armadillo escapes and closes the entrance. Ayots remains below, supports the earth, and produces earthquakes when he struggles to escape.

Poporrona' remains with his aunt and an infant cousin. Although still small and ungrown, he possesses adult capacities and Ayots’s transformative power. While his aunt is absent, he turns the baby into rotten wood and flees.

The people pursue him and find him bathing in the river Ayots once dammed—or habitually tried to dam—to flood the country, kill its inhabitants, and eat them. Poporrona' plans the same act and speaks of humans as edible aquatic larvae.

His pursuers shoot arrows; the arrows strike one another. They swing clubs; they club one another. They try to cut him apart; their blades wound themselves. Poporrona', said to take pity on them, reveals the sole effective method. They must build a beautiful balsa throne, sit and bind him upon it, drive a hardwood stake through his head into the earth, and return after five days. They obey. On returning they find a tall peach-palm heavy with fruit.

This summary in the 2002 article was intentionally incomplete. Santos-Granero says the full myth contains two further episodes, published in his 1991 book.

### B. The maize-garden sequence

**[Yanesha regional variant; published 1991/1992]**

During his downriver flight Poporrona' clears and plants six maize gardens. His pursuers encounter them successively as ripe, fully fructified, fructifying, flowering, young, and newly sown. The sequence reverses the actual six-month maturation from July to December.

Santos-Granero reads this as a mythic representation of agricultural time. Peach-palm fructification and the position of Antares help organize the wet-season ritual calendar and women’s preparation of maize beer. This makes Poporrona' simultaneously:

- a malevolent transformer;
- the origin-person of an important food plant;
- a figure through whom agricultural succession is conceptualized.

It would therefore be reductive to identify him only as the prototype of an accused child witch.

### C. The vulture and the palm’s thorns

**[Yanesha folklore/etiological continuation]**

A published synopsis of the fuller version states that after Kíri/Poporrona' becomes the palm, a yellow-headed vulture climbs or remains upon it while the trunk bends toward different cardinal directions and fruit is lost in some regions. When the vulture descends, it passes wind or rubs its rear after being pricked; thorns subsequently grow on the trunk. The episode explains both the spiny stem and unequal geographic abundance or size of fruit.

The vulture is not securely named as a deity; it is a primordial bird-person or etiological actor. Because Santos-Granero omitted this portion from the 2002 article, it should not be silently combined with the condensed version as though both texts were identical.

### D. The Asháninka Avíreri–Kíri cycle

**[Early ethnographic attestation]**

Avíreri travels among primordial Asháninka people carrying his grandson Kíri. Kíri’s questioning repeatedly provokes Avíreri to identify encountered persons as animals or objects and thereby transform them. Through the cycle Avíreri produces animals, insect nests, landscape formations, day and night, wet and dry seasons, and rock formations marking encounters with colonial actors.

After Avíreri’s sister and brother-in-law trap him, he extends his escape tunnel to Otsitiríko at the river’s end. Pachákama asks him to remain and help hold up the earth; a strangler vine binds him there.

Kíri is left behind. Grieving, he follows. Others pursue him because of his complicity in Avíreri’s deeds. Their attempts to kill him fail. Kíri tells them to drive a wooden stake through his head, through his body, and into the earth. He becomes the *kíri*, or peach-palm, whose fruit is made into fermented drink.

This version differs sharply from the Yanesha account:

- Kíri seeks his grandfather rather than planning a flood.
- Weiss’s published summary does not mention the rotten-wood baby.
- The elaborate balsa throne and five-day return are absent.
- The execution is still self-prescribed.
- Kíri’s emotional attachment to Avíreri is explicit.
- In another Asháninka version, Kíri disappears as a separate person and Avíreri becomes the palm.

### E. Nomatsigenga Mábireri–Quërí

**[Regional variant]**

Shaver and Dodd’s 1990 account establishes Mábireri and Quërí as the local counterparts. Santos-Granero groups it with the narratives in which the evil child’s questions activate the older transformer and in which the child becomes a model for the child witch. Because the accessible scan does not expose pages 92–93 as searchable text, details not independently visible should remain attributed to Shaver and Dodd through Santos-Granero rather than presented as freshly verified quotation.

### F. Matsigenka analogue: Pachakamui and Iguiane

**[Regional analogue; not a Kíri text]**

Pareni, the Salt Lady, and Kinteroni the primordial armadillo lose children transformed by Iguiane, a small carried figure associated with Pachakamui. Kinteroni captures Pachakamui in a hole and nails Iguiane to the ground. Iguiane transforms Kinteroni into the giant armadillo and himself into the *kamona* palm.

Renard-Casevitz considers this narrative probably borrowed through trade with neighboring Asháninka. Santos-Granero notes both the resemblance and the significant fact that the Ayots/Kíri myth is otherwise absent among Matsigenka and Piro populations who did not share the documented child-sorcery institution. The relationship is thus one of regional circulation and structural transformation, not proof of a single pan-Arawak god.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No documented cult institution

**[Not established]** No temple, shrine, priesthood, festival dedicated specifically to Poporrona'/Kíri, sacrificial economy, oracle, pilgrimage, votive corpus, theophoric personal names, or organized worship was found.

The Yanesha possessed temples and ritual specialists, and the Asháninka had shamans and concepts of divine beings, but this does not demonstrate a Poporrona' cult. Poporrona' appears primarily in narrative, moral instruction, agricultural symbolism, and plant ontology.

### Peach-palm use

**[Ethnographic attestation]** The strongest practice associated with Kíri is ordinary and ceremonial use of the peach-palm. Weiss records that its fruit is made into a fermented beverage. Santos-Granero connects its fructification with maize beer and the seasonal ceremonial cycle. Eating or fermenting the fruit is not explicitly described as worshipping Kíri.

### Child-sorcery practice

Santos-Granero argues that the myth supplied an ideological model for the Southern Arawak concept of the child witch. The terms are:

- **Yanesha:** *amaseñet̃* in the 2002 typography;
- **Asháninka:** *máci*, *machi*, or *matsi*;
- **Nomatsigenga:** Shaver and Dodd describe children instructed by *tsiricóti*, a sparrow-like or malignant spirit bird.

Children were thought to become witches through dreams, contact with other witches, malignant birds or insects, or, in Yanesha accounts, a special plant rubbed on the body. Accused children allegedly buried food scraps, hair, scabs, sweat, or excrement—personally in dreams or through insect helpers—to produce illness.

Herbalists and shamans identified suspects through coca and tobacco divination or induced dreams. This is an ethnographic account of an accusation system, not evidence that accused children actually caused disease.

### Punishment and execution

**[Historically documented accusation practice, chiefly 1880–1960s]** Sources describe severe torture: beatings, smoke suffocation, starvation, near-drowning, and confinement. If the patient recovered, a child might be cleansed and released. If the patient died, execution could follow by bludgeoning, strangling, drowning, arrows, burning, burial in an armadillo hole, or exposure covered in honey near an anthill. Bodies might be burned or thrown into a river to prevent the dead child’s “shadow” from teaching others.

These reports occur in González 1880; Ordinaire 1885; Sala 1893; Batlle 1905; Navarro 1924; Palomino Arana and collaborators 1936; Pérez Marcio 1953; Eichenberger 1966; Weiss 1975; Smith 1977; Brown and Fernández 1991; Shaver and Dodd 1990; and Rojas Zolezzi 1994. Their agreement establishes the historical existence of accusations and lethal punishments, though not every reported case can be independently reconstructed.

The connection to Poporrona'/Kíri is interpretive but substantial:

- the accused is regarded as an adultlike evil person disguised as a child;
- both mythic child and accused witch possess invulnerability;
- both consume human flesh;
- both harm close kin;
- burial in an armadillo hole recalls Armadillo’s trapping of Ayots/Avíreri.

### Collective cleansing

During accusation waves in the 1890s, 1910s, 1950s, and 1960s, Yanesha communities reportedly held collective temple ceremonies. Suspected boys and girls drank tobacco syrup to induce vomiting and were secluded for a week with fasting, vigil, and sexual abstinence before reintegration. This is the closest documented ritual complex connected to the Poporrona' model, but it was directed at purging alleged witchcraft, not venerating Poporrona'.

### Human sacrifice?

**[Contested-material finding]** No sacrificial offering of humans *to Poporrona'/Kíri* is documented. The killing of accused children was punitive and apotropaic according to the sources, not temple sacrifice to the being. Labeling it “human sacrifice to Kíri” would be unsupported.

### Temple prostitution, cult violence, and political priesthood

**[Not established]** No evidence was found for temple prostitution, sacred sexual service, armies maintained by a Kíri cult, or priestly political institutions in his name.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before 1635: unattested

No archaeological, iconographic, epigraphic, or documentary evidence securely dates Poporrona'/Kíri to the precontact period. The possibility of an older transformer-and-palm story cannot be excluded; it is simply not directly demonstrable.

### 1635–1742: Franciscan mission period

Franciscan missions brought Christian teaching, saints’ images, crosses, medals, and epidemics into Southern Arawak country. A 1730 inventory for the Quimiri mission reportedly listed eleven statues, eleven large paintings, and numerous engravings. It does not identify a Saint Christopher image.

Colonial writers report sorcerers and missionary campaigns against practices they classified as pagan, but not child-witch executions and not Poporrona'/Kíri by name.

### 1742–c. 1752: Juan Santos Atahualpa and restored autonomy

Juan Santos Atahualpa led Yanesha, Asháninka, and allies against Spanish missions, combining Andean, Christian, and Amazonian religious ideas. The Franciscans were expelled from much of the region.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Santos-Granero proposes that between 1742 and 1847 the Christian Saint Christopher/Child Christ complex was transformed into Ayots/Poporrona' or Avíreri/Kíri. He connects this with indigenous appropriation of Christian objects, the experience of epidemics, and a reversal in which plague-protecting figures came to be perceived as plague-bringers.

This chronology is inferred. No eighteenth-century text records the transformation as it occurred.

### 1847–1895: recolonization, epidemics, and first child-witch reports

Franciscan recolonization and expanding national settlement brought displacement and epidemic disease. Yellow fever and smallpox struck Yanesha communities in 1879–80; measles devastated the new Quillazú mission in 1881. González’s 1880 report is the earliest identified record of Yanesha child sorcery.

In 1885 smallpox struck the upper Ucayali, and Ordinaire recorded Asháninka child-witch accusations. Reports proliferated between 1880 and 1895. This is the secure documentary emergence of the practice—not of the myth itself.

### 1890s–1910s

Colonization, armed resistance, mission expansion, and epidemics coincided with further accusation waves. The 1896–98 Asháninka, Ashéninka, Nomatsigenga, and Yanesha resistance movements provide the social context, but no source says that rebels mobilized under Poporrona' or Kíri’s name.

### 1920s–1930s

Adventist missionary Ferdinand Stahl reached the region in 1921. A 1928 transformative movement promised resurrection, an end to sickness, and destruction of the existing evil order. Measles epidemics followed. In 1933 almost half of some 300 converts at Sutziki reportedly died; accusations surged and children and adolescents sought refuge at the mission.

A Yanesha oral history recorded in 1983 describes a 1930s measles epidemic at the Iscozacin–Palcazu confluence: returnees found everyone dead except a nursing infant and killed him as the supposed cause. This is oral history about a historical epidemic, not a contemporaneous written court record.

### 1950s–1960s

Measles killed approximately half of some Upper Pangoa and Anapati Nomatsigenga in 1956; severe outbreaks also occurred among Asháninka populations in 1964. Child-witch accusations and executions were still documented during this period. By the later twentieth century the practice had substantially declined in many communities, although beliefs did not disappear.

### 1960–75: entry into the scholarly archive

Weiss’s fieldwork, dissertation, 1970 paper, and 1975 monograph produced the earliest durable scholarly record of Kíri. The transition from oral transmission into an anthropological corpus is the first securely traceable “publication history” of the figure.

### 1980s–1995: internal war and renewed accusations

The Shining Path and Túpac Amaru Revolutionary Movement entered Asháninka territories, producing killings, captivity, displacement, epidemic disease, and community militarization. From 1989 indigenous self-defense organizations fought to expel the insurgents, a process Santos-Granero dates through approximately 1995.

Santos-Granero reports that child-sorcery accusations re-emerged strongly in the Pichis, Tambo, and Ene valleys amid warfare, displacement, and epidemics. This is a documented modern revival of the accusation complex. There is no evidence that Shining Path itself promoted a Kíri cult or consciously used the myth.

### 1991–2005: historical reinterpretation

Santos-Granero published the fuller Yanesha cycle in 1991 and the Saint Christopher hypothesis in 2002, followed by the Spanish version in 2005. These works moved Poporrona' from a locally transmitted being into comparative scholarship on colonialism, mimesis, violence, and the history of myth.

### 2010s–present

French-language ethnobotanical research places Poporrona' within living Yanesha classifications of divine plant origins. A recent government schoolbook uses Póporrona' in Yanesha-language education. These sources demonstrate cultural and pedagogical continuity, though they do not establish organized worship.

No reliable evidence of a contemporary neopagan, occult, reconstructionist, or commercial Poporrona'/Kíri movement was found.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Anthropology and ethnobotany

Poporrona'/Kíri’s chief modern afterlife is academic. The figure has been used to investigate:

- transformation and the creation of species;
- the relationship between myth and mapped landscape;
- indigenous historical consciousness;
- agricultural calendrics;
- plant personhood;
- Christian–Amazonian syncretism;
- child-witch accusations and colonial violence.

### Indigenous education

**[Modern reception]** The Yanesha-language school text is the clearest evidence of contemporary presentation to younger readers. It converts the oral/ethnographic figure into a literacy and cultural-education subject. Any illustration in that book is newly commissioned educational art.

### Saint Christopher identification

Santos-Granero compares:

- Christopher carrying the Child Christ with Ayots/Avíreri carrying Poporrona'/Kíri;
- Christopher’s cannibal or monstrous origins with Ayots/Avíreri’s cannibalism;
- the Child Christ’s tremendous weight and questioning child motif with the vexing Amazonian child;
- Christopher’s survival of iron rods and arrows with Kíri’s invulnerability;
- Christopher’s seat and heated iron through the head with the balsa throne and hardwood spike;
- Christopher’s miraculous palm-bearing staff with Kíri’s transformation into a palm;
- Christ’s relation to the creator God with Ayots’s descent from Yato' Yos;
- Saint Christopher’s plague protection with Amazonian experience of epidemic catastrophe.

These parallels are real features of the compared texts. The direction, date, and mechanism of borrowing remain reconstructed.

A principal weakness is the lack of a mission document proving that Saint Christopher’s particular legend or image was taught in an affected Yanesha or Asháninka settlement. A second is that palm-person etiology and transformer narratives fit broader Amazonian patterns independently of Christianity. A principal strength is the unusually dense cluster of specific correspondences, including the carried child, failed attacks, seat, head-piercing, and palm.

The responsible conclusion is **possible colonial syncretism, strongly argued but not demonstrated by a transmission document**.

### Occultism and neopaganism

A dedicated search combining all name forms with *occult*, *esoteric*, *neopagan*, *reconstructionist*, and comparable terms found no substantiated movement, grimoire tradition, Golden Dawn identification, chaos-magic current, New Age cult, or Jungian school centered on the being.

### Literature, film, games, commerce, and politics

No materially influential literary, cinematic, comics, gaming, nationalist, extremist, or commercial appropriation was located. Popular internet mythology tends instead to foreground pan-Peruvian beings such as the Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, Bufeo, Tunche, and Sachamama. Those are separate folklore cycles. Their general Amazonian setting and transformative capacities do not make them forms of Poporrona'/Kíri.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### The principal debate: indigenous antiquity or colonial transformation?

Three positions must be distinguished.

1. **An older indigenous core is possible.** Transformer beings who create animals, plants, and landscape are widespread in Amazonian myth. The peach-palm person has a transparent botanical identity in Yanesha and Asháninka.

2. **The extant form may contain colonial material.** Priests, launches, merchandise, mission landscapes, Christian images, and colonial people appear within Asháninka transformer narratives. The traditions are historical and absorb new persons and technologies.

3. **Santos-Granero’s specific Saint Christopher derivation is a reconstruction.** He dates the initial appropriation to the Franciscan period, its inversion to the era of epidemic and anti-missionary reaction, and its completion to 1742–1847. The evidence is comparative morphology plus historical plausibility, not a chain of dated indigenous texts.

These positions need not be mutually exclusive: an indigenous transformer-and-plant complex could have absorbed hagiographic motifs. The current record cannot identify the exact proportion of each.

### Does the myth cause child-witch persecution?

Santos-Granero calls Ayots/Poporrona' or Avíreri/Kíri the mythical referent of child sorcery because:

- it occurs among the four Southern Arawak groups where he finds child-witch belief;
- it is absent among Piro and Matsigenka groups without the same institution;
- its child is adultlike, malevolent, cannibal, antisocial, kin-killing, and difficult to kill;
- adults tell it to children as a warning about anger and self-control.

But mythic correspondence does not establish a single-direction cause. Epidemic mortality, orphan vulnerability, colonial pressure, existing adult-witchcraft concepts, and social crisis are also necessary parts of his explanation. The strongest historically supported correlation is between accusation waves and periods of epidemic, violence, or displacement.

### Regional differentiation

Weiss’s Asháninka Kíri is emotionally attached to Avíreri and less elaborately monstrous in the published ending than Yanesha Poporrona'. Avíreri is ambivalent and landscape-forming; Ayots and Poporrona' are characterized as wholly evil. The regional moral contrast is a major finding, not noise to be harmonized away.

The Matsigenka analogue further complicates Santos-Granero’s initial distributional claim. Renard-Casevitz treats it as a different, probably borrowed narrative. It shows that motifs circulated across linguistic and trade networks even where the name Kíri and the child-sorcery institution were absent.

### Textual state and archives

- Weiss’s major record exists as a dissertation, congress paper, and American Museum monograph.
- Santos-Granero’s 1983 Yanesha recordings underlie the 1991 and 2002 publications; the dossier found no public audio archive for them.
- Shaver and Dodd’s Nomatsigenga book survives in an open but weakly OCRed scan.
- Franciscan records survive in printed compilations and the Archivo del Convento de Ocopa; Rodríguez Tena’s 1780 work is cited as Manuscript 84.
- Much of the evidence is mediated through ethnographers’ translations and selections. Santos-Granero expressly omitted two episodes from his 2002 synopsis.
- No indigenous precolonial manuscript tradition, inscriptional corpus, or ancient iconographic corpus exists for this being.

### Terminological problems

“Campa” is the historical exonym used in older titles. Modern discussion should specify Asháninka, Ashéninka, Nomatsigenga, or the particular regional population where possible.

“Witch” and “sorcerer” are translations. Santos-Granero deliberately uses them interchangeably because Evans-Pritchard’s classic distinction between innate witchcraft and learned sorcery does not fit the Southern Arawak material neatly.

### Discredited or unsupported claims

- **“Kíri is simply Jesus Christ under an indigenous name.”** Unsupported. Santos-Granero proposes partial modeling, not identity.
- **“The name Kíri is proven to derive from Cristo.”** Unsupported; the article says it *could* derive from *Kiristu*.
- **“Poporrona' is a pre-Columbian god documented by early missionaries.”** Unsupported.
- **“Children were sacrificed to Poporrona'.”** Unsupported.
- **“Poporrona', Kíri, and Quërí have identical narratives.”** False to the recorded variation.
- **“The being belongs to the general Iquitos Bufeo–Yacuruna–Chullachaqui folklore complex.”** No evidence found.
- **“There is a continuous formal cult from antiquity to the present.”** No evidence found.
- **“The whole narrative is a modern fabrication by anthropologists.”** Also unsupported: multiple researchers documented related versions among several communities, beginning with fieldwork in 1960–64.
