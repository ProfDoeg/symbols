# Ayots / Avíreri (Mábireri): Research Dossier

**Class:** Male transformer-deity/culture hero (*tasórentsi*, “transforming deity,” and *pinkátsori*, “one who is feared/ruler,” in Asháninka classification); gigantic and morally dangerous or ambivalent rather than simply a “demon.” In Yanesha accounts Ayots is a malevolent divine son of the creator Yato’ Yos; the Nomatsigenga Mábireri is a legendary creator of animals.  
**Earliest secure attestation:** Gerald Weiss’s Asháninka field recordings from 1960–64, incorporated into his 1969 doctoral dissertation, followed by the published “Campa Cosmology” and its expanded 1975 monograph. A Nomatsigenga Mábireri narrative was published in the proceedings of the XXXIX International Congress of Americanists, based on twentieth-century field collection. An apparent 1935–37 Matsigenka reference concerns Pachákama/Avíreri equivalence, not an independently preserved full Ayots–child narrative. No presently identified seventeenth- or eighteenth-century Franciscan source names Ayots, Avíreri, or Mábireri.  
**Geographic and linguistic setting:** Yanesha, Asháninka/Ashéninka, and Nomatsigenga communities of Peru’s Selva Central, especially the Perené, Ene, Tambo, Pichis, Gran Pajonal, Satipo, Pangoa, and adjoining drainage systems. Matsigenka material is a neighboring, probably borrowed or interwoven variant and must not be treated as proof that the complete cycle was universal among Southern Arawak peoples.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EARLY DOCUMENTED]** Earliest securely recoverable ethnographic record, principally fieldwork from the 1960s onward.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** A version particular to Yanesha, Asháninka/Ashéninka, Nomatsigenga, Perené, Tambo, or Matsigenka narrators.
- **[LATER DOCUMENTED]** Later field collection, educational publication, dictionary, government account, or retelling traceable to Indigenous testimony or earlier ethnography.
- **[COLONIAL CONTEXT]** Documentary evidence concerning missions, epidemics, or political history, not necessarily naming the deity.
- **[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** An anthropologist’s historical, structural, or comparative interpretation.
- **[SYNCRETISTIC HYPOTHESIS]** A proposed historical fusion or borrowing, not an identity stated straightforwardly by the narrators.
- **[FOLKLORE]** A circulating oral narrative without a demonstrable ancient or colonial textual date.
- **[MODERN REINVENTION]** A recent civic, environmental, commercial, literary, or popular use.
- **[NOT ESTABLISHED]** A claim for which the searched record produced no adequate evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and classifications

### Ayots

**[REGIONAL VARIANT: Yanesha]** Ayots is the name in the Yanesha version recorded by Fernando Santos-Granero from Pedro Ortiz and Espíritu Francisco in 1983 and published in *The Power of Love* (1991), then abridged in 2002. Ayots is a son of **Yato’ Yos** (“Our Grandfather Yos”), the supreme creator. He is described as a gigantic, evil divinity whose spoken classifications transform persons into stones, bees, termites, or animals. His child is **Poporrona’**, explicitly his son rather than grandson.

The Yanesha dictionary gives *ayots* both as “earth tremor/earthquake” and as the name of a legendary personage. That lexical convergence is significant because the myth makes the imprisoned Ayots the cause of earthquakes. It does not by itself determine whether the common noun produced the personal name, the name produced the earthquake term, or both preserve an older association. No published historical etymology establishing the direction of derivation was located.

### Avíreri

**[EARLY DOCUMENTED: Asháninka/Ashéninka]** *Avíreri*, also printed without diacritics as *Avireri*, is the principal Asháninka/Ashéninka form. Weiss calls him a *tasórentsi*, a nominal derivative associated with “blowing”: transformer-deities alter beings by a puff of breath. They may also be called *pinkátsori*, literally “one who is feared,” hence “ruler.” Avíreri is accordingly not merely a hero or generic spirit. He belongs to the highest class of powerful transforming beings, alongside Pachákama, Inkanítari, Kashíri, Pává, and Inka. Weiss nevertheless distinguishes Pává/Sun as the paramount beneficent deity. Avíreri is an ancestral Asháninka “among Asháninka,” a world-shaper whose actions are alternately creative, protective, capricious, cannibalistic, and catastrophic. [Weiss’s classification and narrative appear in the open proceedings text.](https://repositorio.iep.org.pe/server/api/core/bitstreams/f3e7aad0-3db7-46f2-a682-7ea329e87fa6/content)

The spelling **Avíeri** appears occasionally in secondary Spanish reproductions but is probably a typographical omission of *r*, not a demonstrably separate divine name. No secure lexical etymology of *Avíreri* was located. Modern explanations that treat the word as transparently meaning “creator” appear to infer a meaning from the figure’s function.

### Mábireri

**[REGIONAL VARIANT: Nomatsigenga]** *Mábireri* is the Nomatsigenga counterpart, with spelling variants **Mabireri, Nabireri, Yabireri** in later linguistic and ethnographic materials. Shaver and Dodds’s dictionary defines him cautiously as “personaje legendario que creó a los animales”—“a legendary personage who created the animals.” Their ethnography recounts him carrying a young relative and transforming people, by blowing upon them, into bees, rabbits, monkeys, wild turkeys, and other animals. The child is **Quërí/Querí**, corresponding structurally and perhaps linguistically to Asháninka Kíri. [The Peruvian Ministry of Culture’s Nomatsigenga synthesis reproduces this classification and narrative.](https://repositorio.cultura.gob.pe/bitstream/handle/CULTURA/658/Los%20nomatsiguenga%20de%20la%20selva%20central.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1)

### Composite identity and the danger of flattening it

**[MODERN SCHOLARLY SYSTEMATIZATION]** “Ayots/Avíreri/Mábireri” is a comparative scholarly grouping, not a single pan-Arawak liturgical name. The figures are homologous because their narratives share a transformer carrying a dangerous pseudo-child, interrogative prompting, transformations, kin conflict, and—in the Yanesha and Asháninka cycles—confinement or expulsion. The genealogies, methods of transformation, kin terms, moral emphases, and endings differ.

“A gigantic evil divinity” accurately describes Santos-Granero’s Yanesha summary and his cross-cultural formulation, but it understates Asháninka ambivalence. Weiss’s Avíreri creates day/night, wet/dry seasons, useful plants, ceremonies, animals, and landscape features; he also destroys relatives, attempts a lethal flood, and is removed to stop further chaos. Government and educational retellings consequently call him a “mythical hero” or “semigod,” while older ethnography calls him a god or transformer. These are differing translations of Indigenous categories, not evidence that his class changed from demon to hero.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Absence from the colonial record

**[COLONIAL CONTEXT; NEGATIVE FINDING]** Franciscan contact with Southern Arawak peoples began by approximately 1635. Sources surveyed by Santos-Granero—including Córdoba Salinas, Biedma’s 1682 account, San Antonio’s 1750 report, Amich, and the later collections of Izaguirre—discuss missionization, sorcery, ritual anthropophagy, polygyny, resistance, and the campaigns surrounding Juan Santos Atahualpa. They do not securely name Ayots, Avíreri, or Mábireri, nor do they document the complete child-transformer myth.

This silence cannot prove the narrative did not exist, but it prevents a claim of documented pre-Hispanic antiquity. It is especially relevant to Santos-Granero’s historical argument because missionaries actively reported practices they considered “idolatrous” or immoral. The same colonial sources also do not report the institution of killing alleged child sorcerers. The first reports of that practice date to 1880–95, especially Ordinaire’s 1885 observations. [Santos-Granero lays out the negative colonial evidence and the late-nineteenth-century reports here.](https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/ec1a0175-8552-44f3-a358-bb24cb826716/content)

### Corpus

1. **Gerald Weiss, *The Cosmology of the Campa Indians of Eastern Peru* (1969 dissertation).**  
   **[EARLY DOCUMENTED]** English-language ethnography based principally on fieldwork among River Asháninka in 1960–64. This is the earliest presently secure, substantial documentation of Avíreri located in the accessible record.

2. **Gerald Weiss, “Campa Cosmology,” proceedings of the XXXIX International Congress of Americanists; expanded as *Campa Cosmology: The World of a Forest Tribe in South America* (1975).**  
   **[EARLY DOCUMENTED]** The fullest early Asháninka cycle. It identifies Avíreri as transformer, culture-bringer, creator of temporal divisions, maker of landscape landmarks, attempted flood-maker, and captive earth-supporter. The 1975 monograph occupies pp. 219–588 of *Anthropological Papers of the American Museum of Natural History*, vol. 52. [Bibliographic record.](https://iportal.usask.ca/record/45652)

3. **Mary Ruth Wise, Nomatsiguenga narrative and structural analysis in the XXXIX Congress proceedings.**  
   **[EARLY DOCUMENTED; REGIONAL VARIANT]** A full Nomatsigenga-language Mábireri narrative with free translation and plot/kinship analysis. Its episodes include children changed into termites, people changed into rocks, a baby changed into rock, Mábireri’s attempted killing, and the young relative’s transformation into a peach-palm. This is more primary than summaries in later handbooks because it preserves a narrated text.

4. **Richard Chase Smith, *Deliverance from Chaos for a Song* (1977 dissertation).**  
   **[EARLY DOCUMENTED: Yanesha comparative context]** A study of Yanesha ritual and cosmology. It is foundational for Yanesha sacred geography, ceremonial organization, music, and the ordering of primordial chaos, but the fully published Ayots–Poporrona’ text used here comes from Santos-Granero’s later fieldwork.

5. **Fernando Santos-Granero, *The Power of Love: The Moral Use of Knowledge amongst the Amuesha of Central Peru* (1991).**  
   **[EARLY DOCUMENTED: Yanesha]** Based on long-term field research; publishes the Ayots material at pp. 152 and 154 and discusses anger, morality, and sorcery around p. 102.

6. **Harold Shaver and Lois Dodds, *Los nomatsiguenga de la selva central* and Nomatsigenga lexicographical work (1990).**  
   **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** The main cited ethnographic source for Mábireri and Quërí, especially pp. 92–93. The dictionary treats Mábireri as a legendary animal-maker rather than imposing the Spanish category *dios*. [Government repository edition.](https://repositorio.cultura.gob.pe/bitstream/handle/CULTURA/658/Los%20nomatsiguenga%20de%20la%20selva%20central.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1)

7. **France-Marie Renard-Casevitz, “Le banquet masqué”/Pareni studies (1994), with Renard-Casevitz and Dollfus (1988), and Gerhard Baer (1994).**  
   **[REGIONAL VARIANT: Matsigenka]** The Matsigenka Pareni/Salt Woman cycle contains material interwoven with a narrative similar to Avíreri’s. Renard-Casevitz regarded the Avíreri-like component as belonging to a “different tradition,” probably adopted through trade relations with neighboring Asháninka. It therefore contradicts any simple claim that the complete myth is primordial and uniformly Matsigenka.

8. **Fernando Santos-Granero, “Saint Christopher in the Amazon: Child Sorcery, Colonialism, and Violence among the Southern Arawak,” *Ethnohistory* 49.3 (2002), pp. 507–543.**  
   **[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** The principal comparative argument linking Ayots/Avíreri to child-sorcery accusations and proposing that the myth is an inverted appropriation of Saint Christopher and the Christ Child. The article states that the Yanesha text is an abridgment of two versions recorded in 1983. [Smithsonian repository copy.](https://repository.si.edu/items/55d98de9-c2d4-4ac0-843a-01ab1dbbd079)

9. **Recent Asháninka oral-history, legal, ecological, and educational collections.**  
   **[LATER DOCUMENTED]** These continue to record Avíreri as landscape-transformer and creator. A recent account from Marontoari links him to stingless bees; school and oral-literature collections preserve the nephew-transformation and drinking-party episodes. They demonstrate continuing narration, not independent ancient attestation.

### Sources named in the brief but not part of this deity’s direct corpus

**[NEGATIVE FINDING]** Gow’s Piro/Yine works, Regan’s Loreto study, Iquitos collections by Galeano and Cabrejos, Dean’s Urarina ethnography, and Loreto/Ucayali cycles of the Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, and Pishtaco are important comparative Amazonian materials. The searched evidence does not make those figures forms, epithets, descendants, or cult associates of Ayots/Avíreri/Mábireri. Santos-Granero specifically observes that the Ayots/Avíreri child-sorcery myth is absent among Piro/Yine and from the Matsigenka groups he contrasts with the four communities practicing child-sorcerer accusations. Comparative proximity is not a documented divine relationship.

## 3. Domains, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and powers

**[EARLY DOCUMENTED: Asháninka]**

- Transformation by breath or declaration.
- Production of animal species, rocks, trees, insect nests, and named landscape features from primordial human-like persons.
- Institution of alternating day and night and of wet and dry seasons.
- Creation of wild fruit trees.
- Foundation or origination of festivals and their music in some Asháninka summaries.
- Petrification of dangerous outsiders, including a priest, colonist settlements, merchandise, and a steam launch.
- Attempted construction of a dam at Kentipánko, potentially to flood and consume humanity.
- Permanent support of the earth at River’s End after Pachákama prevents his return.
- Association with earthquakes through struggle beneath or beyond the inhabited world.

**[REGIONAL VARIANT: Yanesha]** Ayots transforms by authoritative speech: his child asks what a being is; Ayots answers “stone,” “bee,” “termite,” or an animal, and the person becomes the named thing.

**[REGIONAL VARIANT: Nomatsigenga]** Mábireri normally transforms by blowing. He is especially remembered as the agent who made most animal species.

### Physical form

**[DOCUMENTED ORAL DESCRIPTION]** He is male, anthropomorphic, and gigantic in Santos-Granero’s Yanesha synthesis. Weiss’s Asháninka presentation emphasizes that primordial deities were originally human-like Asháninka rather than giving a fixed monstrous anatomy. He carries a child or grandchild on his shoulders or back.

No horns, wings, animal head, prescribed color, sacred number, calendrical day, planet, weapon, or standardized costume is securely attested.

### Iconography

**[NOT ESTABLISHED]** No precolonial image, temple statue, petroglyph securely identified as Ayots/Avíreri/Mábireri, or standardized Indigenous iconographic program was located. The primary “images” are topographic: rocks and landforms understood as his transformations or as traces of his activity.

**[MODERN SCHOLARLY COMPARISON]** Santos-Granero juxtaposed the carried-child motif with medals and paintings of Saint Christopher carrying the Christ Child, including a sixteenth-century Peruvian painting in Cuzco Cathedral. The Christian art is evidence for the proposed model, not surviving Indigenous iconography of Ayots.

### Recognizable material signs

- **Imóro Avíreri:** the hole through which Avíreri fell, identified by Weiss on Mapínini grassland above the Yorinaki tributary of the Upper Perené.
- **Kentipánko:** a dam-like mountain on the Upper Tambo, said to be the remains of his attempted dam.
- **Tívíha salt spring cliff:** a malevolent owner whom Avíreri petrified.
- **A bathing woman’s rock near Tonkáma:** a woman transformed while bathing.
- **Manihiróni/Peña de Wertheman:** a steam launch and its white crew petrified while ascending to molest or abduct Asháninka.
- **Oararoncipánko:** stone outcrops identified as a white-built landing, crates, or merchandise transformed into rock.
- **Kíri/pijuayo:** the peach-palm produced by the impalement and self-transformation of the child/grandchild.

These are narrated places and etiological identifications, not archaeologically verified remains of supernatural transformations.

### Every documented named divine or primordial-being interaction

- **Yato’ Yos — father and son.** Ayots is one of the sons of the supreme creator Yato’ Yos. Source: Yanesha versions recorded in 1983; Santos-Granero 1991 and 2002. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Poporrona’ — father carries son; the son’s questions trigger transformations.** Poporrona’ later exercises the same power independently. Source: 1983 Yanesha versions, published 1991/2002. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Kíri — grandfather carries grandson; Kíri is complicit in transformations, mourns Avíreri, and becomes the peach-palm.** Source: Weiss’s 1960–64 Asháninka fieldwork, published from 1969 onward. **[EARLY DOCUMENTED]**
- **Quërí/Querí — Mábireri carries a young relative whose questions precipitate transformations.** Source: Nomatsigenga narrative; Shaver and Dodds 1990; Santos-Granero 2002. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Armadillo/Etíni/Kinteroni — brother-in-law and captor.** After Avíreri/Ayots transforms Armadillo’s children, Armadillo digs or uses an underground house/trap and confines him. In Weiss’s Asháninka version Avíreri turns the frightened brother-in-law into the armadillo *etíni*. Source: Weiss 1969/1975; Santos-Granero 1991/2002. **[EARLY DOCUMENTED; REGIONAL VARIANTS]**
- **Pachákama — receives and restrains Avíreri at Otsitiríko/River’s End.** Pachákama asks Avíreri to help hold up the earth because he does not want him returning to cause chaos; a strangler vine binds Avíreri. Source: Weiss 1969/1975. **[EARLY DOCUMENTED: ASHÁNINKA]**
- **Pachacamac — proposed external source of Pachákama’s name.** Weiss judges Asháninka *Pachákama* “surely derived” from the pre-Conquest coastal Peruvian deity Pachacamac. This is an etymological/syncretistic proposal, not a narrated meeting between Avíreri and coastal Pachacamac. **[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]**
- **Pareni/Kipátsi — no secure direct meeting with Avíreri.** Her Matsigenka cycle is interwoven with Avíreri-like material, but no source examined establishes a scene in which named Pareni and named Avíreri interact. **[ABSENCE OF EVIDENCE]**
- **Ciraráto — thwarts Avíreri’s flood.** In a later summarized Tambo narrative, this named bird-being prevents Avíreri from completing a dam intended to kill the Asháninka. Source transmitted through modern Asháninka ethnographic retelling. **[REGIONAL VARIANT]**
- **Saint Christopher — no Indigenous narrative meeting.** The two are compared by Santos-Granero; they do not meet within a traditional episode. **[MODERN SCHOLARLY COMPARISON]**
- **Christ Child — no Indigenous narrative meeting.** Kíri/Poporrona’ is hypothesized to be a transformed counterpart, not an explicitly named Christ in the Indigenous story. **[SYNCRETISTIC HYPOTHESIS]**

No documented interaction was found with Pává, Kashíri, Inkanítari, Inka, Juan Santos Atahualpa, Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, or Pishtaco merely because these figures occur in adjoining cosmological or folkloric corpora.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The Yanesha Ayots–Poporrona’ cycle

**[REGIONAL VARIANT; recorded 1983]** Ayots, gigantic son of Yato’ Yos, carries Poporrona’ because the latter cannot walk. Poporrona’ looks like a child and refuses to grow but possesses adult intelligence, appetites, and mystical power. On encountering anyone he pesters Ayots—“Who is that?”—until Ayots angrily names the person as a stone or animal. The utterance changes the victim into the named form.

Ayots’s sister sends her son and daughter to welcome the visitors. Poporrona’ questions their identity; Ayots transforms them into a woolly spider monkey and an agouti. Their parents prepare retaliation. They hold a drinking and dancing feast for five days, exhaust Ayots with manioc beer, and have Armadillo invite him into an underground house. When Ayots sleeps, Armadillo escapes and seals him inside. Ayots remains there supporting the earth; his attempted escapes produce earthquakes.

Poporrona’ remains with his aunt. In her absence he transforms an infant cousin into rotten wood and flees. Pursuers find him bathing at a river which Ayots formerly dammed in order to flood the land, kill people, and eat them. Poporrona’ plans the same act and calls humans edible aquatic larvae. Arrows, clubs, and cutting weapons rebound on his attackers. He finally explains how he can be killed: build him a balsa-wood throne and drive a hardwood stake through his head into the ground. Five days after the execution, a fruit-laden peach-palm stands where he was impaled. [The full published abridgment is available in Spanish here.](https://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?pid=S0254-92122005000100002&script=sci_arttext)

This cycle explains earthquakes, animal origins, the peach-palm, the danger of uncontrolled anger, and the anti-social potential assigned to the pseudo-child. Its explicit linkage to actual accusations of child sorcery is Santos-Granero’s interpretation, albeit one supported by strong structural parallels.

### The Asháninka Avíreri cycle

**[EARLY DOCUMENTED]** Avíreri is an Asháninka primordial person and transformer who travels with Kíri. Through repeated transformations he differentiates a once more homogeneous world into animals, plants, rocks, insects, and landmarks. He establishes day and night, dry and rainy seasons, and—in some versions—festival music and wild fruit trees.

The cycle is topographically cumulative rather than one fixed epic. At Tívíha, Tonkáma, Manihiróni, Oararoncipánko, and Kentipánko, particular landforms are narrated as the durable outcome of his acts. Some victims are relatives; others are dangerous whites and colonists, allowing later episodes to encode Asháninka experience of foreign intrusion. The steam-launch episode cannot predate the introduction of steam navigation and therefore is a historical accretion to an adaptable transformer cycle.

When Avíreri’s sister and other kin tire of his transformations, his brother-in-law digs a hole beneath the dancing ground. The sister induces him to dance until the roof collapses. Denied help, Avíreri transforms her into the *shimáshiri* tree (*Myroxylon balsamum*) and her husband into armadillo. He extends the hole to Otsitiríko, emerges at River’s End, and encounters Pachákama. Pachákama, fearing renewed chaos, asks him to remain and help support the earth; a strangler vine secures him.

Kíri follows in grief but is overtaken by people seeking vengeance for his complicity. Ordinary attacks fail. Kíri instructs them to impale him with a wooden stake, after which he becomes the peach-palm whose fruit makes fermented drink. Weiss records an alternate version in which Avíreri and Kíri are not differentiated and Avíreri himself becomes the palm. This is a genuine variant, not an error to be harmonized.

### The Nomatsigenga Mábireri cycle

**[REGIONAL VARIANT]** Mábireri carries a boy or young relative. When the child asks who someone is, Mábireri blows upon the person and names the creature into which the victim changes. Bees, rabbits, monkeys, wild turkeys, and other species arise this way.

The published structural analysis divides the narrative into transformations of children into termites, people into rocks, and a baby into rock. Mábireri is the “villain” in parts of that analytic scheme, but this is a narratological role, not proof that Nomatsigenga theology classifies him as an absolute devil. One sequence has opponents attempt to kill Mábireri; another ends with the young co-villain or relative becoming a fruit-bearing peach-palm. The variations show a creator whose destructive transformations nevertheless yield the present edible and ecological world.

### The Matsigenka Pareni complex

**[REGIONAL BORROWING HYPOTHESIS]** Pareni, Salt Woman, is a powerful female figure associated with Kipátsi/Earth. Her cycle includes husbands transformed into hummingbird, dung beetle, and sweat bee, followed by marriage to the primordial armadillo Kinteroni. Renard-Casevitz found an Avíreri-like narrative interwoven with this material but treated it as a different tradition, probably obtained through Asháninka trade.

This qualifies Santos-Granero’s broader statement that the myth was absent among Matsigenka: the specific child-sorcerer configuration was not an established indigenous Matsigenka institution in his comparison, but an adjacent Avíreri-like sequence had entered at least one Pareni corpus. Presence of a borrowed episode and absence of the complete child-sorcery complex are compatible findings.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

**[NOT ESTABLISHED]** No temple dedicated specifically to Ayots, Avíreri, or Mábireri; no specialized priesthood bearing his name; no calendrical feast uniquely addressed to him; no standard sacrifice; no votive inscription; and no documented oracle were located. Calling the figure a “god” must not import a Mediterranean temple-cult model.

Asháninka festivals involving manioc beer, dancing, panpipes, and gratitude to Pává are documented. Avíreri is credited in some accounts with originating festivals or their music, and a drinking/dancing party is the mechanism of his defeat. That does not demonstrate ritual worship of Avíreri.

Yanesha ceremonial centers and priestly leaders existed, including a twentieth-century temple at Palmazu containing stone figures of three divinities. Santos-Granero cites this as evidence of Christian ritual imitation; he does not identify those figures as Ayots. It would therefore be unsafe to assign Ayots a temple cult.

### Child-sorcery accusations and killings

**[DOCUMENTED HISTORICAL PRACTICE, NOT SACRIFICE]** Between the late nineteenth and twentieth centuries, children accused of sorcery could be beaten, strangled, drowned, shot, burned, buried in an armadillo hole, or coated with honey and tied near an anthill. Some were instead ritually purged or delivered to missions; from the 1920s some were sold as servants to colonists. These deaths were punitive executions within sorcery accusations, not offerings sacrificed to Ayots/Avíreri.

Santos-Granero notes a suggestive ritual resemblance: burial in an armadillo hole recalls Armadillo’s confinement of Ayots. Executions could occur during nocturnal manioc-beer feasts. The sources do not show victims being “given” to the deity, and “human sacrifice to Avíreri” would be misleading.

The earliest reports of child-sorcery accusations occur in 1880–95 amid military recolonization and epidemics. Missions at Puerto Ocopa and on the Perené later became refuges for accused children. The practice diminished among Yanesha and Perené Ashéninka during the twentieth century but persisted longer elsewhere. [The documentary chronology and execution forms are assembled in Santos-Granero’s article.](https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/ec1a0175-8552-44f3-a358-bb24cb826716/content)

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before European contact

**[NOT DOCUMENTED]** Comparative linguistics and the broad Southern Arawak transformer pattern make some precontact ancestry possible, but no surviving pre-Hispanic text, inscription, or securely identified image names the figure. Claims that the complete Ayots–child cycle is demonstrably pre-Columbian exceed the evidence.

### Approximately 1635–1742: Franciscan mission period

**[COLONIAL CONTEXT]** Franciscans introduced Christian preaching, saints’ images, crosses, medals, and hagiographic material into the Selva Central. Colonial sources document Indigenous sorcery concepts but not the named Ayots/Avíreri cycle or child-sorcerer executions.

### 1742–1847: political autonomy after Juan Santos Atahualpa

**[HISTORICAL FACT + RECONSTRUCTION]** Juan Santos Atahualpa’s multiethnic revolt expelled Spanish missions from much of the region. Santos-Granero proposes that the transformation of Saint Christopher’s legend into Ayots/Avíreri was completed during this autonomous interval. No text from the interval records that transformation; its placement here is inferential.

### 1847–1895: reconquest, colonization, epidemic crisis

**[DOCUMENTED CONTEXT]** Renewed Peruvian military occupation, settlement, displacement, forced labor, and epidemics destabilized the region. Yellow fever and smallpox devastated Yanesha communities in 1879–80; smallpox affected Asháninka in 1885. Reports of alleged child sorcery appear for the first time between 1880 and 1895. This chronology supports a postcolonial development of the accusation complex, though it does not date the transformer myth itself.

### 1890s–1960s: recurring crises and ethnographic visibility

**[DOCUMENTED]** Accusations intensified during epidemic and political crises in the 1890s, 1910s, 1930s, 1950s, and 1960s. Missionaries sometimes condemned the killings and sheltered accused children. Avíreri also absorbed historical-material episodes: priests, white colonists, merchandise, and a steam launch became petrified features of Asháninka territory.

### 1960s–1990s: systematic recording

**[EARLY/LATER DOCUMENTED]** Weiss’s 1960–64 fieldwork produced the first full scholarly Asháninka corpus presently located. Smith, Santos-Granero, Shaver and Dodds, Renard-Casevitz, Baer, Brown and Fernández, and others subsequently documented Yanesha, Nomatsigenga, Matsigenka, and Asháninka variants and their historical settings.

### 1980s–1995: armed conflict

**[DOCUMENTED HISTORY]** Sendero Luminoso and the MRTA brought killings, displacement, epidemics, forced recruitment, and severe disruption to Asháninka and neighboring communities. From 1989 Indigenous self-defense organizations increasingly expelled insurgent forces. Santos-Granero reports renewed child-sorcery accusations in the Pichis, Tambo, and Ene valleys under these pressures. The evidence supports association with crisis; it does not show that Sendero worshipped or appropriated Avíreri.

### Twenty-first century

**[CONTINUING TRADITION AND MODERN CIVIC RECEPTION]** Avíreri remains part of oral history, place interpretation, Indigenous education, ecological knowledge, and public presentations of Asháninka heritage. In 2021 UNESCO designated the **Avireri-Vraem Biosphere Reserve**, explicitly explaining its name through the Asháninka world-shaper who distinguished day/night and rainy/dry seasons, created their music, and petrified invaders. This is the clearest large-scale modern public commemoration of the figure. [UNESCO’s official account.](https://www.unesco.org/en/mab/avireri-vraem)

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Anthropological reinvention

**[MODERN SCHOLARLY SYSTEMATIZATION]** Ethnographers made separate oral cycles legible as a comparative “Ayots/Avíreri” complex. Santos-Granero’s 2002 essay particularly reshaped interpretation by reading it through colonial mimesis and child-sorcery history.

### Cultural-heritage figure

**[MODERN REINVENTION]** Government, UNESCO, school, and tourism materials increasingly present Avíreri as a benign “mythical hero,” “semigod,” or “creator.” This public image selects the ordering and anti-invasion episodes while often omitting cannibalism, kin transformations, attempted flooding, and dangerous anger. It is not false that he shapes and protects the world, but the one-sided benevolent profile is modern simplification.

The Avireri-Vraem name now joins an Indigenous divine name to the modern acronym VRAEM, “Valley of the Apurímac, Ene and Mantaro Rivers.” It is a twenty-first-century conservation and regional-identity formation, not an ancient cult title.

### Literature, occultism, and neopaganism

**[NEGATIVE FINDING]** No substantiated Avíreri-centered occult order, ceremonial-magical current, neopagan reconstructionist organization, nationalist extremist cult, commercial devotional cult, or continuous institutional worship was found. Searches combining the three names with “occult,” “neopagan,” “syncretism,” “sacrifice,” “suppression,” and related terms predominantly returned ethnography and conservation material.

Modern websites that place Avíreri in generalized “pantheons” tend to convert contingent oral variants into fixed attributes and divine equivalences. Claims that he is the Moon, has a canonical symbol set, or belongs to a formal universal pantheon are not supported by Weiss, who clearly distinguishes Avíreri from the male Moon god Kashíri.

## 9. Historiography and contested questions

### Was the cycle derived from Saint Christopher?

**Santos-Granero’s case [SYNCRETISTIC HYPOTHESIS]:**

- Christopher and Ayots/Avíreri are giants with extraordinary power and alleged cannibal traits.
- Each carries a wondrous child.
- Each becomes associated with bearing the world.
- Christopher protects from earthquakes; imprisoned Ayots causes them.
- The Christ Child and Poporrona’/Kíri are powerful pseudo-children carried on shoulders.
- Both child figures vex or burden the carrier.
- Attempts to kill Christopher and Kíri/Poporrona’ fail until an impalement-like procedure.
- Both legends culminate in miraculous palm imagery.
- Asháninka *Kiristu* for “Cristo” could have been shortened to *Kíri*.
- Missionaries circulated Christopher images and presented him as protection against plague, flood, fire, tempest, and earthquake.
- Southern Arawak communities adopted crosses, medals, clothing elements, and imitations of Catholic ceremonial practice.

Santos-Granero therefore proposes “mimesis gone wrong”: the protector against plague became an inverted plague-bringer and sorcery model. The article dates initial appropriation to the colonial mission period and completion of the transformation to 1742–1847. [The structural comparison appears in the article’s central discussion.](https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/ec1a0175-8552-44f3-a358-bb24cb826716/content)

**Limits and counter-considerations [CONTESTED]:**

- There is no colonial text documenting an Indigenous narrator equating Avíreri with Christopher.
- No transitional narrative has been recovered.
- Giant transformers, culture heroes, animal transformations, and earth-supporters need not derive from Christian hagiography.
- The argument begins from visual and structural similarity; Santos-Granero openly recounts that the hypothesis occurred to him on seeing a Saint Christopher medal.
- *Kíri* from *Kiristu* is plausible but not demonstrated by a historical chain of forms.
- Weiss’s broad Avíreri cycle contains extensive material—season-making, topographic petrifications, river engineering, relations with Pachákama—not explained by Christopher.
- Yanesha *Poporrona’* has no Christopher/Christ-derived name.
- A Matsigenka neighboring analogue may indicate wider Indigenous circulation, although Renard-Casevitz considered its Avíreri-like component borrowed.

The most defensible conclusion is that Santos-Granero demonstrated a remarkable cluster of parallels and a plausible colonial channel, not a proven total derivation. A pre-existing transformer complex may have absorbed Christopher imagery; wholesale Christian origin, partial syncretism, and independent convergence cannot currently be decisively separated.

### Did the myth cause child-sorcerer killings?

**[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** Santos-Granero argues that the pseudo-child supplies the mythical referent for accusations because the relevant version occurs among the four Southern Arawak populations where such accusations were documented, while being absent among Piro and the contrasted Matsigenka. The child and accused children are both imagined as prematurely adult, antisocial, magically dangerous, cannibalistic, and capable of harming kin.

The correlation is strong but does not prove a one-way causal mechanism. Epidemics, adult mortality, warfare, colonization, displacement, local political struggles, and shamanic diagnosis also shaped accusations. The myth could legitimate an emergent practice, be remodeled by it, or do both.

### Is Avíreri evil?

- **Yanesha Ayots:** explicitly malevolent in Santos-Granero’s sources.
- **Santos-Granero’s comparative Avíreri:** “evil divinity” and cannibal.
- **Weiss’s Avíreri:** dangerous and destructive but also a foundational transformer responsible for useful cosmic, ecological, and social order.
- **Nomatsigenga Mábireri:** remembered as creator of animals; narrative episodes cast him as antagonist.
- **Modern civic Avíreri:** predominantly benevolent world-shaper and defender.

“Ambivalent transformer whose anger is socially and cosmically dangerous” preserves more of the total evidence than either “devil” or “benevolent creator.”

### Pachákama and Pachacamac

**[MODERN SCHOLARLY RECONSTRUCTION]** Weiss proposes derivation of the Asháninka name Pachákama from the coastal Andean god Pachacamac. He also reports that River Asháninka distinguish Pachákama from Avíreri, whereas related Matsigenka use the names interchangeably for an equivalent figure. Possible explanations include Asháninka division of an earlier composite deity, Matsigenka merger of two figures, or later Andean borrowing differently accommodated in each tradition. The available evidence does not choose securely among them.

### Source-critical conclusion

The securely documented figure emerges not from an ancient inscription or colonial chronicle but from twentieth-century Indigenous oral testimony preserved by linguists and anthropologists. That testimony is internally rich and geographically anchored: Avíreri is embedded in named rivers, cliffs, salt springs, plants, animal species, kin relations, and histories of intrusion. The record supports a widespread Southern Arawak transformer complex with sharply differentiated Yanesha, Asháninka/Ashéninka, and Nomatsigenga realizations.

It does not support a single canonical biography, a uniform evil-god classification, a formal temple cult, or claims of demonstrated pre-Columbian antiquity. Santos-Granero’s Saint Christopher theory remains the most ambitious explanation of the carried-child cycle and its relationship to child-sorcery violence. Its parallels and colonial mechanism are substantial, but the absence of a transitional source makes it a powerful reconstruction rather than a settled textual genealogy.
