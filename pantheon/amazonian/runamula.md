# The Runamula: Research Dossier

**Class:** In documented tradition, the Runamula is a nocturnal metamorphic monster or punished human soul—not a god or goddess and not securely a member of a Southern Arawak pantheon. It belongs principally to Quechua-Andean and mestizo Amazonian folklore and has subsequently entered the shared oral culture of some Indigenous Amazonian communities.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest presently located Amazonian record is a field-collected narrative from San Martín, Peru, dated **9 October 1946**, narrated by **Amalia Macedo, aged seventy-two**, and written down through Centro Escolar no. 192. It describes women who live with married men becoming fire-breathing mules ridden through town by the Devil. A second San Martín record from **June 1946**, collected under Francisco Izquierdo Ríos, treats relations with a married man or priest as the cause. These records survive in the regional folklore collection commonly catalogued as *Folklore de San Martín: Rioja–Tarapoto*. A Peru-wide scholarly treatment followed in **1952**, when Efraín Morote Best published *La mula, un personaje fabuloso del Perú*.

**Evidentiary conclusion:** The Runamula is securely documented as a twentieth-century name for a considerably older post-Conquest South American legend complex. Its mule, priest, Christian sin, Devil, and Tuesday/Friday motifs are necessarily colonial or postcolonial. No pre-Hispanic attestation has been found, and none is possible for the mule itself, an Old World hybrid introduced after the Spanish invasion. Claims that the Runamula is an ancient Southern Arawak deity are **modern misclassification**.

## Evidentiary labels

- **Secure early documentation:** A dated record or publication close to an identifiable oral source.
- **Folklore:** An oral belief or narrative documented without implying that its supernatural events occurred.
- **Regional variant:** A geographically or socially restricted version.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** A historical or symbolic interpretation proposed by researchers.
- **Syncretistic development:** A demonstrable or strongly supported combination of Christian, Iberian, Andean, and Amazonian materials.
- **Modern retelling/invention:** A literary, educational, tourist, artistic, or internet form not shown in the earlier record.
- **Unestablished:** A proposition for which the searched evidence supplies no adequate support.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

**[Folklore]** The Runamula is usually a living woman—or her soul—transformed at night into a mule because she has had a prohibited sexual relationship. The paradigmatic offense is intercourse or cohabitation with a Catholic priest, but documented variants substitute adultery, incest, sex between ritual kin such as compadres, or relations with a married man. She gallops through streets or forest paths, screams or neighs, throws sparks or flames, and is often ridden and beaten by the Devil or by her lover.

The tradition is better understood as a **legend complex** than as the biography of a single supernatural individual. Different women can become runamulas; the word therefore designates both a type of being and an episodic condition. Some accounts say the woman bodily transforms; others say her soul leaves her sleeping body and assumes mule form.

### Names

- **Runamula / Runa Mula / Runa-mula.** A hybrid Quechua–Spanish compound: Quechua *runa*, “person, human being, people,” plus Spanish *mula*, “mule.” The most defensible gloss is therefore “human-mule,” “person-mule,” or “people-mule.” A modern linguistic study of Amazonian Spanish likewise analyzes it as Quechua *runa* plus Spanish *mula*.
- **Nuna mula.** **[Regional phonological variant]** Recorded especially in the Callejón de Huaylas and other Quechua-speaking areas where *runa/nuna* alternation occurs.
- **Nina mula.** Quechua *nina*, “fire,” plus Spanish *mula*: “fire-mule.” This name foregrounds the flames, sparks, or infernal character.
- **Warmimula / warmi mula.** Quechua *warmi*, “woman,” plus *mula*: “woman-mule.” Néstor Taipe records it among Loreto/Napo nomenclature.
- **Mula, mula infernal, mula del Diablo.** Spanish descriptive forms.
- **Almamula / mula ánima / mulánima.** Cognate South American names, particularly prominent in northwestern Argentina. They belong to the comparative legend complex; they are not merely alternative spellings of the Peruvian form.
- **Mula sem cabeça** (“headless mule”), Portuguese Brazilian. A close analogue involving a priest’s lover, nocturnal galloping, and fire. Equation with Runamula is comparative rather than evidence that every version is historically identical.

The online gloss “*runa* means evil person” or “Devil” is **incorrect or at best a folk etymology**. Standard Quechua usage and the compound’s recorded analysis support “person/human,” not “evil.” One comparative article’s gloss “*runa* ‘diablo’” should not be repeated as settled etymology.

### Classification through time

- **[Secure early documentation, 1946]** A transformed woman, called a popular *leyenda* or *creencia*.
- **[Scholarly folklore, 1952]** One member of the Peruvian fabulous-mule complex.
- **[Amazonian ethnography, 2016–2017]** A *ser mitológico del monte* among Napo river communities, alongside—but not genealogically related to—the Chullachaki, Tunche, Maligno, Yakumama, sirens, and bufeos.
- **[Modern popular taxonomy]** Frequently called a demon, spirit, mythological being, cryptid, or “Amazonian deity.” Only the first three approximately reflect the narratives; “cryptid” and “deity” are modern genre classifications unsupported by the earlier sources.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The 1946 San Martín records

**[Secure early Amazonian documentation]** The record attributed to Amalia Macedo states that women who “make life with married men” rise while asleep, roll in street sand, become mules, and are mounted by the Devil. They travel through town throwing fire from the mouth while spurs and bridle sound *chilín, chilín, chilín*. Before four in the morning, the Devil dismounts; the mule rolls again and becomes a woman who wakes ill and unaware of the night’s events.

A second 1946 item says that cohabitation with a married man—or, in another case, with a priest—causes a woman and man to undergo the condition. The woman undresses, urinates, rolls in it, and becomes a spirited mule; the man mounts her. A cut or ink mark inflicted on the mule will appear on the woman the following morning. These texts are important because they already contain most of the later cycle: prohibited sex, transformation, rider, public nocturnal circuit, detection by bodily marks, and morning exhaustion. The explicitly priest-centered form existed beside a broader adultery version, rather than necessarily preceding it.

### Morote Best, 1952

**[Secure scholarly documentation]** Efraín Morote Best’s eighteen-page *La mula, un personaje fabuloso del Perú*, first published in the *Revista Universitaria* of Cuzco in 1952, surveyed traditions from numerous Peruvian departments. Contemporary review evidence says he treated **nuna mula, runa mula, and nina mula**, compared examples from elsewhere in the Americas, and traced the complex to the Spanish Conquest.

A later summary by Morote describes the being as a woman who, because of sacrilegious love, becomes a mule by night and permanently after death. Sometimes the transformed party is the priest himself. The mule may be ridden by the Devil, who spurs her udders and lashes her with a snake. This is the earliest located scholarly synthesis, but it is nationwide and mainly Andean—not proof of an ancient Amazonian cult.

### Later Amazonian corpus

- **Jaime Regan, *Hacia la tierra sin mal* (1983; later editions).** A major ethnographic synthesis of popular religion in Loreto and the Peruvian Amazon. It provides the appropriate setting for mestizo, Cocama, Lamista, and Napo-area narratives, but the Runamula should not be attributed to every Indigenous religion discussed in the volumes.
- **Daniel Cossíos, *Breve bestiario peruano* (2004).** Places the Runamula in a comparative Peruvian bestiary.
- **Juan Carlos Galeano, *Folktales of the Amazon* (2009).** Includes a collected “Runamula” narrative among forty-one Amazonian tales. Its corpus spans several countries and Indigenous and mestizo informants; inclusion demonstrates modern regional circulation, not common prehistoric ancestry.
- **Néstor Godofredo Taipe Campos, “Los seres mitológicos en la tradición oral de los pueblos ribereños del Napo” (fieldwork 2016; publication 2017).** The strongest modern ethnographic source located. Taipe’s teams visited 110 localities in the Loreto districts of Las Amazonas, Mazán, Napo, and Torres Causana, among mestizo, Kichwa, Yagua, Murui-Muinani, and Quechua populations. He classes the Runamula among forest beings and records contemporary names, representations, and oral explanations.
- **Modern educational collections.** Ministry, university, tourist, and school compilations reproduce a standardized form: a woman’s soul becomes a fire-breathing mule on Tuesday and Friday nights; the Devil rides and beats it; the woman awakens bruised; a healer may release her through herbal baths and ayahuasca sessions. These are evidence for late-twentieth- and twenty-first-century circulation, not for antiquity.

### Audit of the Southern Arawak corpus named in the brief

Targeted searches were made for the Runamula in connection with Gerald Weiss’s *Campa Cosmology* (1975), Michael F. Brown and Eduardo Fernández’s *War of Shadows* (1991), Fernando Santos-Granero’s *The Power of Love* (1991) and “Saint Christopher in the Amazon” (2002), Richard Chase Smith’s *Deliverance from Chaos for a Song* (1977), Peter Gow’s Piro/Yine works, Harold Shaver and Donald Dodd on the Nomatsiguenga, Renard-Casevitz on the Matsigenka, and the relevant mission histories.

**Finding:** No securely attributable Runamula episode, indigenous-language name, genealogy, ritual, or cult was located in those foundational Asháninka/Campa, Yanesha, Nomatsiguenga, Matsigenka, or Yine/Piro sources. Their omission matters. These works document rich and internally differentiated Southern Arawak cosmologies, but they do not establish the Runamula as one of their ancestral divine beings.

The most accurate formulation is therefore:

> **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Runamula traditions entered multiethnic Amazonian social worlds through Quechua, Spanish, mission, urban, and riverine circulation and could subsequently be known or retold by people who also belonged to Indigenous communities. That does not make the figure an original Southern Arawak god.

### Mission chronicles

José Amich’s mission history was completed about 1771 and first published in 1854; Bernardino Izaguirre’s fourteen-volume history appeared in 1922–1929 and incorporated earlier mission diaries and reports. These sources document the Franciscan penetration of the central selva, mission settlements, epidemics, conflict, and conversion. No verified Runamula passage was found in the searchable versions of Amich, Izaguirre, Biedma, Sala, Ordinaire, or the 1750 San Antonio materials.

Consequently, the frequently repeated statement that the legend “was born when the first missionaries arrived” is a **plausible historical inference**, not a quotation from a sixteenth-, seventeenth-, or eighteenth-century Amazonian document.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain

**[Folklore]** The Runamula personifies prohibited sexual relations made publicly visible. Her domain is not fertility, war, water, or forest guardianship in the manner of a deity. She patrols the boundary between concealed domestic conduct and communal knowledge: the secret affair becomes noise, flame, bruises, hoofprints, and gossip in the street.

The offenses vary:

- intercourse with or concubinage to a priest;
- adultery with a married man;
- female adultery generally;
- incest with a brother or other blood relative;
- relations with a compadre, cuñado, or ritual relative;
- in one Tikuna–Cocama-area testimony, a father improperly cutting the newborn’s umbilical cord, violating a ritual-kinship prohibition.

### Form

The sources preserve incompatible forms:

- an ordinary but unusually powerful white, black, or dark mule;
- a woman transformed completely into a mule;
- the woman’s soul as a mule while her human body sleeps;
- a mule with a woman’s head and breasts;
- a centaur-like woman whose torso joins a mule body;
- a male and female pair transformed together;
- occasionally the priest himself as the transformed mule.

No version should be treated as anatomically canonical.

### Recognitional signs

**[Folklore and regional variants]**

- nocturnal galloping through streets, beaches, or forest paths;
- loud neighing and the metallic sound of bridle, bits, spurs, or horseshoes;
- flames or sparks from mouth and nostrils;
- sparks from hooves or hindquarters;
- a black-clad rider, dwarf-like rider, lover, priest, or Devil;
- Tuesday and Friday nights, especially at midnight;
- full moon in many modern retellings;
- return before dawn, commonly around four;
- bruises, cuts, exhaustion, torn clothing, or blood on the woman the next morning;
- bodily correspondence: a cut, stain, rope mark, or blow applied to the mule appears on the suspected woman.

Tuesday and Friday are recurrently described as ominous nights in Christianized Andean and Amazonian folklore. The full-moon rule is prominent in modern summaries but is not present in every early account.

### Earliest and important images

No pre-Columbian or colonial image securely identifiable as the Runamula is known.

Taipe documents two significant modern representations:

1. **Juancho Playa, Napo district, Loreto:** a school mural showing a black mule entering the river, ridden by a nearly naked long-haired male; the mule throws fire from its mouth. Recorded during 2016 fieldwork.
2. **Chullachaqui thematic park, Tarapoto:** a copper-toned woman-mule statue, upright on hind legs, with exposed human torso and raised mule forelegs.

Tourist drawings, coloring sheets, book illustrations, online digital art, and monumental sculptures now commonly use the woman–equine hybrid. That form is visually efficient but should not be back-projected onto the 1946 texts, which describe a complete mule.

### Powers and limitations

The Runamula transforms, travels at supernatural speed, breathes fire, and may pass while the woman remains apparently asleep. It can nevertheless be intercepted, struck, marked, roped, or followed. Dawn, identification of the woman, separation from the forbidden lover, and healing by a specialist can terminate or expose the condition in some versions.

No securely attested sacred color, number, celestial body, plant, weapon, temple emblem, priesthood, or festival belongs to the figure. Black, fire-red, moonlight, Tuesday, and Friday are narrative motifs, not elements of an organized cult.

## 4. Genealogy and relations with other beings

### Genealogy

**Absence of evidence is decisive:** the Runamula has no documented divine parents, siblings, consort, offspring, heavenly office, or position in a pantheon. She begins as a human being and becomes monstrous through an offense or curse.

One modern children’s cultural project calls **Runachaqui/Mashboa** the child of the Chullachaqui and Runamula. This is a **modern creative invention**, documented online in 2014, not an older genealogical tradition.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine or supernatural being

- **The Devil / Diablo:** rides, spurs, whips, sexually torments, or otherwise punishes the transformed woman; 1946 San Martín documentation and Morote Best’s 1952 synthesis. **[Secure early folklore; Christian syncretistic figure]**
- **God / Dios:** in modern summaries, imposes or permits the transformation as punishment for adultery, incest, or sacrilege. The agent is absent or less explicit in some early records. **[Later canonicalized popular explanation]**
- **Runachaqui/Mashboa:** said in a 2014 educational-cultural project to be the child of Runamula and Chullachaqui. **[Modern invention]**
- **Chullachaqui:** alleged parent of Runachaqui with Runamula; no independent early tale of a meeting, mating, combat, or alliance was located. **[Modern invention]**
- **Awara:** in a modern Tikuna testimony, a great monkey punishes violators of kinship rules; it appears as an alternative danger rather than interacting with the Runamula. **[Regional comparative association; no direct interaction]**

No documented interaction was found with the Yacuruna, Yacumama, Sachamama, Bufeo Colorado, Tunche, Ayaymama, Pachamama, Arawak creator beings, Christian saints, or Juan Santos Atahualpa. Their appearance beside Runamula in bestiaries and tourism lists is not a mythic encounter.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. The priest’s lover

**[Folklore; colonial/postcolonial core]** A woman secretly cohabits or has intercourse with a priest. At night she becomes a mule. The Devil, the priest, or a mysterious rider mounts and beats her while she races through town. In San Martín variants she may be a beautiful white mule ridden by a small figure with a whip. The community follows or traps her and identifies the woman through wounds or signs the next day.

A localized Habana version names **Águeda** and **Father Villacorta**, saying villagers cried that Águeda was passing with the priest on her back. These names create local verisimilitude but cannot be treated as verified biography.

The legend condemns the woman, yet the priest’s conspicuous presence also encodes a critique of clerical concubinage and failed celibacy. In some Andean versions the priest transforms too, and calling parishioners his spiritual “children” makes sex with them symbolically incestuous. This interpretation is supported by Néstor Cavero’s analysis of the relation between *ninamula/runamula* and *qarqacha* traditions.

### B. The adulteress and the Devil

**[Secure early Amazonian folklore, 1946]** A woman involved with a married man rises in a trance, rolls in sand, becomes a mule, and is ridden by the Devil. Fire issues from her mouth; infernal tack announces her passage. She returns before dawn and awakens ill but ignorant.

This version is not secondary merely because it lacks a priest. It is one of the earliest recorded Amazonian forms. Modern summaries frequently combine it with the priest narrative, producing the generalized proposition that all women in “forbidden love” risk transformation.

### C. The sleeping body and wandering soul

**[Later canonical elaboration]** The woman’s body remains asleep while her soul becomes the mule. Morning bruises reveal what happened to the wandering double. This version avoids the problem of an absent body and resembles wider Andean and Amazonian ideas about detachable souls, dreaming, shamanic travel, and nocturnal animal doubles. Direct derivation from a particular pre-Hispanic Arawak doctrine, however, is unestablished.

### D. Exposure and cure

**[Regional variant]** Residents stretch a horsehair rope across the path, strike the mule, mark it with ink, cut it, or follow it home. The same injury appears on a woman the next day. Some Loreto versions send the exposed woman to a curandero, who treats her with plant baths and ayahuasca sessions. This treatment is part of modern Amazonian versions; it is absent from the located 1946 account and must not be treated as the legend’s universal ending.

### E. Incest and ritual kinship

**[Andean–Amazonian regional variant]** The prohibited partner may be a brother, father, compadre, godparent, cuñado, or priest. Cavero relates the mule complex to the *qarqacha*, in which incestuous persons become anomalous animals. The mule’s biological hybridity—a sterile product of parents from different species—provides an apt metaphor for a socially forbidden union. That is a scholarly interpretation, not an explanation voiced in every tale.

### F. Loreto and Ucayali forms

**Loreto:** Napo-area evidence documents *runamula*, *warmimula*, hybrid imagery, a male rider, fire, riverbank settings, and transmission among ethnically mixed river communities. Loreto educational versions emphasize the sleeping soul, Tuesday/Friday appearances, bruising, and curandero treatment.

**Ucayali:** Collected and republished narratives emphasize an identifiable local woman, adultery with a priest, pursuit through urban streets, and neighbors recognizing the human offender. One Requena/Ucayali oral text names a woman called **Sajina Mapcha** and a priest called **David**; such names belong to the teller’s localized narrative and are not independently verified persons.

The difference is not absolute: both regions share priest, adultery, rider, nocturnal pursuit, and bodily-mark motifs. “Loreto version” and “Ucayali version” describe clusters, not sealed canons.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

There is **no documented Runamula cult** in the institutional sense:

- no temple or shrine;
- no priesthood;
- no sacrificial calendar;
- no votive inscriptions;
- no theophoric personal names;
- no oracle;
- no human or animal sacrifice;
- no established worship;
- no historical temple prostitution;
- no evidence that people petitioned the Runamula as a deity.

The practices surrounding her are defensive or diagnostic: staying indoors, following the animal, stretching a rope, marking it, checking suspected women for injuries, praying, or seeking a curandero. These are responses to a feared transformation, not cult acts offered to the being.

Modern murals, statues, festivals, dances, tourist viewpoints, books, and school projects commemorate a regional legend. They constitute heritage display and commercial folklore rather than proof of worship.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1532

**[Unestablished]** No Runamula exists in the surviving pre-Hispanic record. Neither the Spanish word *mula* nor the animal itself can belong to a pre-Conquest Peruvian narrative in its present form. Pre-Hispanic traditions of human–animal metamorphosis may have provided a receptive framework, but no continuous textual chain has been demonstrated.

### Sixteenth to eighteenth centuries: conditions of formation

**[Syncretistic reconstruction]** Spanish colonization introduced mules, Catholic sexual categories, clerical celibacy, the Devil as punitive rider, mission settlements, and intensive contact among Quechua speakers, Amazonian peoples, Europeans, Africans, and mestizo river populations. The legend could only have assumed its recognizable form after these changes.

Franciscan and Jesuit missions are historically documented across the central and northern Peruvian selva. Santa Rosa de Ocopa was founded in 1725 as a base for evangelization. Mission chronicles document conversion, resettlement, Indigenous resistance, violence, epidemics, and clerical authority. They do not yet provide a verified Runamula text.

The phrase “the colonial priest as the sin” is therefore interpretively useful: the priest is not simply the moral judge but often the offender, rider, or fellow transformed person. Nevertheless, stories also shift responsibility disproportionately onto the woman.

### Nineteenth century

No securely dated nineteenth-century Amazonian Runamula text was located. Related fire-mule, priest’s concubine, and infernal-mule traditions circulated widely in the Andes, Brazil, Argentina, Ecuador, and Colombia. Direct borrowing routes remain disputed: Iberian supernatural-horse material, colonial Catholic preaching, Andean transformation legends, and interregional oral circulation may all have contributed.

### 1946–1952: entry into the documentary record

The San Martín school collections establish the Amazonian legend by 1946. Morote Best’s 1952 comparative monograph establishes that *runa mula*, *nuna mula*, and *nina mula* were already geographically widespread in Peru.

### 1970s–1990s: regionalization and ethnographic context

Amazonian literary and ethnographic production increasingly represented the Runamula alongside the Chullachaqui, Tunche, Yacuruna, Sachamama, and Bufeo. Juaneco y su Combo’s early-1970s cumbia **“Me robaron mi runa mula,”** composed by Noé Fachín, transformed the frightening being into an emblem of Amazonian popular culture.

The Southern Arawak ethnographies of Weiss, Smith, Santos-Granero, Brown and Fernández, Gow, Shaver and Dodd, Renard-Casevitz, and others documented cosmologies, mission history, violence, sorcery, and social change. Their failure to establish a Runamula deity is significant evidence against the “Southern Arawak pantheon” label.

### The internal-war years

**[Documented historical context; no demonstrated Runamula episode]** Sendero Luminoso, military counterinsurgency, Asháninka self-defense, displacement, enslavement, and mass death profoundly transformed the central selva in the 1980s and 1990s. *War of Shadows* and later historical literature document fear, sorcery accusations, and political violence in Indigenous communities. No credible evidence located ties the Runamula specifically to Sendero ideology, atrocities, militant symbolism, or counterinsurgent ritual. The conflict belongs to the regional history but should not be inserted into this legend without evidence.

### 2000s–present

The Runamula now circulates in schoolbooks, tourist promotion, folklore websites, academic theses, murals, public sculpture, short film, music reissues, illustrated bestiaries, and contemporary fiction. Its current visibility may be greater than its archival antiquity because modern media standardize and redistribute local variants.

There is no documented continuous or revived Runamula worship. Claims of an occult or neopagan cult were not found.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Music

Juaneco y su Combo recorded **“Me robaron mi runa mula”** in the early 1970s; it appeared among the group’s early singles and on its first-LP repertoire. The song helped make the name a marker of Pucallpa/Amazonian popular identity. Later international “chicha” reissues expanded its audience.

### Film

Sebastián Palominos’s Chilean short film ** *Runa Mula* (2013, 15 minutes)** appeared in the 2014 Poitiers Film Festival program. It is a modern fictional appropriation, not ethnographic testimony.

### Literature

Contemporary Amazonian writers repeatedly rework the legend. A San Martín anthology titled *Runamula*, arising from the regional “Amazonía Ancestral” story competition, gathers multiple modern stories. A more recent edition advertises eleven contemporary reinterpretations. Their value lies in showing active literary reinvention, not in preserving a single oral original.

### Public art and tourism

The Tarapoto-area statue, Napo school mural, coloring books, tourist inventories, and a Runamula-themed viewpoint in Lamas convert the being into visual heritage. The woman–mule hybrid with exposed breasts has become especially common, although it can intensify the sexualization of Amazonian women beyond the early complete-mule accounts.

### Gender critique

Modern critics read the legend as a mechanism for public surveillance and punishment of women’s sexuality. That interpretation fits narratives in which neighbors attack the animal, inspect women for injuries, and expose a supposed adulteress. Yet the corpus is internally ambivalent: it may also ridicule priests who violate celibacy and reveal relationships that official religious discourse conceals.

Claims that the Runamula directly caused a general stereotype of the sexually available Amazonian woman require more demonstration than popular commentary usually supplies. The legend participates in that representational field, but it is not its sole origin.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated searches for Runamula combined with *ocultismo*, *esoterismo*, *neopaganismo*, sacrifice, temple, and worship produced no credible historical occult order, grimoire, ceremonial invocation, reconstructionist community, or New Age lineage centered on the being. Generic “archetypal” internet writing is **modern psychological reinterpretation**, not evidence of an esoteric tradition.

### Political appropriation

No nationalist, fascist, extremist, Senderista, or other organized political appropriation was found. Regional branding and tourism are documented; organized ideological cult is not.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is it Amazonian, Andean, or European?

The best evidence supports a layered answer.

1. The name is Quechua–Spanish.
2. The mule, priestly celibacy, Devil, and sacrilege require colonial conditions.
3. The legend was widely documented in Andean Peru, not merely the Amazon.
4. By 1946 it was firmly localized in San Martín.
5. Later Loreto and Ucayali communities adapted it to river-town life, ayahuasca healing, local priests, and Amazonian visual styles.
6. Analogues in Brazil, Argentina, Ecuador, and Colombia demonstrate a continental colonial legend family, but do not by themselves establish a single point of origin.

Accordingly, “Amazonian myth” is correct as a description of living regional tradition; “pre-Hispanic Amazonian deity” is not.

### Is the woman or priest punished?

The corpus does not give one answer. In many versions the woman bears the mule body and wounds while the priest disappears from the punishment. In others he is the rider, transforms himself, or is openly named and mocked. The asymmetry is historical evidence about gender and ecclesiastical power, not a discrepancy to harmonize.

### Body transformation or soul travel?

Both are documented. The 1946 San Martín record describes bodily transformation after rolling in sand or urine. Later Loreto texts often describe the soul leaving an unconscious body. No textual warrant exists for selecting one as the “true” canonical version.

### Incest or adultery?

Both. Morote Best and Cavero place the mule beside *qarqacha* traditions about incest. San Martín evidence includes adultery and relations with a priest. Modern web summaries frequently collapse all prohibited unions into one list.

### The state of the archive

The tradition’s evidence is unusually fragile:

- oral accounts were often recorded decades after the teller first heard them;
- early school collections preserve valuable testimony but inconsistent spelling and little performance context;
- folklore compilations frequently omit precise informants or collection dates;
- tourist and educational retellings copy one another;
- internet texts routinely present recent composite descriptions as timeless ancient myth;
- repository metadata sometimes date digitization rather than original publication.

The most important chronological anchors are therefore the dated 1946 San Martín field sheets, Morote Best’s 1952 publication, Regan’s 1983 synthesis, Galeano’s collected corpus, and Taipe’s 2016 fieldwork/2017 article.

### Discredited or unsupported propositions

- **“Runamula is an ancient Southern Arawak goddess.”** Unsupported and contradicted by its colonial vocabulary and absent cult.
- **“*Runa* means Devil or evil person.”** Linguistically defective.
- **“The first Spanish missionary immediately created the legend.”** Plausible generalization, not a documented event.
- **“All Amazonian peoples share one Runamula belief.”** False flattening of heterogeneous communities.
- **“The fire-breathing hybrid woman is the original form.”** Unproved; early records also describe a complete mule.
- **“The Runamula had temples, sacrifices, priestesses, or a festival.”** No evidence.
- **“Ayahuasca cure is universal and ancient.”** A regional late-recorded variant.
- **“She is the mother of Runachaqui in traditional cosmology.”** Modern creative genealogy.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before 1532:** No attestation. The recognizable figure cannot yet exist under its hybrid Quechua–Spanish mule name.
- **Sixteenth century:** Spanish conquest introduces equids and Catholic demonological and sexual frameworks into Peru. **[Historical precondition, not attestation]**
- **Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries:** Jesuit and Franciscan missions expand into Maynas and the central selva. **[Context]**
- **1725:** Santa Rosa de Ocopa is founded as a Franciscan missionary center.
- **c. 1771:** José Amich completes his mission history; no verified Runamula passage located.
- **1854:** Amich’s historical compilation receives its first publication.
- **1880s–1920s:** Ordinaire, Sala, Navarro, Izaguirre, and other mission writers publish accounts of eastern Peru; no secure Runamula attestation was located in the searched materials.
- **June 1946:** San Martín folklore collection records prohibited relations with a married man or priest causing mule transformation.
- **9 October 1946:** Amalia Macedo’s fire-breathing, Devil-ridden Runamula narrative is written down in San Martín.
- **1952:** Efraín Morote Best publishes *La mula, un personaje fabuloso del Perú*, the earliest located specialist study.
- **1953:** Morote’s later comparative summaries classify *mula/nina mula/nuna mula/runa mula* among Peru’s transformed supernatural beings. Pérez Marcio’s *Los hijos de la selva* belongs to the same period of literary collection, but no securely verified Runamula passage was recovered here.
- **1972, approximately:** Juaneco y su Combo records “Me robaron mi runa mula,” making the figure part of Amazonian cumbia’s public vocabulary.
- **1983:** Jaime Regan publishes *Hacia la tierra sin mal*, a foundational account of popular religion in the Peruvian Amazon.
- **1991:** Santos-Granero’s *The Power of Love*, Gow’s *Of Mixed Blood*, and Brown and Fernández’s *War of Shadows* deepen the history of central-selva and Arawak worlds without establishing a Runamula deity.
- **2004:** Daniel Cossíos includes Runamula in *Breve bestiario peruano*.
- **2009:** Juan Carlos Galeano includes “Runamula” in *Folktales of the Amazon*.
- **2013:** Sebastián Palominos makes the short film *Runa Mula*.
- **2014:** The modern Runachaqui project describes its namesake as the offspring of Runamula and Chullachaqui.
- **2016:** Taipe’s teams collect oral and visual evidence in 110 Napo river settlements.
- **2017:** Taipe publishes the principal modern ethnographic study of Napo mythological beings.
- **2021–2024:** Public sculpture, tourism projects, illustrated books, academic theses, and story anthologies continue converting the figure into regional heritage.
- **2026:** The Runamula remains an active folkloric and artistic figure, but no organized cult or demonstrated Southern Arawak divine status is documented.
