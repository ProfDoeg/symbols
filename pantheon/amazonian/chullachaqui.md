# The Chullachaqui: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A forest spirit, “owner” or guardian of wild animals and trees, trickster, shapeshifter, and dangerous nonhuman person in Peruvian Amazonian folklore. Christianized tellings often classify it as a *demonio*, *diablo del monte*, or *duende*. It is not securely documented as a supreme god or as a member of a formally organized “pantheon.”
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The Quechua-derived name **Chullachaqui** is securely documented in twentieth-century Peruvian Amazonian folklore. Pérez Marcio’s *Los hijos de la selva* (1953) is an early published source named in the scholarly bibliography, but its relevant text could not be directly inspected here. The earliest directly verifiable substantial description located is Francisco Izquierdo Ríos’s account in the first issue of *Revista Peruana de Cultura* (1963), which calls it the “Demonio Chullachaqui,” explains its unequal feet, shapeshifting, abduction of children, and misleading of travelers. Cristóbal de Acuña’s 1641 publication concerning his 1639 Amazon voyage mentions people or beings with reversed feet, but not the name *Chullachaqui*; this is a possible motif antecedent, not a secure attestation of the named being.

## Evidentiary key

- **[Early attested]** Earliest securely located records of the named figure.
- **[Folklore]** Oral testimony or customary belief recorded by collectors or ethnographers.
- **[Regional variant]** A version limited to, or especially associated with, a locality or community.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A historically contingent equation with another religious being.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An interpretation proposed by an ethnographer, historian, or comparativist.
- **[Modern literary elaboration]** A printed, cinematic, artistic, or educational reworking not demonstrably inherited in all its details.
- **[Modern invention/unverified]** A recent claim for which no adequate earlier source was located.
- **[Negative finding]** A requested feature for which the examined record supplies no evidence.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

The Chullachaqui belongs most securely to the oral culture of Quechua-speaking and Spanish-speaking *ribereños*—riverine inhabitants—of the Peruvian *selva*, particularly Loreto, San Martín, Ucayali, and Madre de Dios. It is also narrated in Indigenous communities, including Asháninka and Matsigenka settings, but the widespread Spanish/Quechua name must not be assumed to designate an unchanged pan-Amazonian deity. Southern Arawak peoples possess their own named forest masters, demons, owners of game, and predatory spirits; the Chullachaqui can function as a translation or regional umbrella term for analogous beings without being historically identical to all of them.

Its defining narrative paradox is that it is both the forest’s custodian and an aggressor against human beings. It protects animals and trees, grants or withholds game, and punishes wasteful hunters; it also imitates a trusted relative, friend, child, or animal and leads a person away from the human path. Its concealed asymmetry—one foot smaller, animal-like, rounded, reversed, or otherwise unlike the other—betrays the imitation.

### Names and forms

- **Chullachaqui / Chullachaki / Chulla chaki.** From Quechua *ch’ulla* or *chulla*, “unpaired, unequal, odd, singular,” and *chaki*, “foot” or “leg”: approximately “unequal-foot,” “odd-foot,” or “the one with mismatched feet.” **[Folklore; linguistically transparent]**
- Spanish spelling **Chullachaqui** substitutes *-qui* for Quechua orthographic *-ki*. Spellings such as *Chullachaki*, *Chulla Chaqui*, and *Chullachaque* appear in modern publication and transcription.
- **Shapshico / Shapshingo / Shapingo.** Regan’s collected evidence treats *Shapshico* as another name for the *sacharuna* or Chullachaqui. Modern Peruvian cultural materials also list *Shapingo*. The equivalence is regional rather than a demonstrated ancient identity. **[Regional variant]**
- **Sacharuna**, Quechua *sacha* (“forest, wild”) + *runa* (“person”): “forest person.” This is a broader category and cannot always be reduced to Chullachaqui. Regan reports that across much of the *selva* the *sacharuna* is commonly called Chullachaqui or Shapshico. **[Learned/ethnographic systematization based on folklore]**
- **Supay.** Highland migrants in Madre de Dios reportedly equated the Chullachaqui with *Supay* or *To*, a Quechua/Andean demon or dangerous spirit already known to them. This is a migrant and Christian-colonial syncretism, not evidence that Chullachaqui was originally an Inca god. **[Syncretistic identification; recorded by Regan, 1983]**
- **Diablo del bosque / demonio del monte / duende.** Spanish Christian folk classifications. Izquierdo Ríos uses “Demonio Chullachaqui” and “Diablo del Bosque” in 1963. These terms reflect translation and demonization; they do not establish identity with the Christian Satan. **[Early attested Christianized classification]**
- **Dueño del monte / dueño de los animales / Madre del monte.** Functional titles: master or owner of forest spaces, animals, and sometimes trees. In Amazonian Spanish, *madre* can mean a spirit-owner or animating master and need not denote female sex. **[Folklore]**
- **“Dios ecológico del bosque.”** A striking modern educational title in *Narraciones de la Amazonía*. It summarizes the conservationist interpretation but is not an old cult title. **[Modern pedagogical elaboration]**

No Indigenous script or precolonial inscription records the name. The surviving name is transmitted in Latin-script Spanish and Quechua orthographies.

### Number and gender

Most Spanish accounts use masculine grammar and imagine a male dwarf, old man, hunter, or disguised male relative. Some sources speak collectively of *chullachaquis*. There is no securely attested genealogy establishing that all appearances are one immortal individual. Treating “the Chullachaqui” as a single god is therefore less faithful to the sources than treating it as a named kind of forest person that stories often individualize.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Possible antecedent: Acuña, 1639–1641

Cristóbal de Acuña traveled the Amazon in 1639 and published *Nuevo descubrimiento del gran río de las Amazonas* in 1641. Regan cites Acuña for a report of beings or people with their feet turned backward. **[Early comparative motif]** Acuña does not, on the evidence located, use *Chullachaqui* or establish the later Peruvian complex of forest ownership, familiar-person mimicry, and hunting contracts. The report therefore cannot responsibly be called the first Chullachaqui attestation.

Comparable reversed-foot beings include the Tupí/Brazilian Curupira. Similarity establishes a regional motif complex; it does not prove that Curupira and Chullachaqui are names for one original deity.

### Franciscan and mission chronicles

The requested Franciscan corpus—Biedma, Amich, the 1750 San Antonio material, Sala, Ordinaire, Izaguirre, and Navarro—documents conversion, Indigenous resistance, mission geography, and colonial descriptions of the montaña. No securely verified occurrence of the name *Chullachaqui* was located in the searchable material examined for this dossier. **[Negative finding]**

That absence matters. It prevents the modern figure from being projected without qualification into the seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Southern Arawak record. Missionaries frequently translated Indigenous beings as devils, but the generic word *demonio* is not itself evidence for Chullachaqui.

### Twentieth-century printed record

1. **Pérez Marcio, *Los hijos de la selva* (1953).** Frequently identified as an early literary-folkloric source. A reliable full-text copy of the pertinent passage was not available for direct verification. **[Bibliographically attested; exact content unverified here]**

2. **Francisco Izquierdo Ríos, “Folklore amazónico,” *Revista Peruana de Cultura* 1 (1963), approximately pp. 154–56.** This is the earliest substantial, directly inspectable named description found. It says:
   - the name means “one foot” or, by Amazonian extension, unequal feet;
   - the left foot may resemble a child’s foot, hairy ball, feline paw, or tree root;
   - it can become a human, animal, tree, flower, stream, or dog;
   - disguised as a relative or friend, it leads victims into the forest;
   - it leaves adults trapped or tied and children in high trees;
   - its deformed foot remains the telltale flaw in an otherwise convincing disguise.  
   **[Early attested literary recording of folklore]**

3. **Gerald Weiss, *Campa Cosmology* (1975).** A major account of Asháninka/Campa cosmology, essential for reconstructing Southern Arawak categories. It should not be mined as proof that every Asháninka forest being is “really” Chullachaqui. **[Comparative ethnographic corpus]**

4. **Richard Chase Smith, *Deliverance from Chaos for a Song* (1977).** Documents Yanesha cosmology and ritual performance. Again, it supplies regional cosmological context rather than proof of an ancient Chullachaqui cult. **[Comparative ethnographic corpus]**

5. **Jaime Regan, *Hacia la tierra sin mal* (1983; later editions).** The richest accessible collection for the Chullachaqui. Regan prints reports from Cocama, Quechua, mestizo, and other informants in Nauta, Iquitos, Lamas, Madre de Dios, Tocache, and surrounding regions. His testimony numbers R204–R215 describe unequal feet, imitation, animal forms, abducted children, hunting assistance, tobacco protection, ayahuasca diagnosis, tree ownership, and combat with the spirit. **[Folklore recorded ethnographically]**

6. **Shaver and Dodd, work on Nomatsiguenga religion and narrative (1990); Brown and Fernández, *War of Shadows* (1991); Renard-Casevitz and Rojas Zolezzi on Matsigenka/Asháninka worlds.** These works document named Indigenous spirit systems and histories of violence. They are indispensable controls against treating “Amazonian mythology” as a homogeneous pantheon. **[Comparative corpus; identity with Chullachaqui must be demonstrated case by case]**

7. **Juan Carlos Galeano, *Folktales of the Amazon* (2009).** Includes “Chullachaki: Owner of Trees and Animals” and “The Flute of the Chullachaki.” Galeano’s English literary retellings preserve oral materials but are mediated compositions, not verbatim ancient texts. **[Modern literary folklore collection]**

8. **Educational and community collections.** Modern Asháninka story publications, Peruvian school anthologies, and *Narraciones de la Amazonía* continue the cycle. Their value lies in demonstrating active transmission; their polished conservationist language may reflect contemporary educational framing.

### Oral transmission and manuscript problem

There is no canonical scripture, inscriptional corpus, priestly recension, or fixed “original myth.” The principal evidence is orally transmitted testimony written down in the twentieth and twenty-first centuries. A story’s publication date is consequently only the latest secure date for that version, not its date of origin.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

The Chullachaqui governs the boundary between cultivated, socially ordered space and *monte*, the forest beyond ordinary human mastery.

Its principal functions are:

- owner and protector of game animals;
- guardian of trees and forest resources;
- enforcer of hunting restraint;
- source of hunting luck;
- maker or owner of uncanny forest gardens;
- misleading trickster who disrupts paths and perception;
- abductor, especially of unattended or unbaptized children in Christianized versions;
- helper or adversary of hunters;
- spirit helper in some curanderismo traditions.

Regan calls it “symbol of the wild” and “guardian of ecological equilibrium,” while noting a local opinion that its stories proliferated during the rubber boom. The ecological formulation is an ethnographic interpretation of the narratives; it should not be mistaken for an ancient doctrinal title.

### Unequal feet

The one stable identifying sign amid extensive variation is asymmetry:

- one ordinary foot and one smaller foot;
- one human foot and one deer, goat, dog, peccary, tortoise, feline, or other animal foot;
- one rounded stump without toes;
- a heel pointing forward;
- one foot reversed;
- in some accounts, only one foot.

Loreto testimony recorded by Regan includes a malformed left foot “like a closed fist.” A Quechua informant from Lamas gives a deer foot. Modern educational retellings frequently standardize the feature as one human foot and one goat hoof, probably under the influence of European devil and faun imagery.

### Bodily appearance

When not disguised, it may be:

- a short dark man or dwarf;
- an aged, wrinkled man;
- a child-sized being;
- a hunched figure in dirty clothes and a broad hat;
- a forest hunter;
- a large or otherwise uncanny human;
- a being whose face is obscured.

Red eyes, pointed ears, a conspicuous nose, and goatlike anatomy are common in recent illustrations and tourist accounts but are not universal early attributes.

### Metamorphosis

Izquierdo Ríos says it may assume human, animal, vegetal, or even landscape forms. Regan’s informants mention relatives, friends, tapir, peccary, monkey, and other game. The disguise usually preserves the anomalous foot, allowing an alert person to expose it.

A characteristic sequence is:

1. the traveler hears a familiar voice or sees a familiar person;
2. the apparent friend invites the traveler to follow;
3. paths cease to make sense;
4. the guide disappears or reveals itself;
5. the victim is left lost, entangled, or mentally altered.

### Forest gardens

Chullachaqui’s *chacra* appears deceptively cultivated but may contain wild or poisonous analogues of food plants. Regan records Quechua testimony that expanses of *bijao* resembling cultivation are gardens of the *Supay*. Modern versions make the garden a parallel nonhuman household: forest beings possess their own crops, paths, animals, and sociality.

### Trees and substances

- **Chullachaqui-caspi.** Regan associates the spirit with a tree whose bark has psychoactive or visionary use and calls the Chullachaqui its *Madre*. Modern sources often identify a *Tovomita* species, but botanical identification varies and should not be universalized.
- **Lupuna, catahua, renaco.** Contemporary retellings especially associate the guardian with large or spiritually potent trees.
- **Tobacco.** Protective and diplomatic. Regan records hunters blowing tobacco smoke over themselves or placing tobacco at a tree buttress to obtain release from the spirit’s bewilderment.
- **Ayahuasca.** Used by a curandero in one collected narrative to locate a child believed taken by Chullachaqui. This makes the spirit an object encountered or diagnosed in shamanic vision, not automatically the object of a standardized ayahuasca cult.
- **Pucacuro ants.** In a Regan narrative, a defeated Chullachaqui applies biting ants to a hunter’s eyes as part of giving him exceptional sight and hunting success.

No stable sacred color, number, weekday, planet, constellation, or formal emblem is documented.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No early or broadly shared parentage, birth myth, consort, offspring, or sibling group is securely documented. **[Negative finding]**

A recent claim that the being is the offspring of a *duende* and a demon appears in popular summaries but lacks a located early source. **[Modern invention or unverified local elaboration]**

The most defensible “relation” is categorical: Chullachaqui belongs among Amazonian forest owners and dangerous nonhuman persons. That is a relational ontology, not a family tree.

### Documented divine-being interactions and equations

Every located named relation is listed separately:

- **Supay** — Highland migrants in Madre de Dios equate Chullachaqui with the Andean *Supay* or *To*. Source: testimonies summarized in Regan, *Hacia la tierra sin mal* (1983). **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Curupira** — Regan compares Chullachaqui with the Tupí/Brazilian small forest spirit whose feet point backward, citing Galvão and Wagley. No narrative meeting between them is recorded. **[Modern scholarly comparison; no interaction]**
- **The Christian Devil/Satan** — Spanish-speaking narrators call Chullachaqui a *diablo* or *demonio*, and Christian signs may repel it. Source: Izquierdo Ríos (1963); Regan (1983). This is demonological translation, not a recorded meeting between two independently acting beings. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **God/Christ** — Christian prayer, baptism, crosses, or the sign of the cross protect victims in some accounts, but no located narrative presents a direct face-to-face meeting between Christ and Chullachaqui. **[Christianized folklore; no direct interaction]**
- **Yacuruna** — Regan discusses Chullachaqui, tree Mothers, and Yacuruna together as spirits linked especially to masculine activities such as hunting, fishing, and curing. No specific shared episode or interaction was located. **[Co-classification only]**
- **Bufeo** — Regan contrasts the two: abduction by Chullachaqui is ordinarily not interpreted sexually, whereas encounters with dolphin beings often are. No joint episode was found. **[Analytical contrast only]**
- **Tree Mothers** — Chullachaqui is called the *Madre* of chullachaqui-caspi, and Regan groups it with tree-owner spirits. This is an ownership/identity relation, not a documented dialogue or combat. **[Folklore/ethnographic classification]**
- **Shapshico/Shapingo** — Alternative name or locally equated forest being rather than a separate character in a recorded encounter. **[Regional identification]**

No securely documented interaction with named Asháninka, Yanesha, Nomatsiguenga, Matsigenka, or Piro deities was established. Similar functions do not constitute interaction.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. The false relative

**[Core twentieth-century attested cycle]**

The Chullachaqui assumes the appearance and voice of a victim’s father, mother, sibling, spouse, friend, or companion. It beckons the victim away from a house, field, hunting trail, or camp. Only after traveling deep into the forest does the follower notice the unequal foot or discover that the guide has vanished.

Izquierdo Ríos’s 1963 version presents this as the being’s standard practice. Regan’s R204 has a Cocama informant from Nauta say that it appears as an acquaintance or animal. R206, from a Quechua informant at Lamas, says it steals unbaptized children and has a deer foot. Contemporary educational versions soften or moralize the ending: children are found crying rather than killed, and the story teaches vigilance.

There is no single canonical outcome. Victims may:

- wander until found;
- be abandoned in thorns;
- appear on a tree buttress or in a high canopy;
- return dazed;
- be kept and well fed;
- disappear permanently;
- become, in recent elaborations, another Chullachaqui.

The last motif is poorly documented in early sources and should be treated cautiously.

### B. The deceptive game animal

**[Folklore; hunting cycle]**

The spirit becomes a desirable deer, peccary, monkey, curassow, or other quarry. It remains just within sight, repeatedly inviting pursuit, until the hunter is far from the trail. It then disappears, leaving him lost.

The cycle expresses the risk of excessive desire: the hunter who will not relinquish an improbably available animal loses his orientation. Modern school retellings explicitly make this punishment for cruelty or overhunting. That ecological moral is consistent with the owner-of-game complex but may be stated more systematically in recent texts than in older testimony.

### C. The pact for hunting luck

**[Folklore recorded by Regan]**

Chullachaqui is not only a punisher. A disciplined and fearless hunter may form a relationship with it and obtain plentiful game.

In Regan’s R208, a hunter encounters an uncanny agouti-like animal, becomes unable to find the trail, and uses tobacco smoke. He later dreams that the being tested his courage and grants him access to animals. The experience combines waking disorientation, tobacco, dream revelation, and subsequent hunting success.

Another narrative describes combat between a rubber worker or forest laborer and Chullachaqui. When the human wins, the spirit accepts defeat, shares tobacco, applies *pucacuro* ants to his eyes, and gives him extraordinary vision and hunting capacity. The “pact” is thus not a written compact or Satanic bargain. It is an exchange involving courage, ordeal, secrecy, tobacco, dietary discipline, dream or visionary encounter, and restraint.

No evidence was found for human sacrifice as a condition of the pact.

### D. The stolen child and curandero

**[Loreto-area folklore recorded by Regan]**

A child named Carlitos fails to return after being sent for leaves. His family consults a curandero, who drinks ayahuasca and sees the Chullachaqui leading the boy over high forest terrain. The next day the healer sings, calls the child by name, and blows consecrated tobacco toward the forest. At dusk the family hears crying and finds the boy sitting on a large tree buttress.

This version combines Indigenous-Amazonian curing technologies with Christianized language about demons. The curandero, not an institutional priesthood, negotiates the boundary between settlement and forest.

### E. Chullachaqui’s gifts and the broken prohibition

**[Folklore]**

In one collected hunting story, Chullachaqui helps a hunter carry an enormous quantity of meat in a small basket and warns him not to set it down before reaching home. The hunter forgets, puts the load down to drink, and loses the extraordinary benefit. This is a common folklore structure: a nonhuman patron grants abundance under a precise condition; ordinary appetite or curiosity breaks the condition.

### F. The forest garden

**[Regional folklore]**

Travelers encounter a clearing resembling a human garden, but its crops are uncanny, poisonous, or transformations of wild plants. The site belongs to Chullachaqui or the *Supay*. The story represents the forest not as empty nature but as another subject’s cultivated estate. Entering it without permission is trespass.

### G. The flute

Galeano’s “The Flute of the Chullachaki” gives the being a musical manifestation. **[Modern literary presentation of oral folklore]** The flute is not sufficiently widespread in the earlier accessible corpus to be called a universal attribute.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No institutional cult

There is no archaeological or documentary evidence of:

- temples dedicated to Chullachaqui;
- a centralized priesthood;
- canonical hymns;
- festival calendars;
- idols authenticated as precolonial Chullachaqui images;
- royal patronage;
- theophoric personal names;
- a sacrificial institution.

Calling this a “cult” therefore risks imposing a temple-religion model on relations with forest persons.

### Actual practice

Documented or credibly reported practices include:

- asking permission before hunting, cutting trees, or entering a forest domain;
- observing dietary and behavioral discipline;
- avoiding excessive kills;
- offering or using tobacco;
- blowing tobacco smoke for protection;
- placing tobacco against a tree;
- prayer and the sign of the cross in Christianized communities;
- using ayahuasca to diagnose an abduction;
- singing and calling a missing person’s name;
- interpreting dreams as communication from the forest owner;
- maintaining secrecy concerning gifts or hunting power.

These are episodic practices embedded in hunting and curing, not evidence of daily congregational worship.

### Sacrifice and violence

No reliable source examined documents human sacrifice, temple prostitution, ritual killing, or an organized blood cult for Chullachaqui. **[Negative finding]** Hunters kill animals within the spirit’s domain, but the record characteristically presents Chullachaqui as limiting rather than demanding indiscriminate killing.

Stories themselves contain violence: abduction, abandonment, entanglement, blows, wrestling, dangerous initiation, and possible death through becoming lost. Those narrative dangers must not be converted into claims of historical sacrificial practice.

### Material and visual record

No ancient or securely precolonial image has been identified. Modern iconography appears in:

- book and schoolbook illustrations;
- paintings and Amazonian popular art;
- tourist sculpture and crafts;
- ceramics, including contemporary Kukama-Lukamiria work;
- comics;
- cinema.

A Bicentennial educational exhibition juxtaposes David Hewson’s 2009 image with the figures produced by the L.U.C.U.M.A. comics workshop in 2007. These demonstrate modern plural iconography, not the recovery of an ancient canonical portrait.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before the named record

Backward-footed forest beings and owners of animals are widely distributed in Amazonia. Acuña’s seventeenth-century reversed-foot report and the Brazilian Curupira establish antiquity for parts of the motif complex. They do not prove that a being called Chullachaqui existed unchanged before conquest.

### Colonial period

Spanish and missionary expansion reclassified many Indigenous nonhuman persons as *demonios*. At the same time, Quechua served as a language of colonial administration, missionization, trade, and migration in parts of Amazonia. A Quechua descriptive name could therefore have spread over locally distinct beings.

The Franciscan destruction or demonization of Indigenous ritual worlds forms necessary historical context, but no located order specifically suppressing “the cult of Chullachaqui” exists. **[Negative finding]**

### Nineteenth century and rubber boom

Regan reports the local opinion that Chullachaqui stories multiplied during late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century rubber exploitation. He interprets the spirit as representing what lies beyond human society and suggests that stories of its attacks could indirectly express the frustration of exploited laborers.

This is plausible social interpretation, not proof that rubber workers invented the being. The forest trickster misdirects laborers rather than the patrons responsible for exploitation; for Regan, that displacement reflects the workers’ marginality and limited power.

### Mid-twentieth-century consolidation

By 1953–1963 the named Chullachaqui was established in published Peruvian regional folklore. The recurring complex already included unequal feet, metamorphosis, the deceptive relative, kidnapped children, and “devil of the forest” terminology.

### Late twentieth-century ethnographic expansion

Regan’s work demonstrates broad distribution among ribereño, Cocama, Quechua, mestizo, and migrant communities. Ethnographic works on Asháninka, Yanesha, Nomatsiguenga, Matsigenka, Piro/Yine, and Urarina peoples simultaneously show why a single “Amazonian pantheon” is misleading. Each people has its own language, history, and ontology.

### The internal armed conflict

The Shining Path war and counterinsurgency transformed the central-selva social world, especially for Asháninka communities, through massacres, forced displacement, enslavement, militarization, and village defense. Brown and Fernández’s *War of Shadows* and later conflict documentation are vital history.

No reliable evidence located here shows an official Shining Path appropriation of Chullachaqui, a Chullachaqui militia cult, or a systematic political theology built around the figure. **[Negative finding]** The conflict nonetheless altered the communities and forests in which such stories circulated. Later memory can place traditional beings beside very real disappearances and armed violence, but that contextual conjunction is not proof of doctrinal use by insurgents.

### Twenty-first century

The figure now circulates simultaneously as:

- an actively narrated forest being;
- a warning to children and travelers;
- an environmental guardian;
- a regional identity symbol;
- a horror monster;
- a school-literature character;
- a subject of Amazonian visual art, comics, tourism, and film.

Continuing belief should not be reduced to either “surviving paganism” or entertainment. For some narrators it remains testimony about actual encounters; for others it is folklore, ethical allegory, regional heritage, or fiction.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature

Francisco Izquierdo Ríos helped carry the figure into national print culture. Galeano’s 2009 English collection internationalized it as “Owner of Trees and Animals.” Gustavo Rodríguez’s *El chullachaki en la otra selva* (2011) and many children’s books relocate the being into environmental and urban pedagogies. Darwin Córdova Vásquez’s *Chullachaqui* collects fifteen modern stories; the Peruvian ISBN agency records a 2020 second edition.

Modern children’s literature increasingly makes the Chullachaqui an unambiguous conservation hero. This is a selective development from the earlier morally ambivalent owner who can reward, torment, abduct, or kill.

### Comics

**Selva misteriosa**, created by Javier Flórez del Águila and originally published in the 1970s, drew on Loreto legend. In a later interview, the artist recalled two Chullachaqui forms told by his mother: a giant with backward-pointing feet and a dwarf with feet of unequal size. The recollection is valuable evidence for coexistence of variants rather than a single canonical body.

The L.U.C.U.M.A. workshop’s 2007 Chullachaqui designs portray aged human figures and were later used in Peruvian Bicentennial educational material. Contemporary webcomics and school assignments commonly standardize the plot into a child recognizing a false relative by its foot.

### Cinema

- **Chullachaqui** (Dorian Fernández-Moris, 2007), an approximately thirty-minute independent horror/suspense production shot around Iquitos, follows biology students entering an area associated with disappearances. Contemporary reporting describes it as an early locally produced Amazonian fiction film with a predominantly Loretano crew. **[Modern horror adaptation]**
- **El abrazo de la serpiente** (Ciro Guerra, 2015) uses *chullachaqui* in a transformed Amazonian philosophical vocabulary for an emptied likeness or remnant of a person. This should not be mistaken for a documentary reproduction of Peruvian Chullachaqui folklore. **[Cinematic reinvention]**
- A Peruvian production catalog describes a later adventure project in which a lost girl is guided by an enigmatic Chullachaqui and helps rescue a peccary from a trafficker. Here the former abductor becomes an ecological companion. **[Modern benevolent reinvention]**
- **Chullachaqui** (Diego Ruiz de Somocurcio, 2025) is cataloged as a twelve-minute fiction short about a young man investigating the legend. **[Contemporary horror/fantasy adaptation]**

### Art and commerce

Chullachaqui appears in paintings, ceramics, coloring books, illustrated anthologies, souvenirs, commercial storybooks, and tourist promotion. A 2025 illustrated publication, *Chullachaqui de la lupuna* by Elvis Luna, explicitly narrates an origin story. Such newly authored origins should be labeled modern literary constructions unless independently documented in older oral testimony.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated search found no substantial evidence that Renaissance occultists, theosophists, the Golden Dawn, historical grimoire traditions, or established Western neopagan organizations incorporated Chullachaqui. **[Negative finding]**

Internet pages may list it beside “elementals,” gnomes, fauns, cryptids, or nature spirits. These taxonomies are modern comparative classifications. No documented organized reconstructionist Chullachaqui religion, membership figures, formal liturgy, or continuous temple institution was found.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is it Indigenous, mestizo, Quechua, or pan-Amazonian?

The best answer is historical layering rather than exclusive ownership:

1. Amazonia contains old, locally distinct traditions of animal owners, forest persons, shapeshifters, and beings with reversed or anomalous feet.
2. The name *Chullachaqui* is transparently Quechua.
3. Spanish-speaking riverine society circulated and recombined these materials.
4. Christianity supplied *diablo*, baptism, cross, and demonological classifications.
5. Modern folklore publishing elevated one regional name into a symbol of the entire Peruvian Amazon.

Calling it simply an “Asháninka god” or “Southern Arawak deity” therefore exceeds the evidence. It is present in the modern narrative environment of Southern Arawak communities, but their older named beings must be studied on their own terms.

### Curupira origin theory

The reversed feet, small stature, forest guardianship, and misleading of hunters make comparison with Curupira unavoidable. The evidence permits several models:

- shared old Amazonian motif complex;
- diffusion between neighboring populations;
- convergence produced by similar hunting economies;
- later scholarly and popular conflation.

No source located establishes direct descent of Chullachaqui from Curupira. **[Unresolved scholarly comparison]**

### Ecological reading

Regan and modern educational authors interpret Chullachaqui as guardian of ecological equilibrium. Narrative evidence supports restraint: it owns animals, punishes excessive hunters, and defends trees. Yet “ecological god” is contemporary vocabulary. Older stories also emphasize abduction, danger, social estrangement, and acquisition of personal hunting power, not abstract biodiversity conservation.

### Psychological and social readings

Regan proposes that a false relative’s aggression may externalize otherwise repressed hostility and that spirit attacks can express the position of marginalized persons, including exploited workers and women. These are analytical hypotheses, not meanings explicitly stated by every narrator.

### Christianization

The ability of crosses, prayer, baptism, and Christian invocations to defeat the being demonstrates historical incorporation into a Christian moral universe. Tobacco and curanderismo persist beside those devices. The result is not simple replacement but a layered repertoire in which Christian and Amazonian protections operate together.

### Weak and discredited claims

The following popular claims are insufficiently supported:

- that Chullachaqui was a formally worshipped pre-Inca god;
- that it had ancient temples or priests;
- that a single canonical genealogy existed;
- that goat feet were always its original form;
- that Acuña’s reversed-footed people were certainly Chullachaqui;
- that every Amazonian “master of animals” is the same being;
- that it demanded human sacrifice;
- that it belongs to an ancient global race of gnomes or elementals;
- that all persons taken by it become Chullachaquis;
- that it is simply Satan under another name.

## 10. Chronology

- **1639–1641:** Cristóbal de Acuña records a report of backward-footed beings during his Amazon journey; possible motif antecedent, not a named Chullachaqui attestation.
- **Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries:** Mission expansion in the Peruvian montaña translates diverse Indigenous beings into Christian demonological categories. No verified named Chullachaqui occurrence was located in the requested Franciscan chronicles.
- **Late nineteenth century:** Rubber extraction expands drastically. Regan later records the opinion that Chullachaqui stories proliferated during the rubber era.
- **1885–1924:** Ordinaire, Sala, Izaguirre, Navarro, and related mission historians describe montaña peoples and missions; no securely verified named passage was established in this review.
- **1953:** Pérez Marcio publishes *Los hijos de la selva*, an early bibliographic locus for Amazonian folklore; the precise Chullachaqui passage requires direct archival confirmation.
- **1963:** Francisco Izquierdo Ríos prints a detailed account of the “Demonio Chullachaqui” in *Revista Peruana de Cultura*.
- **1975:** Gerald Weiss publishes *Campa Cosmology*, providing a major control for Asháninka/Campa cosmological categories.
- **1977:** Richard Chase Smith publishes *Deliverance from Chaos for a Song* on Yanesha cosmology and ritual.
- **1983:** Jaime Regan publishes *Hacia la tierra sin mal*, preserving numerous geographically identified Chullachaqui testimonies.
- **1980–2000:** Peru’s internal armed conflict devastates central-selva Indigenous communities. No institutional Shining Path use of Chullachaqui is established.
- **1990:** Shaver and Dodd publish major Nomatsiguenga material.
- **1991:** Brown and Fernández publish *War of Shadows*; Santos-Granero publishes *The Power of Love* on Yanesha history and cosmology.
- **1990s:** Matsigenka, Asháninka, Yanesha, and broader Amazonian ethnographies increasingly emphasize perspectivism, owners of animals, and historically specific Indigenous categories.
- **2002:** Santos-Granero publishes “Saint Christopher in the Amazon,” examining colonialism, child sorcery, and violence among Southern Arawak peoples; it reinforces the need to historicize, rather than homogenize, regional spirit traditions.
- **2007:** Dorian Fernández-Moris releases the Iquitos-made short film *Chullachaqui*; L.U.C.U.M.A. produces modern comic/visual interpretations.
- **2009:** Juan Carlos Galeano publishes *Folktales of the Amazon*, including two Chullachaki narratives.
- **2011:** Gustavo Rodríguez publishes *El chullachaki en la otra selva*.
- **2015:** *El abrazo de la serpiente* gives *chullachaqui* a new cinematic and philosophical meaning.
- **2018–2025:** School anthologies, illustrated books, cultural exhibitions, web folklore, and films increasingly emphasize environmental guardianship.
- **2025:** Diego Ruiz de Somocurcio’s short *Chullachaqui* is cataloged; Elvis Luna publishes *Chullachaqui de la lupuna*.
- **2026:** The figure remains active in oral testimony, heritage education, tourism, art, horror, and environmental storytelling. No evidence establishes a standardized modern institutional cult.

### Assessment

The securely reconstructable Chullachaqui is not an ancient god with a recoverable temple cult or canonical biography. It is a historically layered Amazonian forest person whose documented record emerges clearly in twentieth-century Peruvian folklore. The complex combines an old regional language of animal owners and anomalous forest beings; a Quechua descriptive name; Spanish-Christian categories of devil, baptism, and exorcistic protection; hunting testimony; rubber-era labor history; curanderismo; and modern environmental, literary, comic, and cinematic reformulation.

Its most durable features are not a genealogy or fixed portrait but a relation: human beings enter a forest already owned and inhabited by other persons. Chullachaqui tests whether they recognize that fact. Its uneven foot is the visible remainder that prevents the forest’s imitation of human familiarity from ever becoming completely seamless.
