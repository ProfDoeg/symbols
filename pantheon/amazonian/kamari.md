# The Kamari: Research Dossier

**Class:** **collective/category of dangerous nonhuman beings**, variously rendered “malignant beings,” “dangerous spirits,” “monsters,” “demons,” or, under Christian influence, “devils.” *Kamári* is not securely documented as the proper name of one god. Some recent accounts use the singular as an abstraction for evil or assimilate it to Satan, but the primary ethnographic referent is plural and internally diverse.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest explicit lexical and systematic documentation securely established here is Gerald Weiss’s Asháninka fieldwork of **1960–1964**, first presented fully in his 1969 dissertation and published as *Campa Cosmology* in 1975. Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century missionary and travel accounts document associated beliefs—dangerous souls, illness-causing spirits, divination, and accusations of child sorcery—from at least **1885**, but the accessible passages do not securely demonstrate that those authors recorded the word *kamári* itself. Earlier use of the term therefore remains **unestablished**, not disproved.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested ethnographic material:** documented from named Asháninka or Ashéninka interlocutors or early observers.
- **[RV] Regional or community variant:** localized and not safely generalized to every Asháninka-speaking population.
- **[CA] Christian-contact accretion:** native categories recast through Catholic, Adventist, or evangelical Devil, Hell, sin, and judgment doctrines.
- **[SA] Southern-Arawak comparison:** Yanesha, Nomatsiguenga, Matsigenka, or other related evidence; comparative, not automatically Asháninka.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an investigator’s historical or structural interpretation.
- **[F] Wider Peruvian-selva folklore:** potentially incorporated into local discourse but not an original or universal member of the category.
- **[MI] Modern invention or unsupported systematization.**

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

The Asháninka term normally appears as **kamári** or **kamari**. It designates a class rather than a unique personality. Oscar Espinosa describes *kamari* as a classificatory term encompassing various dangerous or monstrous entities, including beings named *kashivoreri/katsivoreri*, *míroni*, and *imparavanto/irampavanto*. Translation as Spanish *demonio* or *diablo* is convenient but historically loaded: missionaries and Christianized speakers helped make the indigenous category analogous to the Christian Devil and demons. [MSR/CA; S1, S2]

Recorded discourse nevertheless permits several overlapping uses:

1. **A generic dangerous nonhuman being** encountered in forests, rocky places, riverbanks, dreams, or altered states. [EA]
2. **A classificatory attribute** applied to particular beings: “X is a *kamari*.” [EA]
3. **A dead person’s dangerous or transformed residue**, overlapping with *peyari/piari*. [EA/RV]
4. **A morally cruel human being**, especially someone whose violence exceeds ordinary anger, envy, or conflict. [EA, modern]
5. **“The Devil” or Satan**, particularly in Christianized speech. [CA]
6. **An abstract principle of destructive evil or death**, recorded in modern Indigenous and anthropological formulations. [MSR/modern learned formulation]

“The Kamari,” capitalized and singular, is therefore potentially misleading. No securely attested text describes a singular god with a stable biography, temple, image, spouse, and mythology comparable to a named pantheon deity.

### Etymology

Weiss considered the etymology uncertain but suggested the root **-kam-**, “die” or “be dead.” Espinosa accepts this as plausible while emphasizing that it is not a completed historical derivation. The same root occurs in expressions associated with death, mortality, and some illness symptoms. [EA/MSR; S1]

Related forms include:

- **kamári**: dangerous or malignant being; often masculine or unmarked in later elicitation.
- **kamáro**: reported feminine form, using the feminine ending *-ro*. Some modern informants glossed it as a female demon, witch, or dead woman’s dangerous manifestation. [RV; S3]
- **kamori**: a rare recorded variant associated by one informant with the *tunche*; it was not repeated by other consultants and should not be treated as standard. [RV; S3]
- **kamagarini/kamarageni**: Matsigenka comparison, commonly glossed as “those who bring death” or predatory demons. It is a cognate Southern-Arawak category, not simply an alternative spelling of Asháninka *kamari*. [SA; S1]
- **kamikari**: the dead in some Asháninka accounts; it must not be silently equated with *kamari*. [RV; S4]
- **Kamavéni/Kamabeni**: the mortal terrestrial world or “land/place of death” in some accounts; this is a cosmographic term, not the name of the beings. [EA/RV; S3, S5]
- **kamarampi/kamárampi**: ayahuasca preparation. Espinosa connects the form to vomiting rather than deriving it from “demon”; similarity to *kamari* is not evidence that it means “devil drink.” [EA/MSR; S1]

### Translations and false equivalences

“Demon” is usable only if accompanied by a warning. Christian demons form a species under Satan in a moralized cosmic rebellion; *kamari* may instead classify predatory beings, transformed dead, animal-appearing entities, dangerous owners of places, harmful essences, or exceptionally cruel humans. [EA/MSR]

The following equations are historically secondary:

- **kamari = diablo/demonio**: missionary and bilingual translation. [CA]
- **Korioshpiri/Koroshípiri = Satan**: explicit in Christianized accounts, but its origin is disputed. [CA/RV]
- **Šarinkavéni = Hell**: an analogy found in ethnography and present-day explanation; location and character vary. [CA/RV]
- **maninkari = angels**: given by some speakers but rejected by others, who translate the word simply as “the invisible ones.” [CA/RV; S3]

The **Kamari ista** of Canelos Kichwa Ecuador is a distinct ritual whose *kamari* means an offering or ceremonial gift. It is not evidence about Asháninka malignant beings. Likewise, the Hindu epithet *Kāmāri*, Georgian Kamari, and unrelated personal or place names are homonyms.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Oral rather than scriptural corpus

There is no ancient inscription, canonical scripture, priestly codex, or precolonial image naming the *kamari*. The evidentiary corpus consists of:

- oral narratives and remembered encounters;
- shamanic and therapeutic practice;
- missionary and traveler descriptions;
- lexical collections and dictionaries;
- twentieth- and twenty-first-century ethnography;
- recent Indigenous testimony and cultural documentation.

Consequently, the surviving record is late relative to the age of the traditions. An entity’s first appearance in print is not its point of origin.

### Nineteenth-century antecedents

**Charles Wiener, 1880; Olivier Ordinaire, 1885; Gabriel Sala, 1893.** These writers record mortuary avoidance, dangerous souls, divination, accusations of sorcery, and treatments involving tobacco or coca. Santos-Granero cites Ordinaire’s 1885 account for the identification and punishment of alleged witches and Sala’s 1893 description of divination. These are the earliest directly traceable printed witnesses to the surrounding complex in the present search, but not securely to the lexical form *kamari*. [EA; S6]

**Franciscan mission chronicles.** Amich, San Antonio, Biedma, Sala, Izaguirre, and Navarro preserve valuable evidence for mission contact, epidemics, Indigenous resistance, and missionary demonological interpretation. Their language must be read critically: “devil,” “superstition,” and “witch” may be missionary translations rather than transparent native categories. No directly checked passage from these chronicles in the present research securely supplies an earlier lexical attestation of *kamari*.

### Twentieth-century corpus

**Navarro, 1924.** Cited for divination and for accusations particularly affecting vulnerable women and fatherless children. [EA through later critical citation; S6]

**Manuel Pérez Marcio, *Los hijos de la selva* (1953).** Adventist representation of Asháninka life. It records child-sorcery cases but belongs to a missionary genre with an explicit conversion program. [CA/documentary witness]

**Gerald Weiss, fieldwork 1960–1964; dissertation 1969; *Campa Cosmology* 1975.** This is the foundational systematic source. Weiss records *kamári* as a category of demons or dangerous beings; Koriošpiri as their father or ruler; named subclasses; illness by encounter; child sorcery; the dead; and the layered cosmos. His material distinguishes River Campa and Pajonal Campa but sometimes organizes fluid testimony into a comparatively rigid good/evil system. [EA/MSR; S2]

**Shaver and Dodd, Nomatsiguenga material, principally collected in the later twentieth century.** Nomatsiguenga accounts place demons in the lowest world and name Singerinti and his sons. They illuminate a related Arawak demonology but do not establish Asháninka genealogy. [SA]

**Richard Chase Smith, *Deliverance from Chaos for a Song* (1977).** Yanesha evidence for Yosoper, child sorcery, pathogenic objects, animals, and ritual specialists. This is comparative Southern-Arawak evidence. [SA]

**Fernando Santos-Granero, *The Power of Love* (1991).** Yanesha cosmology and social relations; essential comparative evidence for malignant beings, Yosoper, and *peyári*-like postmortem transformations. [SA]

**Michael Brown and Eduardo Fernández, *War of Shadows* (1991).** Asháninka warfare, political violence, healing, sorcery, and the historical pressures surrounding such beliefs. [EA/MSR]

**Rojas Zolezzi.** His work on Asháninka social life, sorcery, and fauna classification emphasizes that *peyari* and *kamari* classifications interact with perceptions of animal condition, transformation, and dangerous resemblance. [EA/MSR]

**Santos-Granero, “Saint Christopher in the Amazon” (2002).** A historical-comparative study of child sorcery across the Southern Arawak. It collates nineteenth- and twentieth-century testimony and argues that epidemic experience and colonial violence are crucial to the development or intensification of child-sorcery accusations. [MSR; S6]

**Espinosa, “Kamari: mal, enfermedad y violencia…” (2022) and “Los kamari…” (2023).** These works explicitly reject treatment of Kamari as a singular deity and interpret the category as an oblique memory of epidemics, colonial intrusion, and exceptional cruelty. [MSR; S1, S7]

### Manuscripts, editions, and transmission

The problem is not a gap between ancient composition and medieval manuscripts but the gap between **oral circulation and late transcription**. Spellings vary according to missionary orthographies, older “Campa” terminology, Asháninka/Ashéninka dialects, Spanish phonology, and modern standardized writing. A philologically complete edition collating all *kamari* narratives does not exist.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and characteristic action

The category’s central field is **danger, predation, illness, terror, bodily deformation, violent death, and destructive transformation**. [EA]

Kamari beings may:

- cause illness through bodily contact, gaze, proximity, fright, or dream encounter;
- contaminate a person after being seen or mocked;
- assume the appearance of animals, spouses, attractive strangers, skeletons, lights, mist, or wind;
- inhabit trees, rocks, cliffs, riverbanks, forest depths, or lower cosmic layers;
- use ants, termites, bees, and wasps as agents;
- steal or bury food remains, hair, sweat, scabs, excrement, or other bodily traces;
- dismember, crush, penetrate, seduce, beat, or devour victims;
- temporarily revive a victim who returns home only to sicken and die;
- become indistinguishable from exceptionally cruel human actors.

Santos-Granero states that demons, evil spirits, and malignant essences of trees, rocks, ants, termites, bees, and wasps cause illness by bodily or visual contact while awake or dreaming. Insect agents bury food debris and bodily substances associated with their victims. [EA/SA synthesis; S6]

### Appearance

There is **no single iconography**. Named forms include:

- **Irampavanto/imparavanto:** an attractive woman, sometimes resembling a man’s wife, associated with a toucan; the victim is seduced and then beaten to death. Some informants instead described a dark flying creature or a being capable of becoming a log. [EA/RV; S3]
- **Katsivoreri/kashivoreri:** a small flying or birdlike nocturnal being, luminous like a firefly or lamp, sometimes bleeding and carrying a smaller being. Weiss’s more violent account attributes lethal sexual assault and transformation to it. [EA/RV; S3]
- **Sashintsi:** extremely thin, skeletal, humanlike, or bird-associated; whistles, breaks the victim’s body, restores it, and sends the victim home to die. [EA/RV; S3]
- **Mankoite:** human-shaped but normally invisible, hairy or mane-headed, dressed in ragged tunics and wicker crowns; inhabits cliffs, rocky outcrops, rapids, and riverbanks. Encounter predicts death. [EA/RV; S3, S4]
- **Peyari/piari:** a dead person’s dangerous transformed manifestation or an animal whose diseased, emaciated, scarred, worm-infested, or otherwise abnormal body signals that it is not safe game. [EA/RV; S3]
- **Ashinsateri:** in modern Ashéninka public documentation, a whistling white mist whose contact produces madness and death. [RV; S5]
- **Kitamari:** a spirit appearing as a white donkey. The introduced animal makes this particular image necessarily post-contact. [RV/late]
- **Kaganiri:** a nearly invisible, silent wind that freezes its victim. [RV]
- **Katzereri:** child-sized, bright-eyed spirits borne on the shoulders of *peyari*, making a “double demon.” [RV]
- **Nantatziri:** dwarf-like owners of animals living in rocky areas. Whether every community classifies them as *kamari* is uncertain. [RV]

### Material iconography

No archaeological image is securely identified as “Kamari,” and no standard cult statue, mask, color, weapon, number, celestial body, or emblem is attested. Modern drawings elicited by researchers are records of particular consultations, not copies of an ancient iconographic canon.

### Sacred and defensive substances

The relevant substances are therapeutic rather than offerings to the beings:

- **tobacco:** drunk, chewed, smoked, spat, or blown;
- **coca:** chewed in divination in several older accounts;
- **kamarampi/ayahuasca:** permits experienced specialists to perceive agents and relations invisible to ordinary sight;
- **medicinal leaf baths and steam treatments:** strengthen bodies and counter harmful beings;
- **protective plants**, including *kamarishi/kamaripintsi*, “devil leaf,” used for fright, fever, visions, “bad air,” and bodily pain attributed to *kamari*. Recent ethnobotanical fieldwork found several botanical taxa carrying that name. [EA, continuing practice; S8]

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### No unitary genealogy

As a collective category, *kamari* have no single parentage, spouse, offspring, or family tree. Claims that they were all “created by Kientibakori” or constitute a coherent race are over-systematizations unless restricted to a named Matsigenka or mission-influenced source.

### Koriošpiri/Koroshípiri

Weiss reports **Koriošpiri** as father and ruler of demons. Santos-Granero compares him with the Yanesha **Yosoper**. Later Asháninka informants explicitly identify Koroshípiri with Satan. [EA/CA/SA]

The name’s origin is disputed. A modern linguistic thesis notes dictionary forms *coroshi* “cross” and *coroshipiiro* “cross of stars,” raising the possibility of derivation from Spanish *cruz*. That would make the demonic ruler a post-contact formation or transformation, but the proposed derivation remains unproven. [MSR; S3]

### Pawa and the Tasorentsi

Some contemporary accounts say that Pawa or Tasorintsi created all beings, including the dangerous ones, judges sinners, or sends the wicked to the kamari. These narratives combine Asháninka creator-transformer concepts with Christian sin, Hell, and final judgment. Other testimony does not give Pawa direct rule over the kamari. [RV/CA]

### Kamari and peyari

*Peyari* derives in one analysis from *pey-aantsi*, “disappear/be transformed.” Weiss and later lexicographic work define a *peyari* as a dead person’s soul—or sometimes another dangerous being—taking the form of game. [EA; S3]

Earlier distinctions appear to have been:

- **kamari:** broad class of dangerous or malignant beings;
- **peyari:** transformed or wandering dead, especially in animal form.

Espinosa reports that this boundary has blurred in contemporary discourse. The dead are especially dangerous to their own surviving relatives, which makes “kin” central: the threat is not merely from an alien demon but from a transformed person with prior relations to the living. [EA/modern change; S1]

### Every documented named divine-being interaction

Because *kamari* is collective, “interaction” often means hierarchy or conflict rather than a narrative meeting.

- **Koriošpiri — rules and is called father of the kamari/demons.** Weiss, fieldwork 1960–1964, published 1975. **[EA; possibly CA in its Satan-like systematization]**
- **Yosoper — comparative Yanesha analogue to Koriošpiri, not an Asháninka interaction.** Smith 1977; Santos-Granero 1991 and 2002. **[SA; no direct interaction attested]**
- **Pawa/Tasorintsi — said in some recent Christianized testimony to have created kamari or to consign sinners to them.** Twenty-first-century elicited accounts. **[RV/CA]**
- **Maninkari/amatsenka — structurally contrasted as benevolent or “our” invisible beings against harmful kamari.** Weiss 1975 and later summaries. **[EA/MSR; no specific mythic encounter securely attested]**
- **Mankoite — classified as a powerful kamari, sometimes nearly tasorentsi in power.** Weiss 1975 and later testimony. **[EA/RV; membership, not an episode]**
- **Kashiri, the Moon — consumes or transports the dead in some accounts, but no direct encounter with named kamari is securely attested.** **[RV; absence finding]**
- **Avíreri/Nabireri — transformer figure whose narratives include the production of dangerous beings and animals, but no securely checked episode explicitly stages Avíreri meeting “the Kamari” as a singular entity.** **[EA/absence finding]**
- **Kientibakori — Matsigenka evil creator sometimes compared with Koriošpiri.** **[SA/syncretistic comparison; no Asháninka interaction securely attested]**
- **Singerinti — Nomatsiguenga ruler or greatest evil spirit, with sons Mócobá, Sinquiréri, and Comantsáo.** **[SA; not an Asháninka Kamari genealogy]**
- **Christian God, Satan, and angels — Pawa/Tasorintsi, Koriošpiri, and maninkari are sometimes equated with these figures.** Mission-era through present. **[CA, variable and disputed]**

No secure interaction with Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, or the Pishtaco has been found. Modern speakers may place the *tunche*, Chullachaqui, or Pishtaco under the very broad category *kamari*, but this is taxonomic assimilation in contemporary Spanish-influenced discourse, not evidence of an old shared narrative.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Complexes

### 5.1 Encounter, fright, sickness, and delayed death

The most recurrent narrative pattern is not a creation myth but an **encounter account**:

1. A person travels alone, hunts, bathes, or stays outside after dark.
2. A strange animal, whistle, light, mist, wind, person, or familiar-looking spouse appears.
3. Contact, sight, ridicule, sexual approach, or fear breaches bodily safety.
4. The person returns home apparently alive.
5. Fever, pain, madness, wasting, or otherwise incurable sickness follows.
6. A specialist identifies the nonhuman agent, attempts extraction or protection, or announces that death is inevitable.

This pattern appears in Weiss and in modern elicited testimony. A Peruvian linguistic thesis records speakers saying that meeting a *kamari* causes fright, then illness, and finally death despite attempted medicines. [EA; S3]

### 5.2 Dangerous animal semblance

An apparently huntable animal may be *peyari* or another kamari manifestation. Abnormal thinness, lesions, worms, scars, or diseased organs reveal its condition. Eating it is dangerous. The narrative protects neither “species” nor “totem” in a simple sense; it concerns the instability of visible bodies and the possibility that game is a transformed person or predator. [EA; S2, S3]

### 5.3 Sexual predators

Irampavanto and some versions of Katsivoreri assume sexually attractive or spouse-like forms. Seduction culminates in lethal beating, penetration, or transformation. Weiss compared Irampavanto to a Western succubus, but “succubus” is an analytical gloss, not an Indigenous equivalence. [EA/MSR; S3]

### 5.4 Child sorcery

A child dreams of a human witch, bird, or insect in human guise. The visitor places a thorn, bone, or palm sliver in the child’s hand; when it falls and becomes buried, someone falls ill. Repeated instruction teaches the child to see victims as game and, in some accounts, to acquire a taste for human flesh. The child eventually dreams of burying pathogenic materials deliberately. [EA; S6]

Among Yanesha, a child may be rubbed with a special plant or approached by a curved-billed hummingbird associated with an evil shaman. Among Nomatsiguenga, a sparrow-like *tsiricóti* or another malignant spirit bird provides instruction. These are comparative variants, not interchangeable Asháninka details. [SA; S6]

The dispute over intention is explicit:

- Weiss and Smith allowed that young children might initially act unwittingly or serve a chthonic ruler.
- Santos-Granero’s informants insisted that although initiation was involuntary, children later knew what they were doing. [Competing EA interpretations; S6]

### 5.5 The dead as dangerous kin

After death, the person’s soul or essence may linger near the settlement, particularly threatening relatives. Older reports describe abandoning or burning a dead person’s dwelling, burying the body away from habitation, or otherwise separating the dead from the living. A soul may become animal-like or depart toward other cosmic levels. [EA, with regional variation; S3]

The *peyari* cycle preserves the uncomfortable continuity of kinship: the dangerous being may be a relative who is no longer safely human. Espinosa argues that the older *peyari* category has increasingly merged with *kamari*. [MSR/modern development; S1]

### 5.6 Epidemic and white-person narratives

Espinosa proposes that the association between *kamari*, bodily destruction, and illness is an **oblique historical memory** of European contact and epidemics. Certain traditions describe *wirakocha*, whites, or outsiders as emerging from aquatic or subterranean regions and bringing lethal contamination. The argument does not prove that the word *kamari* originated after contact; it proposes that colonial epidemics transformed and intensified its semantic field. [MSR; S1]

Weiss did not establish a general equation *wirakocha = kamari*. Descola later emphasized a dualistic division that could place outsiders, predatory beings, and harmful powers in the negative domain. Espinosa warns that the historical link is often implicit rather than stated by present-day speakers. [Scholarly dispute; S1]

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No cult in the classical sense

There are no known temples, priesthoods, festivals, public sacrifices, votive images, devotional hymns, pilgrimages, or theophoric naming system dedicated to “The Kamari.” The beings are **avoided, diagnosed, repelled, treated, or narrated**, not normally worshipped.

Accordingly:

- no Kamari temple has been archaeologically identified;
- no priest of Kamari is attested;
- no human sacrifice to Kamari is documented;
- no “temple prostitution,” cult prostitution, or sexual rite is documented;
- no canonical calendar, holy day, color, number, or planet is established.

Violence against people accused of sorcery is, however, documented and must not be disguised as sacrifice.

### Shipokantaseri and seripiari

Santos-Granero distinguishes:

- **shipokantaseri:** Asháninka herbalist or vegetal specialist;
- **seripiari/sheripiari:** shamanic specialist, strongly associated with tobacco.

Both could identify alleged child sorcerers through coca and tobacco divination or by inducing a patient to dream of the aggressor. [EA; S6]

The *sheripiari* may:

- ingest concentrated tobacco juice or ayahuasca;
- chew coca in accounts influenced by Andean exchange;
- blow tobacco smoke or spit tobacco juice over the patient;
- suck the afflicted place;
- extract a thorn, fragment, insect, charcoal, or bone representing the pathogenic agent;
- communicate with helpful beings;
- diagnose the disrupted relationship responsible for illness.

Recent ethnomedicine cautions against dividing illnesses into neatly “natural/herbal” and “supernatural/shamanic” classes. Herbalism and shamanism can address the same illness at different levels: ordinary plant treatment strengthens the body, while the shaman seeks the predatory relationship behind serious disease. [EA/MSR; S9]

### Child-sorcery violence

Historical sources describe accused children being beaten, smoked, confined, tortured to reveal supposedly buried objects, expelled, or killed. The victims were often socially defenseless: young girls, orphans, captives, widows, or women without a male protector. Sources disagree about whether children, women, or both predominated. [EA; S6]

Santos-Granero’s major historical claim is that the institution intensified amid epidemics, enslavement, displacement, mission settlements, and colonial demographic catastrophe. Mission concentration could itself increase epidemic transmission, after which accusations sought a comprehensible internal agent. [MSR; S6]

Calling this “human sacrifice” would be inaccurate. It was punitive killing or execution under sorcery accusations, not an offering to Kamari.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before sustained written documentation

Arawak cognates and the wide regional distribution of predatory spirits, transformed dead, shamanic tobacco, and dangerous animal semblances make a wholly recent origin unlikely. Yet the lack of precolonial texts prevents reconstruction of a pure “original Kamari.” [MSR]

### Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: missions, epidemics, and rebellion

Franciscan expansion into the central Peruvian forest produced mission settlements, flight, resistance, and recurring epidemics. Juan Santos Atahualpa’s uprising beginning in 1742 disrupted missions and was interpreted through Asháninka, Yanesha, Piro/Yine, Andean, and Christian messianic idioms. The available evidence does not show that Kamari was a named patron or opponent in the uprising.

Santos-Granero’s epidemic thesis treats this era as formative for later associations between outsiders, infection, death, and dangerous beings. [MSR]

### Nineteenth century: early descriptions

Travelers and Franciscans documented fear of the dead, divination, witchcraft accusations, medicinal practice, and the treatment or killing of alleged witches. Their accounts are indispensable but translated native categories through *demonio*, *brujo*, *alma*, and *superstición*. [EA/colonial mediation]

### Early twentieth century: rubber, missions, and conversion

Slave raiding, rubber extraction, mission concentration, and epidemic disease destabilized communities. Catholic and especially Adventist mission discourse increasingly mapped indigenous dangerous beings onto Satan and his demons.

Adventist settlements provided literacy, schooling, medicine, and political resources but also promoted a sharper Devil/God opposition. In some accounts:

- Pawa becomes “God”;
- Koriošpiri becomes Satan;
- maninkari become angels;
- Šarinkavéni becomes Hell;
- misconduct becomes Christian sin;
- the dead undergo judgment.

These equations were never fully uniform. [CA]

### 1960s–1970s: systematic ethnography

Weiss’s work fixed *kamari* in the anthropological record as a major category in an apparently dualistic cosmos. His analysis remains foundational but can overstate systematic binary organization. Later scholarship emphasizes relational predation, bodily transformation, historical violence, and community-level variation. [EA/MSR]

### 1980s–1990s: Shining Path and mass violence

Asháninka communities suffered killings, captivity, forced labor, displacement, hunger, disease, and forced recruitment under Sendero Luminoso; state and paramilitary violence also affected the region. Official and scholarly estimates commonly cited for the late twentieth century include approximately 10,000 displaced, 5,000 held captive, and 6,000 dead, with dozens of communities disappearing, although numerical reconstruction remains difficult. [Documented history; S5, S10]

Espinosa reports that *kamari* became a category through which some Asháninka interpreted Senderista cruelty. This was not a myth claiming that Sendero members literally belonged to an ancient species; it was a moral-ontological classification of extraordinary cruelty, bodily destruction, and antisocial predation. [EA/MSR; S7]

### Twenty-first century

Kamari discourse remains active in accounts of illness, fright, forest danger, dreams, children’s behavior, violence, and social breakdown. Biomedical, herbal, shamanic, Catholic, Adventist, and evangelical explanations coexist. Official Peruvian cultural documentation continues to describe *kamaris* as malignant beings capable of causing illness. [Continuing tradition; S5]

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Indigenous continuity

The strongest modern reception is not occult revival but continued Asháninka and Ashéninka use. Kamari remain part of health reasoning, moral discourse, oral narrative, and historical memory.

### Peruvian-selva folklore

*Tunche*, Chullachaqui, Pishtaco, Yacuruna, and Bufeo cycles circulate across Loreto, Ucayali, and the wider selva. Modern bilingual speakers may call any frightening forest being a *kamari* or compare one with the Chullachaqui or Tunche. [F]

These identifications should be treated as modern regional translation, not proof that all those beings were historically members of a single Asháninka pantheon:

- **Tunche:** frequently assimilated to *kamori*, Sashintsi, or a whistling dead spirit.
- **Chullachaqui:** used as a Spanish-regional comparison for a deceptive forest being.
- **Pishtaco:** associated with outsiders and bodily extraction but, according to Espinosa, not normally with epidemic illness in the same way as *kamari*.
- **Yacuruna and Bufeo:** no securely documented Kamari interaction found.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated search found **no documented Renaissance, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, Wiccan, chaos-magical, or reconstructionist cult of Kamari**. Claims presenting “Kamari” as an ancient Amazonian god with a fixed sigil, summoning rite, planetary correspondence, spouse, or standardized mythology should be classified **[MI]** unless supported by identified Asháninka sources.

A recent online “pantheon registry” presents Kamari in a conventional deity-database format and states that the beings were “brought forth by the dark creator Kientibakori.” This collapses Asháninka and Matsigenka material and converts a class into a mythological taxon. It is a useful example of modern systematization, not an independent source. [MI; S11]

### Commercial and popular use

No major film, novel, game, comic, or commercial franchise was found to have decisively shaped the Kamari’s public image. Search noise is substantial because *Kamari* is also a personal name, Greek place-name, Georgian figure, Hindu epithet, Canelos Kichwa ritual term, and word in unrelated languages.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### “Demon” or broader dangerous-being category?

Earlier ethnographers and missionaries often translated *kamari* as “demon.” Espinosa argues that “dangerous beings” or “monsters” better preserves the category’s range. The disagreement is partly historical: contemporary speakers themselves frequently say *diablo*, so the Christian translation has become part of the living tradition even if it is not the category’s timeless essence. [Debate; S1, S3]

### Primordial dualism or contact-shaped moral opposition?

Weiss portrayed Asháninka cosmology as strongly divided between benevolent beings and kamari. Descola used this as an example of a broad ontological division. Later scholars warn that:

- beings may be ambivalent;
- classifications change with circumstances;
- the Christian God/Satan binary sharpened older distinctions;
- “good” and “evil” are inadequate translations of relational safety and predation.

Thus the binary is ethnographically real in some accounts but historically intensified and not metaphysically uniform. [Debate; S1, S4]

### Indigenous antiquity or colonial epidemic memory?

Two positions need not be mutually exclusive:

- cognate Arawak forms and widespread shamanic complexes imply older foundations;
- epidemics, enslavement, and missions reshaped those foundations and attached new meanings to illness, whites, Hell, and moral cruelty.

Espinosa’s “oblique memory” thesis is a historical interpretation, not a demonstrated etymological origin. [MSR; S1]

### Kamari and peyari

Earlier ethnography distinguishes kamari from the transformed dead more clearly than some contemporary accounts do. Espinosa interprets present overlap as historical semantic change. Rojas Zolezzi’s fauna studies support the importance of *peyari* as dangerous transformation rather than merely “ghost.” [EA/MSR]

### Koriošpiri’s origin

Possibilities include:

1. an older Asháninka chthonic ruler;
2. diffusion from neighboring Southern-Arawak traditions;
3. restructuring under Christian Satanology;
4. a post-contact term related to “cross.”

Existing evidence does not decide among them. Treating Koriošpiri as an unambiguously precolonial Satan-equivalent is unjustified.

### Ethical reassessment of witchcraft sources

Missionaries often described violence against accused witches as evidence of savagery while underplaying the role of epidemics, mission aggregation, slave raiding, and social collapse in producing accusation crises. Recent scholarship neither denies the killings nor isolates them from colonial history. [Historiographical revision; S6]

### State of the evidence

The principal record is dispersed among:

- American Museum of Natural History publications;
- Smithsonian repositories;
- Franciscan archives and published chronicles;
- Peruvian university repositories;
- missionary linguistic collections;
- ethnographic monographs;
- oral testimony retained within communities.

No archaeological layer can presently be assigned to “Kamari worship,” and no ancient artifact carries a secure label.

## 10. Chronology

| Date/period | Historical record |
|---|---|
| Precolonial period | No written or iconographically identifiable Kamari evidence. Comparative Arawak vocabulary and regional structures suggest older dangerous-being and transformed-dead complexes, but their exact antiquity is unrecoverable. |
| Seventeenth century | Franciscan penetration of the central forest begins a prolonged era of missionization, epidemic exposure, translation into Christian demonology, and Indigenous resistance. No secure lexical *kamari* attestation checked. |
| 1742–c. 1756 | Juan Santos Atahualpa’s movement disrupts central-selva missions. Kamari plays no documented named role, though the period matters to later histories of outsider violence and religious synthesis. |
| 1880 | Wiener records mortuary practices and fear of dangerous postmortem presence later cited in Asháninka death studies. |
| 1885 | Ordinaire documents sorcery accusations and divinatory identification; earliest directly verified antecedent in the present dossier. |
| 1893 | Sala documents comparable Southern-Arawak divination and vulnerable accused persons. |
| 1905 | Batlle records Yanesha sorcery diagnosis and punishment. |
| 1918 | Influenza pandemic reaches the region; later historical scholarship treats such epidemics as central to malignant-being and witchcraft interpretations. |
| 1924 | Navarro records Asháninka and Yanesha sorcery accusations and divination. |
| 1928 | Measles and other diseases kill large numbers gathered around Adventist missions in the Perené region. |
| 1933 | Measles kills approximately 120 of 300 neophytes at the Adventist mission of Sutziki, according to later historical compilation. |
| 1937–1939 | Malaria and measles outbreaks affect Yanesha and Asháninka communities. |
| 1953 | Pérez Marcio publishes *Los hijos de la selva*, an Adventist account containing child-sorcery narratives. |
| 1960–1964 | Gerald Weiss conducts fieldwork among River and Pajonal “Campa,” securely recording *kamári*, named subtypes, Koriošpiri, layered cosmology, and child sorcery. |
| 1969 | Weiss completes the fuller dissertation underlying *Campa Cosmology*. |
| 1975 | *Campa Cosmology* published, becoming the foundational systematic source. |
| 1977 | Smith publishes *Deliverance from Chaos for a Song* on the Yanesha, important comparative evidence. |
| 1980s–1990s | Sendero Luminoso, military action, captivity, forced labor, displacement, epidemics, and mass death transform Asháninka society; *kamari* becomes one means of classifying exceptional human cruelty. |
| 1991 | Santos-Granero’s *The Power of Love* and Brown and Fernández’s *War of Shadows* deepen relational, political, and historical analysis. |
| 1994 | Renard-Casevitz publishes on Asháninka experience of violence in the central forest. |
| 2002 | Santos-Granero publishes “Saint Christopher in the Amazon”; Rojas Zolezzi publishes fauna classification work central to *peyari* interpretation. |
| 2006–2007 | Ethnomedical research stresses relational illness and the complementary roles of herbal and shamanic treatment. |
| 2008 | Peru recognizes traditional ayahuasca knowledge and use as national cultural heritage; this is recognition of a broad Amazonian practice, not a Kamari cult. |
| 2010s | Research increasingly connects Kamari discourse to colonial epidemics, outsiders, capitalism, and twentieth-century political violence. |

## 11. Conclusions

The reconstructable subject is not “The Kamari,” a singular Amazonian god, but **the historically changing category of kamari beings**. Its secure center consists of dangerous nonhuman agencies that cause fright, illness, wasting, madness, bodily destruction, and death. They may appear as animals, humans, sexual partners, lights, mist, wind, skeletons, or owners of places. Their action crosses waking experience and dreams.

The most important distinctions are historical:

- *kamari* and *peyari* were not always interchangeable;
- “demon,” Satan, Hell, sin, and divine judgment reflect genuine modern Asháninka discourse but also centuries of Christian contact;
- neighboring Yanesha, Nomatsiguenga, Matsigenka, and Piro/Yine evidence is comparative rather than automatically Asháninka;
- Tunche, Chullachaqui, Pishtaco, Yacuruna, and Bufeo belong to wider regional cycles and cannot be retroactively placed in one primordial pantheon;
- the category has expanded from nonhuman danger to name extraordinary human cruelty, including the violence of Sendero Luminoso;
- no temple, image cult, sacrifice, priesthood, occult order, or neopagan reconstruction devoted to a singular Kamari is documented.

The figure’s historical “life” lies precisely in these changes: from an orally transmitted field of predation, dangerous transformation, and dead kin, through epidemic catastrophe and missionary demonology, to a contemporary language for illness, memory, and radical antisocial violence.
