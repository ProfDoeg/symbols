# The Tunche: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A nocturnal death-spirit, wandering soul, ominous bird-call, or malignant forest apparition in the mestizo and Indigenous-influenced folklore of the Peruvian Amazon. It is not securely documented as a god with a formal cult, priesthood, genealogy, or stable bodily form.  
> **Earliest secure attestation located:** José Toribio Torres Lara, *Las mariposas blancas: episodios de la montaña* (Lima, 1898), pp. 31–34: in a story set during travel through the Pichis–Pachitea region, a servant identifies a bird’s call as “el tunche,” glosses it as “the dead,” and interprets it as an omen that someone dear to the listener has died.  
> **Evidentiary caution:** “The Tunche” is best treated as an evolving Amazonian folklore complex, not as a timeless member of a unified “Southern Arawak pantheon.” Later sources associate it particularly with Yine/Piro belief, while Asháninka, Yánesha, Nomatsiguenga, Matsigenka, Cocama, Kichwa, and mestizo communities possess partly analogous but separately named categories of dead, demons, forest beings, and dangerous souls. Those categories must not automatically be collapsed into *tunche*.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early attestation]** Documentary evidence from 1898 through the mid-twentieth century.
- **[Folklore]** Oral belief or narrative recorded from living narrators.
- **[Regional variant]** A formulation limited to a locality, community, or identifiable recording context.
- **[Christianized elaboration]** A version employing sin, purgation, baptism, God, or the Devil.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An academic interpretation rather than a statement found verbatim in tradition.
- **[Modern retelling]** Literature, film, education, tourism, games, or internet circulation.
- **[Modern invention]** A claim unattested in the historical record and first visible in recent popular presentation.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Classification

### Basic identity

The most conservative reconstruction is that *tunche/tunchi* originally denoted either a dead person’s surviving presence or the ominous nocturnal sound through which that presence was recognized. Torres Lara’s 1898 witness supplies both components: a bird-call is identified as *el tunche*, while the explanation is simply *el muerto*, “the dead.” The episode does not describe a visible monster, a forest god, a guardian of ecology, or an organized mythology. It associates the sound with bereavement and November, the Catholic month of the dead. The omen proves false within Torres Lara’s narrative, whose educated coastal narrator disparages the servant’s belief. This is nevertheless the earliest secure printed attestation found in the present investigation. [Early attestation; colonial-republican contact-zone account.] A modern study reproduces and contextualizes the passage in detail ([Laya Alcedo 2022](https://revista.agn.gob.pe/ojs/index.php/ragn/article/download/144/238/)).

By 1942, Francisco Izquierdo Ríos’s story “El Tunchi,” printed in the Iquitos periodical *Trocha* 1.5, described the Tunchi as *el difunto, sombra del alma, espíritu*—“the deceased, shadow of the soul, spirit”—and said that the song of the tunchi bird announced death. [Early literary-folkloric attestation.] The record is preserved in the Instituto de Investigaciones de la Amazonía Peruana’s retrospective bibliography ([IIAP, *Bibliografía de la Amazonía peruana*](https://api-repositorio.iiap.gob.pe/server/api/core/bitstreams/d0a6343f-0481-4828-ac62-43ba9ce05662/content)).

Later Loreto and Ucayali traditions expand this minimal complex into several partially conflicting beings:

1. the soul of any dead person;
2. an unquiet soul replaying its former routes;
3. a sinner purging guilt before going to God;
4. a condemned or violently dead soul;
5. an incorporeal fright-agent causing *susto*, *airado*, or *manchari*;
6. the “malignant” spirit or Devil;
7. a bird, or a human soul speaking through a bird-call;
8. a shapeshifter mimicking a loved person;
9. in recent ecological retellings, a punitive guardian of the forest.

The first six have ethnographic or folkloric documentation. The shapeshifter appears in modern lexicography but is not part of the earliest record. The ecological “guardian” is predominantly a recent pedagogical and internet formulation and should not be presented as an ancient defining trait.

### Names and spellings

- **Tunche** — the most common national-Spanish spelling. The Asociación de Academias de la Lengua Española defines it as a mythical being of eastern Peru that assumes the form of someone loved by its victim in order to attract that victim. That entry documents current lexical usage, not the antiquity of the shapeshifting motif ([ASALE, *Diccionario de americanismos*](https://www.asale.org/damer/tunche)). [Modern lexicographic systematization.]
- **Tunchi** — especially common in Loreto and Amazonian Spanish; it is the form used by Izquierdo Ríos and Juan E. Coriat. [Early and continuing regional variant.]
- **El Tunche / El Tunchi** — Spanish definite-article forms sometimes suggest an individual monster. Much oral evidence instead permits indefinite or plural *tunchis*: the dead or dangerous wandering souls as a class.
- **Tunchi supay** — an Ecuadorian Amazonian Kichwa dictionary records *tunchi* both as a nocturnal bird and, mythologically, an “alma,” while *supay* supplies the spirit/demon component. Its example describes a *tunchi supay* consuming the heart of a bad person. This is relevant comparative evidence for Amazonian Kichwa usage, not proof that the Peruvian complex is wholly Quechua in origin ([Kichwa–Castilian dictionary](https://blog.santillana.com.ec/wp-content/uploads/2022/07/RK_diccionario_kichwa_castellano.pdf)). [Regional comparative attestation.]

### Etymology

No secure etymology was established in the consulted historical or linguistic sources.

Popular websites routinely call *tunche* “Amazonian Quechua,” occasionally claiming that it originally meant “fear” or “a penetrating whistle.” Those claims generally lack a cited historical dictionary or a demonstrated derivational analysis. The Kichwa dictionary confirms contemporary Amazonian Kichwa *tunchi* for a bird and a soul, but that alone cannot determine whether the word originated in Kichwa, entered Kichwa from Amazonian Spanish, or circulated through multilingual contact.

The 1898 spelling *tunche*, the mid-century Loreto form *tunchi*, and modern Kichwa *tunchi* show sustained regional circulation. Establishing deeper origin would require dated lexical comparison across Yine, Asháninka, Yánesha, Cocama, regional Kichwa, and nineteenth-century Amazonian Spanish. Until that work is produced, “Quechua etymology” remains **unestablished**, while multilingual Upper-Amazonian diffusion is the safer historical model. [Modern scholarly caution.]

### Classification through time

| Period/source | Classification |
|---|---|
| Torres Lara, 1898 | “The dead,” signaled by a bird-call; death omen |
| Izquierdo Ríos, 1942 | Deceased person, soul-shadow, spirit; bird-song announcing death |
| Coriat, 1944 | A dead woman’s avenging soul, individualized in literary narrative |
| Late twentieth-century Loreto records | Wandering soul, fright-spirit, sinner in temporary penance |
| Some Loreto narrators | Distinct from the more deadly *Maligno* |
| Other narrators and films | Identical with the *Maligno*, demon, or Devil |
| Contemporary popular culture | Forest monster, shapeshifter, environmental guardian, or boss-creature |

There is therefore no evidence for a single canonical taxonomy.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### 1898: Torres Lara

José Toribio Torres Lara’s *Las mariposas blancas* belongs to the literature of state travel, colonization, and “civilizing” penetration of the central Peruvian montaña. In the relevant inset narrative, Captain Contreras hears a bird. His servant says, mysteriously, “el tunche.” Asked what it is, he answers “the dead” and predicts that someone dear to the captain is no longer alive. November increases the omen’s plausibility because it is the month of the dead. The captain later finds his family alive.

What the text establishes:

- *tunche* was intelligible in the Pichis–Pachitea/central-selva contact zone by 1898;
- it was connected with a bird-call, death, bereavement, and divination;
- colonist or expedition personnel could fear it.

What it does **not** establish:

- a visible monster;
- a whistle that must never be answered;
- Yine authorship of the tradition;
- an ancient cult or pantheon;
- a genealogy;
- a guardian of the forest.

The source is filtered through a coastal officer and a narrator committed to the ideology of republican “progress.” Its dismissive treatment is evidence of a colonial hierarchy of knowledge, not evidence that the servant’s belief was insignificant. [Early attestation.]

### 1942: Francisco Izquierdo Ríos

“El Tunchi,” *Trocha* 1.5 (Iquitos, February 1942), pp. 10–14, is the first clearly identified publication devoted to the figure located in this research. The IIAP bibliography summarizes it as an Amazonian story in which the Tunchi is the dead person, shadow of the soul, or spirit, and the tunchi bird’s song announces death. [Early literary-folkloric attestation.]

### 1944: Juan E. Coriat

Coriat’s *Tunchi: leyendas loretanas* gave the figure extended literary form. Its title story identifies the Tunchi with the soul of a woman deceived by a Lima military officer; she returns as an avenging dead person. The volume also treats the Ayaymaman, rubber-era violence, Fitzcarrald, and Iquitos regional history. It is evidence for the consolidation of *tunchi* as a Loreto literary emblem, although Coriat’s named characters and revenge plot should not automatically be treated as oral canon ([contemporary bibliographic account](https://www.lupuna.org/2015/02/selva-de-letras-el-tunchi-juan-e-coriat.html)). [Early literary elaboration.]

### Later ethnographic folklore

*Hacia la tierra sin mal*, associated with Jaime Regan’s research among the Cocama, Chayahuita, and Napo Runa, contains numbered testimonies rather than a single synthetic legend. One testimony reports a man returning around midnight from his garden and hearing the Tunchi’s whistle; other entries describe sounds passing near houses as warnings. This corpus is especially valuable because it preserves named or locally situated narrators rather than presenting an undifferentiated “Amazonian myth” ([digital edition](https://mail.caaap.org.pe/Libros/2022/Hacia-la-tierra-sin-mal22222222222.pdf)). [Folklore; regional testimony.]

A 2017 study of oral tradition distinguishes *tunche* from *maligno*. Its recorded explanations call the Tunche an errant dead person who did not die in peace, travels through the air, may appear as a vague mass or nocturnal bird, and causes *airado/manchari*, especially in children. A light repeated whistle characterizes the Tunche; one heavy whistle belongs to the more violent Maligno. The study also records the Christian interpretation that a Tunche frightens or sickens someone so that it may finish its penance and proceed toward God. Baptism protects vulnerable children. These details are not universal doctrine; they are locally recorded explanations ([“Los seres mitológicos en la tradición oral…”](https://files01.core.ac.uk/download/pdf/236162916.pdf)). [Folklore; Christianized regional variants.]

### Relation to the Southern Arawak ethnographies

Gerald Weiss’s *Campa Cosmology* (1975), Richard Chase Smith’s Yánesha research, Shaver and Dodd’s Nomatsiguenga materials, Santos-Granero’s Yánesha studies, Renard-Casevitz’s Matsigenka work, Peter Gow’s Piro/Yine ethnography, and Brown and Fernández’s Asháninka history are indispensable for reconstructing neighboring ontologies. They document named creators, transformers, solar beings, demons, souls, sorcery agents, and masters of animals.

They do **not**, merely by proximity, authorize translating every such entity as Tunche. One modern Asháninka-language thesis records Spanish-speaking consultants calling *tunche* “the best-known devil” while also trying to relate it to Asháninka *kamari/kamaro* and other native terms. That hesitation is evidence of translation and lexical change, not proof of an original identity ([UNMSM thesis corpus](https://repositorioletrascorpus.unmsm.edu.pe/server/api/core/bitstreams/6bf09711-ddc7-4837-a860-6100098deed8/content)). [Modern contact-language usage.]

Recent scholarship says the Tunche is “widely attributed” to Yine cosmology and describes it as a formless nocturnal entity causing fear, illness, and insomnia. This is a report about contemporary attribution and diffusion; it does not supersede the lack of a securely located nineteenth-century Yine-language text naming the being ([Rosenthal, *The Earth Is Hungry*](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/342629493_The_Earth_Is_Hungry_Amerindian_Worlds_and_the_Perils_of_Gold_Mining_in_the_Peruvian_Amazon)). [Modern scholarly reconstruction.]

### Franciscan chronicles

The chronicles of Biedma, Amich, Izaguirre, Sala, Ordinaire, and the San Antonio mission tradition provide the necessary colonial setting: missionary advance, Indigenous resistance, demonization of non-Christian beings, the Juan Santos Atahualpa rebellion, and the gradual reopening of the central forest to colonization. No securely identified passage in those chronicles was located that uses *tunche/tunchi* by name. They therefore cannot presently be cited as attestations of the being.

That absence matters. It suggests either that the word was not recorded, was confined to registers missionaries ignored, or became regionally prominent later. It also makes claims of an ancient, well-organized “Tunche cult” untenable.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

The Tunche’s stable domain is nocturnal encounter with death in the forest: the trail, riverbank, garden path, roofline, or margin of a settlement. It is characteristically heard before—or instead of—being seen. [Early attestation; folklore.]

Its functions vary:

- announces a death;
- embodies a dead person;
- warns that a loved one has died;
- retraces the dead person’s former paths;
- frightens a solitary traveler;
- causes *susto*, insomnia, illness, delirium, or madness;
- in harsher versions, pursues or kills;
- in Christianized versions, transfers guilt or obtains release from penance;
- near mines, manifests the dangerous agency of extracted land and guarded wealth.

Modern gold-mining testimony describes miners burning tobacco for protection, locates the being near gold deposits, and says answering its birdlike whistle allows it to enter the body. [Contemporary folklore.] This is the strongest located source for the “must not be answered” thread, but it is a twenty-first-century ethnographic recording, not part of the demonstrable 1898 core.

### Whistle and acoustic presence

The sound is variously transcribed as *fin…fin…fin* or simply described as a thin, penetrating whistle. Different traditions assign meaning to:

- distance that collapses unnaturally;
- a whistle first far away and then overhead;
- length, pitch, repetition, or heaviness;
- a light whistle marking a relatively benign dead soul;
- a single thick or forceful whistle marking the Maligno.

Popular formulations that “if it sounds far away, it is near” resemble widespread Latin American acoustic-horror motifs. They should be labeled recent comparative folklore unless tied to a particular Tunche narrator.

### Form

No fixed form is securely attested. Recorded possibilities include:

- invisible presence;
- nocturnal bird;
- vague *bulto*, or indistinct mass;
- white shape;
- skeleton-like being;
- dead person;
- witch or sorcerer;
- relative or loved person;
- demonic humanoid.

The Tunche’s indeterminacy is therefore not missing iconography but a central characteristic of the record.

### Iconography

There is no known pre-Columbian image securely identifiable as the Tunche, no established iconographic canon, and no archaeological attribute by which museum objects can be recognized as depictions of it.

Modern illustrators commonly employ black silhouettes, hooded humanoids, skeletal faces, luminous eyes, birdlike elements, or spectral fog. These images materialize a being whose oral identity was often acoustic and formless. Coriat’s 1944 volume, illustrated by César Calvo de Araujo and García Rosell, is an early landmark in that visual-literary transition. [Modern literary iconography.]

### Objects and protections

Documented or reported protections include:

- refusing to answer the whistle;
- prayer or making the sign of the cross;
- baptism, particularly as protection for children;
- tobacco smoke;
- ritual blowing over a child’s head.

A Cervantes Institute folklore account describes adult men rubbing and blowing over their hands and then over a child’s head while uttering protective formulas to drive the Tunchi away ([Prieto Osorno 2006](https://cvc.cervantes.es/el_rinconete/anteriores/marzo_06/09032006_02.htm)). [Regional folklore.]

No stable sacred color, number, weekday, celestial body, weapon, temple emblem, or sacrificial animal is attested.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations with Other Beings

### Genealogy

No parentage, spouse, offspring, sibling group, or divine dynasty is attested. The Tunche normally originates from a human death rather than divine procreation. Its “genealogy” is mortuary:

1. a person dies;
2. the soul remains, especially after unrest, sin, violence, or incomplete passage;
3. it becomes perceptible through a whistle, bird-call, apparition, or fright;
4. in some versions it must frighten, sicken, or burden another person before it can rest.

This sequence is not universal. The 1898 source requires only “the dead” and an omen; the elaborate transfer-of-sin scenario is later.

### Every documented named-being interaction

The corpus contains strikingly few narrative interactions with other named supernatural beings.

- **The Maligno — contrast, competition, or conflation.** In some Loreto testimony, the Maligno is a violently dead or condemned soul that pursues the less dangerous Tunche; in other tellings “Tunche Maligno” is one being. Source: late twentieth-/early twenty-first-century Loreto oral-tradition collections summarized in the 2017 study. **[Regional variant; Christianized elaboration.]**
- **God — judgment and eventual reception.** A relatively minor sinner becomes a Tunche, frightens or sickens someone, completes penance, and then goes onward toward God. Source: recorded Loreto explanations in the 2017 oral-tradition study. **[Christianized folklore.]**
- **The Devil / *supay* — identification rather than an episode.** Some speakers translate the Tunche as *diablo*, while Amazonian Kichwa compounds *tunchi supay*. No discrete myth was located in which the Tunche meets or fights a separately acting Devil. **[Syncretistic identification.]**
- **Chullachaqui — no documented narrative interaction located.** The beings co-occur in folklore catalogues and modern films but were not found participating in a single traditional episode. **[Absence of evidence.]**
- **Yacuruna — no documented narrative interaction located.** Co-membership in modern “Amazonian mythology” lists is not an interaction. **[Absence of evidence.]**
- **Bufeo Colorado — no documented interaction located.**
- **Sachamama/Yacumama — no documented interaction located.**
- **Ayaymama — no documented interaction located.**
- **Pishtaco — no documented interaction located.** Comparative scholarship may analyze both as fear figures, but this does not create a mythic encounter.
- **Yine, Yánesha, Asháninka, or Matsigenka creator-transformer beings — no securely documented interaction with a being explicitly called Tunche was located.**

Accordingly, the Tunche has no demonstrated pantheon position comparable to a god with allies and rivals.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The death-omen episode

**Earliest form:** Torres Lara, 1898.

A nocturnal bird calls. A servant names it “the Tunche,” explains that it is the dead, and predicts bereavement. November strengthens the association. The captain fears the omen, but it proves false. The story’s importance lies in the compact relation among sound, death, forest, and colonist fear. [Early attestation.]

### The betrayed woman’s return

**Source:** Coriat, *Tunchi: leyendas loretanas* (1944).

A woman deceived by a Lima officer returns as a Tunchi and exacts retribution. This individualizes the diffuse dead soul, supplies human causation and moral logic, and connects Amazonian supernatural vengeance to unequal relations between transient outsiders and local women. [Literary elaboration; not demonstrably a universal oral cycle.]

### The wandering sinner

**Source:** Loreto oral testimony.

The deceased cannot rest because death was troubled, ritual transition incomplete, or sins remain. The soul follows its former paths and frightens the living. In one version it must give its illness or burden to another person; children are vulnerable because their souls are weak. Baptism supplies Christian protection. [Christianized folklore.]

### Tunche and Maligno

A good or relatively harmless Tunche produces a light repeated whistle and mostly frightens. The Maligno produces a heavy or singular whistle, pursues, kills, or drives victims mad. Another version makes the Maligno a condemned soul hunting the good Tunche in order to transfer guilt and escape punishment. Still others merge the two. These are genuinely conflicting classifications and should not be harmonized. [Regional variants.]

### The unanswered whistle

A traveler hears a birdlike whistle. Responding, imitating it, or otherwise acknowledging it invites pursuit, sickness, possession, or invasion of the body. Contemporary miners in Madre de Dios state the rule explicitly and use tobacco for protection. [Contemporary folklore; no evidence located that this exact prohibition was recorded in 1898.]

### Guardian of forest or minerals

Near mines or exploited forests, the Tunche appears as an agent of dangerous land, guarded gold, or the consequences of extraction. Some recent retellings say it punishes those who cut trees or kill animals. The mining association has ethnographic support; the generalized ecological-lawgiver image appears substantially modern. [Contemporary folklore versus modern pedagogical reinvention.]

### Azucena and localized ghost stories

Stories such as “Azucena, the Tunchi of the highway” convert *tunchi* into a class of roadside or accident ghosts. These participate in modern urban-legend structures: an identifiable dead woman, a repeated location, encounters with drivers, and a moralized death history. [Modern folklore.]

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

No temple, shrine, priesthood, festival calendar, idol, oracle, pilgrimage, landholding cult, theophoric naming system, or archaeological assemblage has been securely attributed to the Tunche.

The evidence instead concerns domestic and situational practice:

- interpreting nocturnal calls;
- avoiding response;
- prayer and Christian gestures;
- baptism as prophylaxis against fright;
- tobacco burning;
- therapeutic blowing;
- curing *susto/manchari*;
- narrating encounters to explain illness, fear, insomnia, disappearance, or death.

These practices belong to household religion, curanderismo, mortuary imagination, and everyday forest knowledge, not an institutional cult. Calling them “Tunche worship” would misrepresent the evidence.

No human or animal sacrifice to the Tunche is documented. Search results that join “Tunche” with ancient Peruvian sacrifice concern unrelated Andean archaeological traditions. There is likewise no evidence for temple prostitution, a dedicated initiatory society, or ritual killing performed in its name.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 1898: unrecorded or differently named antecedents

Southern Arawak and neighboring peoples possessed complex doctrines of souls, transformation, predatory spirits, sorcery, and the dead long before the word *tunche* entered the documentary record. Franciscan evangelization also propagated categories such as Devil, condemned soul, sin, purgation, and baptism. But direct continuity from any one precolonial being to the Tunche cannot presently be demonstrated. [Modern scholarly reconstruction.]

### 1898: central-selva contact zone

Torres Lara records the term during state-sponsored penetration of the Pichis–Pachitea route. The person voicing the belief is a subordinate servant; the coastal officer fears but ultimately rejects the omen. This places the Tunche at the interface of Indigenous or local forest knowledge and colonist skepticism.

### 1940s: Loreto literary consolidation

Izquierdo Ríos (1942) and Coriat (1944) make the Tunchi a named subject of Amazonian literature. The being becomes emblematic of Iquitos and Loreto rather than merely an incidental omen. Periodicals, story collections, illustrations, and later school anthologies stabilize its spelling and recognizable motifs.

### Mid- to late twentieth century: ethnographic diversification

Loreto testimony preserves distinctions among ordinary dead souls, Tunche, and Maligno. Christian soteriology becomes explicit: sin, God, baptism, and release from penance. At the same time, ethnographers studying Yine, Asháninka, Yánesha, Matsigenka, Nomatsiguenga, Cocama, and Kichwa communities document different indigenous terminologies. Spanish *tunche* increasingly operates as a regional translation or umbrella word.

### 1980–2000: war and intensified landscapes of violent death

The internal armed conflict devastated Asháninka and Nomatsiguenga communities. Shining Path columns imposed forced recruitment, executions, displacement, captivity, and camp regimes; state and paramilitary violence compounded insecurity. The Peruvian state’s Indigenous database calls the Asháninka one of the peoples most affected and records the creation of armed Indigenous defense organizations after 1989 ([Ministry of Culture, Asháninka profile](https://bdpi.cultura.gob.pe/pueblos/ashaninka)). Detailed research documents recruitment, rape, execution, massacre, and coercive camps ([Villasante, IDEHPUCP](https://idehpucp.pucp.edu.pe/publicaciones/la-guerra-interna-entre-los-ashaninka-y-nomatsiguenga-de-la-selva-central-del-peru-1980-2000/)).

This history enlarged the social field of unburied, disappeared, violently killed, and restless dead in precisely those regions where Tunche discourse circulated. That makes wartime intensification a plausible interpretive context. Nevertheless, no evidence located shows Shining Path formally appropriating the Tunche, adopting it as an emblem, or conducting violence “for” it. Direct claims of such use would exceed the evidence. [Historical context; cautious inference.]

### Twenty-first century: national folklore and environmental horror

The Tunche now circulates beyond Loreto and the central forest through school materials, web bestiaries, tourism, cinema, visual art, and games. The figure is increasingly singular, corporeal, and combatable. This differs from earlier evidence emphasizing sound and formlessness.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Film

Nilo Inga’s *El tunche, misterios de la selva* was filmed in the Pichanaki/Chanchamayo setting and released commercially in 2006. Its medical students encounter a human-appearing forest spirit after ignoring local warnings ([Audiovisual Peru catalogue](https://www.audiovisual.pe/es/titulo/1456/el-tunche-misterios-de-la-selva)). The film helped shift the Tunche from oral acoustic presence to visible horror antagonist. [Modern retelling.]

Paco Bardales and Martin Casapía’s *Maligno* (2016), filmed in Iquitos, presents an ancient, powerful being released from confinement in a hospital. Bardales called it the “gran tunche negro” that collects souls for pleasure ([TV Perú interview](https://tvperu.gob.pe/noticias/miscelanea/paco-bardales-cuenta-detalles-de-la-pelicula-maligno); [*El Peruano*](https://elperuano.pe/noticia/43753-terror-de-la-selva)). Its imprisonment, black coloration, hospital mythology, and recreational soul-collecting belong to cinematic invention rather than the 1898 record. [Modern invention founded on folklore.]

### Video games

LEAP Game Studios’ *Tunche*, released on 2 November 2021, is a hand-drawn action roguelike set in an Amazon-inspired jungle ([Steam release page](https://store.steampowered.com/app/887450/_/?l=english); [Xbox announcement](https://news.xbox.com/en-us/2021/11/02/tunche-is-now-available-for-xbox-one-and-xbox-series-xs/)). Its characters, combat systems, bosses, and conquerable titular creature translate the legend into global genre conventions. Marketing commonly associates it with Yine mythology, but the game is creative adaptation, not an ethnographic edition. [Modern reinvention.]

### Literature and visual art

From Izquierdo Ríos and Coriat onward, Amazonian writers have used the Tunchi to mark regional voice, forest knowledge, and the permeability of death. A 2023 collection titled *Tunchi* assembled twelve stories from the “Amazonía ancestral” writing competition, each accompanied by an illustration. Its multiple individualized Tunchis—guardians, seducers, victims, and hunters—show that contemporary culture treats the term as a flexible story-generator rather than a fixed canon ([Lupuna cultural notice](https://www.lupuna.org/2023/07/tunchi-resultados-de-amazonia-ancestral.html)). [Modern literary elaboration.]

### Tourism and commerce

Businesses, tours, beaches, publications, and cultural programs use *Tunche/Tunchi* as a regional Amazonian brand. Such naming demonstrates familiarity and identity value, not cult continuity.

### Occultism, neopaganism, and New Age use

The dedicated search found no documented Renaissance hermetic, Theosophical, Golden Dawn, ceremonial-magical, chaos-magical, or established neopagan Tunche tradition. Nor was a reconstructionist church, continuous priesthood, or measurable community of Tunche worship located.

Individual internet invocations or generic “Amazonian shamanism” presentations may exist, but they do not constitute documented historical traditions. Claims that the Tunche has an ancient esoteric sigil, planetary correspondence, formal invocation, or pre-Columbian temple ritual should be classified as **modern invention unless independently sourced**.

### Political appropriation

No nationalist, party, military, Shining Path, or extremist organization was found to have adopted the Tunche as an official symbol. Its strongest political dimension is indirect: it embodies landscapes marked by colonization, extractive labor, violent death, unequal knowledge, and the fear of outsiders in the forest.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is the Tunche Indigenous, mestizo, Christianized, or all three?

The evidence favors historical layering.

- The dangerous dead, bird omens, forest agency, and soul illness fit broader Indigenous Amazonian ontologies.
- *Alma en pena*, sin, God, Devil, November as month of the dead, and baptism are unmistakably Catholic.
- Spanish-language literature and urban Iquitos folklore reorganized these elements into a widely recognizable regional figure.

The appropriate conclusion is neither “purely Indigenous” nor “European import,” but a contact-zone formation whose components require individual dating.

### Is it specifically Yine?

Modern scholarship frequently attributes the Tunche to Yine cosmology, and the 2021 game amplified that association. Yet the earliest located text does not identify its speaker as Yine, and the word appears broadly in Loreto, central-selva, Kichwa, and mestizo contexts. A Yine association may preserve a genuine local tradition, but “the Yine god Tunche” overstates the source base. [Contested modern attribution.]

### Is it the Devil?

Some speakers explicitly say *diablo* or *maligno*. Others distinguish the Tunche from the Maligno and make the former merely frightening. The contradiction reflects Christian translation and regional taxonomy; it cannot be resolved by choosing one definition.

### Bird or soul?

The earliest evidence permits both: the heard bird is “the Tunche,” but the term is glossed as “the dead.” Izquierdo Ríos likewise pairs the soul with the tunchi bird’s fatal song. Later narratives may treat the bird as embodiment, messenger, imitation, or merely the sound mistaken for the spirit. No zoological species is canonically established.

### Ancient guardian of nature?

The mining evidence supports a recent association with dangerous extraction zones and protected mineral wealth. A universal environmental guardian who punishes deforestation is not present in the 1898 attestation and appears largely in contemporary educational or ecological retellings. It is best labeled modern moralization, potentially built from older Amazonian master-of-animals concepts.

### State of the evidence

The evidence is unusually dispersed:

- nineteenth-century travel literature;
- regional periodicals such as *Trocha*;
- rare Iquitos story collections;
- oral-history and ethnographic monographs;
- educational folklore compilations;
- recent film and game archives.

The Biblioteca Amazónica’s digital repository contains issues of the Iquitos publication *Tunchi* and related regional periodicals ([Biblioteca Amazónica repository](https://repositorio.bibliotecaamazonica.pe/)). IIAP’s bibliography is essential for identifying otherwise difficult-to-find publications. The major evidentiary weakness is the scarcity of fully searchable early regional print and the tendency of popular summaries to erase narrator, place, date, and linguistic setting.

## 10. Chronology

| Date | Event in the documentary history |
|---|---|
| Seventeenth–nineteenth centuries | Franciscan missions record and suppress Indigenous religions of the central forest, introducing Christian categories of Devil, sin, soul, and baptism. No securely located *tunche* passage has been identified. |
| 1742–c. 1756 | Juan Santos Atahualpa’s rebellion expels or restricts mission-colonial power across much of the central forest. Important historical background, but no documented Tunche role has been found. |
| 1880s–1890s | Colonization and state exploration intensify in Chanchamayo, Pichis, Pachitea, and adjacent regions. |
| **1898** | Torres Lara publishes *Las mariposas blancas*: bird-call named “el tunche,” glossed as “the dead,” interpreted as a death omen. Earliest secure attestation located. |
| 1911–1912 | An Iquitos periodical titled *El Tunchi* is documented. Its title shows early urban familiarity with the term, though it does not by itself define the being. |
| **1942** | Francisco Izquierdo Ríos publishes “El Tunchi” in *Trocha* 1.5, defining it as deceased person, soul-shadow, or spirit; the bird-song announces death. |
| **1944** | Juan E. Coriat publishes *Tunchi: leyendas loretanas*, including the avenging soul of a deceived woman. |
| 1953 | Pérez Marcio’s *Los hijos de la selva* belongs to the mid-century corpus popularizing selva lore; any Tunche details require confirmation from the text rather than bibliographic association alone. |
| 1970s | Major ethnographies of Asháninka/Campa, Yánesha, and neighboring peoples systematize Indigenous cosmologies under their own terminology; they provide comparative context rather than automatic Tunche attestations. |
| 1980–2000 | Internal armed conflict produces mass death, disappearance, displacement, and captivity in the central forest. No official Shining Path cultic use of the Tunche is documented. |
| 1990s–2000s | Loreto ethnographic publications record distinctions among Tunche, Maligno, dead souls, *susto*, and Christian protection. |
| **2006** | *El tunche, misterios de la selva* gives the being a visible cinematic horror form in Pichanaki. |
| **2016** | *Maligno* presents the “Tunche Negro” as an ancient imprisoned soul-collector in Iquitos horror cinema. |
| **2017** | Published oral-tradition analysis records the Tunche/Maligno distinction, *airado/manchari*, baptism, and penitential-soul explanations. |
| **2020** | Contemporary mining ethnography describes the Tunche near gold extraction, answering the whistle as dangerous, and tobacco as protection. |
| **2021** | LEAP Game Studios releases *Tunche*, turning the being into the center of an international action game associated in marketing with Yine mythology. |
| **2023** | The *Tunchi* “Amazonía ancestral” story collection demonstrates continuing regional literary reinvention. |
| **2026** | The figure remains active in oral narration, film studies, regional identity, digital folklore, tourism, education, and commercial entertainment; no institutional cult or securely demonstrated continuous worship is documented. |

## 11. Sources and Conclusions

The secure history begins modestly: a bird-call, “the dead,” and a frightened traveler in 1898. During the twentieth century the term became a Loreto literary emblem and a broad vernacular category for wandering souls. Oral narrators then supplied divergent moral and theological structures: benign versus malignant whistles, unfinished death, transferable guilt, baptismal protection, demonic identification, and bodily invasion after answering the call. Contemporary mining, film, art, and games have further materialized and politicized the figure.

The dossier’s principal negative findings are as important as its positive ones:

- no pre-Columbian depiction securely represents the Tunche;
- no colonial chronicle passage naming it was securely located;
- no temple, priesthood, sacrifice, festival, genealogy, or institutional cult is documented;
- no stable interaction with Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, Bufeo, or other catalogued Amazonian beings is attested;
- “Southern Arawak god” is too rigid a classification;
- Yine attribution is important but later and not exclusive;
- forest-guardian imagery is substantially modern;
- no formal occult, neopagan, extremist, or Shining Path appropriation was established.

The fullest defensible classification is therefore: **a historically layered Peruvian-Amazonian category of ominous dead and nocturnal forest presence, transmitted across Indigenous, Christian, mestizo, literary, and commercial settings without ever acquiring a single canonical identity.**
