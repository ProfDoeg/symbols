# Tasorentsi: Research Dossier

**Class:** In Asháninka sources, *tasorentsi* is primarily a class/category of exceptionally powerful transforming beings—often translated “gods,” “divinities,” or “benevolent spirits”—whose characteristic power is transformation by blowing. In some regional and Christianized usage, **Tasorentsi** is singular: the supreme creator, Pavá/the Sun, or the Christian God. It is also an honorific applied to unusually powerful human world-transformers. These meanings coexist and must not be collapsed.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest securely identifiable evidence for the **name** located in this research is twentieth-century ethnographic and linguistic documentation. Fernando Torres López recorded a singular creator *tasorentsi* in field research published in 1964/1966; Gerald Weiss collected Asháninka/Campa cosmological material in 1961–64 and first published his synthesis in 1972, with the full monograph in 1975. Mary Ruth Wise’s 1972 text likewise treated Tasorentsi as a named character. Seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Franciscan reports record related beliefs—a heavenly lord, Sun and Moon beings, demons, immortality, prophecy, and world renewal—but no colonial occurrence of the word *Tasorentsi* has been securely demonstrated here. Consequently, claims that the name itself is documented in the seventeenth or eighteenth century remain **unestablished**.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### The central identification problem

**[Secure ethnographic attestation, 1961–75]** Gerald Weiss defined *tasórentsi* as the substantive form of a verb meaning “to blow.” It designates the highest and most powerful supernatural beings, capable of transforming one thing into another with a puff of breath. Weiss’s principal examples are Avíreri, Pachákama, Inkanítari, Kashíri, Pává, and Inka. A second term is *pinkátsori*, “ruler,” literally “one who is feared.” Weiss also found singular usage: speakers could use *Tasórentsi* for “God,” normally meaning the Sun under its theological name Pává, “our Father.” His formulation remains the indispensable starting point, but it reflects particular communities and fieldwork conducted in 1961–64, not an immutable Asháninka creed. [Weiss, *Campa Cosmology*](https://acervo.socioambiental.org/sites/default/files/documents/KPD00017.pdf)

**[Modern scholarly correction/debate]** Enrique Rojas Zolezzi argues that the apparent singular deity may partly result from linguistic and missionary misunderstanding. Asháninka normally leaves number unmarked; accordingly, *tasorentsi* may mean either one powerful being or several. Rojas found no independent narrative cycle about a creator whose personal name is Tasorentsi. He interpreted Mary Ruth Wise’s “Tasorentsi” character as Avíreri/Navireri bearing a class-title, and the creation of the present world as the accumulated transformations of multiple beings in the primordial era *páirani*, not creation *ex nihilo*. He expressly criticizes the equation of *tasorentsi* with an exclusively benevolent category: even powerful malevolent *kamari* can, in some usages, be *tasorentsi*. [Rojas Zolezzi, “Origen y clasificación de las plantas”](https://dialnet.unirioja.es/descarga/articulo/5042072.pdf)

These interpretations are not mutually exclusive in every locality. The evidence supports at least four usages:

1. **Class noun:** transforming divinities or extraordinarily powerful spirits.
2. **Quality/title:** supernatural power, especially world-changing power manifested through breath.
3. **Singular divine title:** the foremost creator, frequently Pavá/the Sun.
4. **Christian lexical equivalent:** “God,” and sometimes, in contemporary conversion discourse, the Holy Spirit.

### Documented forms

**[Secure linguistic/ethnographic attestation]**

- **Tasorentsi / Tasórentsi** — common Asháninka spelling; singular and number-neutral.
- **Tasórenci / Tasorenci** — Portuguese/Brazilian orthographic rendering.
- **Tasorintsi / Tasorintzi** — dialectal or older transcription.
- **Tajorentzi** — Ashéninka spelling reported by Peru’s official Indigenous Peoples Database.
- **Tosorintsi** — Nomatsiguenga cognate/form.
- **Tasulinchi, Tasormtsi, Tahormasi** — imperfect outsider transcriptions associated especially with the twentieth-century chief José Carlos Amaringo Chico; these are not separate gods.
- **Amachénga / amatsenka / amatxenka** — “our people,” “countrymen”; benevolent human-shaped celestial beings, overlapping contextually with *tasorentsi*.
- **Maninkari** — “hidden ones”; good or ancestral spirits. Sources sometimes align or overlap them with *tasorentsi*, but they are not universally synonymous.
- **Pinkátsori / pinkatsari** — ruler, chief, feared or respected one; a political-religious title rather than a proper divine name.
- **Itomi Pavá / itome Pawa** — “son/child of Pavá,” applied to celestial benevolent beings and, in prophetic movements, their human emissaries.

### Etymology

**[Secure modern linguistic reconstruction]** The usual derivation is from **tasonk-/tasonkaantsi**, “to blow,” with nominalizing morphology commonly analyzed as *-re-* plus nonpossessed nominal *-ntsi*. Thus the literal sense is approximately “one characterized by blowing,” “blower,” or “blowing/transforming power.” The exact morphological segmentation varies between authors and dialect descriptions.

The meaning is not merely meteorological. In mythic speech, blowing is an efficient act: a being handles, addresses, or blows upon a person or object and turns it into an animal, plant, rock, river, celestial body, or another kind of person.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Santos-Granero renders the category as “all-powerful blowers/world transformers,” while stressing that transformation, not creation from nothing, is the more exact sense.

**[Missionary semantic overlay]** David Payne’s dictionary definition—“legendary person, son of Pavá, of extraordinary powers,” and “power of God or spiritual beings, glory”—preserves the blow/power connection but adds Christian theological vocabulary. Rojas Zolezzi specifically regards “glory” as a Judeo-Christian semantic intrusion rather than an independently demonstrated premission concept.

### Pavá, the Sun, and the Christian God

**[Secure ethnographic attestation]** In Weiss’s account, **Pává** is the foremost *tasorentsi*, the Sun in theological language, source of heat, light, useful plants, and cosmic order. In ordinary descriptive language the Sun may be **Katsirinkáiteri**, “the hot/burning one,” or **Oriatsiri/Oriáa**, “the one who illuminates.” Festival dancing with panpipes honors Pavá.

**[Regional variation]** Some speakers say Tasorentsi *is* Pavá; others distinguish the singular Tasorentsi from Pavá; still others call Tasorentsi a child of Pavá. Modern interviews preserve all three positions. They cannot be reduced to one genealogy.

**[Syncretistic identification, twentieth century onward]** Adventist, Catholic, and evangelical teaching encouraged *Tasorentsi* and *Pavá* as translations for the biblical God. One interviewee summarized the converted position: formerly people spoke of *Paba Oriatsiri* and *Pava Kashiri*, but evangelists taught that one heavenly Father created everything. Another identified Tasorentsi with the Father, Son, and Spirit. Rojas cautions that it is often impossible to decide whether a statement equating Pavá, Tasorentsi, and the Christian God represents an older interpretation, an Indigenous appropriation of Christianity, or repetition of missionary doctrine. [UNMSM linguistic study](https://repositorioletrascorpus.unmsm.edu.pe/server/api/core/bitstreams/6bf09711-ddc7-4837-a860-6100098deed8/content)

**[Contemporary regional formulation]** Among some Apiwtxa Asháninka in Brazil, an ethnographer reports the Christian mapping **Pawa = God** and **Tasorentsi = Holy Spirit** among converts, while noting that Apiwtxa itself is not collectively Christian. This is a recent local theology, not an ancient universal equation. [Comandulli thesis](https://discovery-pp.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/10126497/7/Comandulli_10126497_Thesis_id_redacted.pdf)

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Colonial evidence: relevant but not a named attestation

**[Early-modern colonial report, 1651]** Fernando de Contreras’s report, known through later historical discussion, says that the people called *Campa* did not worship Sun, Moon, or idols but believed in a lord in heaven and in a hell and demon called *kamagari*. This resembles later missionary descriptions but is already framed through Christian categories. It neither records *Tasorentsi* nor proves a pre-Christian high-god doctrine. The possibility that interpreters translated local categories into “Lord,” “hell,” and “devil” must remain open. [UNMSM discussion of Contreras/Varese](https://repositorioletrascorpus.unmsm.edu.pe/server/api/core/bitstreams/6bf09711-ddc7-4837-a860-6100098deed8/content)

**[Colonial corpus, 1682–1750]** Biedma, San Antonio, and related Franciscan reports document missions, Indigenous resistance, Juan Santos Atahualpa’s rebellion, prophecy, and missionary interpretations of native religion. They are essential for the contact history but, in the material inspected, do not supply a secure written occurrence of *Tasorentsi*. The same caution applies to Amich’s later compilation of mission history.

### Ethnographic corpus

**[Secure attestation, fieldwork 1961–64; publication 1972/1975]** Gerald Weiss’s *Campa Cosmology* is the first full systematic corpus. It records:

- the class *tasorentsi*;
- transformation by breath;
- the singular use for Pavá/the Sun;
- Avíreri’s transformation cycle;
- Pachákama, Inkanítari, Kashíri, Pává, and Inka;
- multilayered worlds above and below the human earth;
- shamans’ relations with spirit beings;
- *kamari* and their ruler Korioshipíri;
- death, souls, festivals, and cosmic catastrophe.

The work is based on oral testimony, not pre-Columbian manuscripts; its “texts” are twentieth-century performances whose antiquity must be inferred comparatively.

**[Published oral narrative, 1972]** Mary Ruth Wise published a story in which she treated Tasorentsi as a personal name. Rojas Zolezzi’s later linguistic reading identifies the actor as Avíreri/Navireri with *tasorentsi* functioning as a description or title.

**[Ethnographic/linguistic elaboration, 1975–94]** Payne, Shaver, Fernández, and Rojas Zolezzi documented further variants. Fernández’s informant answered “Who made the world?” with “Tasorentsi made it with one breath,” but the larger narrative corpus depicts gradual ordering through several transforming beings. This is direct evidence for a singular formulation, not proof that it was universal or historically prior.

**[Official contemporary synthesis]** Peru’s Indigenous Peoples Database places Tasorentsi in **Jenókiite**, the upper world, with Sun, Moon, and Stars; human beings inhabit **Kamavéni**, “land of death”; **Sharingaveni** is a lower place of suffering. Its entry appropriately notes that Weiss found local and generational variation rather than one unified creation narrative. [Peruvian Ministry of Culture](https://bdpi.cultura.gob.pe/pueblos/ashaninka)

### Neighboring Southern Arawak traditions

**[Comparative evidence, not interchangeable identity]**

- **Nomatsiguenga:** Tosorintsi is placed above Paba in some accounts and sometimes identified with Mabireri. Shaver lists Manchacori, Paba, Mabireri, and Nijorontsi among ancestral miracle-workers designated *tosorintsi*. This supports a cognate transformer category but not identity with every Asháninka formulation.
- **Yanesha:** Richard Chase Smith’s *Deliverance from Chaos for a Song* documents a related but independently structured Yanesha cosmology. Its solar Yompor Ror and creator-transformer traditions provide comparative Southern Arawak evidence, not alternate names of Tasorentsi.
- **Matsigenka:** Renard-Casevitz documents cognate transformer and creator figures, including Tasorintsi-type terminology in comparative literature. These cannot simply be inserted into Asháninka genealogy.
- **Yine/Piro:** Peter Gow’s work shows how myths change through historical relations, missionization, slavery, and intermarriage. The Yine elements in José Tasorentsi’s “Celestial River Song” are historical multilingual mediation, not evidence of a pan-Arawak god with a fixed biography.
- **Loreto and Ucayali folklore:** Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, and Pishtaco cycles belong to wider Amazonian and mestizo folklore. No primary source located documents Tasorentsi interacting with these beings. Regan, Galeano, Cabrejos, Chirif, Dean, Weiss, and Ansión are therefore contextual rather than direct Tasorentsi witnesses.

### Christian textual corpus

**[Syncretistic translation, twentieth–twenty-first centuries]** Asháninka New Testament translations use *tasorentsi* in Christian divine vocabulary. The 2008 Wycliffe edition is titled *Irineane tasorentsi oquenquetsatacotaqueri Avincatsarite Jesoquirishito*. A 2026 commercial edition styles the deity *Tasorentsi Dios*. These editions demonstrate an established Christian lexical equation; they do not establish that Christian Trinity doctrine was present before missionization. [2008 Asháninka New Testament](https://www.divinerevelations.info/bible7/ash%C3%A1ninka_pe_cni_ash%C3%A1ninka_new_testament/cnint_a4.pdf)

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain and powers

**[Secure ethnographic attestation]**

Tasorentsi’s defining domain is **ontological transformation**: changing what kind of being something is. The class collectively accounts for the differentiation of a previously human or undifferentiated primordial world into humans, animals, plants, landscape forms, weather, and celestial phenomena.

The characteristic powers include:

- transforming by blowing;
- creating or arranging portions of the inhabited cosmos;
- changing persons into species or landscape features;
- bestowing useful plants and technologies;
- regulating cosmic levels and relations;
- sending benevolent spirit helpers;
- in singular creator formulations, sustaining life and holding or ordering the world.

**[Modern local systematization]** A Satipo regional report says the flat, table-like world is suspended by great cords held by Tasorentsi. This is a documented contemporary formulation, not securely traceable to Weiss’s earliest corpus. [IIAP Satipo study](https://www.iiap.gob.pe/Archivos/publicaciones/Publicacion_1499.pdf)

**[Modern educational tradition]** Asháninka school material recounts Tasorentsi making fire available for people. Such publications show living transmission and pedagogical standardization; they should not automatically be projected into a prehistoric canon. [Peruvian education text](https://umc.minedu.gob.pe/wp-content/uploads/2019/09/lectura-favorita-ashaninka-2019.pdf)

### Physical form

**[Absence of fixed iconography]** No canonical anthropomorphic image, cult statue, mask, painted panel, temple relief, or securely identified pre-Columbian representation of Tasorentsi was located. The divinities are described verbally and encountered in stories, dreams, visions, songs, and shamanic experience.

The benevolent *amachénga* may appear as radiant human beings or disguised outsiders. This is a mode of manifestation, not a standardized portrait of the singular creator.

### Symbols and associations

**[Secure or well-supported]**

- **Breath/blowing:** defining emblematic action.
- **Sun:** when Tasorentsi is identified with Pavá.
- **Height/upper worlds:** residence of principal benevolent beings.
- **Panpipes, song, and dance:** Pavá is honored at festivals; prophetic leaders employ songs.
- **Kamarampi/ayahuasca and tobacco:** means through which shamans engage the spirit world generally, though no exclusive Tasorentsi sacrament is demonstrated.
- **Light, heat, crops, cosmic order:** attributes of Pavá that enter singular Tasorentsi descriptions.

**[Not established]** No traditional canonical color, number, weekday, sacred animal, exclusive plant, planetary identification beyond the solar equation, or standardized weapon is securely attested. Modern deity websites that supply tidy “domains,” “enemies,” and genealogical fields exceed the documentary record.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical structures

**[Weiss’s regional formulation]** Pává is the foremost of the *tasorentsi* and father/source of creatures. Other transformer divinities occupy the same highest class; this is hierarchy rather than a complete family tree.

**[Regional variant]** Tasorentsi is sometimes said to be Pavá himself.

**[Regional variant]** Tasorentsi is sometimes said to be a son of Pavá or a legendary son endowed with extraordinary powers.

**[Modern Christianized variant]** Tasorentsi is the one creator and Father; in another formulation Tasorentsi is associated with the Trinity or specifically the Holy Spirit.

**[Recent published oral tradition]** A RENIEC-sponsored Asháninka naming volume says Oitasatsi and Tasorentsi created and married the male and female lunar beings Kashiri Ash and Kashiri Aah, whose children are four lunar phases. This is a valuable contemporary narrative, but it is absent from Weiss’s older corpus and may contain school-era astronomical systematization: the wedding guests are represented as constellations with modern Spanish names such as dragon, lion, wolf, and bear. It must therefore be labeled a recent documented formulation, not an ancient canonical cosmogony. [*Tesoro de nombres asháninkas*](https://cdn.www.gob.pe/uploads/document/file/1669923/TESORO_NOMBRES_ASHANINKA.pdf.pdf)

### Every documented divine interaction located

Because *tasorentsi* is often a class-title, the following separates interactions by a singular Tasorentsi from membership or identity claims.

- **Pavá/Pawa — identity, hierarchy, or parentage.** Tasorentsi is variously Pavá, the class headed by Pavá, or a child/emissary of Pavá. Weiss 1972/1975; Payne; Rojas Zolezzi; contemporary Amônia traditions. **Tier:** securely attested ethnographic material with regional conflict.
- **Avíreri/Navireri/Mabireri — class membership or mistaken personal naming.** Avíreri is one of Weiss’s chief *tasorentsi*; Rojas argues that a published “Tasorentsi” story is actually Avíreri’s transformation cycle. **Tier:** securely attested; later scholarly correction.
- **Pachákama/Pachakamáite — joint membership.** Listed by Weiss among the principal *tasorentsi*; no discrete meeting or dialogue with a separate singular Tasorentsi was located. **Tier:** securely attested taxonomy; interaction absent.
- **Inkanítari — joint membership.** Rain deity listed among principal *tasorentsi*; no discrete singular interaction located. **Tier:** securely attested taxonomy.
- **Kashíri — joint membership; disputed creator relation.** Weiss lists Moon among principal *tasorentsi*. Recent naming traditions say Tasorentsi and Oitasatsi create/marry lunar beings. **Tier:** early ethnographic taxonomy plus recent regional elaboration.
- **Inka — joint membership.** Weiss includes Inka among the principal *tasorentsi*, reflecting the absorption of Andean political-sacred power into Asháninka cosmology. No discrete encounter with singular Tasorentsi was located. **Tier:** securely attested ethnographic syncretism.
- **Oitasatsi — co-creator and co-officiant.** Together they create Kashiri Ash and Kashiri Aah, convene the divinities, and perform the marriage. RENIEC naming collection, published 2019–20. **Tier:** recent documented oral/pedagogical tradition.
- **Kashiri Ash and Kashiri Aah — creators/arrangers of marriage.** Tasorentsi and Oitasatsi create and marry them. Same source. **Tier:** recent documented tradition.
- **Asha, Ashe, Ashi, Asho — grandparental/creator relation.** Four lunar-phase children are named according to the creators’ plan. Same source. **Tier:** recent systematization.
- **Kamari — opposition is common in dualistic summaries; strict taxonomic opposition is disputed.** Brown and Fernández portray benevolent *tasorentsi/amachénga* against demonic *kamari*; Rojas notes that sufficiently powerful malevolent beings may themselves be called *tasorentsi*. No narrative duel with singular Tasorentsi was securely located. **Tier:** ethnographic systematization with scholarly disagreement.
- **Korioshipíri — cosmological opposition only.** Weiss identifies him as ruler of demons in Sharingavéni. No personal encounter with Tasorentsi is recorded. **Tier:** securely attested cosmological relation; direct interaction absent.
- **Kientibakori — reported contest/descent.** Later secondary discussions refer to Tasorentsi descending into Kientibakori’s realm for a duel. The underlying complete primary narrative was not recovered in the materials inspected, so details and dating remain **not independently established**. **Tier:** regional narrative reported secondarily.
- **Christian God — translation and fusion.** Tasorentsi becomes a vernacular name/title for the biblical God. **Tier:** nineteenth–twenty-first-century missionary syncretism.
- **Holy Spirit — local Christian equation.** Reported among some converted Brazilian Asháninka. **Tier:** contemporary regional syncretism.
- **Jesus Christ — textual Christian relation.** Modern New Testament translations present Jesus as Lord/Son in texts whose divine vocabulary uses *tasorentsi*. **Tier:** missionary translation, not precontact myth.
- **Tzijo — no interaction established.** Ashéninka tradition says Tzijo, a vulture-form god, aids the last shaman against *wiracocha* enemies. Tasorentsi does not appear in the episode as summarized. **Tier:** regional Ashéninka narrative; absence noted.
- **Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, Pishtaco — no documented interaction located.** Their cycles belong to other Indigenous or mestizo Amazonian corpora. **Tier:** absence of evidence.
- **Saint Christopher and the Christ Child — no direct interaction with Tasorentsi.** Santos-Granero traces Southern Arawak child-sorcery traditions to transformed colonial imagery of Christopher and the Child; this is a history of missionary mimesis, not a Tasorentsi episode. **Tier:** modern scholarly reconstruction. [Santos-Granero article](https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/ec1a0175-8552-44f3-a358-bb24cb826716/content)

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The primordial transformations

**[Secure ethnographic core]** In *páirani*, “long ago/primordial time,” humans, animals, plants, and topographic forms had not yet acquired their present distinctions. Powerful beings transformed preexisting persons or materials, characteristically by blowing. Conduct, kinship incidents, curiosity, aggression, error, or encounters with a transformer could determine the resulting species or form. The present forest is therefore sedimented social history: animals and plants were formerly persons, and morphology records what happened to them.

**[Interpretive dispute]** Torres López and Fernández support a singular creator: “Tasorentsi made it with one breath.” Weiss and Rojas’s larger corpora instead show multiple world-transformers and no creation from nothing. The safest reconstruction is that both singular summaries and plural narrative performances were current in the twentieth century.

### Avíreri’s transformations

**[Secure ethnographic cycle]** Avíreri/Navireri travels through the world with a child or grandchild. Encounters prompt questions and transformations; persons become animals, plants, and landscape features. The cycle explains the differentiated environment.

**[Textual correction]** Mary Ruth Wise’s edition called the transformer Tasorentsi; Rojas argues that *tasorentsi* there is a class-description and the personal actor is Avíreri. This is the clearest case in which grammar and missionary editorial expectations may have created a singular god-character.

### Pavá and cosmic beneficence

**[Secure ethnographic material]** Pavá, the solar Father and foremost *tasorentsi*, supplies warmth, light, useful products, and order. At festivals people dance with panpipes in gratitude. This is not a continuous adventure narrative but a complex of prayers, dances, cosmological propositions, and origin accounts.

**[Regional variant]** Elsewhere the visible Sun is born from Kashiri/Moon and a human woman. This conflicts with a simple “Pavá created everything and is literally the Sun” genealogy. Sources may distinguish the visible luminary, its divine person, and theological titles differently.

### World destruction and renewal

**[Attested eschatological tradition]** The present world is not permanently secure. Asháninka traditions describe earlier cataclysmic change and anticipate renewed transformation. Benevolent emissaries may announce the end of the current order, restore access to the celestial realm, or promise immortality.

**[Historical development]** Mission preaching about resurrection and the Second Coming provided a powerful new vocabulary for an existing concern with immortality, world renewal, and broken contact with celestial beings. Adventist eschatology and Asháninka transformation traditions became mutually translatable without becoming identical.

### The Oitasatsi–Tasorentsi lunar wedding

**[Recent regional elaboration]** Oitasatsi and Tasorentsi create the male and female Kashiri beings, convene celestial beings at Jananeria near Orion, marry the pair, and name their four children after lunar phases. It is a fully documented modern story, but its constellation list and its absence from older corpora counsel against dating it to antiquity.

### Human Tasorentsi and the Celestial River

**[Historical prophetic cycle, c. 1912–49]** José Carlos Amaringo Chico became known as Tasorentsi, a shaman-chief and world transformer. His movement promised liberation from slavery and debt, Indigenous unity, and recovery of immortality. A remembered “Celestial River Song,” recorded from Carlos Pérez Shuma by Jeremy Narby in the 1980s, mixes or appears to mix Asháninka, Yine, and perhaps other linguistic material. It describes Indigenous peoples returning to immortality and rejoining a celestial order.

Linguists consulted by Santos-Granero disagreed about the lyrics: some heard Asháninka and Yine elements; others found most of it unintelligible; ethnomusicologist Bernd Brabec de Mori found no known Asháninka musical form; Peter Gow recognized a few Yine words but said it was not a Yine text. The uncertainty is part of the evidence: the singer learned it as a child around 1948–49, decades before recording. [Santos-Granero, *Slavery and Utopia*](https://dokumen.pub/slavery-and-utopia-the-wars-and-dreams-of-an-amazonian-world-transformer-9781477317150.html)

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Temples and images

**[Negative finding]** No dedicated temple, permanent sanctuary, priestly estate, cult statue, or archaeological building securely attributable to Tasorentsi has been documented. Applying a Mediterranean temple-cult model would misrepresent the evidence.

### Ritual specialists

**[Secure ethnographic attestation]** The **sheripiari**, broadly “shaman/healer,” mediates with upper-world beings through disciplined knowledge, songs, tobacco, and psychoactive preparations, especially *kamarampi*. Spirit spouses and helpers provide songs, healing power, and information. Ritual authority is personal and relational rather than a centralized Tasorentsi priesthood.

A **satantinkari** heals with steam baths and may avoid hallucinogens. Mission observers noted such practice continuing within Christian settlements because practitioners did not necessarily regard it as manipulation of spirits.

### Prayer, dance, song, and offerings

**[Secure ethnographic material]**

- Panpipe dancing honors Pavá.
- Communal manioc beer (*masato*) accompanies festivals and many rites.
- Songs mediate with or manifest spirit power.
- Tobacco and psychoactive plants facilitate shamanic relations.
- Hunters negotiate morally with masters/owners of animals, especially in Ashéninka regional traditions.

No standardized animal or human sacrifice to Tasorentsi was located.

### Human killing and the sacrifice question

**[Contested-material finding]** There is **no secure evidence of human sacrifice offered to Tasorentsi**. Southern Arawak communities did, however, kill children accused of sorcery. Santos-Granero shows that reports multiplied during epidemics, forced displacement, slavery, and missionary/colonial pressure. The executions sometimes occurred during nocturnal feasts with large quantities of manioc beer. Sources conflict on whether participants celebrated the death of the alleged child-witch or mourned both the accused child and the original victim. The practice is better documented as punitive or cleansing violence within a theory of sorcery than as sacrifice to a deity.

Santos-Granero argues that the mythology of dangerous child sorcerers developed partly through colonial appropriation of images and stories of Saint Christopher carrying the Christ Child, transformed into an account of a cannibal giant and deadly child. This is a historically argued genesis, not a claim that one image mechanically “caused” every killing. [Spanish version](https://repository.si.edu/bitstreams/ebdceac5-06db-434d-943c-1ecaeb31a53e/download)

### Temple prostitution and cult violence

**[Negative finding]** No evidence was located for temple prostitution, sexual service to Tasorentsi, institutional human sacrifice, or a Tasorentsi priesthood conducting cult wars. These motifs should not be imported from unrelated traditions.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before sustained European contact

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Comparative Southern Arawak evidence makes a precolonial background of transformer beings, animate landscape, shamanic mediation, and celestial powers plausible. Yet no precontact inscription, manuscript, or securely identifiable image names Tasorentsi. Exact antiquity remains unprovable.

### 1570–1742: missions, translation, and resistance

Spanish expeditions reached Asháninka regions by the late sixteenth century; formal Franciscan mission entry is dated to 1635. Missionaries founded unstable settlements, baptized residents, and translated local categories into Christian “God,” “devil,” “heaven,” and “hell.” Epidemics repeatedly accompanied concentration at missions.

In 1742 Juan Santos Atahualpa initiated a multiethnic anti-colonial movement in the Gran Pajonal and Selva Central. Franciscan sources cast him as an impostor, heretic, and enemy of God; later historians variously made him an Inca nationalist, Christian millenarian, Indigenous messiah, or strategic political leader. There is no secure contemporary evidence that his personal name was Tasorentsi, although later Asháninka prophetic leadership was interpreted through the *itomi Pavá/tasorentsi* complex.

### 1742–late nineteenth century: mission retreat and renewed incursion

The rebellion expelled or restricted missions across much of the central forest. Later Franciscan return, colonization, and the Cerro de la Sal economy renewed pressure. Chronicles by Amich, Izaguirre, Sala, Navarro, Ordinaire, and others preserve valuable observations but frequently impose “idolatry,” “superstition,” and “devil” as explanatory categories.

The fact that colonial records do not clearly name Tasorentsi may indicate omission, translation into “God,” local terminology not encountered by missionaries, or later semantic consolidation. It cannot by itself prove that the term did not exist.

### Rubber, slavery, and prophetic leadership, c. 1880–1950

Rubber extraction and settler expansion brought debt peonage, kidnappings, forced labor, raids, epidemics, and displacement. In this setting José Carlos Amaringo Chico—later called José Tasorentsi—emerged as a multilingual shaman-chief.

**[Documented historical appropriation]** Tasorentsi here was not a birth name in the ordinary sense but a title asserting world-transforming power. He could be understood as a singular divine incarnation, an *amachénga* in human form, an emissary or child of Pavá, or simply a man possessing *tasorentsi* power. Outsiders photographed him and transcribed his name in several distorted forms. His activity in the 1910s–1940s converted a cosmological category into a political-religious office promising freedom and immortality. [Smithsonian overview](https://stri.si.edu/story/slavery-and-utopia)

### Adventist and evangelical transformation, twentieth century

Adventist missionization expanded strongly from the 1920s. Mission schooling, medicine, trade goods, rescue of accused child-sorcerers, and preaching made Christianity socially consequential. Missionaries equated benevolent spirits with angels or divine forces and *kamari* with Satanic demons. Adventist cosmic dualism—Christ and angels against Satan—mapped readily onto ethnographic descriptions of *tasorentsi/amachénga* against *kamari*.

This was not simple replacement. Converts continued steam healing, kinship restrictions, Indigenous diagnostics, and other practices while treating them as compatible with Christianity. Christian resurrection and the Second Coming were interpreted through aspirations to renewed immortality and reopened celestial relations. [La Serna Salcedo](https://doi.org/10.4000/bifea.1580)

### 1965 and revolutionary utopia

In 1965 some Asháninka allied with the Movimiento de Izquierda Revolucionaria. Brown and Fernández show that revolutionary promises were heard alongside a history of messianic expectations, anti-colonial leaders, and *amachénga/itomi Pavá* emissaries. The alliance was destroyed by the Peruvian military. This was not “worship of Tasorentsi” but an episode in which a vocabulary of deliverance and world transformation shaped political reception. [Brown and Fernández](https://books.google.com/books?id=na0wDwAAQBAJ)

### 1980–2000: Shining Path, MRTA, and catastrophe

The internal armed conflict devastated Asháninka communities. Shining Path imposed forced recruitment, labor, relocation, punishment, and execution; state forces and other armed actors also committed abuses. The Truth and Reconciliation Commission identified Indigenous rural Peru, including the Asháninka, as disproportionately victimized and Shining Path as the principal perpetrator nationally. Estimates widely cited in official policy speak of roughly 10,000 displaced or captive Asháninka from a population of about 55,000, with thousands killed or missing.

No reliable source located says Shining Path systematically appropriated Tasorentsi as a deity. The relevant history is instead the violent attempt by an external millenarian-political project to reorganize Asháninka life, and Asháninka self-defense and survival. [Peruvian Truth and Reconciliation Commission](https://www.cverdad.org.pe/ingles/ifinal/conclusiones.php)

### Twenty-first century

Tasorentsi remains active in three overlapping registers:

- traditional and educational cosmology;
- Christian Bible translation and Indigenous Christianity;
- cultural, ecological, and political revitalization.

The **Instituto Yorenka Tasorentsi**, associated with Benki Piyãko in Acre, uses the name in a living Indigenous project of territorial defense, ecological restoration, education, healing, and interethnic encounter. This is not evidence of a centralized “Tasorentsi church”; it is a contemporary extension of the word’s associations with knowledge and transformative power.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Anthropology and popular mythology

**[Modern scholarly reception]** Weiss’s singular phrasing—“Sometimes the Campa use Tasorentsi to mean God”—has often been simplified online into “Tasorentsi, supreme creator god of the Asháninka.” That formula is not wholly false, but it suppresses the class noun, regional variation, unmarked number, Pavá identification, and missionary influence.

**[Modern invention/systematization]** Commercial pantheon databases now give Tasorentsi fixed “domains,” allies, enemies, genealogies, and rankings. Such schemas are modern cataloguing devices. Their source lists may point to Weiss, but their clean ontology does not reproduce his ambiguity.

### José Tasorentsi in history and memory

Santos-Granero’s 2018/2020 *Slavery and Utopia* restored José Carlos Amaringo Chico to public history as a major Asháninka leader. The book treats him as a historically documented person using a cosmological title, not as proof that the divine being became biologically incarnate. Smithsonian publicity calls him a “divine messenger and world transformer,” accurately reflecting followers’ political-religious idiom while necessarily compressing contested meanings.

### Ecological and ayahuasca reception

The names **Yorenka Tasorentsi** and **Yorenka Ãtame** now circulate in ecological activism and international ayahuasca networks. Courses, retreats, and fundraising descriptions sometimes present “ancestral Tasorentsi wisdom” in generalized spiritual language. Some activity is Indigenous-led and tied to concrete reforestation and territorial projects; other online reception packages Asháninka religion for global wellness consumers. Each claim requires separate provenance.

### Occult, neopagan, and esoteric claims

**[Dedicated negative finding]** Searches combining Tasorentsi with “occult,” “Hermetic,” “Golden Dawn,” “Theosophy,” “chaos magic,” “neopagan,” and “sacrifice” produced no evidence of a historically significant Western occult cult, formal neopagan reconstructionist body, or pre-twentieth-century grimoire tradition devoted to Tasorentsi. Any such presentation should presently be treated as modern individual invention unless independently documented.

### Political appropriation

Tasorentsi’s strongest documented political use is Indigenous, not nationalist-occult: prophetic resistance to enslavement and colonial domination; promises of immortality and restored autonomy; present-day cultural and environmental projects. Broader Peruvian historiography has repeatedly recast Indigenous leaders as either savage rebels, Christian millenarians, Inca patriots, Marxist precursors, or ecological sages. Those are dated interpretive frameworks, not neutral descriptions.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### One god or a class?

This is the central controversy.

- **Torres López/Fernández:** a creator called Tasorentsi.
- **Weiss:** a class of gods, with occasional singular usage chiefly referring to Pavá.
- **Payne:** a legendary son of Pavá and a word for extraordinary divine power.
- **Rojas Zolezzi:** primarily a power/category; singularization may arise from unmarked number, editorial misunderstanding, and Christian monotheistic expectations.
- **Santos-Granero:** both class and singular title are historically operative; the ambiguity enabled powerful humans to claim or receive world-transformer status.
- **Contemporary converts:** Tasorentsi may be the biblical God, Trinity, or Holy Spirit.

No single formulation supersedes all others.

### Indigenous origin or missionary construction?

The breath-transformer complex is deeply integrated into narratives of animals, plants, and landscape and is unlikely to be wholly invented by missionaries. But singular creator language, “glory,” heaven/hell dualism, and equations with Father, Son, and Holy Spirit show sustained Christian influence. The historical task is to trace translation, not to sort every sentence into “pure” Indigenous versus “foreign.”

### Pavá’s etymology

Weiss entertained derivation of *Pavá/Pabá* from Spanish *padre* or a “father” term, while other writers defend deeper Indigenous or regional usage. A seventeenth-century reference to *Pabate* complicates any simple recent-loan theory but does not settle the etymology. The equation of Pavá with “our Father” may combine sound resemblance, missionary translation, and older solar kinship language.

### Benevolent spirits or power beyond morality?

Brown and Fernández’s dualistic account distinguishes benevolent *tasorentsi/amachénga* from evil *kamari*. Rojas’s linguistic analysis says the word can characterize any exceptionally powerful being, including harmful ones. The disagreement may reflect locality, elicitation, Christian dualization, or two semantic levels: a narrow benevolent class and a broader quality of power.

### “Creation” versus transformation

Calling Tasorentsi a “creator god” can mislead. Most narrative actions rearrange or transform a prior world. “Creator” remains acceptable if defined as one who produces the present order, but **creation ex nihilo is not established**.

### Limits of the archive

The archive is structurally uneven:

- colonial texts are written by missionaries and officials;
- oral performances were recorded late;
- older publications used the exonym “Campa”;
- orthography varies;
- translators often supplied Christian equivalents;
- many recordings remain unpublished;
- statements may represent one speaker, river system, mission, or generation.

The record therefore supports a history of plural formulations rather than recovery of a single original theology.

## 11. Sources and evidentiary assessment

The most authoritative direct source remains Weiss’s ethnographic corpus, read alongside Rojas Zolezzi’s linguistic critique. Santos-Granero supplies the strongest historical treatment of human world-transformers and colonial violence. Brown and Fernández document the twentieth-century political afterlife of messianic and celestial-emissary expectations. Official Indigenous-language and cultural publications are important evidence for living tradition, but their recent editorial form must be dated rather than projected backward.

The Franciscan chronicles are indispensable for contact history, missionization, epidemic disease, rebellion, and colonial categories. They are much weaker as transparent descriptions of Indigenous theology. Conversely, modern oral accounts give Indigenous formulations but cannot, without corroboration, be assigned precise precontact dates.

The dossier’s firmest conclusion is therefore not that Tasorentsi has one recoverable biography. It is that **tasorentsi has a documented semantic and historical career**: from a number-neutral category of breath-transforming powers, through a singular solar or creator title, into a word for the Christian God and Holy Spirit, a title assumed by anti-colonial prophetic leaders, and a name used today for cultural and ecological transformation.
