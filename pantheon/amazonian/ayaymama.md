# The Ayaymama: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Primarily an etiological bird-being and transformed human or humans in Peruvian Amazonian oral folklore—not securely a god, goddess, demon, or independently worshipped spirit. In different recorded traditions *ayaymama* may designate the real potoo, two abandoned children transformed into potoos, an Awajún woman transformed into the bird, or the bird’s ominous or sorrowful voice.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest presently verifiable printed narrative is **“Ayamaman,” collected by Irene Izquierdo Ríos at Saposoa, San Martín, and published by José María Arguedas and Francisco Izquierdo Ríos in *Mitos, leyendas y cuentos peruanos* (1947)**. Jenaro Herrera’s *Leyendas y tradiciones de Loreto* first appeared in 1918, but the accessible bibliographical evidence does not establish that its original edition contained an Ayaymama narrative. Assertions that Arturo Jiménez Borja printed the tale in 1937 also require verification against the book itself.
>
> **Evidentiary conclusion:** The requested classification as a member of a “Southern Arawak pantheon” is not established. The best-documented orphan narrative belongs to the regional folklore of **San Martín and the Spanish-/Quechua-influenced ribereño Amazon**, with later Loreto, Ucayali, Shipibo-associated, Awajún, and Napo Runa variants. The standard Southern Arawak ethnographies named in the brief do not, on the searchable evidence, establish an Ayaymama deity or cult.

## 1. Identification, names, and classification

### Ayaymama, Ayamaman, Ayaymaman

The central referent is a nocturnal bird whose call is heard as human lament. The Association of Academies of the Spanish Language defines Peruvian *ayaymama* simply as a gray-brown nocturnal bird of the family Nyctibiidae, up to about thirty-eight centimetres long. That lexical evidence supports a vernacular bird name, not divine status. **[Modern lexicographical attestation]**

The principal forms are:

- **ayaymama / ayaymamá**: the widespread Peruvian Spanish form, conventionally explained onomatopoeically as Spanish *“ay, ay, mamá”*—“oh, oh, mother” or “alas, mother.” **[Folklore/modern interpretation]**
- **ayamaman / ayaymaman**: San Martín forms, sometimes with a final *-n*. The 1947 narrative is commonly titled “Ayamaman.” **[Securely attested, 1947]**
- **Ayamamay**: a vocative-like form in the printed song, glossed as “Madrecita muerta,” “little dead mother.” **[Securely attested, 1947]**
- **Ayaymama huishchuhuarca / wischusqam kaniku** and related spellings: unstable transcriptions of a regional Quechua expression attached to the bird’s cry. The 1947 recension prints:

  *Ayamamay, wischusqam kaniku!*  
  “Little dead mother, we are lost/abandoned!”

  It also gives:

  *Ayamamay, maypitaq kachkanki?*  
  “Little dead mother, where are you?”

  **[Securely attested printed literary-folkloric text, 1947; spelling dependent on editor]**

One printed explanatory note analyzes *aya* as “dead person/corpse” and *mama* as “mother,” yielding “dead mother.” That is a plausible Quechua reinterpretation. It competes with the transparent Spanish auditory interpretation *ay, ay, mamá*. The name may therefore exemplify **folk-etymological convergence**: an avian call perceived both as Spanish lament and as a Quechua compound. No historical-linguistic study located here demonstrates which analysis is prior. **[Disputed etymology]**

Claims that the whole expression translates as “Mother, why did you abandon us?” are paraphrases of the story, not literal translations of *ayaymama* alone. **[Modern elaboration]**

### What kind of being is it?

The sources produce several classifications rather than one identity:

| Corpus or region | Classification |
|---|---|
| Saposoa, San Martín, 1947 | A male and female child transformed into two nocturnal birds |
| Common Loreto/Iquitos epidemic version | Two children compassionately transformed into birds |
| Shipibo-associated account recorded in Pucallpa | A bird reproducing the cries of children whose mother was killed; sometimes the children themselves are treated as the birds |
| Awajún account | A named woman, wife of Moon, transformed into the bird |
| Napo Runa comparative notice | A woman associated with Moon becomes Ayaymama |
| Dictionaries and ornithology | A common name for a potoo or, more broadly, for members of Nyctibiidae |
| Internet “mythology” catalogues | Sometimes mislabeled a spirit, demon, “neutral entity,” or pantheon member |

No reliable source establishes Ayaymama as a supreme being, tutelary god, divine office-holder, or recipient of an organized cult. Calling it a “god” is therefore **modern classificatory inflation**.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest secure narrative

The most securely recoverable early text is the San Martín account collected by **Irene Izquierdo Ríos in Saposoa**, capital of Huallaga Province, and published by Arguedas and Francisco Izquierdo Ríos in 1947. The published narrative already calls attention to variant tellings, indicating prior oral circulation, but that does not furnish a date for the tale before collection.

It says the birds were once a little boy and girl. Their mother died; their initially affectionate father remarried; the stepmother abused the children and, after bearing a child of her own, induced their father to abandon them. The boy defeats the first attempt by scattering maize to mark the route home. In the eventual successful abandonment, the children become birds, return to the house, and cry for their dead mother. Their remorseful father calls them back, but they return to the forest. **[Securely attested, San Martín recension, 1947]**

The tale’s maize trail and repeated abandonment resemble “Hansel and Gretel.” Juan Carlos Galeano consequently proposes possible European influence. Similarity alone cannot decide whether the motif was borrowed into an older Amazonian bird etiology, added by a collector, or arose independently. **[Modern comparative hypothesis]**

### Other important textual witnesses

- **Francisco Izquierdo Ríos, *Pueblo y bosque* and later literary work:** cited in later regional scholarship as an Ayaymama source. Izquierdo helped move orally collected Amazonian narratives into national literature and schooling. **[Mid-twentieth-century literary elaboration]**
- **Juan Díaz del Águila, *Folklore Amazónico* (1959):** part of the early corpus of written Amazonian folklore; its exact Ayaymama wording must be checked in the physical edition before treating it as an independent witness.
- **Ciro Alegría, “Leyenda del Ayaymama,” later collected in *El sol de los jaguares* (1979):** literary retelling, not an early indigenous manuscript. **[Modern literary retelling]**
- **José R. Romero Ch., “El ayaymaman,” *Revista Selva* 36 (January–March 1985):** cited by Carlos Dávila Herrera in connection with Pucallpa/Shipibo and mestizo variants. **[Regional folklore record]**
- **Carlos Dávila Herrera, *Brujos y hechizados*:** distinguishes a Shipibo-associated account from mestizo belief. In the former, the mother is killed by **Anitetepue**, a great “monkey-hawk”; the bird imitates the abandoned children’s cry, and hearing it may bring happiness. In the mestizo account, the cry portends tragedy. **[Regional ethnomedical/folkloric report]**
- **Juan Carlos Galeano, Amazonian folktale collection (2014):** identifies the bird as *Nyctibius grandis*, notes the abandonment tale’s variants and possible Grimm influence, and reports a Napo Runa Moon-related transformation story. **[Modern scholarly-literary synthesis]**
- **Awajún Ministry of Education collection, *Relatos ancestrales del pueblo Awajún* (2025):** prints “Auju nantujai / La ayaymama y el luna,” in which Ayaymama is Moon’s wife. **[Recent publication of an indigenous-language tradition]**
- **Educational, tourist, and internet versions, 2000s–present:** usually repeat either the cruel-stepmother story or the epidemic-rescue story, often without informant, collection place, or textual genealogy. **[Modern retelling]**

### Named corpora that do not establish an Ayaymama deity

The dossier brief names major works by Fernando Santos-Granero, Gerald Weiss, Richard Chase Smith, Harold Shaver and Lois Dodd, Michael Brown and Eduardo Fernández, France-Marie Renard-Casevitz, Enrique Rojas Zolezzi, Peter Gow, James Regan, Harry Dean, and Franciscan chroniclers. These works are indispensable for Yanesha, Asháninka, Nomatsiguenga, Matsigenka, Yine/Piro, Urarina, missionary, messianic, and regional historical contexts. But no specific Ayaymama passage in them was located or demonstrated by the available indexes and searchable text.

Accordingly:

- Ayaymama cannot safely be inserted into Campa/Asháninka cosmology merely because Weiss studied that cosmology.
- It cannot be assigned to Yanesha tradition on the authority of Smith or Santos-Granero without an actual passage.
- It cannot be tied to Juan Santos Atahualpa, Franciscan mission resistance, or the Piro/Yine myth-history studied by Gow without a source that makes that connection.
- These are **negative findings**, not claims that no unindexed oral parallel could exist.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain and characteristic action

Ayaymama’s defining act is **nocturnal lamentation**. Its narrative domain is bereavement, abandonment, failed parenthood, children lost in the forest, and irreversible human-to-animal transformation. In the epidemic version it additionally embodies maternal self-sacrifice: the mother leaves her children near water, fish, and fruit to save them from contagion. **[Loreto/Iquitos folklore variant]**

The tale is primarily etiological: it explains why a real bird cries as though calling for its mother. Later interpreters read it psychologically as an emblem of grief, family separation, child vulnerability, or memory. Those abstractions are interpretations, not propositions stated by the earliest text.

### Bird identification

There is no wholly stable species identification.

- The dominant published identification is the **Common Potoo, *Nyctibius griseus***. Peru’s IIAP explicitly calls that species *ayaymama* and describes it as the protagonist of many Amazonian legends. **[Modern ornithological identification]**
- Galeano identifies the legendary bird as the **Great Potoo, *Nyctibius grandis***. Cornell’s account describes that species’ vocalization as a harsh descending scream. **[Modern alternate identification]**
- Some recent educational material identifies it as the **White-winged Potoo, *Nyctibius leucopterus***. **[Modern inconsistent identification]**
- A Peruvian bird reference uses *ayaymamas* collectively for the whole family **Nyctibiidae**, including six Peruvian species. This broader vernacular usage explains some inconsistency.

The legend itself predates or operates independently of Linnaean species assignment. Its “bird” is recognized acoustically and narratively, not through a preserved type specimen. The safest formulation is therefore: **a potoo (*Nyctibius* spp.), most often identified in print as *N. griseus*, but sometimes as *N. grandis* or *N. leucopterus*.**

### Appearance and emblems

The earliest narrative provides little formal iconography. Modern depictions derive from potoo biology:

- bark-mottled gray or brown plumage;
- very large eyes;
- a broad mouth;
- rigid, vertical daytime posture;
- camouflage against dead branches;
- nocturnal activity;
- a haunting descending vocalization.

There is no attested sacred weapon, regalia, color code, number, weekday, celestial body, temple image, or canonical anthropomorphic portrait. Moon becomes narratively important only in particular Awajún and Napo Runa cycles.

## 4. Genealogy and relations with other beings

### Family structures

**San Martín orphan version, 1947:** Ayaymama is a pair of siblings, a boy and a girl. Their mother is dead; their father remarries; the stepmother engineers their abandonment. None receives a personal name. **[Securely attested early printed version]**

**Loreto epidemic version:** two children have a living mother who believes herself infected during a fatal epidemic. She leaves them at a resource-rich forest site so that they may survive. No stepmother is required. **[Regional variant]**

**Shipibo-associated version:** two children lose their mother to the predatory Anitetepue. The account is not a divine genealogy but a narrative relation between bird, children, mother, and hawk. **[Regional variant, reported 1985 or later]**

**Awajún version:** Ayaymama/Aúju is a woman married to **Nántu**, Moon, and has at least one son who reports his father’s departure. **Etsa** appears as an authoritative transformer. This is a different mythic structure from the orphan pair and should not be harmonized with it. **[Awajún variant, recently published]**

### Every documented interaction with another named divine or superhuman being

- **Nántu (Moon):** Ayaymama is his wife. She hoards ripe squash and serves him unripe food; he discovers the deceit and climbs a rope toward heaven. She follows, the rope breaks, and she falls. *Source:* “Auju nantujai / La ayaymama y el luna,” Awajún Ministry of Education collection, 2025. **[Regional indigenous variant; recent textual attestation]**
- **Etsa:** after Ayaymama’s fall, Etsa pronounces the transformation: the squash spilled from her body becomes clay, while she becomes the lamenting bird. *Source:* same Awajún text, 2025. **[Regional indigenous variant]**
- **Moon-man, Napo Runa version:** a woman sleeps incestuously with her brother, discovers his identity by marking his face with *huito*, and becomes Ayaymama after he rises to the sky. Galeano reports the episode comparatively rather than printing a full primary transcript. **[Regional variant; secondary report, 2014]**
- **“Spirit of the forest” / “Lord of the forest”:** in modern epidemic retellings, an unnamed forest power pities the children and changes them into birds. Because it is not consistently named, it cannot be cross-referenced as a particular deity. **[Modern or late folklore elaboration]**
- **Anitetepue:** a named great monkey-hawk kills the children’s mother in the Shipibo-associated account. The Ayaymama bird then reproduces the children’s lament. *Source:* Dávila Herrera’s account citing Pucallpa materials and *Revista Selva* 36 (1985). **[Regional variant]**

No documented interaction was found with the Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, Sachamama, Tunche, Juan Santos Atahualpa, Christian saints, or the beings central to the standard Yanesha, Asháninka, Matsigenka, Nomatsiguenga, Yine, or Urarina ethnographies. Co-presence in compilations of Amazonian folklore is not an interaction.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. The dead mother, father, and cruel stepmother

**Earliest secure source:** Saposoa account, 1947.

After their mother dies, a boy and girl remain with their father. He remarries. The new wife mistreats the children, makes them work excessively, and becomes still more hostile after bearing her own child. She persuades their father that the older children are burdens.

The first attempted abandonment fails because the boy overhears the plan and drops maize kernels along the path. The children follow the trail home. In the successful attempt, they cannot retrace the route. Their grief for their mother becomes their defining voice. They become nocturnal birds, fly to their former home, and sing that their little dead mother is absent and that they are lost. The father, now remorseful, asks them to return, but they fly back to the forest. Their cry remains audible at night.

The tale explains:

1. the bird’s call;
2. why the birds are perceived as a male-female pair;
3. why their voice sounds human;
4. why they inhabit the forest rather than the house;
5. why the lament cannot be resolved.

The maize trail is the strongest possible European-story element. Its presence does not prove that the whole narrative is a Grimm import; it may be a portable motif inserted into an existing bird-transformation explanation. **[Secure early printed text with possible intercultural motif]**

### B. The epidemic and the self-sacrificing mother

**Attestation:** modern Loreto/Iquitos versions; widely reproduced in environmental and educational materials.

A fatal illness sweeps a community. A mother begins to show symptoms and takes her children deep into the forest, leaving them near a stream with fish and fruit. She expects to die but hopes the children will survive. At night they become frightened, call for her, and wish they could fly. A forest spirit, Moon, or impersonal transformative force grants the wish. They fly home only to find the settlement dead and deserted. They continue searching and crying *“Ay, ay, mamá.”*

This version reverses the ethical structure of the San Martín telling. The parent does not abandon the children through cruelty or weakness; abandonment is an act of protection. The children nevertheless experience it as loss. The epidemic may preserve memories of recurrent Amazonian disease catastrophes, but no particular smallpox, measles, influenza, or yellow-fever outbreak can be identified from the undated narrative. **[Regional folklore; historical interpretation unproven]**

### C. The Shipibo-associated Anitetepue version

The mother is killed by Anitetepue, a large predatory hawk. Her children remain alone, and the Ayaymama’s voice imitates their crying. Dávila Herrera reports that, for Shipibo informants, hearing the bird may ultimately bring happiness, whereas mestizo settlers interpret it as a tragic omen. This is crucial evidence against treating “the Ayaymama belief” as uniform. **[Regional variant]**

Whether the children literally become birds is less clear in the summarized passage than in the standard orphan story: it says the bird imitates their crying. The distinction should be preserved.

### D. The disobedient children and maternal curse

A military-border memoir preserves a harsher version: a widowed mother’s eight-year-old children rebel; she curses and casts them into the forest; the devil seizes or enchants their souls into birds, giving them an eternal sepulchral lament. This recension explicitly calls the bird cursed in popular belief.

Christian demonology—the devil possessing souls—is likely a colonial or postcolonial layer. It is not in the 1947 Saposoa text. **[Regional Christianized accretion]**

### E. Ayaymama/Aúju, wife of Moon

In the Awajún story, Moon’s favorite food is squash. His wife Ayaymama secretly consumes ripe squash while serving him green squash and claiming none is ripe. Moon spies on her, discovers the deception, and departs up a rope to the sky. Their child tells her where he has gone. Heavy with the squash she consumed, she follows; the rope breaks and she falls, her belly bursting. Etsa declares that the spilled squash will become clay and transforms her into the Ayaymama bird. She cries whenever Moon appears.

This is simultaneously an origin of:

- the bird and its lunar lament;
- clay used in Awajún pottery;
- the permanent separation of Moon and his wife;
- in related accounts, the severing of ordinary communication between earth and the upper world.

It is not a variant of two abandoned children in any simple sense. Its protagonist is an adult wife whose own deceit precipitates separation. **[Awajún regional cycle]**

### F. Napo Runa Moon-incest account

Galeano reports that a Napo Runa Ayaymama story is linked to a Moon-man who sleeps incestuously with his sister. She identifies him by marking his face with *huito*. After he ascends to the sky, she becomes Ayaymama. This belongs to the widespread Amazonian cycle explaining Moon’s facial markings, incest, celestial separation, and the transformation of a pursuing woman. The available notice is too compressed to reconstruct every episode. **[Regional variant; secondary summary]**

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

No temple, shrine, priesthood, festival, sacrifice, formal prayer, pilgrimage, oracle, theophoric personal name, votive object, or cult economy dedicated specifically to Ayaymama has been documented.

The evidence instead concerns attitudes toward hearing or handling the bird:

- its call may be understood as sorrowful but not dangerous;
- in one Shipibo-associated account it can herald happiness;
- in a mestizo account it portends tragedy;
- in the military-border testimony, soldiers blamed a captured bird for severe rain and flooding and threw it into the Corrientes River despite orders not to do so.

That last episode documents fear-driven action around a real bird, not an organized rite.

No human sacrifice, temple prostitution, cult violence, or ritual killing belongs to the documented Ayaymama record. Search results that place “Ayaymama” next to sacrifices concern different tales in the same compilations. Joining them would be source contamination.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1947: undated oral circulation

The exact age of the orphan-bird story is unknown. Its regional Quechua vocabulary, Amazonian bird referent, transformation motif, and later distribution indicate oral circulation before publication. No pre-Columbian image, indigenous manuscript, colonial description, or Franciscan chronicle passage securely naming Ayaymama has been identified.

The chronicles of Biedma, Amich, Izaguirre, and other missionaries are important records of missionization and suppression in eastern Peru, but they cannot be cited as Ayaymama evidence without a passage. **[Absence of evidence]**

### 1947–1960s: folkloric inscription

Arguedas and Izquierdo Ríos’s 1947 anthology converted a locally collected Saposoa narration into a nationally circulating printed text. Mid-century folklore and literary books then incorporated Ayaymama into the recognized repertory of “selva” traditions. This transition inevitably involved selection, translation, standardization, and literary editing.

### 1970s–1990s: ethnographic differentiation

Late-twentieth-century documentation greatly expanded knowledge of Amazonian cosmologies but did not reveal one pan-Amazonian pantheon. Instead it showed distinct Asháninka, Yanesha, Matsigenka, Nomatsiguenga, Shipibo, Awajún, Yine, Urarina, and ribereño systems.

The Ayaymama record likewise differentiates:

- San Martín stepmother folklore;
- Loreto epidemic folklore;
- Pucallpa Shipibo/mestizo omen traditions;
- Awajún Moon-wife mythology;
- Napo Runa lunar-incest mythology.

This period also overlapped with mission schooling, bilingual education, urban migration, and the circulation of indigenous narratives through edited anthologies.

### Shining Path and the internal war

The Asháninka and other eastern Peruvian peoples suffered forced recruitment, displacement, killings, and concentration during the Shining Path years. That history is essential to the modern history of the region, particularly the Ene, Tambo, and Perené valleys. No source located, however, makes Ayaymama a Shining Path symbol, resistance figure, or explanatory myth. Linking the bird legend to that violence without testimony would exploit historical suffering rather than document tradition.

### Twenty-first century: heritage and generalized “Amazonian mythology”

Ayaymama now circulates through:

- school readers and bilingual educational collections;
- ecotourism and bird-conservation materials;
- municipal and regional heritage presentations;
- blogs, videos, and mythology catalogues;
- literary and film adaptations;
- ayahuasca-tourism branding.

This circulation has widened recognition while erasing provenance. A Saposoa story may be relabeled “Shipibo,” “Asháninka,” “ancient Amazonian,” or simply “Peruvian,” and the bird may become a “demon” or “deity” despite the absence of such classification in the source.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature and education

Arguedas and Izquierdo Ríos established the most influential printed recension. Ciro Alegría and later writers converted regional oral material into literary narrative. Contemporary curricula use the legend to teach comprehension, ecology, cultural identity, family responsibility, or the distinction between myth and science.

The recurrent modern assertion that all versions transmit a single moral—maternal love, obedience, anti-abandonment, or ecological respect—is pedagogical systematization. Different narratives assign blame differently and cannot be reduced to one lesson.

### Film

José Manuel Gustin Terrones and Marco Antonio Tam Orellana’s fifteen-minute short film ** *Ayaymama* **, documented in their 2019 University of Lima production thesis, uses the legend to construct an analogy with child trafficking in the Peruvian jungle. That connection is a deliberate modern adaptation, not an element of the traditional narrative.

### Commercial and spiritual branding

“Ayaymama Mystic” uses the name for an ayahuasca retreat near Iquitos and presents the story as a living memory of transformation, loss, and humility before the forest. This is commercial-spiritual reception. It does not prove a traditional Ayaymama cult or a historical association between the bird and ayahuasca ritual.

### Hoaxes and paranormal media

In 2023 Peruvian press revisited the case of José Ricaldi Valdez, who claimed or performed “possession” by Ayaymama for television; reporting described it as fabricated. “Ayaymama possession” is therefore not evidence of traditional demonology. **[Modern media invention/performance]**

### Occultism and neopaganism

No evidence was found for Ayaymama in Renaissance magic, ceremonial occultism, theosophy, the Golden Dawn, historical grimoires, chaos-magic canons, or an established reconstructionist religion. Generic mythology websites that assign “alignment,” powers, or monster status are modern taxonomic inventions.

No organized Ayaymama neopagan movement, extremist appropriation, or nationalist cult is documented.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly problems

### Indigenous myth, regional folklore, or national literature?

The principal historiographical danger is assuming that “Amazonian” denotes one cultural system. Recent scholarship distinguishes indigenous-language traditions from the Spanish-speaking, intercultural folklore of river towns. Loreto, San Martín, and Ucayali folklore combines indigenous, Quechua, Hispanic, Christian, and modern literary elements.

The 1947 Ayamaman is a collected San Martín narrative. That is not enough to assign it to “the Southern Arawak,” much less to all Amazonian peoples.

### Oral age versus documentary date

The story is undoubtedly older than its printing in the ordinary sense that someone narrated it to the collector. How much older cannot be measured. “Ancient,” “pre-Columbian,” and “thousands of years old” are unsupported unless an independent early witness appears.

### Species dispute

Printed sources identify three different *Nyctibius* species. This is not a theological contradiction; it reflects the difference between vernacular ethno-ornithological categories and modern taxonomy, plus copying errors. *N. griseus* currently has the strongest documentary support, but *ayaymama* can also function as a family-level vernacular name.

### Grimm influence

The maize trail and repeated abandonment make European influence plausible in the 1947 story. Yet the human-to-bird transformation, onomatopoeic call, and Moon-wife cycles have broad indigenous Amazonian parallels. The responsible conclusion is a possibly composite narrative, not either “purely indigenous” or “copied from Grimm.”

### The two Ayaymamas problem

The orphan pair and the adult wife of Moon share a bird name and lamenting call but have different plots, social relations, and cosmologies. They may represent:

1. independent etiologies attached to the same bird;
2. historically interacting traditions;
3. translations that map distinct indigenous bird names onto Spanish *ayaymama*.

Current evidence does not decide among these possibilities.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- **“Ayaymama is an Amazonian god”:** unsupported.
- **“It belongs specifically to the Southern Arawak pantheon”:** unsupported by the cited Southern Arawak ethnographies.
- **“The name literally means ‘why did you abandon us?’”:** paraphrase, not demonstrated etymology.
- **“The bird is certainly *N. griseus/grandis/leucopterus*”:** overstates inconsistent evidence.
- **“All Amazonian peoples tell the same story”:** false homogenization.
- **“The legend is securely pre-Columbian”:** unproven.
- **“Ayaymama is a demon that possesses people”:** modern sensationalism, not established tradition.
- **“The story is simply Hansel and Gretel”:** possible motif influence, not demonstrated wholesale derivation.

## 11. Source assessment and final reconstruction

The fullest defensible reconstruction is not a divine biography but the history of a name, a sound, and several transformations.

At its narrowest and best-attested, Ayaymama is the vernacular name of a potoo whose nocturnal call listeners hear as a bereaved human voice. In the earliest secure printed story, two abused and abandoned children become a male-female pair of birds crying for their dead mother. Loreto retellings replace culpable abandonment with a mother’s attempt to save her children from an epidemic. A Shipibo-associated interpretation introduces the mother-killing hawk Anitetepue and reverses the call’s omen from tragedy to possible happiness. An Awajún tradition makes Ayaymama/Aúju the deceitful wife of Moon and links her fall and transformation to the origin of potters’ clay. A Napo Runa notice places the bird-woman in a lunar-incest cycle.

These accounts need not descend from one original narrative. The common denominator is the potoo’s voice, heard as the permanent speech of a broken human relationship. Everything beyond that—orphans, epidemic, stepmother, hawk, Moon, incest, squash, clay, devil, omen—belongs to particular historical and regional tellings.

The evidence therefore supports **a family of bird etiologies**, not a unitary Ayaymama theology. Its historical “life” consists in movement from local oral narration through twentieth-century folkloric collection, national literature and schooling, regional ethnographic differentiation, environmental education, film, tourism, and internet mythology. The transformation continues in modern media: a bird became a story, the story became a heritage emblem, and the emblem is now sometimes mistakenly promoted as an ancient god.
