# Itomi Pavá (the Son of the Sun): Research Dossier

**Class:** In the securely documented Asháninka/Ashéninka evidence, *itómi Páva/Pavá* is not consistently one individual “god.” Literally “son of Pavá” and, depending on context, “sons/children of Pavá,” the expression designates benevolent celestial beings, sacred animal manifestations, or a singular returning savior embodied in a historical outsider. The messianic figure is best classified as a **benevolent celestial spirit and recurrent savior-role**, sometimes equated with the Inca, Juan Santos Atahualpa, Jesus Christ, a missionary, or a revolutionary leader. These equations are historical interpretations, not stable identities.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest securely dated documentary precursor is Franciscan Gabriel Sala’s diary entry of **10 March 1897**, recording an Asháninka man’s report that an *Amachegua* had descended from heaven to help the Asháninka fight the whites. Sala answered that the true *Amachegua* was Jesus, Son of God. This attests the savior category, not yet the exact phrase *Itómi Pavá*. A story set in **1888**, calling a white Indigenous-dressed stranger the Son of the Sun and reincarnated Juan Santos Atahualpa, is known principally through Ernesto Reyna’s **1942** literary-historical account and therefore is not a securely contemporary 1888 attestation. The exact expression *itómi Páva* is securely published in Gerald Weiss’s ethnography, based primarily on fieldwork beginning in the 1960s and issued in full in **1975**. Oral testimony subsequently collected by Michael F. Brown and Eduardo Fernández documents its application in 1965 to Guillermo Lobatón and retrospectively to earlier missionaries.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identity

**[Secure ethnographic attestation, 1960s–1975]** In Weiss’s Asháninka cosmology, Pavá is the supreme benevolent celestial power, “Father Sun,” and the most powerful of the transforming beings called *tasórentsi*. *Itómi Páva* designates beings related to him as sons or creatures: good spirits, sacred birds and animals, and—under particular historical circumstances—a descending savior.

**[Historically situated messianic usage, documented retrospectively]** In oral histories of nineteenth- and twentieth-century crises, the singular “Itomi Pavá” is a being expected to come from above or from the outside world, correct abuses, defeat oppressors, restore land and justice, and furnish the Asháninka with goods monopolized by whites. The role can be occupied—or mistakenly believed to be occupied—by a human stranger.

This evidence does not support treating Itomi Pavá as a god with one fixed biography, icon, spouse, temple, and canonical mythology. The more accurate model is a **relational category that became a messianic office**.

### Forms of the name

- **Itómi Páva / Itomi Pavá / Itomi Pava:** “son of Pavá,” conventionally translated “Son of the Sun.” In Asháninka kin terminology, *itomi/ityomi* is a third-person possessive form of “son.” Modern linguistic documentation gives forms such as *notyomi* (“my son”), *pitomi* (“your son”), and *itomi* (“his son”). [A modern Ashéninka grammar](https://www.lotpublications.nl/Documents/640_fulltext.pdf) confirms this paradigm.

- **Itómi Pává / itome Pawa:** Depending on dialect and number understood from context, “children/sons of Pawa.” Among Asháninka of the Amônia River, the good spirits of the upper world may collectively be called *itome Pawa*. This plural or categorical meaning is essential: the phrase is not inherently a unique personal name. [Instituto Socioambiental’s Asháninka ethnographic synthesis](https://pib.socioambiental.org/en/Povo%3AAshaninka) preserves this usage.

- **Pavá / Páva / Pawa / Paba:** Orthographic variants for the supreme celestial being or Father Sun. Weiss proposed a possible relation to Spanish *papá/padre*, but this remains disputed. Later linguistic work identifies *paapa* specifically as a recent Spanish loan, while *pawa* occurs in Indigenous kin vocabulary; consequently, the derivation of divine *Pavá* from Spanish cannot be treated as established.

- **Amachénga / amachenga / amachenka / Amachegua:** A class of benevolent, ordinarily invisible beings and, in historical discourse, saviors. Brown and Fernández call this the more securely attested nineteenth-century term. The forms *Amachénga* and *Itomi Pavá* overlap in messianic narratives but are not demonstrated to have been exact synonyms in every region and period.

- **Tasórentsi / Tasorentsi:** A powerful transforming being, sometimes used as a class designation and sometimes for Pavá as supreme divinity. Mission influence has encouraged translations such as “God,” but “God,” Pavá, and Tasórentsi should not automatically be collapsed.

- **Inca / Señor Inca:** A returning ruler and culture hero in Asháninka oral tradition. Some narrators identify Inca with Itomi Pavá; others tell Inca stories without using that expression.

- **Juan Santos Atahualpa:** A historical rebel later incorporated into narratives of the returning Inca or Son of the Sun. No surviving document from the 1740s records Asháninka speakers calling him *Itomi Pavá*.

- **Sacaramentaro:** A divine warrior in a Río Perené narrative collected by Enrique Rojas Zolezzi. The name probably derives from Juan Santos’s recorded title “Jesús Sacramentado.” Its identification with Juan Santos or Itomi Pavá is a modern scholarly interpretation of oral tradition, not an early documentary equation.

### Classification changes

- **Traditional-cosmological:** one or more benevolent beings related to Father Sun.
- **Messianic-historical:** a returning liberator who can assume human form.
- **Christian syncretistic:** Son of God, Christ, divine messenger, angel, or evangelical missionary.
- **Incaizing:** returning Inca or reincarnation of Juan Santos Atahualpa.
- **Political:** liberator identified with an insurgent leader.
- **Modern shorthand:** frequently presented as “the Asháninka messiah” or “the Asháninka god,” formulations that conceal the expression’s plurality and historical variability.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Before 1897: possible antecedents, not exact attestations

**1742–1750s — Juan Santos Atahualpa documents.** Colonial reports describe Juan Santos as an Andean newcomer who entered the Gran Pajonal in 1742, claimed Inca descent and divine authorization, and mobilized Asháninka, Yanesha, Piro and neighboring peoples against the Franciscan mission frontier. Recorded titles include *Juan Santos Huayna Cápac Apu Inca*, “Jesús Sacramentado,” son of the Virgin *Zapa Coya*, and, in hostile clerical testimony, Holy Spirit or absolute God in America. He invoked Jesus and the Virgin in other reports. These contradictions reflect both changing speech and colonial hearsay.

The documents establish a Christian-Inca messianic discourse but do **not** record *Itomi Pavá*. Brown and Fernández therefore ask rather than assume whether Juan Santos activated a pre-existing Indigenous renewal belief or helped create the later messianic complex. Their discussion and extracts from the colonial record appear in [*War of Shadows*](https://dokumen.pub/war-of-shadows-the-struggle-for-utopia-in-the-peruvian-amazon-reprint-2019nbsped-9780520911352.html).

**1876–1877 — Arturo Wertheman.** Piro informants reportedly told Wertheman that “Campas” annually commemorated Juan Santos’s military victories and processed his sword. This is evidence of posthumous ritual memory, not of the name Itomi Pavá.

**1888 — “white Indian” tradition.** Ernesto Reyna’s *Fitzcarraldo, el rey del caucho* (1942) says that in 1888 Asháninka were told that an *Amachengua*, a reincarnation of Juan Santos, had appeared on the Pampas del Sacramento. The stranger was a white man dressed sumptuously in Indigenous fashion, carrying a modern rifle and cartridge belts; he reportedly said Father Sun had sent him. Reyna connected the figure with rubber baron Carlos Fermín Fitzcarrald. Because the account was published more than fifty years after the alleged event, it is **[late literary-historical testimony]**, not a contemporary 1888 record.

### First secure savior-category attestation

**10 March 1897 — Gabriel Sala’s diary.** An Asháninka named Rocas or Lucas told Sala that the Asháninka and whites were fighting in Chanchamayo and that *“había aparecido otra vez el Amachegua, bajado del Cielo, para ayudarlos en los combates”*—“the Amachegua had appeared again, descended from Heaven, to help them in combat.” Sala replied that Jesus was the only true Amachegua. Brown and Fernández regard this as the most credible nineteenth-century report. It proves that an Asháninka celestial-helper category circulated in 1897; it does not prove that Fitzcarrald was the referent.

### Ethnographic corpus

**Gerald Weiss, 1960s fieldwork; 1972 article; 1975 monograph.** *Campa Cosmology: The World of a Forest Tribe in South America* supplies the foundational systematic account. It records:

- Pavá as the supreme celestial power;
- good spirits in true human form, normally invisible;
- lightning as a manifestation of traveling spirits;
- brilliant birds and otters as visible “clothing” of good spirits;
- collective reference to sacred beings as *itómi Páva*;
- the eventual destruction or transformation of the present world by Pavá.

The [digitized 1972 article](https://acervo.socioambiental.org/sites/default/files/documents/KPD00017.pdf) and a [reproduced scholarly excerpt](https://repositorio.iep.org.pe/server/api/core/bitstreams/f3e7aad0-3db7-46f2-a682-7ea329e87fa6/content) preserve much of this material.

**Brown and Fernández, 1980s field interviews; 1991 publication.** *War of Shadows: The Struggle for Utopia in the Peruvian Amazon* is the principal source for the singular messianic figure. Its witnesses include Manuel Ashivánti, Pedro Kintaro, Alfredo Atiri, Alberto Yorini, Marta, Luis Shintori, and others. The book reconstructs nineteenth-century Amachénga traditions, the Stahl and Bulner movements, and the 1965 identification of Guillermo Lobatón as Itomi Pavá. It repeatedly records disbelief and retrospective repudiation alongside belief.

**Santos-Granero, 2002.** “Saint Christopher in the Amazon” places the movements in a wider Southern Arawak history of mission contact, epidemics, violence, and the appropriation of Christian images. Its chronological appendix dates the Bulner mission to about 1960 and the Asháninka/Nomatsiguenga encounter with the MIR to 1965. [The Smithsonian repository provides the article](https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/ec1a0175-8552-44f3-a358-bb24cb826716/content).

**Rojas Zolezzi, 1994.** His Río Perené material records Sacaramentaro, a divine warrior associated by interpretation with the remembered Juan Santos tradition. It is evidence that historical rebellion was mythologized, but not proof of an unchanged eighteenth-century Itomi Pavá myth.

### Comparative corpus: necessary limits

Santos-Granero’s Yanesha studies, Richard Chase Smith’s Yanesha work, Shaver and Dodd on Nomatsiguenga, Renard-Casevitz on Matsigenka, Peter Gow on Piro/Yine, and Loreto collections concerning Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna and “land without evil” traditions illuminate shared processes—missionary translation, mythic transformation, returning culture heroes, enchanted commodities and powerful outsiders. They do **not**, without a direct textual occurrence, enlarge the Itomi Pavá corpus. For example, [Gow’s *An Amazonian Myth and Its History*](https://books.google.com/books/about/An_Amazonian_Myth_and_Its_History.html?id=gkWPCcgRXKcC) concerns Piro/Yine transformations of history and myth, not an independent Piro cult of Itomi Pavá.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**[Secure ethnographic core]**

- celestial benevolence and the upper worlds;
- mediation of Pavá’s power;
- aid to human beings;
- transformation and renewal;
- appearance through brilliant, flying, or otherwise exceptional beings.

**[Messianic elaboration]**

- restoration of justice;
- expulsion or punishment of abusive outsiders;
- recovery of alienated territory;
- acquisition and redistribution of manufactured goods;
- ending deception, coerced labor, poverty and sickness;
- apocalyptic destruction followed by renewal.

The material-goods theme is not incidental. In twentieth-century narratives, followers expected the savior to provide machetes, firearms, metal cookware, clothing, food, medicine and other goods controlled by colonists. Millenarian expectation therefore translated political economy into cosmology.

### Form and manifestations

There is no canonical human portrait.

- Good spirits are human in their true form but normally invisible to ordinary people.
- Shamans may see them in visions.
- Their terrestrial “clothing” includes brilliant or stately birds and otters.
- Lightning may mark their aerial passage.
- A savior can appear as a stranger, including a white man, an Indigenous-dressed white man, a bearded Black revolutionary, or a missionary arriving by aircraft.
- In 1965 Lobatón’s beard, Blackness, outsider status and promise of sudden redress helped make him legible as an extraordinary being; no single physical trait constituted canonical iconography.

### Sacred animals

Weiss records sacred birds including the cock-of-the-rock, heron, cormorant and other striking species, and sacred otters, as manifestations or “clothing” of good spirits broadly called *itómi Páva*. These are categorical manifestations, not necessarily attributes of a single messianic person.

### Modern iconography

The graphic narrative *Memoria y violencia para un líder Asháninka* depicts Itomi Pavá in a cushma-like tunic, standing on a large serpent and holding a luminous or flowering staff fitted with an axe blade. Its text says the returning figure’s shining sceptre can extinguish the stars. This is **[modern artistic codification]**, not evidence for a precolonial standard image. The page is documented by the [University of Manchester digital exhibition](https://www.digitalexhibitions.manchester.ac.uk/s/corala/item/1970).

### Unattested correspondences

No reliable source located assigns Itomi Pavá a canonical color, number, weekday, planet distinct from the sun, dedicated plant, weapon, temple image, or sacrificial victim. Modern lists supplying such attributes without citations should be treated as invention.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical problem

The title means “son of Pavá,” but the evidence does not establish one universally named mother or one unique bearer. When *itómi Páva* is collective, Pavá is relationally the father or origin of numerous good spirits. When singular and messianic, “Son of the Sun” indicates divine mission more than a narrated biological birth.

A separate Asháninka solar myth makes the male Moon, Kashíri, father of the sun by a human woman. In such accounts the visible sun is Kashíri’s son. That relationship concerns Pavá/the solar being and cannot automatically supply a genealogy for every spirit called *itómi Páva*. The apparent paradox—Pavá as father of the sons of Pavá, while the sun is himself son of Moon—reflects layered traditions and possible missionary reinterpretation, not a family tree that can safely be harmonized.

### Every documented divine interaction

- **Pavá / Páva — parent, source or sender.** Itomi Pavá is literally Pavá’s son or one of his children; messianic bearers are described as sent from or descending from Pavá’s celestial realm. **Source:** Weiss, fieldwork of the 1960s, published 1972/1975; Brown and Fernández, 1991. **Tier:** securely attested ethnographic core.

- **Kashíri (Moon) — indirect genealogical relation only.** Some Asháninka narratives make male Moon the father of the Sun. If Pavá is identified with that sun, Kashíri is Pavá’s father and therefore ancestrally related to the “sons of Pavá.” No source found narrates a direct meeting between Kashíri and the messianic Itomi Pavá. **Source:** Weiss 1975. **Tier:** regional cosmological tradition; direct interaction unattested.

- **Jesus Christ — missionary identification.** In 1897 Sala answered that Jesus, Son of God, was the only true Amachegua. Adventist teaching later associated the expected returning Christ with Pavá’s messenger or Son. **Source:** Sala diary, 10 March 1897; Stahl/Bodley materials summarized by Brown and Fernández. **Tier:** Christian syncretistic identification.

- **Christian God — equation and translation.** Mission discourse translated Pavá as God and “Son of Pavá” as Son of God; Lobatón’s statement “I am a son of God; we are sons of God” was interpreted by the shaman Ernesto as *Itomi Pavá*. **Source:** Manuel Ashivánti’s oral account in Brown and Fernández, recorded in the 1980s and published 1991. **Tier:** modern syncretistic interpretation.

- **Virgin Mary / Zapa Coya — relation asserted for Juan Santos, not securely for Itomi Pavá.** Colonial accusers reported that Juan Santos called himself son of the Virgin Zapa Coya. Because later memory sometimes identifies Juan Santos with Itomi Pavá, this becomes an indirect association, but no independent Asháninka myth makes Mary the mother of Itomi Pavá. **Source:** 1752 hostile testimony summarized in Brown and Fernández. **Tier:** colonial syncretistic claim; direct Itomi-Pavá genealogy unattested.

- **Amachénga spirits — categorical overlap.** The returning celestial helper is variously called Amachénga and Itomi Pavá. Whether these were originally one category or became fused through mission history is unresolved. **Sources:** Sala 1897; Weiss 1975; Brown and Fernández 1991. **Tier:** attested historical overlap, exact identity disputed.

- **Inca — identification rather than encounter.** Inca is called Itomi Pavá in some late-twentieth-century Asháninka narratives; Spaniards kill or decapitate him, yet he remains alive in Lima or is expected to return. No narrative located presents Inca and Itomi Pavá as two separate beings who meet. **Source:** oral narratives recorded in the 1980s by Brown and Fernández and Fernández’s “La muerte del Inca.” **Tier:** regional oral variant and modern ethnographic attestation.

- **Juan Santos Atahualpa — identification rather than encounter.** Later accounts treat Juan Santos as a manifestation, predecessor or reincarnating form of the Son of the Sun. The eighteenth-century documents do not use *Itomi Pavá*. **Tier:** postcolonial oral-historical identification.

- **Sacaramentaro — possible transformed identity.** Rojas Zolezzi’s divine warrior probably preserves Juan Santos’s “Jesús Sacramentado” title, but no source establishes a scene in which Sacaramentaro and Itomi Pavá interact as separate beings. **Tier:** modern scholarly reconstruction.

- **Kamári — opposed classification.** In 1965 Manuel Ashivánti warned that Lobatón might not be a helpful spirit but a *kamári*, a deceptive or dangerous being. This is a recorded debate over the claimant’s nature, not a combat myth between named deities. **Source:** Brown and Fernández 1991. **Tier:** securely documented 1965 oral interpretation.

- **Koriošpíri — cosmological opposition only.** As ruler of harmful spirits, Koriošpíri belongs to the opposed lower-world order. No direct episode involving the messianic Itomi Pavá was located. **Source:** Weiss 1975. **Tier:** ethnographic cosmology; interaction unattested.

No spouse, offspring, sibling, servant or mount belonging specifically to a unique Itomi Pavá is securely documented.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. Sons of Pavá as celestial spirits

**[Secure ethnographic attestation]** Benevolent beings live above the ordinary world in true human form. Ordinary people generally cannot see them; shamans perceive them during curing journeys and visions. When these beings cross the human world, their passage can appear as lightning. They may wear the bodies of brilliant birds or sacred animals. The collective expression *itómi Páva* places them in Pavá’s celestial order.

This is the oldest securely recorded cosmological meaning of the exact expression. It contains no singular messiah, return prophecy, earthly mother, or fixed incarnation.

### B. The returning Inca

**[Regional oral variant, documented in the twentieth century]** In one family of narratives, Inca possessed productive knowledge and external goods. Through the misconduct of his son, whites or *viracochas* emerged and killed or captured him. His severed head, or Inca himself, remains alive in Lima. His future restoration promises the recovery of goods and autonomy.

An Río Ene narrator recorded in 1982 explicitly said of the killed Inca: “He was Itomi Pava, the Son of the Sun.” The identification links an Asháninka celestial-helper complex with Andean Inca and Inkarrí motifs. It cannot be projected unchanged into pre-Hispanic antiquity.

### C. Juan Santos Atahualpa

**[Historical event, 1742–1750s; mythologized afterward]** Juan Santos entered the Gran Pajonal in 1742, proclaimed Inca and Christian titles, demanded Spanish withdrawal, and won support across several Indigenous peoples. His forces expelled missionaries and defeated or evaded colonial expeditions. Spanish reports variously depict him as ascetic Christian, heretic, monarch, magician and impostor.

Stories of his death conflict:

- he died or disappeared at Metraro, emitting smoke;
- he was poisoned;
- an Asháninka tested his claimed invulnerability with a sling stone and mortally wounded him;
- he did not truly die and would return;
- his sword and grave continued to receive ritual attention.

The identification “Juan Santos = Itomi Pavá” belongs to later memory. Brown and Fernández explicitly leave open whether Juan Santos used an older renewal complex or became the historical source from which later forms developed.

### D. The 1888 white stranger and Fitzcarrald

**[Late account of an alleged 1888 event]** Reyna says news spread that an *Amachengua*, reincarnated Juan Santos, had appeared in the Pampas del Sacramento. The figure was a white man wearing Indigenous clothing and armed with a modern rifle. He claimed Father Sun had sent him to organize dispersed peoples into settlements with churches.

Reyna identified this person with Carlos Fermín Fitzcarrald or an agent working for him. Rubber interests did circulate messages promising goods or celestial authority to recruit labor. Yet Father Sala’s 1897 diary only says an Amachegua had descended to aid fighting in Chanchamayo; it does not name Fitzcarrald. Brown and Fernández therefore present alternatives:

1. Fitzcarrald or his agent deliberately exploited Asháninka belief;
2. Asháninka independently interpreted a powerful outsider through the Amachénga category;
3. later white authors projected a fantasy of Indigenous credulity onto rubber history;
4. all these processes partly overlapped.

The equation with Fitzcarrald is not securely established. It is especially problematic because his rubber empire relied on coercion, Indigenous raiding and forced labor.

### E. Ferdinand Stahl and imminent transformation

**[Secure historical movement; 1920s]** Seventh-day Adventist missionary Ferdinand A. Stahl established work on Peruvian Corporation land along the Perené. By the mid-1920s, Asháninka attendance increased sharply. Stahl preached the imminent return of Christ and destruction of those rejecting God’s word.

According to John Bodley’s informants, Asháninka expectation radicalized the chronology: Christ would arrive immediately; the dead would rise; evil, disease, death and aging would end; believers would ascend to God’s house. Some reports said God had already descended at Stahl’s Metraro station, and some people identified Stahl himself with the expected figure.

Hundreds—possibly as many as two thousand—assembled along the Perené. Landowners, deprived of workers, retaliated against Indigenous catechists; one was murdered on the Shahuaya. When the cataclysm did not occur, most dispersed, though organized Adventist communities remained.

The item brief’s date “1928” should be treated as an approximate focal date within this 1920s movement, not its sole securely established year.

### F. Werner Bulner

**[Secure oral-historical attestation, late 1950s/c.1960]** A Protestant missionary remembered as Werner Bulner founded a mission at Puerto Rico on the Río Ene. Marta, a Nomatsiguenga witness interviewed by Brown and Fernández, recalled that people called the white missionary Pavá, God and Itomi Pavá. Before departing for the Ene, followers drank manioc beer, sang “Itomi Pavá has come,” danced holding hands, and then traveled to the mission.

The experiment produced illness and deaths; another survivor returned saying: “It was a lie. He was not the Son of the Sun.” Luis Shintori recalled opposition to Bulner’s attempt to suppress manioc beer and other customs. Thus the corpus preserves both belief and Indigenous falsification of the claimant.

### G. Guillermo Lobatón and the MIR, 1965

**[Best-documented singular Itomi Pavá episode]** In 1965 the Túpac Amaru column of the Movimiento de Izquierda Revolucionaria, led by Guillermo Lobatón, retreated from the Andes toward Asháninka and Nomatsiguenga territory. Lobatón promised to punish abusive landlords, restore land, and end exploitation.

The shaman Ernesto Andrés interpreted Lobatón’s claim to be a “son of God” as *Itomi Pavá*. News traveled rapidly: the Son of the Sun had descended and would bring justice, end deception and distribute the goods enjoyed by whites. Some villagers “worshipped the guerrilla”; others doubted immediately. Manuel Ashivánti warned that the claimant might be a deceptive *kamári*.

Asháninka supporters supplied food, guides and fighters, and some received firearms training. The alliance also produced killings of colonists and suspected collaborators. Army counterinsurgency brought bombardment, burned villages, torture, detention and disappearance. When the guerrillas failed to protect communities, faith in Ernesto’s prophecy collapsed. The shaman was subsequently killed amid accusations surrounding the failed movement.

Brown and Fernández stress that the Asháninka did not simply adopt Marxism: shamans and villagers translated an unfamiliar revolutionary promise into their own history of powerful outsiders and anticipated renewal.

### H. Shining Path and MRTA years

**[Documented political history with disputed mythic interpretation]** During the 1980s and 1990s, Sendero Luminoso imposed forced recruitment, labor, relocation, executions and camp discipline on many Asháninka communities; some individuals initially joined voluntarily. MRTA forces also operated in the region. From 1989 onward, Asháninka self-defense organizations fought to recover their communities.

Some later analysts report that promises of a new order resonated with Itomi Pavá expectations, or that supporters hoped insurgents could recover the captive savior and external goods. Such interpretations should not be generalized to all Asháninka or made the primary cause of participation. Coercion, territorial dispossession, schooling, poverty, kinship and local politics are independently documented factors. The [1993 interview with María Heise and Oscar Espinosa](https://argumentos-historico.iep.org.pe/wp-content/uploads/2015/05/1993-argumentos11.pdf) records both the comparison and Sendero’s destruction of Indigenous leadership structures.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

There is no securely documented temple, permanent priesthood or institutional cult dedicated exclusively to one Itomi Pavá.

### Ritual specialists

The relevant Indigenous specialist is the *sheripiári*, a shaman who communicates with benevolent spirits through tobacco, coca, *kamarampi* or ayahuasca and dreams. Shamans interpreted claimants and portents, but they did not constitute an “Itomi Pavá priesthood.”

### Ritual actions

- visionary identification of a claimant;
- singing and dancing to announce the savior;
- communal manioc-beer drinking;
- travel to a mission or gathering place;
- baptism and Christian worship in the Stahl movement;
- awaiting an imminent descent or cataclysm;
- political mobilization and, in 1965, armed participation.

The annual procession of Juan Santos’s sword reported in 1876–1877 and the re-clothing of his alleged grave reported in 1891 concern his posthumous memory. Their later relationship to Itomi Pavá is plausible but not proven.

### Material and architectural evidence

No archaeologically identified Itomi Pavá sanctuary, idol, inscription or precolonial image is known. Relevant material sites instead include:

- Metraro and other locations associated with Juan Santos;
- Franciscan mission ruins;
- the Perené Adventist stations;
- Bulner’s Río Ene mission;
- villages and battle sites connected with the 1965 insurgency.

### Sacrifice and violence

The dedicated searches found no evidence of human sacrifice, animal sacrifice or temple prostitution offered to Itomi Pavá.

Violence is nonetheless integral to the record:

- killings during Juan Santos’s rebellion and colonial reprisals;
- rubber-era slave raiding and forced labor;
- murder of an Indigenous Adventist catechist by interests angered by lost labor;
- killings by MIR-associated Asháninka and guerrillas in 1965;
- military bombardment, burning, torture and disappearance;
- Sendero and MRTA coercion and massacre;
- armed Asháninka self-defense.

These are historical acts conducted around messianic, colonial or revolutionary movements—not sacrificial rites to the being.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 1742: unestablished

No surviving precolonial text, inscription or image records Itomi Pavá. A pre-contact Asháninka celestial-spirit complex may underlie later material, but its antiquity cannot be measured from present evidence.

### 1742–1750s: Christian-Inca rebellion

Juan Santos joined Inca legitimacy, Christian titles and Amazonian resistance. Later traditions made him a returning or undying savior, but contemporary sources do not call him Itomi Pavá.

### Late nineteenth century: documented Amachénga expectation

Rubber expansion, coerced labor and renewed colonization formed the setting for savior rumors. Sala’s 1897 diary securely records a heavenly Amachegua coming to fight whites. Reyna’s later account retrospectively places a Son-of-the-Sun claimant in 1888 and associates him with Fitzcarrald.

### 1920s–1933: Adventist transformation and backlash

Stahl’s millenarian Christianity was localized as immediate celestial renewal. Mission communities grew; landowners retaliated. In 1933 Asháninka attacked the Adventist mission at Sutziki, killing converts accused of collaboration with Peruvians and threatening the San Ramón airstrip. This was not a simple continuation of enthusiastic adherence.

### Late 1950s–c.1960: Bulner movement

Werner Bulner was hailed by some Asháninka and Nomatsiguenga as Itomi Pavá. The identification failed through deaths, dissatisfaction and conflicts over custom.

### 1965–1966: revolutionary incarnation and disconfirmation

Guillermo Lobatón became the most fully documented human claimant. Belief mobilized part—not all—of the local population; military devastation and guerrilla defeat disconfirmed the prophecy for many.

### 1980s–1990s: insurgency, captivity and self-defense

Itomi Pavá language became one interpretive resource amid Shining Path and MRTA violence. It cannot account alone for Indigenous conduct. Memories increasingly emphasized betrayal and disillusionment.

### Twenty-first century: memory and heritage

The figure survives in historical writing, community memory, education, political analysis, art and comics. Contemporary representations increasingly make Itomi Pavá a singular culture hero symbolizing liberation and balance. Evidence for a large, organized contemporary devotional movement or reconstructionist “cult” was not found.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Anthropology and political memory

*War of Shadows* made Itomi Pavá internationally visible as an example of Amazonian millenarianism and Indigenous interpretation of revolution. Later social-science writing sometimes reduces this nuanced history to a formula—“Asháninka repeatedly mistake outsiders for a messiah.” Brown and Fernández’s testimony instead shows active interpretation, disagreement, skepticism and retrospective judgment.

### Graphic narrative

*Memoria y violencia para un líder Asháninka* integrates the returning savior with the life of Abram Calderón and the disappearance of his brother Alejandro after MRTA retaliation. It says Juan Santos will return when the people suffer enslavement and portrays his arrival in 1965 through Lobatón. This is a deliberate modern synthesis of Juan Santos, Itomi Pavá and twentieth-century political memory, preserved in a [Peruvian Bicentennial edition](https://bicentenario.gob.pe/biblioteca/storage/app/uploads/public/61f/c3c/d0c/61fc3cd0ca94d953251677.pdf).

### Political appropriation

Some revolutionary memorial writing calls Lobatón an Asháninka god or presents the identification as unanimous Indigenous endorsement of Marxist guerrilla struggle. The oral corpus contradicts unanimity: belief was locally mediated, doubted by other shamans, and withdrawn after catastrophe.

Conversely, accounts that portray belief as mere gullibility ignore the documented abuses to which the promise of justice responded and the deliberate Indigenous reassessment of failed claimants.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated searches located no documented adoption of Itomi Pavá by Renaissance occultism, theosophy, the Golden Dawn, established grimoire traditions, chaos-magic lineages, or a demonstrable neopagan reconstructionist organization. Generic online “pantheon” pages that give the figure standardized divine relations or fantasy-style attributes are modern systematizations, not Indigenous canonical sources.

### Literature, film and games

Werner Herzog’s *Fitzcarraldo* materially shaped the modern image of the rubber baron but does not accurately reproduce the Itomi Pavá/Amachénga evidence. No major film, commercial game or global literary franchise centered on Itomi Pavá was established in the sources reviewed.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Itomi Pavá one being?

The strongest evidence says no. Weiss documents a collective category of Pavá’s children; Brown and Fernández document a singular messianic usage. “Itomi Pavá” became name-like in Spanish and English retellings, encouraging the construction of a single deity with a continuous biography.

### Indigenous core or Christian creation?

Three positions remain possible:

1. an old Asháninka celestial-helper category absorbed Christian and Inca content;
2. mission Christianity substantially created the savior complex;
3. neither tradition was autonomous: centuries of translation generated a new Indigenous-Christian formation.

The evidence favors the third as historical description, while leaving the depth of the Indigenous substratum unresolved. Pavá, *tasórentsi*, visible animal manifestations and shamanic access cannot be reduced to Christianity; conversely, Son-of-God language, apocalypse, resurrection, churches and baptism are demonstrably missionary.

### Did Juan Santos originate the tradition?

Brown and Fernández explicitly pose this as unresolved. Eighteenth-century records show Juan Santos using Inca and Christian claims, but not *Itomi Pavá*. Later Asháninka mythology identifies Inca or Juan Santos with the Son of the Sun. The direction of influence cannot be reconstructed with certainty.

### Was Fitzcarrald the 1888 Amachénga?

Not proven. Reyna’s 1942 identification is late and literary. Sala’s contemporary 1897 report neither names Fitzcarrald nor places the event in 1888. Scholars reasonably infer a rubber-era connection, but the exact claimant may have been Fitzcarrald, an agent, another outsider, or an entity existing primarily in rumor.

### Was Stahl himself called Itomi Pavá?

The evidence is uneven. Bodley’s material shows that some identified Stahl or his mission station with the already-arrived divine agent; the broad movement focused on Christ’s imminent return. Statements that all followers worshipped Stahl personally overstate the record.

### Was “Werner Bulner” correctly identified?

The name survives through oral recollection. Santos-Granero dates his Río Ene mission to about 1960; Brown and Fernández say late 1950s or early 1960s. Further denominational or archival identification remains uncertain.

### Did Asháninka support the MIR because of the myth?

The mythic interpretation mattered in some communities, especially through Ernesto Andrés. It was neither universal nor sufficient. Land seizure, unpaid labor, colonist abuse, political organizing, kin ties and coercive military conditions were also causal. The “messianism caused support” thesis is too simple.

### Source problems

- colonial records are mainly hostile Franciscan or official reports;
- Juan Santos’s own alleged letters are lost;
- no Asháninka-language eighteenth-century text survives;
- nineteenth-century travelers frequently used the exonym “Campa” and stereotyped Indigenous people;
- Reyna’s account mixes history and literary reconstruction;
- the decisive 1965 evidence was recorded roughly two decades later;
- narrators revised earlier belief in light of death, imprisonment and political change;
- dialect differences affect spelling and semantic range.

These are not reasons to discard oral history. They determine what kind of claim it can support.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before European documentation:** Possible Indigenous antecedents in celestial transforming spirits and shamanic relations with benevolent beings; date unestablished.

- **1742:** Juan Santos Atahualpa appears at Quisopango in the Gran Pajonal, proclaiming Inca and Christian authority. No contemporary use of *Itomi Pavá* is recorded.

- **1742–1752:** Multiethnic rebellion expels Franciscan missions and defeats repeated colonial incursions.

- **1752:** Juan Santos’s forces enter Andamarca. Hostile testimony attributes divine and heterodox Christian claims to him.

- **By 1756:** His military movement has ceased active expansion; his fate is unknown.

- **1766:** Conibo testimony reports his death or supernatural disappearance at Metraro.

- **1876–1877:** Wertheman hears that Piro informants know of annual Asháninka commemorations involving Juan Santos’s sword.

- **1888:** Date assigned by Reyna in 1942 to the appearance of a white, Indigenous-dressed Amachengua/Son-of-the-Sun claimant. Not contemporaneously verified.

- **1891:** An expedition claims to find Juan Santos’s grave at Metraro and reports annual re-clothing of it.

- **10 March 1897:** Sala records the first secure Amachegua report: a being descended from heaven to help fight whites.

- **1897:** Fitzcarrald dies in a river accident. Later accounts associate his rubber empire with manipulation of savior rumors.

- **1920s:** Stahl’s Adventist mission on the Perené catalyzes a large movement expecting Christ’s immediate return and cosmic transformation.

- **c. 1928:** Approximate high point conventionally attached to the Stahl movement; the evidence describes a broader mid-to-late-1920s process.

- **1933:** Asháninka attack the Sutziki Adventist mission, killing converts accused of collaboration.

- **Late 1950s–c.1960:** Werner Bulner establishes a Río Ene mission; some Asháninka and Nomatsiguenga call him Itomi Pavá. The movement loses credibility.

- **1965:** Shaman Ernesto Andrés identifies MIR commander Guillermo Lobatón with Itomi Pavá. Some communities join or support the guerrillas; others doubt.

- **1965–1966:** Counterinsurgency devastates participating communities; the MIR column is destroyed and the claimant’s identification is disconfirmed for many.

- **1960s:** Gerald Weiss records *itómi Páva* as a wider category of sacred beings.

- **1972:** Weiss publishes the major article “Campa Cosmology.”

- **1975:** Weiss publishes the full monograph *Campa Cosmology: The World of a Forest Tribe in South America*.

- **1980s:** Brown and Fernández conduct much of the oral-history fieldwork later published as *War of Shadows*.

- **1980s–1990s:** Sendero Luminoso and MRTA violence affects Asháninka territory. Some later testimony interprets revolutionary promises through Itomi Pavá expectations.

- **1989 onward:** Asháninka self-defense groups expand resistance to insurgent control.

- **1991:** Brown and Fernández publish *War of Shadows*, providing the principal documentary reconstruction of the 1965 episode and its predecessors.

- **1993–1994:** Heise, Espinosa and Rojas Zolezzi publish analyses connecting Itomi Pavá, Inca narratives and contemporary violence.

- **2002:** Santos-Granero publishes “Saint Christopher in the Amazon,” placing Asháninka movements within a broader Southern Arawak history of mission appropriation, disease and violence.

- **Twenty-first century:** Educational, artistic and commemorative works increasingly consolidate Juan Santos, Lobatón and the returning Itomi Pavá into a single liberation narrative.

## 11. Sources and Evidentiary Conclusions

The record supports the following conclusions:

1. **The exact expression originally has a broader range than a single divine name.** It can mean Pavá’s sons or children collectively.

2. **The singular returning savior is securely documented only in modern ethnography and oral history.** Its nineteenth-century precursor is more often called Amachénga/Amachegua.

3. **The first secure written precursor is Sala’s 1897 entry, not the alleged 1888 appearance.** The latter is mediated chiefly through Reyna’s 1942 reconstruction.

4. **Juan Santos is the historical ancestor of the later cycle, but his identification as Itomi Pavá is retrospective.**

5. **Stahl, Bulner and Lobatón were not successive incarnations in a universally accepted canonical doctrine.** Particular communities and interpreters placed them in the savior-role; contemporaries also rejected those identifications.

6. **The 1965 Lobatón cycle is the fullest documented narrative.** It contains prophecy, recognition, dissent, armed alliance, catastrophe and explicit disconfirmation.

7. **There is no evidence for a temple cult, sacrificial system, fixed idol, occult lineage or organized modern neopagan cult.**

8. **Neighboring Southern Arawak and Loreto traditions are comparative evidence, not interchangeable components of an “Amazonian pantheon.”** Yanesha, Nomatsiguenga, Matsigenka, Piro/Yine, Urarina and mestizo Loreto traditions must retain their distinct histories.

9. **Modern singularization is itself part of the being’s history.** Spanish-language scholarship, political memory and illustrated retellings have transformed a relational category into a named culture hero.
