# Yosoper / Korioshpiri: Research Dossier

**Class:** Yosoper is a male Yanesha divinity or chthonic spirit; Korioshpiri is the corresponding Asháninka lord of malignant beings. Both are commonly translated as “devil” or “ruler/father of demons,” but those translations are partly conditioned by Christianity. They are best treated as historically related—but not automatically identical—Southern Arawak figures.

**Earliest secure attestation:** **Korioshpiri** is first securely documented by Gerald Weiss from Asháninka (“Campa”) testimony collected during fieldwork in **1960–1964**, first presented in his 1969 University of Michigan dissertation and published fully in *Campa Cosmology* in 1975. **Yosoper** is first securely documented under that name in Richard Chase Smith’s Yanesha fieldwork, published in his **1977 Cornell dissertation**. Nineteenth-century sources attest the related sorcery complex, but the available evidence does not show either proper name before the twentieth-century ethnographies.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Secure ethnographic attestation]** Directly recorded from named or identifiable Indigenous communities in modern ethnography.
- **[Early historical attestation]** Colonial or nineteenth-century documentary evidence.
- **[Regional variant]** Material documented for one Southern Arawak people but not necessarily the others.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A datable equation with a Christian or neighboring religious figure.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An anthropologist’s historical or comparative hypothesis.
- **[Modern reception]** Recent political, artistic, popular, or commercial reuse.
- **[Not established]** A claim for which the searched record supplies no adequate evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Yosoper

**[Secure ethnographic attestation: Yanesha, published 1977–present]** Yosoper is the jealous classificatory brother—or, in one French ethnographic formulation, “brother by alliance”—of the supreme creator Yato’ Yos. He imitates Yato’ Yos’s creations but produces, or becomes responsible for, harmful, poisonous, foul, demonic, and witchcraft-associated beings. He rules the lowest world, the “land of suffering,” and is the ultimate source or “father” of malignant beings.

Recorded spelling is principally **Yosoper**. No Indigenous script tradition is documented; Yanesha was historically oral, and the spelling is Latin-alphabet ethnographic transcription.

Smith and Santos-Granero propose that the name may derive from Spanish **Lucifer**. This is plausible but not demonstrated by an independently attested sequence of historical spellings. Santos-Granero calls the derivation probable rather than certain. The resemblance is less transparent than in the Asháninka forms, and the proposal must therefore remain **[modern scholarly reconstruction]** rather than established etymology.

No distinct corpus of formal epithets is documented. Functional descriptions include:

- “evil classificatory brother of Yato’ Yos”;
- “father of all malignant beings”;
- ruler of the lowest earth or “land of suffering”;
- source of demons, witches, bad omens, and dangerous species.

These are ethnographic descriptions, not demonstrably fixed liturgical titles.

### Korioshpiri and variant spellings

Recorded forms include **Koriošpiri**, **Koriospiri**, **Korioshpiri**, and **Koroshpiri**. Weiss also reconstructed an intermediate form **iroshiperi** in arguing for derivation from *Lucifer*. Accentuation and *š/sh/s* variation principally reflect different transcription systems.

**[Secure ethnographic attestation: Asháninka, fieldwork 1960–1964]** Korioshpiri inhabits and governs **Sharinkaueni**, the lowest cosmic level, beneath Kivinti. Weiss calls him the “Lord of the Demons.” This is a ruler rather than merely one undifferentiated *kamari*.

Gerald Weiss proposed the following development:

> Lucifer → *iroshiperi* → Koroshpiri/Korioshpiri.

That is a linguistic hypothesis, not a documented chain in colonial manuscripts. Weiss himself complicated it by observing a comparable Matsigenka figure, **Kientibakori**, which could indicate a pre-missionary regional category subsequently renamed or remodeled through Christian vocabulary. Oscar Espinosa accordingly distinguishes the possible Christian loan-name from an older Amazonian conception of a ruler or protector of dangerous beings.

### Relation to *kamari*

**[Secure ethnographic attestation]** Asháninka **kamari** is broader than “demon.” It classifies multiple dangerous beings, disease-causing agents, monsters, and sometimes malign human conduct. Missionaries and some anthropologists commonly translated it as Spanish *demonio*, producing a Christianizing equivalence that can obscure its wider semantic field. Korioshpiri is described as ruler or protector of such beings; he is not necessarily synonymous with the entire category.

### Christian identifications

- **Lucifer:** **[Syncretistic identification / scholarly reconstruction]** Proposed source for both names by Smith and Weiss.
- **The Devil / *diablo*:** **[Syncretistic identification]** Contemporary Spanish explanations regularly equate Korioshpiri with the devil.
- **Antichrist:** **[Regional modern testimony]** A twenty-first-century Asháninka interview recorded the answer “Korioshpiri … es anticristo.” This is evidence of contemporary Christian interpretation, not proof that “Antichrist” was an older Indigenous identity.

The figures should therefore not be labeled simply “Lucifer under an Indigenous name.” The best-supported historical formulation is that an Amazonian structure of malignant beings, subterranean realms, illness, and dangerous transformative agency was rearticulated using Christian names and oppositions.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earlier documents: what they do and do not attest

Franciscan documentation begins with sustained contacts in the Selva Central around **1635**. Missionaries recorded and condemned Indigenous specialists whom they called *brujos*, together with polygyny, ritual anthropophagy, non-Christian ritual, and other practices. Juan de San Antonio’s 1750 report, Amich’s mission history, and related records show that missionaries knew of adult sorcery beliefs.

**[Negative historical finding]** Fernando Santos-Granero found no colonial reference between approximately 1635 and the expulsion of the missions in the 1742 rebellion to children being accused and killed as sorcerers. Since Franciscans actively sought such practices, he argues that this silence weighs against child-sorcerer executions being an unchanged pre-Hispanic institution. The argument is one from documentary silence and is therefore suggestive, not conclusive.

These sources also do **not**, in the material located, name Yosoper or Korioshpiri. It would be unsafe to claim a seventeenth- or eighteenth-century written attestation of either name.

### Nineteenth-century sorcery reports

**[Early historical attestation]** Reports of child sorcery appear between approximately **1880 and 1895**:

- González, 1880, on the Yanesha;
- Olivier Ordinaire, observations dated 1885, on Asháninka communities;
- Carranza;
- Hernández;
- Gabriel Sala, 1893.

The reports describe illness attribution, divinatory diagnosis, searches for buried objects, coercive interrogation, and killings of alleged sorcerers. They do not securely establish the named chthonic ruler.

Santos-Granero connects the first Yanesha report with yellow-fever and measles epidemics beginning in 1879–1881, Franciscan recolonization of the Chorobamba Valley, military defeat, dispossession, and demographic collapse. The first Asháninka reports coincide with the **1885 upper-Ucayali smallpox epidemic**.

### Gerald Weiss

Weiss spent about thirty months with Asháninka teachers between 1960 and 1964. His principal corpus is:

- *The Cosmology of the Campa Indians of Eastern Peru*, PhD dissertation, University of Michigan, 1969;
- “Campa Cosmology,” 1972;
- *Campa Cosmology: The World of a Forest Tribe in South America*, 1975, *Anthropological Papers of the American Museum of Natural History* 52(5), pp. 219–588.

**[Secure ethnographic attestation]** Weiss locates Korioshpiri in Sharinkaueni and calls him ruler of its demons. His work is the earliest presently verified record of the name.

### Richard Chase Smith

Smith’s *Deliverance from Chaos for a Song: A Social and Religious Interpretation of the Ritual Performance of Amuesha Music* was submitted at Cornell in 1977.

**[Secure ethnographic attestation]** At pp. 84–85 and 102–107, Smith records the Yanesha creation opposition between Yato’ Yos and Yosoper, suggests Christian influence, and connects Yosoper with sorcery and the malignant powers that exploit children.

### Fernando Santos-Granero

Major works include:

- *The Power of Love: The Moral Use of Knowledge amongst the Amuesha of Central Peru* (1991; Spanish edition 1994);
- “Saint Christopher in the Amazon: Child Sorcery, Colonialism, and Violence among the Southern Arawak,” *Ethnohistory* 49(3), 2002, pp. 507–543;
- “Pedro Casanto’s Nightmares: Lucid Dreaming in Amazonia and the New Age Movement,” 2003;
- “Beinghood and People-Making in Native Amazonia,” 2012.

These works preserve the fullest published synthesis of Yosoper’s cosmological place and the historical development of the child-sorcery complex.

### Other relevant comparative sources

Shaver and Dodd’s *Los Nomatsiguenga de la Selva Central* (1990) records children allegedly receiving sorcery instruction from **tsiricóti**, a sparrow-like malignant bird, or other spirit birds. It supplies a close regional analogue but does not establish that Nomatsiguenga speakers called the ultimate ruler Yosoper or Korioshpiri.

Renard-Casevitz’s Matsigenka research and the record of **Kientibakori** provide comparative evidence that a comparable malign ruler may predate the adoption of a Lucifer-derived name.

Brown and Fernández’s *War of Shadows* (1991), Rojas Zolezzi’s *Los Ashaninka* (1994), Pérez Marcio’s *Los hijos de la selva* (1953), Navarro, Sala, Ordinaire, Uriarte, and Torre López document sorcery accusations and their social history. They should not all be treated as independent attestations of Korioshpiri: several concern the broader practice rather than the named being.

Gow’s Piro/Yine studies, Dean’s Urarina ethnography, Regan’s Loreto folklore, and collections of Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, and Pishtaco narratives illuminate neighboring Amazonian transformations of dangerous beings. **[Not established]** The searches did not locate a primary-text episode in those corpora naming Yosoper or Korioshpiri. They therefore belong to the comparative horizon, not the being’s direct corpus.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

Yosoper’s securely documented domains are:

- counter-creation and failed or malign imitation;
- production or ultimate origin of dangerous species;
- witchcraft and sorcery;
- malignant spirits and demons;
- illness caused through contact, dreams, or manipulation of human remains;
- the lowest cosmic world and punishment after death.

Korioshpiri’s are:

- rulership of Sharinkaueni;
- lordship or protection of demons and *kamari*;
- association with illness, predation, and malignant human agency;
- later association with colonial and political violence.

### Form

**[Not established]** Neither core ethnography supplies a stable bodily portrait comparable to the canonical iconography of a temple deity. No fixed skin color, number of limbs, weapon, animal mount, or costume is securely attributable to Yosoper or Korioshpiri.

This absence matters. Internet “demon-god” images should not be mistaken for inherited Yanesha or Asháninka iconography.

### Symbols, animals, and helpers

**[Secure ethnographic attestation]** Ants, termites, bees, and wasps are prominent harmful agents. They take food remains—small bones, manioc fibers, fish bones—or bodily substances such as hair, scabs, sweat, urine, semen, blood, and excrement into nests or underground places. Illness is then attributed to the burial or concealment of these substances.

Santos-Granero calls such creatures helpers of demons and devils. The precise claim should not be inflated: the ethnography does not say that every ant or wasp is personally commanded by Yosoper/Korioshpiri in an individualized narrative scene.

Other sorcery agents include:

- demonic birds or insects appearing in human form in dreams;
- a curved-billed hummingbird associated with evil shamans among the Yanesha;
- *tsiricóti* and malignant spirit birds among the Nomatsiguenga;
- harmful spiritual essences of trees, rocks, and animals.

**[Not established]** No sacred number, weekday, color, planet, metal, incense, standardized offering, or sigil is securely documented for either being.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

The only firmly documented divine kinship is Yosoper’s relationship to **Yato’ Yos**:

- Smith and Santos-Granero: classificatory brothers;
- later French-language ethnography: *frère par alliance*, “brother by alliance”;
- some descriptions imply Yosoper is the jealous or rival brother, without establishing biological parentage.

No securely documented parents, consort, offspring, mount, or named personal servant are known for Yosoper. “Father of demons” is primarily a title of origin or rulership, not necessarily ordinary genealogical paternity.

Korioshpiri has no securely recorded genealogy in Weiss’s account. He is ruler of the demons rather than a member of the solar genealogy involving Pava/Katserinkaiteri, Kashiri, Avireri, and Pachakama.

### Every documented god–god interaction

- **Yato’ Yos — rival creation and appropriation of earth.** Yato’ Yos makes a flat world below the gods’ world; jealous Yosoper takes possession. Yato’ Yos requests earth or clay from him to form the human world. Source: Smith 1977, reproduced in Santos-Granero 1991 and later Yanesha ethnography. **[Secure ethnographic attestation; probable syncretistic elaboration]**

- **Yato’ Yos — competing creation of humans.** Yato’ Yos forms one person from clay and gives life by breath; Yosoper makes ten in the same image. Yato’ Yos curses Yosoper’s beings, turning them into demons. Source: Smith 1977; Santos-Granero 1991. **[Secure ethnographic attestation]**

- **Yato’ Yos — competing creation of species.** For each animal, bird, insect, or plant made by Yato’ Yos, Yosoper makes many copies. In one version Yato’ Yos curses and transforms the copies; in another Yosoper’s malice or incompetence makes them harmful. Sources: Smith 1977; Santos-Granero 1991; later Yanesha narrators summarized in 2012. **[Regional variants]**

- **Lucifer — historical equation, not narrative meeting.** Smith and Weiss propose that Yosoper/Korioshpiri and perhaps their names were modeled on Lucifer. No Indigenous narrative has the two figures meet as separate beings. **[Syncretistic identification / modern scholarly reconstruction]**

- **Kientibakori — comparative relation, not attested interaction.** The Matsigenka malign figure is used comparatively to question a wholly Christian origin for Korioshpiri. No source located describes a meeting between them. **[Modern scholarly comparison]**

- **Yompor Rrët — no interaction securely attested.** Yompor Rrët belongs to a subsequent Yanesha cosmic era and is himself malignant, but the sourced creation sequence does not describe a personal encounter with Yosoper. **[Absence of evidence]**

- **Avireri, Kíri, Pachakama, Pava, Kashiri, Inkanitari — no interaction securely attested.** They occur in Asháninka cosmology, but Weiss does not place them in a narrative scene with Korioshpiri. **[Absence of evidence]**

- **Saint Christopher and the Christ Child — no direct meeting with Yosoper/Korioshpiri.** Santos-Granero argues that their transformed legend helped structure beliefs about cannibalistic transformership and child sorcery. This is historical-mythic influence, not an episode in which the saints encounter the demon ruler. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The rival creation

**[Secure Yanesha ethnographic attestation]** Initially the divinities live together. Yato’ Yos creates a flat earth below their world. Yosoper, moved by jealousy, appropriates it. Yato’ Yos asks him for some earth and uses it to make another world between the gods’ world and Yosoper’s realm.

Yato’ Yos molds a human and animates it with breath. Yosoper refuses to be surpassed and makes ten beings of the same form. Yato’ Yos curses them, and they become demons inhabiting the human world. The brothers continue in competition through the creation of animals, birds, insects, and plants. Yato’ Yos’s creations are useful, edible, beautiful, or sacred; Yosoper’s become harmful species, witches, demons, and ominous beings.

Variants concern both materials and moral agency:

- earth or clay versus a mixture involving excrement/dirt and breast milk;
- milk obtained from Yato’ Yos’s mother versus sister;
- Yato’ Yos actively curses Yosoper’s copies;
- alternatively, Yosoper’s own evil or incompetence makes them harmful.

The structure—competitive brothers dividing beneficial from harmful creation—is Amazonian and relational. The names Yos and Yosoper, and the sharpened God/Lucifer polarity, probably show Christian influence.

### Rule of the lowest world

**[Secure Yanesha ethnographic attestation]** Yosoper takes or rules **Concheñtso/Concheñets**, the lowest of five superposed worlds, translated as the “land of suffering.” It is described as a fiery place where morally deficient dead suffer, including in a lake of burning iron.

This hell-like imagery is one of the strongest signs of Christian remodeling. It remains Indigenous tradition as recorded, but it should not be projected unchanged into a pre-contact period.

**[Secure Asháninka ethnographic attestation]** Korioshpiri governs **Sharinkaueni**, below Kivinti, the lowest level of Weiss’s Asháninka cosmos. It is inhabited by demons. Weiss does not document an elaborate Korioshpiri biography or a creation contest equivalent in detail to the Yanesha Yosoper narrative.

### The child-sorcerer complex

The Asháninka account says that a child may be approached in dreams by the soul of a human witch or by a demonic bird or insect in human appearance. The being gives the child a bone, thorn, or palm sliver and knocks it from the child’s hand. When it falls and becomes buried, someone becomes ill. Repeated dream instruction gradually makes the child an active sorcerer. Teachers identify intended victims as game, particularly agoutis, and are said to feed the child human flesh.

The Yanesha account differs: another witch may rub a child with a special plant, or the child may apply its juice; it penetrates the heart and imparts destructive capacity. A curved-billed hummingbird associated with evil shamans may also pass over the child.

Smith thought children could be “innocent victims” used by Yosoper and earthly demons. Santos-Granero’s later informants instead insisted that, though initiation was involuntary, initiated children consciously caused harm. These are conflicting ethnographic interpretations, not stages of a single canonical doctrine.

Yosoper/Korioshpiri seldom appears as a speaking protagonist here. The connection is hierarchical and etiological: malignant agents ultimately belong to his domain.

### Saint Christopher and the malignant child

Santos-Granero argues that a transformed Saint Christopher legend helped furnish an ideological model for child sorcery. Southern Arawak variants concern a cannibalistic giant transformerer and an evil son or grandson who refuses to grow, possesses adult powers, transforms relatives, eats humans, and can cause pursuers to injure one another.

He interprets the giant-and-child structure as a colonial appropriation of Saint Christopher carrying the Christ Child. Epidemics may have reversed the saints’ protective significance: instead of warding off plague, their images became associated with its arrival. The myth’s child then became more prominent during postcolonial measles and chickenpox epidemics, diseases that could kill adults at conspicuous rates.

This is an ingenious and evidence-based reconstruction, but it is not directly proved by a colonial text recording the transformation. It must remain **[modern scholarly reconstruction]**.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Cult institutions

**[Not established]** No temple, shrine, priesthood, festival, pilgrimage, sacrificial calendar, votive corpus, or regular worship dedicated to Yosoper or Korioshpiri has been documented.

The Yanesha did maintain ceremonial centers and temple/ironworks devoted to other divinities. A Palmazu center reportedly housed three stone divine figures, and its priest imitated parts of the Catholic Mass in the early twentieth century. This demonstrates ritual syncretism in Yanesha religion but is not evidence for a Yosoper sanctuary.

The beings are feared, explained, and diagnosed around; they are not shown receiving a positive public cult.

### Diagnosis

**[Early historical and secure ethnographic attestation]** Yanesha **pa’llerr** and Asháninka **seripiari** were specialists capable of diagnosing a child sorcerer. Sources describe:

- chewing coca;
- sucking tobacco juice;
- divinatory examination;
- inducing or interpreting dreams in which the patient sees the aggressor;
- searching the floor or surroundings for bones, thorns, slivers, hair, and other alleged sorcery objects.

Sala observed that such debris could easily be found on an earthen household floor and reported accusations that relatives planted it. Weiss’s informants countered that diagnosed materials were tied, leaf-wrapped, or otherwise intentionally prepared. The disagreement should not be resolved retrospectively.

### Punishment and killing

Children and vulnerable women accused of sorcery were beaten, confined, smoked, tortured to disclose buried objects, and sometimes killed. The accused were disproportionately orphans, captives, widows, unmarried women, and other people lacking an effective male defender. Asháninka reports especially emphasize girls; Yanesha reports are less uniform about gender.

These practices are historically documented. They should neither be dismissed as missionary fantasy nor generalized to every community or every period. Santos-Granero reports their disappearance among the Yanesha by the 1950s and among Nomatsiguenga by approximately the 1960s; Asháninka and Pajonal Ashéninka executions continued in some places until the mid-1960s.

### Sacrifice and offerings

**[Not established]** The killing of accused sorcerers was punitive and therapeutic in local interpretation, not a documented sacrifice to Yosoper/Korioshpiri. No human or animal offering to the ruler himself is securely attested. There is likewise no evidence of “temple prostitution.”

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before sustained European contact

A pre-contact Southern Arawak world of dangerous spirits, transformative beings, illness attribution, and malign shamans is highly plausible and supported comparatively. Whether a single ruler named Yosoper or Korioshpiri existed is **not established**.

### Seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries

Franciscan missions introduced systematic Christian preaching, the Devil/Lucifer opposition, plague-saint imagery, crosses, medals, and Catholic ritual. Indigenous communities selectively appropriated Christian forms. Smith and Weiss place the remodeling or naming of Yosoper/Korioshpiri in this context.

### 1742 and its aftermath

The Juan Santos Atahualpa uprising expelled missionaries from much of the Selva Central. Yanesha and Asháninka people retained and transformed selected Christian symbols during the ensuing period of autonomy. No surviving Juan Santos source located names Yosoper or Korioshpiri.

### 1847–1895: reconquest, epidemics, and sorcery accusations

Peruvian military and colonizing pressure resumed in 1847 and intensified after 1868. Epidemics, forced displacement, loss of ceremonial centers, and expanding plantations accompanied the reconquest. Reports of child sorcery first appear around 1880–1885.

Santos-Granero interprets the clustering of accusations at such moments as a response to otherwise inexplicable disease and violent social dislocation. This is a historical correlation supported by the documentary chronology, though individual accusations cannot be reduced to one cause.

### 1920s–1960s

Catholic and Adventist missions further Christianized Indigenous terminology. Ferdinand Stahl’s Adventist preaching inspired an Asháninka millenarian movement in the late 1920s, promising resurrection and an end to illness and death. Sorcery accusations remained documented through the mid-twentieth century but declined in the communities described after the 1950s–1960s.

### 1980–2000: internal armed conflict

Sendero Luminoso imposed recruitment, killing, sexual violence, hunger, displacement, and camp confinement on Asháninka and Nomatsiguenga communities. Later testimony sometimes classified Senderistas through the moral category *kamari*. Espinosa presents this as an Indigenous interpretive continuity linking malignant beings, epidemics, and organized violence.

That generalization is disputed. Mariella Villasante notes direct evidence from Nueva Esperanza in the Bajo Urubamba equating Senderistas with *kamari* but argues that extending it to all Asháninka society risks exoticizing diverse testimony. The secure conclusion is therefore local and plural: **some** Asháninka narrators used *kamari* to interpret Senderista cruelty; it is not demonstrably a universal Asháninka identification, still less a claim that Sendero personally served Korioshpiri.

A later strand reportedly identifies Korioshpiri/Koroshpiri as protective owner of the dangerous red outsiders or *kityonkari*, with former president Alan García imagined as their worldly patron and as protector of harmful corporations. This is a modern political-cosmological elaboration documented in particular Ene/Pichis testimony, not a pan-Asháninka canon.

### Twenty-first century

Yosoper remains present in Yanesha cultural and educational descriptions of cosmology. Korioshpiri remains intelligible to some Asháninka speakers as devil, Antichrist, or lord of malignant beings, while *kamari* continues as the more productive category for dangerous agency.

This is survival through oral teaching, ethnographic publication, education, and political interpretation—not evidence of a separately organized cult.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

No documented Golden Dawn, Theosophical, ceremonial-magic, chaos-magic, reconstructionist, or neopagan tradition centered on Yosoper or Korioshpiri was found. Claims of an ancient formal demon cult would be **modern invention unless independently sourced**.

A modern “Pantheon Registry” webpage classifies Yosoper as a continuing Yanesha deity. Its summary broadly follows Santos-Granero, but it is derivative and should not be used as primary evidence.

The Colombian metal band Lucifera’s 2019 album *La cacería de brujas* generated at least one English-language review retelling Korioshpiri and child-sorcerer material as pre-Christian demonic lore. That presentation is inaccurate in two respects:

1. the documented form of the tradition bears substantial Christian and colonial influence;
2. the historical depth of the child-sorcerer institution cannot securely be extended “for centuries” into the pre-Christian past.

No major film, novel, game, comic, nationalist movement, or commercial brand materially centered on either figure was located. Yosoper/Korioshpiri remain far less commercialized than Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, or Bufeo cycles.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### The Lucifer question

Smith regarded **Yos** as probably derived from Spanish *Dios* and **Yosoper** as possibly derived from *Lucifer*. Weiss proposed a more specific phonological development for Korioshpiri. Santos-Granero accepted substantial Christian influence while emphasizing that the beings were reorganized within Indigenous kinship and cosmological categories.

Against a wholly Christian derivation stands the Matsigenka comparison with Kientibakori. This permits at least three models:

1. **Christian-import model:** Lucifer became an Indigenous evil ruler.
2. **Renaming model:** an older Southern Arawak dangerous ruler received a Lucifer-derived name.
3. **Hybrid model:** Indigenous dangerous-being complexes and Christian demonology jointly produced the recorded figure.

The hybrid or renaming models currently fit the evidence better than a claim of either pristine pre-contact antiquity or simple missionary transplantation.

### Dating the child-sorcery institution

Santos-Granero argues for postcolonial consolidation because:

- colonial missionaries documented adult sorcery but remained silent about child executions;
- the first reports appear in the late nineteenth century;
- accusations intensify during epidemics, colonization, and violent upheaval;
- the Saint Christopher/Child motif offers a possible colonial ideological source.

The weakness is that documentary silence cannot prove nonexistence, and missionaries did not record everything evenly. His thesis is a strong historical reconstruction, not a demonstrated date of invention.

### Translation and category problems

Older publications use **Campa** and **Amuesha**, while current usage favors **Asháninka/Ashéninka** and **Yanesha**. “Campa” can obscure substantial linguistic and regional differentiation.

Likewise, *kamari*, “demon,” “witch,” and “sorcerer” are not exact equivalents. Santos-Granero used “witch” and “sorcerer” interchangeably because the classic Evans-Pritchard distinction did not fit the Southern Arawak material. A dossier should preserve the source’s terms and avoid turning a locally variable category into universal European demonology.

### Evidence state

There is no pre-Hispanic inscription, codex, temple image, or archaeological representation securely naming either being. The record is overwhelmingly:

- oral narratives recorded ethnographically;
- missionary and traveler descriptions of sorcery;
- twentieth-century anthropological fieldnotes, dissertations, and monographs;
- recent Indigenous testimony and educational summaries.

The principal evidentiary problem is therefore not a damaged ancient manuscript tradition but the dating of oral forms first written down in the twentieth century.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before 1635:** Dangerous spirits, sorcery, transformative beings, and illness etiologies likely existed among Southern Arawak peoples; the names Yosoper and Korioshpiri are **not securely attested**.

- **c. 1635:** Sustained Franciscan contact in the Selva Central begins. Christian God/Devil language, saints, crosses, and plague imagery enter the region.

- **1651–1750:** Mission documents report Indigenous religious specialists and sorcery but, in Santos-Granero’s survey, do not report child-sorcerer executions or name Yosoper/Korioshpiri.

- **1742:** Juan Santos Atahualpa’s rebellion expels missions from much of the Selva Central. Christian signs continue to be selectively preserved and transformed.

- **1750:** Juan de San Antonio reports Indigenous accusations that missionaries introduced deadly epidemics. The source does not securely name Yosoper or Korioshpiri.

- **1847:** Peruvian reconquest of the Selva Central begins.

- **1868–1878:** Military expeditions intensify territorial invasion and displacement.

- **1879–1881:** Yellow fever and measles affect the Yanesha; the first report of Yanesha child sorcery appears around 1880.

- **1885:** Smallpox strikes the upper Ucayali; Ordinaire records Asháninka accusations of child sorcery.

- **1893:** Sala documents divination, alleged planted objects, and punishment in the sorcery complex.

- **1921–1930s:** Adventist missionary Ferdinand Stahl’s work stimulates an Asháninka millenarian movement promising the destruction of evil and death.

- **1950s:** Child-sorcerer executions are reported to have ceased among the Yanesha.

- **1960–1964:** Gerald Weiss conducts Asháninka fieldwork and records Korioshpiri as ruler of Sharinkaueni’s demons.

- **Mid-1960s:** The execution of accused child sorcerers persists in some Asháninka/Ashéninka communities but subsequently declines in the ethnographic record.

- **1969:** Weiss’s dissertation supplies the earliest presently verified written attestation of Korioshpiri.

- **1975:** *Campa Cosmology* publishes the account and the proposed Lucifer etymology.

- **1977:** Smith’s dissertation provides the earliest presently verified detailed written account of Yosoper and the rival-creation myth.

- **1991:** Santos-Granero’s *The Power of Love* publishes the principal synthesis of Yanesha cosmology and morality.

- **2002:** “Saint Christopher in the Amazon” reconstructs the colonial and epidemic history of child-sorcery beliefs and compares Yosoper with Korioshpiri.

- **2003:** Santos-Granero again treats Yosoper in relation to Yanesha dream experience and Franciscan influence.

- **2012:** “Beinghood and People-Making in Native Amazonia” records continuing Yanesha variants of Yato’ Yos and Yosoper’s creation contest.

- **2019:** A metal-album review reproduces Korioshpiri material in a modern occult-entertainment frame.

- **2021–2024:** Scholarship debates how widely Asháninka narrators equate Sendero Luminoso with *kamari* and warns against converting localized testimony into a universal belief.

- **Present:** Yosoper and Korioshpiri survive in oral knowledge, Indigenous cultural descriptions, ethnography, and localized Christian or political reinterpretation. No organized cult, canonical iconography, or substantial neopagan revival is documented.

- Amich, José. *Historia de las misiones del convento de Santa Rosa de Ocopa*. Written/published in nineteenth-century form; edition 1975.
- González, 1880 report on Yanesha child sorcery, as cited and analyzed by Santos-Granero.
- Navarro, M. *La tribu Campa* (1924) and *La tribu Amuesha* (1924; later edition 1967).
- Ordinaire, Olivier. Reports dated 1885; later collected as *Del Pacífico al Atlántico y otros escritos*.
- Pérez Marcio, M. F. *Los hijos de la selva* (1953).
- Sala, Gabriel. Mission chronicle for 1882–1893, later published in the Ocopa history.
- San Antonio, Juan de. *Colección de informes sobre las misiones del Colegio de Santa Rosa de Ocopa* (1750).
- Shaver, Harold, and Lois Dodd. *Los Nomatsiguenga de la Selva Central* (1990).
- Smith, Richard Chase. *Deliverance from Chaos for a Song* (Cornell University dissertation, 1977).
- Weiss, Gerald. *The Cosmology of the Campa Indians of Eastern Peru* (1969); *Campa Cosmology* (1975).

### Negative findings from the requested comparative pass

The searches found no direct attestation of Yosoper/Korioshpiri in the Piro/Yine myths of Gow, Dean’s Urarina material, Loreto’s Bufeo–Chullachaqui–Yacuruna cycles, or Pishtaco scholarship. Nor was there reliable evidence for a temple cult, human sacrifice offered to the being, sexual rites, occult orders, modern reconstructionist worship, formal iconography, or a canonical interaction with the named solar and transformer gods of neighboring pantheons.
