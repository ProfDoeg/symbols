# Kashiri (the Moon): Research Dossier

**Class:** Male lunar deity, primordial person, culture hero, agricultural donor, cannibal or necrophagous receiver of the dead, depending on Asháninka/Ashéninka and neighboring Campan Arawakan traditions. Kashiri is not a goddess. In present-day speech, *kashiri* also simply means “moon” and, by extension, a lunar month.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest presently verifiable documentation of the associated ritual complex is Franciscan observation of Campa full-moon festivities published by Bernardino Izaguirre in 1924, drawing partly on earlier missionary records. Those passages attest a full-moon feast but do not, in the material available for this dossier, securely print the name *Kashiri*. The earliest securely traceable, extensive named documentation is Gerald Weiss’s Asháninka fieldwork of 1960–1964, submitted as a doctoral dissertation in 1969 and published in revised form as *Campa Cosmology* in 1975. Earlier Franciscan descriptions may preserve antecedents, but a pre-twentieth-century occurrence of the name itself has not been securely located.

**Evidentiary caution:** “Kashiri” should not be expanded into a generic “Amazonian moon god.” Closely related Matsigenka traditions use the cognate *Kashiri*, but Nomatsigenga *Manchakori* and Yanesha *Yachor Arrorr* are distinct lunar persons with different genders, genealogies, and narratives. Loreto’s Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, and related mestizo folklore cycles belong to still other traditions. Comparison is warranted; identity is not.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identification

In the most fully documented Asháninka versions, Kashiri was once an anthropomorphic male living on earth. He descended or appeared as a man, approached a young woman—frequently during her first-menstruation seclusion—gave her true manioc, married or impregnated her, and fathered the solar being usually called Katsirinkaiteri, Pawa, or Pavá. The woman died or burned during the Sun’s birth. Kashiri subsequently revealed a cannibal nature, eating relatives or the woman’s corpse, and withdrew into the sky. From there he continues to consume dead people or their souls. His waxing may be explained as the gradual filling of his belly; his markings may be a swallowed figure, a torn garment, blood, body remnants, or pigment, depending on the narrative.

This is not a single canon. Weiss recorded several versions, and later Asháninka communities explicitly disagreed about whether Kashiri fathered the Sun or was himself created by an eternally existing Pawa. Among Asháninka of the Amônia River, Kashiri can function less as the final destroyer of souls than as a transporter who swallows the dead at new moon and carries them onward for purification and selection by Pawa.

### Names and linguistic forms

- **Kashiri / Kashíri** — Asháninka and Ashéninka “moon”; masculine in the mythology. The Peruvian Ministry of Education’s current Asháninka orthographic manual prescribes initial capitalization when the word denotes the mythic person: *Kashiri*, not *kashiri*. **[Secure modern linguistic documentation]**
- **cashiri / casiri** — Older Spanish-language spellings reflecting differing transcription conventions. **[Colonial/modern orthographic variant]**
- **kaširi** — Linguistic transcription used in comparative Arawakan materials. Comparative lists reconstruct or posit a Campan/Arawakan form approximately *kasiri*, but the ultimate etymology has not been securely established. **[Modern linguistic reconstruction]**
- **Kashiri Ash / Kashiri Aah** — A male and female lunar pair reported in a modern Asháninka anthroponymic study. They marry and produce four children representing lunar phases. This system is not found in the classic Weiss corpus and should be treated as a local or recent learned elaboration, not as the primordial Asháninka canon. **[Regional or modern systematization]**
- **Kashiri, “Moon”** — The usual gloss; it is a proper name and the ordinary celestial noun. The semantic extension to “month” reflects lunar time-reckoning. **[Secure lexical usage]**
- **Pichokiti / Pichókiti, “Seven-Colored Bird”** — In one collected Anato/Ayacucho narrative, the bird who impregnates the woman assumes or reveals the identity Kashiri. This is a narrative identification, not a universally attested epithet. **[Regional variant]**
- **Pawa/Pavá** — Normally the Sun or a high paternal deity, not an alternative name of Kashiri. Sources that speak loosely of “the father” can obscure this distinction. **[Source-dependent classification]**

No securely attested ancient script, pre-Columbian inscription, indigenous manuscript, temple dedication, or theophoric personal name for Kashiri is known. All surviving evidence is post-contact: missionary writing, ethnography, dictionaries, educational publications, and continuing oral testimony.

### Neighboring lunar names that must not be conflated

- **Manchakori** — Nomatsigenga Moon, male creator or “father of all,” also equated in some modern accounts with *Tosorintsi*, a wonder-working being. Shaver and Dodd’s corpus and later Nomatsigenga testimony place Manchakori in a different cosmogonic structure. **[Related Campan tradition, not Kashiri’s proper name]**
- **Yachor Arrorr**, “Our Mother Moon” — Yanesha female lunar divinity, sister and wife of Yompor Ror. Her gender, genealogy, and historical-territorial saga distinguish her sharply from male Kashiri. **[Distinct Southern Arawakan tradition]**
- **Arrorr** — Yanesha “Moon”; not an Asháninka synonym.
- Matsigenka **Kashiri** — A genuine cognate name and closely parallel mythic person, but Matsigenka versions must remain labeled as Matsigenka rather than silently added to an Asháninka composite.

No indigenous equivalent of *interpretatio graeca* or *interpretatio romana* is documented. Christian equations—Kashiri as Father, the woman as Santa María, and the Sun as Jesus—are colonial or missionary-period syncretisms, not timeless equivalences.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The documentary problem

Asháninka myth was transmitted orally. “Composition date” therefore cannot be equated with the date an ethnographer wrote a performance down. A 1962 field text is secure evidence that a particular narrator gave that version in the early 1960s; it does not establish that the story originated then. Conversely, resemblance to other Arawakan moon narratives suggests historical depth but does not supply a date.

There is no known pre-contact writing system, inscription, codex, or archaeological image labeled Kashiri. Identification of prehistoric lunar imagery with him would therefore be speculative.

### Missionary and travel sources

#### Franciscan records, seventeenth–nineteenth centuries

Biedma, Amich, the 1750 Franciscan reports, San Antonio, Ordinaire, Sala, and later compilations document missionary penetration, reductions, revolt, displacement, evangelization, and Campa/Amuesha practices. They are indispensable context for the conditions under which later lunar traditions were recorded. Yet the accessible texts examined here did not yield a securely verifiable early named occurrence of *Kashiri*. **Absence of a name in an accessible missionary description is not proof that the being was absent from oral tradition.**

Missionary nomenclature—“idol,” “superstition,” “devil,” “bacchanal”—must be read as Christian polemical classification rather than indigenous taxonomy.

#### Izaguirre, *Historia de las misiones franciscanas* (1922–1929)

Izaguirre reports Campa full-moon festivities in which participants dressed ceremonially, carried bows and arrows, drank manioc beer, and performed mock combats. A passage dated in modern citation to volume/page “1924:292” describes a full-moon *masateada* and a game of feigned arrow combat. Another passage emphasizes that baptized mission residents continued the feast. **[Secure early twentieth-century observation or compilation; Kashiri’s name in the quoted passage remains unverified]**

#### Navarro, *La tribu Campa* (1924)

Navarro belongs to the same documentary moment and supplies ethnographic-missionary background. A specific Kashiri passage has not been verified here. It should not be cited as direct evidence for the lunar mythology without page-level confirmation.

#### Pérez Marcio, *Los hijos de la selva* (1953)

This is part of the mid-century representational corpus, but no page-specific Kashiri account was securely recovered in the present search. It is therefore bibliographic context rather than evidence for a particular claim.

### Gerald Weiss

Weiss conducted approximately thirty months of fieldwork between 1960 and 1964. His dissertation, *The Cosmology of the Campa Indians of Eastern Peru* (University of Michigan, 1969), became *Campa Cosmology: The World of a Forest Tribe in South America* (American Museum of Natural History, 1975, pp. 217–588).

This remains the foundational corpus because Weiss supplies Asháninka-language texts, close translations, narrators’ variants, and a systematic account of the layered cosmos. His Kashiri material, especially around pp. 264–284 in the published study, establishes:

- Kashiri is male.
- He existed on earth in anthropomorphic form.
- Human beings subsisted on earth or termite nests before receiving manioc.
- He approached a young woman, often his sister’s daughter.
- He fathered the Sun.
- The mother burned or died giving birth.
- Kashiri killed and ate nephews or other people.
- He fled upward when exposed or threatened.
- He continues to consume the dead.
- Lunar phases can be explained by his filling and emptying.
- Aroshi’s figure or remains can account for the marks seen at full moon.

Weiss’s material is **securely attested twentieth-century oral tradition**, not a verbatim survival from a datable pre-Columbian text.

### Torre López, Varese, Rojas Zolezzi, and later Asháninka scholarship

Fernando Torre López’s “Fenomenología de la tribu anti o campa” (1966) and Stefano Varese’s “Dos versiones cosmogónicas campa” (1970) belong to the early post-Weiss analytical corpus. Rojas Zolezzi’s work develops the relationship between personhood, death, knowledge, and Kashiri’s trap for the dead.

Rojas Zolezzi reports that souls traveling along a river toward the country of the dead must avoid a great fishing trap made by Kashiri. Those caught are eaten and retained in his stomach. Knowledge matters: persons with insufficient cosmological knowledge—represented in the account especially as children and women—are more vulnerable. **[Regional ethnographic account; recorded late twentieth century]**

### Matsigenka corpus

Gerhard Baer’s work, Renard-Casevitz’s studies, and later syntheses preserve a closely related Matsigenka complex. In a full Matsigenka version:

1. Kashiri approaches a secluded menstruating girl who has been eating earth.
2. He supplies manioc and other cultivated foods.
3. He teaches preparation, including chewing or fermenting manioc.
4. He marries the girl.
5. She bears luminous sons.
6. She dies in childbirth.
7. Kashiri gives instructions that could have restored her.
8. Her mother rejects or insults him and tells him to eat the corpse.
9. He removes or preserves the living child and eats the woman.
10. He returns to the sky, taking the finest manioc.
11. He later traps and consumes souls of the dead.

Some accounts enumerate four sons: the visible Sun, Venus, the underworld Sun, and a firmament Sun that lights the stars. This fourfold solar genealogy is Matsigenka, not securely Asháninka. **[Related regional variant]**

Matsigenka agricultural theology treats manioc, plantain, maize, and sweet potato as Kashiri’s daughters. If people mistreat the crops, the plants may complain to their father, who can withdraw them to the sky. **[Matsigenka ethnographic tradition]**

### Nomatsigenga corpus

Shaver and Dodd’s *Los Nomatsiguenga de la selva central* (1990) documents the distinct Manchakori complex. Recent Nomatsigenga writers describe Manchakori as creator of beings, father of Navireri, teacher of the ancestors, and source of instructions by which crushing primordial darkness became ordinary night. This corpus is comparative evidence for a widespread male-Moon complex among Campan speakers, not permission to rename Manchakori “Kashiri.”

### Yanesha corpus

Richard Chase Smith’s 1977 dissertation and Fernando Santos-Granero’s *The Power of Love* (1991) document Yanesha ritual, sacred music, political theology, and mythology. Yachor Arrorr is female, “Our Mother Moon,” and is paired with Yompor Ror, “Our Father Sun.” Their birth initiates normal human reproduction and their ascent helps inaugurate the present cosmic-historical era. **[Distinct Yanesha tradition]**

Santos-Granero’s “Saint Christopher in the Amazon” (2002) analyzes how a Catholic image and story were transformed among Southern Arawak peoples into traditions about a cannibal giant and dangerous child. It demonstrates that Southern Arawak mythology absorbed colonial imagery creatively. It does **not** establish that Kashiri derives from Saint Christopher.

### Yine/Piro corpus

Peter Gow’s *Of Mixed Blood* and *An Amazonian Myth and Its History* analyze Yine/Piro myth as historical transformation rather than as a fossilized canon. Gow records a moon story in which Moon came to earth, slept with a girl, and received dark facial markings made with huito pigment. He also records narrators forgetting, refusing, or no longer knowing previously collected narratives. The evidentiary lesson is critical: disappearance or transformation in present narration is part of myth’s history, not necessarily ethnographic “error.”

### Loreto and Ucayali folklore

Regan, Galeano, Cabrejos, Chirif, and regional collections document Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, Pishtaco, and other beings. These cycles illuminate the broader environment of riverine and mestizo folklore but do not securely document Kashiri as a participant. No textual interaction between Kashiri and Bufeo, Chullachaqui, or Yacuruna was found. **[Absence of evidence]**

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

#### Moon and lunar time

Kashiri is first the Moon as a person, not merely “god of” a separate physical object. His changing body explains the lunar phases. *Kashiri* can also designate a month, linking him to practical time measurement and agricultural scheduling. **[Secure linguistic and ethnographic attestation]**

#### Manioc and cultivated food

The most stable beneficent role is donor of manioc, *kaniri*. Before his intervention, humans eat earth or termite nests. By giving actual manioc, Kashiri converts them into properly eating human beings. In different versions he also gives maize, plantain, sweet potato, and the knowledge necessary to plant, cook, chew, or ferment them. **[Asháninka core with Matsigenka elaborations]**

This is more than a simple “agriculture god” function. Manioc links:

- women’s labor;
- menarche and reproductive maturity;
- marriage and affinity;
- cooking and fermentation;
- hospitality through manioc beer;
- social humanity as opposed to undifferentiated primordial existence;
- mortality, because the gift accompanies the fatal birth of the Sun.

#### Death and consumption

Kashiri is an anthropophage in narratives where he murders and eats living relatives. In later cosmological descriptions he more precisely becomes a necrophage: he eats corpses, souls, or already dead humans. Hvalkof and Veber explicitly prefer “necrophagous” for the Gran Pajonal conception while acknowledging the earlier narrative cannibalism. **[Variant and analytical distinction]**

Among Amônia Asháninka, ingestion may be transport rather than annihilation. Kashiri swallows the dead at new moon, takes them to Pitsitsiroyki, and gives them to a star for washing and anointing; Pawa later chooses legitimate children. **[Regional variant]**

#### Reproduction and menarche

The young woman is commonly secluded at first menstruation. Kashiri’s arrival joins lunar periodicity, female maturation, cultivated food, marriage, pregnancy, and cosmic reproduction. Modern educational materials describe him as connected with reproduction. **[Secure thematic association; not evidence for a centralized fertility cult]**

#### Eclipse

Some Amônia accounts say Kashiri periodically fights the Sun during eclipses. In another Asháninka narrative, Kashiri chokes while eating the dead; his two wives help him, and people make noise during an eclipse to assist them. These are distinct explanations and should not be harmonized. **[Regional variants]**

### Physical form

- On earth: adult Asháninka man, sometimes strikingly handsome.
- Matsigenka: handsome young man wearing a yellow feather crown.
- Some Asháninka narratives emphasize an abnormally large penis carried in his shoulder bag, his pursuit of nieces, and incestuous sexuality.
- In the sky: the visible lunar disk, with markings interpreted narratively.

### Lunar markings

The markings are not governed by one canonical explanation:

- Aroshi is visible in Kashiri’s stomach at full moon.
- Aroshi tore Kashiri’s tunic while trying to cut a piece from him.
- A woman marked Moon’s face with huito in Yine/Piro tradition.
- Blood marks Moon’s face in a Matsigenka version.
- Remains of the consumed wife, or portions not eaten, mark the disk in some retellings.

These are local explanatory myths, not interchangeable iconographic dogma.

### Emblems and material iconography

Kashiri has no securely documented ancient statue type, temple image, weapon, sacred animal, canonical color, or fixed emblem comparable to Mediterranean state cults. His principal visible sign is the Moon itself.

Associated objects include:

- manioc;
- manioc beer or *masato/piyarentsi*;
- a fishing weir or soul-trap;
- feather crown in Matsigenka narration;
- Aroshi’s necklace or resurrecting amulet;
- bow and arrows in full-moon festivities;
- the menarche hut;
- the Milky Way or celestial river in which celestial persons bathe.

Modern paintings of Kashiri are artistic interpretations, not surviving pre-Columbian cult images.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Competing genealogies

#### Asháninka version A: Moon fathers Sun

Kashiri impregnates an Asháninka woman, often his sister’s daughter. She gives birth to Katsirinkaiteri/Pawa and burns or dies. Kashiri is consequently Sun’s father. **[Weiss corpus; securely attested twentieth-century oral tradition]**

#### Asháninka version B: Pawa precedes and creates Moon

Amônia informants told later ethnographers that Pawa always existed and created everything, including Kashiri. This directly reverses the Weiss genealogy. Christian influence may have encouraged a supreme creator model, but the evidence does not permit reducing every such statement to missionary corruption. **[Regional variant; possible syncretistic transformation]**

#### Syncretic version: Father, Mary, and Jesus

Some versions reported through Weiss identify the woman as Santa María and the solar child with Jesus, producing an indigenous-Christian triad: Kashiri as father, Mary as mother, Katsirinkaiteri/Jesus as son. This is datable only to the post-contact Christian environment and should be labeled syncretism. **[Christian-period elaboration]**

#### Matsigenka four sons

Kashiri fathers the visible Sun, Venus, an underworld Sun, and a firmament Sun. **[Matsigenka regional variant]**

#### Modern paired moons

Oitasatsi and Tasorentsi create male Kashiri Ash and female Kashiri Aah, marry them in Jananeria, and they produce four children personifying lunar phases. **[Modern/local systematization; absent from the classic corpus]**

### Every documented named divine interaction

- **Katsirinkaiteri / Pawa / Pavá (Sun):** Kashiri fathers the Sun by an Asháninka woman; the mother burns or dies at the solar birth. Weiss, fieldwork 1960–1964, dissertation 1969, publication 1975. **[Securely attested ethnographic core]**
- **Pawa (creator-Sun):** In Amônia accounts, Pawa instead precedes and creates Kashiri; after death he selects those brought through Kashiri’s lunar transit. Late twentieth-century Brazilian Asháninka ethnography. **[Regional variant]**
- **Katsirinkaiteri/Pawa (Sun):** Kashiri and Sun periodically fight, explaining eclipses. Amônia Asháninka account reported in modern ethnographic synthesis. **[Regional variant]**
- **Aroshi:** Aroshi, a resurrecting trickster or *tasorintsi*, seeks a piece of Kashiri to replace a damaged necklace or revive a dead boy. Kashiri tries to swallow him; Aroshi escapes after tearing Kashiri’s garment. In another summary Kashiri succeeds in devouring him, and Aroshi’s remains are visible at full moon. Weiss 1975; Rojas Zolezzi, later twentieth century. **[Attested competing variants]**
- **Conkíri / Conkiri (Hummingbird):** Conkíri steals Aroshi’s necklace, prompting Aroshi’s expedition to Kashiri. Weiss 1975. Kashiri and Conkíri do not necessarily meet directly; their relation is mediated through Aroshi. **[Attested narrative linkage, not direct encounter]**
- **Pichokiti, the Seven-Colored Bird:** In an Anato/Ayacucho story, Pichokiti impregnates the woman and subsequently assumes or is revealed as Kashiri. Recent collection cited in studies of Asháninka women and myth. **[Regional identification]**
- **Oitasatsi:** Creates Kashiri Ash and helps arrange the lunar marriage. Modern anthroponymic corpus. **[Modern/local systematization]**
- **Tasorentsi:** Co-creates the paired lunar beings and arranges their marriage in Jananeria. Modern anthroponymic corpus. **[Modern/local systematization]**
- **Kashiri Ash and Kashiri Aah:** The male and female lunar beings marry one another and produce four phase-children. Modern anthroponymic corpus. **[Modern/local systematization]**
- **Ash, Ashe, Ashi, Asho:** Children of the paired moons, representing new moon, waxing quarter, full moon, and waning quarter. Modern anthroponymic corpus. **[Modern/local systematization]**
- **Unnamed star at Pitsitsiroyki:** Kashiri delivers swallowed dead people to a star who washes, perfumes, and keeps them for Pawa. Because the star is not given a proper name in the accessible account, this cannot become a cross-reference to a named being. **[Regional Amônia variant]**
- **Avireri / Navireri:** Both occupy Campan mythic history, but no direct Kashiri–Avireri encounter was located in the verified corpus. Joint membership in a pantheon is not an interaction. **[Absence of evidence]**
- **Manchakori:** Cognate-type Nomatsigenga Moon, not a being who meets Kashiri. **[Comparative relation only]**
- **Yachor Arrorr:** Yanesha Moon, not a documented interaction partner or secure equation. **[Comparative relation only]**
- **Yompor Ror:** Yanesha Sun and Arrorr’s brother-husband; no interaction with Kashiri. **[Absence of evidence]**
- **Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, Pishtaco:** No verified story makes any of these figures meet Kashiri. **[Absence of evidence]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The coming of Moon and the gift of manioc

**Tier:** Securely attested twentieth-century Asháninka core, with Matsigenka and other Campan variants.

Primordial humans do not know proper food. They eat earth, clay, or termite nests and may swallow rather than chew. Kashiri appears as a man to a young woman, commonly isolated in a menarche hut. When he asks what she is eating, she calls the substance manioc. He corrects her: it is only earth. He supplies true manioc, which she and her relatives prefer. He teaches cultivation and, in fuller Matsigenka versions, processing and fermentation.

The girl becomes his wife or sexual partner. This union produces the Sun. Agriculture and celestial differentiation consequently arise through an affinal and reproductive encounter.

Interpretations that call Kashiri simply an “agriculture god” miss the story’s structure: cultivated food, menstruation, marriage, cooking, and human sociality arrive together.

### Birth of the Sun and death of the mother

**Tier:** Securely attested Asháninka/Matsigenka cycle; endings vary.

The woman becomes pregnant by Kashiri. At delivery, the solar child’s heat burns or kills her. In some Matsigenka accounts, Kashiri tells her mother how the woman can be restored. The mother-in-law, furious with him, rejects the procedure and tells him to eat her daughter. Kashiri removes or preserves the child, consumes the corpse, and departs.

Matsigenka tellers use the mother-in-law’s refusal to explain the origin of permanent human mortality: had she permitted Kashiri to revive his wife, resurrection might thereafter have been possible for women. This explicit etiological conclusion should not automatically be attributed to Asháninka versions that lack it.

### Kashiri the cannibal and ascent to heaven

**Tier:** Weiss corpus; Asháninka regional variants.

Kashiri takes nephews or other male relatives hunting, kills them, and eats them. When his behavior is discovered, a brother-in-law or other relative threatens him. Kashiri escapes upward and becomes the celestial Moon.

This episode bridges living anthropophagy and the later consumption of the dead. It also makes his withdrawal a consequence of failed affinity: the giver of cultivated food proves unable to live safely among humans.

### Aroshi and the markings on the Moon

**Tier:** Asháninka regional cycle, especially Perené; divergent versions.

Aroshi possesses a necklace or charm with which he can restore life. Conkíri steals or damages it, or the charm breaks during an attempted rescue of a drowned boy. A piece of Kashiri’s brilliant body could replace it. Aroshi or the bereaved woman waits along Moon’s route and tries to strike or cut him. Kashiri mocks the woman by wearing her as a necklace. Aroshi attempts to cut a piece from his grandfather Kashiri; Kashiri tries to swallow him. Aroshi escapes in one version, tearing Kashiri’s tunic, while summaries of another say Kashiri eats him and his figure is visible in the full moon.

The versions cannot be reconciled without destroying their evidentiary value. They separately explain lunar markings, the full moon’s interior figure, and the loss or limitation of resurrection.

### Kashiri’s trap and the fate of the dead

**Tier:** Late twentieth-century Asháninka ethnography; regional variants.

Souls travel toward a land of the dead along a river. Kashiri erected a large fishing weir or trap. Those without sufficient knowledge or spirit assistance fall into it and are eaten, ending or compromising their continued existence.

In the Amônia version, the sequence is less terminal. The dead first remain below the earth. At new moon Kashiri swallows them and transports them to Pitsitsiroyki, where a star purifies and preserves them. Pawa periodically chooses those he recognizes as legitimate children.

These are different eschatologies: annihilating consumption, imprisonment in the lunar stomach, and protective transport must be distinguished.

### Waxing and waning

**Tier:** Ethnographically attested explanatory tradition.

Waxing can represent Kashiri’s stomach filling as he consumes dead people. Waning represents digestion or emptying. Elsewhere the lunar phases are formulated as separate children of a paired male and female Moon. The latter is a modern/local classification and should not be projected backward onto Weiss’s account.

### Eclipses

**Tier:** Regional variants.

One account describes Moon and Sun fighting. Another describes Kashiri choking on corpses while his two wives struggle to save him; people shout and make noise to assist them. The historical record supports both as reported explanations, not one synthetic eclipse doctrine.

### Yine/Piro moon-marking myth

**Tier:** Related Southern Arawakan comparison, not Kashiri canon.

Moon visits a girl who avoids local men. She sleeps with him and paints or marks his face with huito, producing the dark features seen on the full moon. Matsigenka stories substitute blood in some versions. Gow’s later inability to elicit an earlier moon narrative from the same narrator demonstrates historical forgetting and the instability of oral repertoires.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No temple institution

No dedicated Kashiri temple, permanent shrine, centralized priesthood, state endowment, cult statue, pilgrimage center, or archaeological sanctuary is securely documented. Terms such as “cult” and “pantheon” can therefore mislead if they imply an institution like an ancient Mediterranean temple system.

Religious expertise was exercised by *sheripiari* shamans, household elders, singers, and ritual hosts. Kashiri participated in a lived cosmology organized through gardens, food, kinship, celestial observation, healing, song, and communal drinking.

### Full-moon festivities

Franciscan accounts collected by Izaguirre describe a Campa feast at full moon:

- ceremonial dress;
- bows and arrows;
- families attending together;
- mock combat or “quites”;
- drinking *masato*;
- persistence after baptism.

A later Adventist memoir attributed to Ferdinand Stahl describes a “great feast of the full moon” with a central fire. Children, women, and men circled it in sequence; meat was gestured or offered toward the Moon before being eaten; the night ended with heavy drinking of manioc beer. The account is valuable but mission-authored and rhetorically calls the rite “strange.” Its language should not be mistaken for an indigenous self-description.

An Asháninka recollection mentions a *sheripiari* called Uma with a large elevated house “where they held their feasts to the Moon.” Another account associates full moon with shamans bringing the dead down. These are locally remembered practices, not evidence for a universal Asháninka liturgy.

### Manioc beer

The *masateada* or *piyarentsi* is the most concrete ritual-social continuation of the manioc complex. Women prepare fermented manioc drink; dispersed households assemble, exchange hospitality, sing, dance, renew relations, and settle obligations. Not every modern *masateada* is an act directed explicitly to Kashiri. Treating all manioc drinking as “Moon worship” would overstate the evidence.

### Menarche

Girls’ seclusion, dietary discipline, hair cutting, bathing, and reintroduction into adult life form the social setting of the manioc myth. The myth supplies a precedent connecting menstruation, cultivated food, and adult womanhood. Whether every historical menarche rite consciously reenacted Kashiri’s marriage cannot be established.

### Offerings and sacrifice

Animal meat offered or gestured toward the Moon is attested in Stahl’s missionary memoir. Manioc beer, song, dance, and food are more broadly documented.

No verified evidence was found for **human sacrifice to Kashiri**. Mythic killing, cannibalism, and consumption of the dead are not evidence that worshippers ritually killed humans for him.

No evidence was found for temple prostitution, organized sexual service, or a sacrificial priesthood. The sexual content of the myths—incest, pursuit of nieces, and relations with a menstruating girl—must not be converted into an invented ritual institution.

### Eclipse action

Noise-making during an eclipse is explained in one narrative as assistance to Kashiri’s wives when he chokes. The practice is therefore participatory cosmology rather than passive observation. Its geographic distribution remains insufficiently established.

### Images and votives

No securely identified ancient votive image exists. The Moon itself is the primary manifestation. Modern paintings and school illustrations are reception history, not archaeological cult objects.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before sustained European documentation

Comparative linguistic cognates and related Campan narratives suggest that a male lunar person named from a form like *kasiri* predates modern ethnography. That is a reasonable historical reconstruction, not a directly dated attestation. The manioc-donor cycle may likewise be old, but there is no manuscript chronology by which to isolate its pre-contact wording.

### Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: missions and revolt

Franciscan expansion into the central selva brought reductions, Christian instruction, epidemics, labor demands, military expeditions, and resistance. Santa Rosa de Ocopa, founded in 1725, became a major missionary center.

Juan Santos Atahualpa’s uprising beginning in 1742 expelled missions from much of the central selva. Sources collected by Loayza and analyzed by Zarzar illuminate prophetic and anti-colonial transformations among Asháninka and neighboring peoples. No secure evidence makes Kashiri the uprising’s patron or identifies Juan Santos with Kashiri. Claims of such an equation would be modern invention unless a primary passage can be produced.

### Nineteenth century: renewed mission contact

Franciscan re-entry, exploration, rubber extraction, and frontier colonization reshaped settlement and transmission. Ordinaire, Sala, and related authors record regional knowledge through European categories. Their silence or brevity concerning Kashiri cannot measure the importance of oral lunar traditions.

### Early twentieth century: documented full-moon practice

Izaguirre’s 1924 compilation supplies the earliest securely recoverable description of Campa full-moon festivities used in this dossier. The practice persisted among baptized mission inhabitants, demonstrating that conversion was neither immediate cultural erasure nor simple continuity: ritual could continue under transformed meanings.

Adventist and Protestant missions increasingly translated Christianity into Arawakan languages. Some missionaries opposed older practice; others attended established festivals as opportunities to preach. This helps explain later narratives that retain Kashiri while subordinating him to a creator God or translating the solar family into Christian genealogy.

### 1960s–1970s: ethnographic stabilization

Weiss’s fieldwork converted variable performances into an extensive printed corpus. His achievement also created a scholarly “classic” against which later narratives were measured. “Weiss’s Kashiri” is thus both indigenous oral evidence and a product of ethnographic selection and publication.

Torre López, Varese, and other researchers broadened the corpus. During the same period, linguistic missions and schools standardized written Asháninka forms.

### Late twentieth century: variation, Christianization, and reclassification

Later informants sometimes said Kashiri “used to be a god” but is now simply the Moon or “only a story.” Others retained him as giver of manioc, father of the Sun, eater or transporter of the dead. This is evidence of active religious change, not proof that earlier ethnographers fabricated him.

At the Amônia River, a high creator Pawa could precede Kashiri. Elsewhere Christian figures entered the lunar genealogy. These represent syncretistic transformations with no single endpoint.

### Armed conflict, 1965 and 1980–2000

Brown and Fernández’s *War of Shadows* documents the 1965 Asháninka encounter with Marxist guerrillas and the struggle over competing utopian projects. It does not establish a Kashiri-centered insurgent theology.

The Shining Path period caused a demographic and cultural catastrophe. Peru’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission and the International Committee of the Red Cross report that, from an Asháninka population of roughly 55,000, approximately 10,000 were displaced, 5,000 held captive, and 6,000 killed. Both insurgent coercion and state counterinsurgency transformed settlement, authority, ritual transmission, and the conditions under which myths could be narrated.

No verified evidence shows Shining Path appropriating Kashiri as an emblem. The conflict belongs in Kashiri’s historical dossier because it killed narrators, dispersed communities, interrupted ritual life, and reconfigured the social transmission of the tradition—not because Kashiri caused or ideologically governed it.

### Twenty-first century

Kashiri remains present in oral narration, intercultural bilingual education, dictionaries, Indigenous cultural programs, anthropological scholarship, and visual art. Current educational publications capitalize *Kashiri* as a named historical-mythic person and distinguish him from the common celestial noun.

There is continuing Indigenous religious and cultural practice, but no reliable numerical estimate exists for “worshippers of Kashiri.” Census affiliation, Christian identity, and continued participation in Asháninka cosmology are not mutually exclusive.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature

Mario Vargas Llosa’s *El hablador* (*The Storyteller*, 1987; English translation 1989) literaryizes a Matsigenka version of Kashiri’s arrival, the primordial absence of cultivated food, the menstruating girl, and the gift of manioc. It materially widened the myth’s international readership but is a novel, not a primary ethnographic transcript. **[Modern literary retelling]**

### Visual art

Contemporary Amazonian and Peruvian artists, including work associated in recent scholarship with Gino Ceccarelli and student depictions from Sampantuari, visualize Kashiri anthropomorphically. Such paintings are legitimate modern cultural production, but their composition, colors, and anatomy are not evidence of an ancient canonical iconography.

### Education and heritage

Modern bilingual curricula teach Kashiri, Aroshi, and the associated narratives as culturally significant named persons. This institutionalizes a written, standardized version of traditions previously transmitted principally through performance. Capitalization rules are themselves part of this modern textual history.

### Neopaganism, occultism, and esotericism

The dedicated searches found no reliable evidence that Kashiri entered Renaissance hermeticism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, established chaos-magic manuals, Wicca, or an organized international neopagan cult.

Recent internet “pantheon registries” package Kashiri as a discrete deity with standardized domains, relatives, and status. These pages can help locate references but often flatten Asháninka and Matsigenka variants into a game-like profile. They are **modern digital systematizations**, not indigenous canonical texts.

No credible evidence was found for a continuous secret occult order, a pre-Columbian Kashiri temple tradition, or modern mass revivalism. Claims of that kind require documentation.

### New Age and psychological interpretation

Modern writers sometimes interpret the Moon, menstruation, and cassava through generalized fertility symbolism, Jungian archetypes, or claims about lunar effects on hormones and behavior. These are modern interpretive overlays. They are not traditional teachings unless independently documented from named Asháninka narrators.

### Political appropriation

No established nationalist, fascist, communist, or extremist Kashiri symbolism was located. Asháninka cultural organizations may use celestial and mythic imagery in heritage or territorial advocacy, but that should not be mislabeled “extremist appropriation.”

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Kashiri a god, spirit, ancestor, or celestial person?

“God” is convenient but imperfect. Weiss and many later authors call him a god; narrators can present him as an earlier Asháninka man, *tasorintsi*-type being, or the animate Moon. Some contemporary speakers demote the theological classification and call him only the physical moon or a story. Classification therefore changed with region, language, Christianity, and the interviewer’s categories.

### Cannibal or necrophage?

Weiss’s narratives support literal cannibalism: Kashiri kills living relatives and eats them. Later cosmology emphasizes consumption of the already dead. Hvalkof and Veber therefore argue that “necrophagous” more precisely describes the contemporary celestial function, while “cannibal” remains correct for the primordial narrative. Both terms are justified when tied to their respective sources.

### Father of Sun or creation of Sun?

Weiss’s corpus makes Kashiri Sun’s father. Amônia narrators sometimes make Pawa eternal and creator of Kashiri. Matsigenka accounts expand the children into multiple Suns and Venus. Christianized narratives map the family onto Father–Mary–Jesus. There is no source-critical basis for declaring one timelessly orthodox.

### Ancient core or Christian borrowing?

The wide Campan distribution of male lunar figures and manioc-gift narratives supports an indigenous historical complex. Christian material—Mary, Jesus, creator-God hierarchy—is demonstrably post-contact. Santos-Granero’s analysis of Saint Christopher proves that colonial images could be transformed into Southern Arawak mythology, but it does not prove that Kashiri himself began as Saint Christopher.

### Is Yachor Arrorr “the same goddess”?

No. Yachor Arrorr is female, Yanesha, and embedded in the Ror-Arrorr sibling-spouse cycle. Kashiri is male in classic Asháninka and Matsigenka accounts. Treating both as one being because both are lunar erases precisely the historical and regional differences that the evidence preserves.

### Is Manchakori simply another spelling of Kashiri?

The names may belong to related Campan linguistic and mythological history, but they are not interchangeable in source citation. Nomatsigenga narratives make Manchakori a more expansive creator and father of Navireri. Comparative relationship is secure; personal identity is not.

### Does the full-moon feast prove a formal lunar cult?

It proves communal full-moon ritual and, in later testimony, food offerings or gestures to the Moon. It does not prove temples, professional lunar priests, compulsory doctrine, or a centralized ecclesiastical organization. “Festival of the Moon” in popular summaries can sound more standardized than the evidence allows.

### Etymology

Modern dictionaries securely gloss *kashiri* as “moon.” Comparative Arawakan work connects Asháninka/Ashéninka/Matsigenka forms and suggests an older *kasiri*-type root. No persuasive derivation from a transparently analyzable native phrase was located. Popular etymologies should therefore be marked **unestablished**.

### Oral variability and “forgetting”

Gow’s work is important because it shows that failure to retell an earlier myth is historical evidence. Narratives can lose relevance, merge, change genre, or be displaced by Christianity, schooling, violence, and altered social life. Researchers should not manufacture a static canon from all versions ever printed.

### Problematic modern claims

The following are unsupported or overgeneralized:

- Kashiri is “the male moon throughout the entire Peruvian Amazon.” Many Amazonian traditions have female moons or unrelated lunar persons.
- Yachor Arrorr is another name for Kashiri.
- Kashiri possessed monumental temples.
- Humans were historically sacrificed to him.
- Every *masateada* is formal Kashiri worship.
- The four lunar-phase children belong to all Asháninka traditions.
- Kashiri is derived from Saint Christopher.
- Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, and Kashiri form one coherent pantheon.
- The Shining Path appropriated Kashiri.
- Modern internet deity profiles are ancient canonical theology.

### Assessment of named but non-probative corpora

Weiss and Ansión’s Pishtaco work concerns predation, alterity, and violence. None of the material verified here establishes a direct Kashiri episode involving Urarina beings, Pishtaco, Bufeo, Chullachaqui, or Yacuruna. They should remain comparative corpora until a specific text supplies an interaction.
