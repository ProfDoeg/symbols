# The Maninkari: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A collective category of normally invisible, predominantly benevolent or ambivalent spirits/ancestral persons in Asháninka cosmology—not a single god or goddess.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Gerald Weiss’s Asháninka fieldwork of 1960–1964, first recorded in his 1969 doctoral dissertation and published in expanded form as *Campa Cosmology* in 1975. No earlier occurrence of the word *manínkari* has been securely located in the Franciscan chronicles reviewed or cited by later scholars.  
> **Evidentiary correction:** “The Maninkari” is a potentially misleading English singularization. Asháninka testimony uses *maninkari* collectively and also, contextually, for “a maninkari,” an individual member of that class. It is therefore safest to speak of **the maninkari / Maninkari (“Hidden Ones”)** rather than a proper-named deity.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** Weiss’s records from 1960–1964, dissertation of 1969, and critical publication of 1975.
- **[ET] Later ethnographic testimony:** subsequently recorded Asháninka statements, narratives, and practices.
- **[RV] Regional or speaker-specific variant:** a position not shared uniformly among Asháninka communities or informants.
- **[SC] Syncretistic Christian identification:** equation produced through Catholic, Adventist, evangelical, or Bible-translation contact.
- **[CR] Comparative Southern Arawak reconstruction:** comparison with cognate-looking categories among Matsigenka, Nomatsiguenga, Yanesha, or Yine; not evidence that all are one being.
- **[F] Folklore/oral history:** a narrated event whose historical occurrence is not independently established.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an analytical classification proposed by an ethnographer, linguist, or historian.
- **[MI] Modern invention or appropriation:** a recent teaching, artistic use, or commercial claim not documented in the Asháninka record.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

In Weiss’s earliest full account, the maninkari are one designation for the good spirits who surround the Asháninka world. They are normally invisible but possess a human or humanoid “true form”; visible birds, animals, stars, lightning, or other phenomena may be their temporary “clothing.” Many were formerly Asháninka people, and a sufficiently good person may join them after death. They live in settlements on mountain ridges, at the world’s geographic margins, in celestial and cloud strata, and in the first underground stratum. They do not ordinarily suffer sickness, deprivation, aging, or death and renew themselves in the celestial river Hananerite. Only trained shamans normally see or communicate with them directly. [EA]

This is not a neatly bounded “pantheon” in the classical Mediterranean sense. *Maninkari* is an ontological and relational category overlapping with “good spirits,” ancestors, invisible persons, spirit-owners, and shamanic helpers. Later speakers disagree over whether every maninkari is good: Bajo Perené interviews also describe dangerous beings that abduct, conceal, or disorient people as *maninkari*. [RV] The category’s history is therefore not a progression from one fixed doctrine to another but a record of local classifications altered by mission Christianity, displacement, warfare, and changing shamanic practice.

### Name and etymology

- **Asháninka:** *manínkari*, frequently printed *maninkari*. [EA]
- **Proposed derivation:** from Asháninka *man-aantsi*, “to hide; to conceal oneself.” Hence “hidden one(s),” “those who are hidden,” or more literally beings characterized by being unseen. A linguistic study based on Bajo Perené interviews explicitly gives this derivation. [MSR]
- **Number:** English and Spanish sources often leave the uninflected Asháninka form unchanged: *the maninkari*, *los maninkari*. Individual narratives can nevertheless say *un maninkari*. Treating “Maninkari” as the unique proper name of a god is unsupported.
- **Reported equivalents within Asháninka:** Weiss gives *asháninka*, “our fellows/our people,” and *amatsénka* or *amachenga*, approximately “our spirit fellows,” as overlapping names for good spirits. Rojas Zolezzi likewise reports *amachenka* as an alternative designation. These are not necessarily exact dictionary synonyms in every region. [EA/ET]
- **Place-defined classes in Weiss:**  
  - *otishisáti*: mountain-ridge dwellers;  
  - *henokísáti*: upper/celestial dwellers;  
  - *menkorisáti*: cloud-stratum dwellers;  
  - *kivintisáti*: dwellers in the first underground stratum;  
  - *intatonisáti*, *otisitirikosáti*, and *irimakasáti*: inhabitants of particular margins of the known world. [EA]
- **Related but non-identical terms:** *henokiniri* and *inkitisati* mean inhabitants of the upper world or sky. Contemporary Christianized speakers frequently gloss these as “angels,” but they should not automatically be substituted for *maninkari*. [SC]
- **Matsigenka comparison:** *saangaríite/saanka’rite*, invisible, beautiful, immortal superhuman persons created by Tasorintsi. [CR]
- **Nomatsiguenga comparison:** *sáangatsíri*, glossed “one who disappears” or “one who is invisible.” [CR]
- These comparison terms reveal a wider Southern Arawak pattern of invisible, socially organized nonhuman persons. They do not prove descent from one historically reconstructable proto-deity.

### Classification disputes

Weiss’s presentation classifies the maninkari firmly among good spirits. [EA] Bajo Perené testimony collected decades later is less uniform:

- one consultant said their character “depends”—a maninkari who seizes and makes a person disappear is bad;
- another called them bad beings found deep in the forest or near waterfalls;
- another equated a waterfall abductor with a devil;
- another said they were angels;
- another rejected “angel” and defined *maninkari* simply as “one who cannot be seen”;
- one allowed even *kamári*, ordinarily a harmful spirit/demon category, to be described as *maninkari* insofar as it too was invisible. [RV/SC]

The term therefore appears capable of shifting from a morally marked class—Weiss’s benevolent Hidden Ones—to a perceptual category, “invisible being,” which can contain helpful, neutral, or dangerous agents. Arturo Rojas’s semantic study interprets this as historical change under mission and social pressure, although the available evidence cannot establish that every apparently “negative” meaning is necessarily recent.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest secure record

Gerald Weiss conducted fieldwork among Asháninka communities between 1960 and 1964. An early version of his cosmological study became his 1969 dissertation; the definitive published form appeared in 1975 as *Campa Cosmology*, *Anthropological Papers of the American Museum of Natural History* 52(5). Weiss uses the then-standard ethnographic name “Campa,” now widely avoided because many Asháninka regard it as imposed or derogatory.

Weiss records that good spirits are called *asháninka*, *amatsénka*, and *manínkari*, “Hidden Ones.” Their settlements occupy ridges, cosmic margins, celestial strata, clouds, and the upper underground. He records sacred birds as visible forms or “clothing” of spirits; postmortem incorporation of virtuous persons into the maninkari; shamanic contact through tobacco and ayahuasca; and the relation between deceased shamans, jaguars, and good spirits. [EA]

What this evidence does **not** establish:

- It does not date the origin of the word or belief.
- It does not prove that all Asháninka communities held an identical classification.
- It does not identify one supreme being named Maninkari.
- It is an ethnographic transcription of living oral knowledge, not a pre-Columbian manuscript or inscription.
- No securely identified pre-Hispanic image, archaeological object, inscription, or theophoric personal name bears this name.

### Earlier Franciscan documentation

Franciscans formally entered Asháninka territory in the seventeenth century; Quimiri was founded in 1635. Chronicles associated with Biedma, Amich, San Antonio, Izaguirre, Sala, Ordinaire, and Navarro preserve information about missions, resistance, mortuary customs, ritual specialists, and what missionaries classified as “superstition.” They are indispensable for the history of colonial contact. However, the present investigation did not locate a securely quoted occurrence of *maninkari* in those chronicles. Later scholars cite them for contextual practices, not for an early attestation of this exact term.

That absence has several possible explanations:

1. missionaries may not have recorded the category;
2. they may have translated it generically as *espíritus*, *ánimas*, *ángeles*, or *demonios*;
3. relevant vocabulary may remain buried in unindexed manuscripts;
4. the term may have been regional or omitted by intermediaries.

It would therefore be unjustified both to claim a colonial attestation and to claim that colonial Asháninka did not recognize such beings.

### Principal corpus

1. **Gerald Weiss, *Campa Cosmology* (fieldwork 1960–1964; dissertation 1969; publication 1975).** English ethnography containing Asháninka terms and oral narratives. This is the foundational documentary source. [EA]

2. **Gerald Weiss, “Shamanism and Priesthood in the Light of the Campa Ayahuasca Ceremony” (paper first presented 1968; published in *Hallucinogens and Shamanism*, 1973).** Describes the shaman’s direct dealings with spirits and the social form of ayahuasca ceremonies. It supplies ritual context rather than a separate Maninkari mythology. [EA]

3. **Enrique Rojas Zolezzi, studies culminating in *El morral del colibrí* (2014), and articles of 2019 and 2022.** Spanish-language ethnography on *tasorentsi, maninkari,* and *kamari*, the shaman’s marriage to the tobacco spirit, changing gender roles in shamanism, birds, jaguars, and the postmortem destinations of shamans. [ET/MSR]

4. **Bajo Perené semantic-change thesis, c. 2009–2010.** Records extensive direct interviews comparing earlier literature with Christianized contemporary definitions. This is the clearest documentary source for disagreement over whether maninkari are beneficent, dangerous, local, celestial, demonic, or angelic. [ET/RV/SC]

5. **CARE, *Cuaderno de trabajo: Reserva Comunal Asháninka y Parque Nacional Otishi* (early 2010s).** Contains a narrative collected at Paveni in which refugees meet a maninkari, kill one through misrecognition, suffer epidemic punishment, and are saved by the seripiari Shimityoki. [F/ET]

6. **Ashéninka Perené Bible translations.** In Revelation 20–22, *Maninkari* translates “angel.” Revelation 21 accordingly speaks of a maninkari measuring the heavenly city and twelve maninkari guarding its gates. This is strong evidence for modern Christian semantic accommodation but cannot be projected backward as proof that traditional maninkari were biblical angels. [SC]

7. **Jeremy Narby, *The Cosmic Serpent* (1998).** Popular anthropological memoir based partly on Asháninka testimony and heavily on Weiss. It introduced maninkari to a large international psychedelic audience. Narby reports that tobacco attracts them, that ayahuasca and tobacco permit perception of otherwise invisible souls, and that they are present in living forms. His later DNA/biophoton interpretation is Narby’s hypothesis, not an Asháninka doctrine documented by Weiss. [ET/MSR]

8. **Contemporary Indigenous and collaborative publications.** A 2019 narrative by Cueva Mantari describes a forest-owner maninkari admonishing a wasteful hunter; a testimony attributed to Rosa Díaz in 2002 locates invisible spiritual relatives on mountain summits, protecting human well-being. [ET/F]

### Named comparative sources and limits

Smith’s Yanesha work, Santos-Granero’s *The Power of Love* and “Saint Christopher in the Amazon,” Renard-Casevitz on Matsigenka, Shaver and Dodds on Nomatsiguenga, and Gow on Yine/Piro are valuable for Southern Arawak comparison. They document cognate themes—transformer beings, invisible persons, shamanic helpers, Christian appropriation, and myths changing through colonial history. They do **not**, merely by comparison, expand the Asháninka Maninkari corpus.

The same limitation applies to Loreto and Ucayali cycles concerning Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, Pishtaco, and regional “owners” of rivers or forests. Similarities in invisibility, abduction, seduction, or mastery of animals do not make those beings maninkari unless a particular narrator says so.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

The maninkari have no single exclusive portfolio. Their documented functions include:

- maintaining or inhabiting the normally unseen social world surrounding humans; [EA]
- serving as potential ancestors or postmortem destinations of morally successful persons; [EA]
- living in and around mountains, river headwaters, clouds, celestial regions, subterranean strata, waterfalls, and forests; [EA/ET]
- acting as shamanic interlocutors, helpers, teachers, healers, or sources of songs and knowledge; [EA/ET]
- owning or protecting forest beings and game in some later accounts; [RV/ET]
- hiding refugees or assisting endangered humans; [F]
- punishing violence, misrecognition, or excessive hunting by withdrawing animals, transforming offenders, or allowing disease; [F/ET]
- abducting, concealing, or disorienting humans in some Bajo Perené narratives. [RV]
- translating Christian angels in Ashéninka scripture. [SC]

### Visibility and form

Their defining attribute is not bodilessness but **concealment from ordinary perception**. In Weiss’s formulation, their genuine appearance is generally human or humanoid, while ordinary people may see only an animal, bird, star, flash of lightning, or physical object that functions as the spirit’s “clothing.” [EA]

Sacred birds documented by Weiss as good spirits in visible form include the swallow-tailed kite, several hummingbirds, caciques, a paradise tanager, a barbet, the cock-of-the-rock, the white-necked heron, anhinga, and jacana-like waterbird. The swallow-tailed kite holds the highest rank among the bird forms listed by Weiss. [EA] These species are not all individually named “Maninkari gods”; they are visible manifestations or garments of spirits.

Contemporary descriptions add translucence, luminosity, beautiful designs, rich feather ornaments, pleasant odor, or exceptional beauty. Some of these features are clearer in comparative Matsigenka *saangaríite* material and should not be generalized indiscriminately to every Asháninka region. [CR/RV]

### Settlements and social life

Weiss describes good spirits as living in settlements much like Asháninka ones, but possessing desirable things without ordinary sickness, misery, senescence, or death. They bathe in Hananerite, the celestial River of Eternal Youth. [EA] Comparative Matsigenka accounts say invisible peoples cultivate gardens, form families, sing, dance, drink manioc beer, and bathe in the Milky Way; these details illuminate a regional model of nonhuman sociality but are not all separately confirmed for the Asháninka maninkari. [CR]

### Shamanic perception

The *sheripiari*—often analyzed as a tobacco specialist or “one transformed by tobacco”—trains through isolation, dietary restriction, tobacco preparations, ayahuasca/*kamarampi*, and relations with spirit persons. Continual or disciplined ingestion of tobacco and ayahuasca permits perception of maninkari who remain invisible to ordinary people. [EA/ET]

Tobacco is not merely an intoxicant in this system. It is food or an attracting substance for spirits, a medicine, and the means by which the shaman establishes relations. Narby records an interlocutor saying that tobacco “attracts” the maninkari and provides the best means of contact. [ET]

### Jaguar transformation

The shaman’s jaguar transformation is documented but must not be collapsed into “the Maninkari is a jaguar.”

- Weiss reports that prowling jaguars may be souls of living jaguar-shamans in animal form or shamans bodily transformed into jaguars after death. [EA]
- Later Asháninka testimony describes training in which a shaman sees his mentor transform during ayahuasca sessions. [ET]
- Rojas Zolezzi reports that a shaman may acquire the power to become a jaguar for terrestrial movement or a hummingbird for aerial movement after establishing a relation—sometimes represented as marriage—with the female spirit of tobacco. [ET]
- More accomplished deceased shamans may ascend and join the maninkari; less accomplished ones may become jaguars attached to particular mountains. [RV/ET]
- Jaguars perceive humans as peccaries in several Asháninka accounts. Transformation therefore includes a change of embodied viewpoint, not merely disguise. [ET/MSR]

### Material iconography

No securely identified pre-Hispanic statue, temple image, mask, or standardized painted icon represents “the Maninkari.” Their defining invisibility and variable clothing work against a fixed iconographic canon.

Asháninka textiles and painted designs can be attributed to knowledge obtained from spirits, and contemporary work sometimes calls luminous or patterned forms “designs of the maninkari.” This is living visual practice, not evidence of an ancient canonical emblem. [ET]

No exclusive sacred color, number, weekday, planetary body, weapon, or heraldic symbol is securely attested. Stars may be visible manifestations of sky spirits; lightning is sometimes interpreted as the weapon or activity of upper beings, but neither belongs exclusively to the maninkari.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

The maninkari have no single documented parentage, consort, or family tree. They are a class into which multiple kinds of persons may fall:

- primordial or former Asháninka transformed into nonordinary beings; [EA]
- the souls of good deceased people; [EA]
- powerful deceased shamans who reach the upper world; [ET]
- inhabitants of particular cosmic regions; [EA]
- locally conceived spirit-owners or guardians. [RV]

This is an **open category**, not a biological lineage.

### Position in the cosmos

Weiss places them among the beneficial spirit hosts, opposed in moral function to hostile demons and dangerous witchcraft beings. Some later writers divide good powers into maninkari and *tasorentsi*, but Weiss’s materials do not support a rigid universal taxonomy. [EA/MSR] Bajo Perené speakers sometimes dissolve the moral distinction by using *maninkari* primarily for invisibility. [RV]

### Every documented named divine interaction

Because maninkari are collective, most evidence records relations between categories rather than a single protagonist. Only discrete, textually supported relations are listed.

- **Tasorintsi/Tasórentsi** — Matsigenka *saangaríite*, compared with maninkari, are said to have been created by Tasorintsi; some recent Asháninka classifications place maninkari alongside or beneath *tasorentsi*. Source: Baer and Dominican comparative material, summarized in the Bajo Perené study; 1994/2006. **[CR; not a securely attested Asháninka narrative episode]**
- **Pavá** — Christianized speakers place upper-world inhabitants and angels with Pavá/God; some Bible translations use *Maninkari* for God’s angels. Sources: Bajo Perené interviews, c. 2009–2010; Ashéninka Perené New Testament. **[SC]**
- **Jesus / Ipiraitari (“the Lamb”)** — in the Ashéninka translation of Revelation, maninkari serve within the heavenly court surrounding God and the Lamb; an angel/maninkari shows John the Lamb’s bride and measures the New Jerusalem. Source: Revelation 21–22, ancient Greek text translated into Ashéninka in the modern missionary era. **[SC; source narrative is Christian, lexical identification modern]**
- **Kamaari/Satan** — Revelation 20 presents a maninkari seizing and binding the dragon identified as Kamaari/Satan. Source: Ashéninka Perené Revelation 20. **[SC]**
- **Kamári** — one Bajo Perené consultant allowed that a *kamári* could be called *maninkari* because it too is invisible; another opposed beneficent maninkari to evil beings. Source: Bajo Perené interviews, c. 2009–2010. **[RV/SC-influenced terminology; classification dispute, not a mythic episode]**
- **Koroshipiri/Satan** — a consultant discussed Koroshipiri as Satan immediately after explaining the possible overlap of *kamári* and *maninkari*. No independent narrative of a maninkari meeting Koroshipiri was recorded. **[RV/SC; absence of direct interaction]**
- **Avíreri/Nabireri** — modern popular summaries sometimes call Avíreri “the most powerful of the maninkari.” Weiss certainly presents Avíreri as a preeminent transformer, but the exact taxonomic claim should be treated cautiously: his myths concern Avíreri’s transformations of primordial people rather than a scene in which a corporate body of maninkari meets him. **[MSR; no secure discrete interaction]**
- **Pachákama** — Weiss reports a high-ranking being associated with the river’s end; later zoological accounts call the sacred otter *parari* a daughter of Pachákama. No direct Maninkari–Pachákama episode has been securely isolated. **[EA context; absence of interaction]**
- **Tobacco spirit** — shamans enter a relationship, sometimes a mystical marriage, with the female person/spirit of tobacco and thereby acquire healing and transformational capacities; maninkari are attracted through tobacco. Source: Rojas Zolezzi, 2019, building on Weiss. **[ET]**
- **Hananerite** — not a personal deity but the celestial river in which good spirits renew youth. Source: Weiss 1975. **[EA; environmental relation rather than god–god interaction]**
- **Saint Christopher and the Christ Child** — Santos-Granero demonstrates that Southern Arawak child-sorcery traditions transformed the colonial image and legend of Saint Christopher carrying Christ. His article does not establish a direct Maninkari–Christopher encounter. **[SC/colonial transformation; absence of direct interaction]**
- **Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, Pishtaco** — no securely documented encounter with maninkari was found. Similar motifs do not constitute interaction. **[Absence of evidence]**
- **Juan Santos Atahualpa** — no primary source located calls him a maninkari or records his meeting one. Later messianic interpretations of his rebellion cannot supply that missing link. **[Absence of evidence]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### 5.1 The Hidden Peoples and their animal clothing

**[EA]** Weiss’s dispersed accounts collectively describe a world densely inhabited by good invisible persons. These beings reside in settlements on ridges, cosmic margins, upper strata, clouds, and the upper underground. In their own settlements they appear as persons, but when they visit ordinary human space they may wear the visible form of birds or animals. Certain stars are visible sky dwellers. Virtuous dead people may join them.

This is less a single plotted myth than an ontological cycle explaining sacred species, shamanic perception, death, and the layered cosmos. Later popular writing converts the theme into the claim that maninkari “are inside” all animals, plants, rivers, and stones. That may accurately render particular contemporary testimonies, but it is broader and more pantheistic than Weiss’s careful formulation of spirits using visible forms. [ET/MSR]

### 5.2 The maninkari who shelters refugees, is killed, and sends punishment

A narrative collected at Paveni connects Maninkari belief with histories of raiding, enslavement, refuge, disease, and survival.

1. Families fleeing warfare and slave raiders reach remote headwaters.
2. A maninkari receives one group and hides them inside a rock.
3. Later refugees see a splendidly feather-adorned person and mistakenly believe he is a pursuing warrior.
4. They shoot and kill him.
5. When they return, the apparent corpse has become a dry trunk.
6. The offended maninkari deceives or transforms perception: a tapir appears, is shot, and becomes stone.
7. Disease then attacks the people.
8. The seripiari Shimityoki explains that the killed person was not an enemy but a maninkari who intended to help them.
9. After drinking ayahuasca, Shimityoki perceives the disease as a person. He deceives it into entering a rock by promising peccaries, then seals it inside.
10. A remnant survives and repopulates the country. [F/ET]

The story should not be reduced to “spirit punishes impiety.” It encodes the peril of misrecognition under conditions of slave-raiding and war: a potential ally looks like an armed stranger; people fleeing violence reproduce violence; epidemic follows. It also connects rocks and highland refuges to remembered episodes of concealment, including later retreats during the Shining Path years. The narrative’s precise age is unknown.

### 5.3 The abducting or concealing maninkari

Bajo Perené testimony describes maninkari near waterfalls or in deep forest who cause people—especially children or young women—to disappear. One narrative says a missing young woman may return after days and should be treated gently and bathed with achiote leaves so that she does not return to the invisible being. [RV/F]

This complex overlaps structurally with Peruvian selva stories of Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, and enchanted river people, but the recorded consultant explicitly used *maninkari*. It therefore belongs in the dossier as a regional Maninkari tradition. Its moral classification conflicts directly with Weiss’s “good spirits.”

### 5.4 The excessive hunter and the owner of the forest

A narrative published in 2019 tells of a young man who hunts wastefully despite warnings that animals have an owner. Animals begin appearing as people. A maninkari identified as owner or protector of the forest rebukes him and commands him to tell hunters not to kill animals merely to waste them. [ET/F]

This is a late-recorded but Indigenous-authored witness to the continued use of *maninkari* in ethical relations with game. It should not automatically be reconstructed as an unchanged pre-contact conservation law.

### 5.5 Death and becoming maninkari

Weiss records that a person’s *ishíre*, soul/heart, can leave the body in dreams and survives bodily death. A sufficiently good deceased person may join the good spirits as a maninkari. Other souls may become harmful spirits; after punishment, even a tortured soul may, in some accounts, be purified and join the maninkari. [EA]

Later Christian teaching restructures this field into heaven, angels, Satan, hell, and moral judgment. Contemporary speakers may consequently describe maninkari as angels and the upper settlements as paradise, while others reject the equivalence. [SC/RV]

### 5.6 Shamanic transformation and the jaguar

A shaman’s soul may travel while the body remains behind; he may become a hummingbird, swallow-tailed kite, otter, or jaguar according to task and route. Jaguars that prowl close to settlements may be living shamans in transformed form or deceased shamans who became jaguars. [EA/ET]

The postmortem outcome varies:

- the most accomplished shaman may reach the upper world and join the maninkari;
- another may remain associated with a mountain as a jaguar;
- harmful shamans or witches may join dangerous spirit classes.

This is not a single universal ladder of merit, and sources differ by region and period.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No temple cult

No temple, permanent shrine, state priesthood, idol, sacrificial altar, festival calendar, or centralized institution devoted exclusively to the maninkari is documented. Calling their religious setting a “cult” risks imposing a Mediterranean model.

Mountain peaks, river headwaters, waterfalls, rocks, caves, cloud strata, and forest zones are spiritually charged localities, but they are not temples in the architectural sense. Some are avoided; others are destinations of shamanic travel or remembered refuges.

### Religious specialists

The principal mediator is the *sheripiari*, the tobacco-and-ayahuasca specialist. Training may include:

- prolonged consumption of tobacco preparations;
- ayahuasca/*kamarampi*;
- dietary restrictions;
- avoidance of sex and manioc beer during training;
- isolation from ordinary village life;
- instruction by an established shaman;
- obtaining songs, vision, or relationships with spirit persons. [EA/ET]

The shaman diagnoses illnesses as disruptions involving souls, spirit aggression, broken relations, or punishment. He negotiates with nonhuman persons, recovers souls, moves through cosmic layers, and may combat or trick personified diseases. In the Paveni narrative, Shimityoki sees disease as a person only after taking ayahuasca and imprisons it in rock. [ET/F]

Older descriptions frequently present the office as male. Rojas Zolezzi documents expanding roles for women *sheripiaro*, including relations with the tobacco spirit, healing, and transformation into birds. [ET]

### Tobacco, ayahuasca, and songs

Tobacco is the most consistently attested medium of Maninkari relations. It feeds, pleases, or attracts spirits and changes the shaman’s capacity to perceive. Ayahuasca opens a field in which invisible persons become visible and audible. Songs may be understood as learned from or sung with spirit persons rather than individually composed in the Western sense. [EA/ET]

These practices are not evidence that every Asháninka person routinely consumes ayahuasca or sees maninkari. Weiss emphasizes specialist discipline and variation.

### Offerings and sacrifice

Tobacco and manioc beer function within reciprocal relations, but no securely documented rite of human sacrifice to the maninkari was found. Animal killing is chiefly hunting, subsistence, or exchange with owners of game, not a documented Maninkari sacrificial liturgy.

The killing of alleged witches, including accused child sorcerers in the wider Southern Arawak region, is documented historically. Santos-Granero connects child-sorcery beliefs to colonial violence, epidemic, and the transformed Saint Christopher legend. This violence must not be falsely described as “sacrifice to the Maninkari”: the sources do not establish that.

No evidence was found for temple prostitution, formal sexual service to maninkari, or a hereditary Maninkari priesthood. The “marriage” to the tobacco spirit is a shamanic relationship, not temple prostitution.

### Politics and economics

Spirit relations regulate hunting and forest use in some narratives: animals have owners, excess killing causes withdrawal or punishment, and hunters depend on shamanic mediation. [ET] This is an economy of reciprocal restraint, but descriptions of it as modern environmentalism are interpretive translations.

Mission settlements disrupted exchange networks, concentrated populations, introduced epidemic disease, and attempted to displace shamanic authority. Metal tools and trade goods also made missions attractive to some leaders. The resulting field combined resistance, selective appropriation, and Christian reinterpretation rather than simple replacement.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before European documentation

The antiquity of invisible spirit-persons among Southern Arawak peoples is plausible from their broad comparative distribution. [CR/MSR] Nevertheless, no pre-contact text, inscription, or securely identified image names *maninkari*. Precise claims about origin remain unprovable.

### Seventeenth to early eighteenth centuries: missions and unrecorded translation

Franciscan entry is documented from 1635, with earlier Spanish contacts reported from the late sixteenth century. Missions brought baptism, images, saints, heaven, angels, Satan, and new settlement patterns. Indigenous people also treated missions as sources of metal tools, trade, and political leverage.

Missionaries rarely recorded Indigenous myth in sufficient linguistic detail. Consequently, this period supplies the historical conditions for later angel/maninkari equations but not a secure attestation of the term.

### 1742–1750s: Juan Santos Atahualpa and expulsion of missions

Juan Santos Atahualpa appeared in 1742 and organized an alliance centered on Asháninka and Yanesha opposition to Franciscan and colonial expansion. Missions were abandoned or destroyed, and outsiders lost effective control over much of the central selva for more than a century.

Later scholarship calls aspects of the movement messianic and recognizes Christian, Inca, and Amazonian elements. No source securely makes Juan Santos a maninkari. His importance here is historical: he helped interrupt mission institutions that were transforming Indigenous religious vocabulary.

### Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries: renewed colonization

Franciscans, travelers, rubber interests, settlers, and state agents returned. Chronicles by Ordinaire, Sala, Izaguirre, and Navarro recorded fragments of Asháninka religion through Christian categories. Rubber extraction, debt peonage, slave-raiding, epidemics, and displacement transformed settlement and memory. Stories of hidden mountain peoples and spirits sheltering refugees must be read against this history, although no individual myth can automatically be dated to the rubber era.

### 1960–1975: ethnographic fixation

Weiss’s fieldwork produced the first secure extensive record. His work froze neither an untouched tradition nor a uniform one; it documented particular consultants after centuries of mission and colonial contact. Even so, it remains the indispensable baseline:

- maninkari as Hidden Ones;
- principally good spirits;
- cosmic settlements;
- sacred birds as visible clothing;
- shamanic perception through tobacco and ayahuasca;
- morally successful dead joining the spirits;
- jaguar-shaman transformations. [EA]

### Late twentieth century: missions, Bible translation, and semantic restructuring

Catholic, Adventist, evangelical, and SIL-related activity increasingly supplied Asháninka equivalents for Christian vocabulary. *Pavá* and *Tasorintsi* were aligned with the biblical God; upper-world beings became angels; harmful spirits became devils or Satan; traditional afterlife geography was mapped onto heaven and hell. [SC]

The Ashéninka Perené Bible’s use of *Maninkari* for “angel” is a concrete textual endpoint of this process. It does not merely compare two beings; it installs *maninkari* as the regular participant in biblical scenes.

Informants do not agree with the equation. One explicitly said “they are angels”; another answered “not little angels” and insisted the term meant only “one who is not seen.” The disagreement is itself evidence of active religious change.

### 1980s–1990s: Shining Path war

The internal armed conflict devastated Asháninka communities. Shining Path expanded into the Ene basin in the mid-1980s, recruiting or abducting residents, imposing coercive settlements, and prompting mass flight. Asháninka families hid in remote mountains, ravines, caves, and rocky headwaters; state forces and local self-defense organizations also reshaped community life. Peru’s Truth and Reconciliation Commission concluded that rural and Indigenous populations, including Asháninka, bore a disproportionate burden of the conflict.

The violence did not create Maninkari belief, but it changed the circumstances in which narratives of concealment, mountain refuge, vanished children, enemies, and protective invisible people were told. A Bajo Perené consultant explicitly connected disappearances of children with “the guerrilla.” [ET/historical contextualization]

### Twenty-first century: persistence and pluralization

Maninkari remain part of Asháninka discourse about forest knowledge, hunting, dreams, illness, and shamanism. At the same time, speakers may classify them as ancestors, angels, forest owners, spirits, devils, abductors, or an obsolete “old people’s” belief. Contemporary Indigenous publications use them in discussions of sustainable hunting and *kametsa asaike*, living well or beautifully. [ET]

There is no reliable census of people who “worship Maninkari,” and the question may be misconceived: acknowledgment, fear, kinship, reciprocal conduct, visionary encounter, and Christian translation are not interchangeable forms of worship.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Narby and psychedelic popularization

Jeremy Narby’s *The Cosmic Serpent* brought the term to readers of psychedelic, alternative-science, and New Age literature. He accurately drew several core details from Weiss and Asháninka interlocutors: invisibility, tobacco and ayahuasca as means of encounter, ancestral kinship, and manifestations through living forms. [ET]

His larger proposal connecting ayahuasca visions, DNA, molecular intelligence, and biophotons is a modern speculative reconstruction. It is not a teaching found in Weiss’s Asháninka corpus. Later discussions of maninkari as ultraviolet images, molecular beings, DMT entities, or messages from DNA are therefore **[MI/MSR]**, not ancient Asháninka doctrine.

### Psychedelic and consciousness literature

Books and conferences on DMT entities now place maninkari beside “elves,” aliens, archetypes, spirits, and autonomous visionary agents. This comparison may be philosophically productive, but it removes the beings from Asháninka kinship, geography, subsistence, language, and colonial history. It should be labeled modern comparative speculation.

### Crystal guardians

A recent commercial course presents “the Maninkari” as named crystalline intelligences or light beings inhabiting crystals, including a “princess” called Lumira and twenty-three other guardians. The creator explicitly describes this as a personal revelation developed over about twenty-five years.

No Asháninka source documents Lumira, a Maninkari princess, a fixed company of twenty-four crystal guardians, “frequency keepers,” or an initiatory crystal system. This is a clear **modern invention and commercial appropriation [MI]**.

Weiss does report spiritually significant objects and later speakers may locate invisible beings in crystalline stones. That limited resemblance does not authenticate the commercial hierarchy or names.

### Music and art

A Paris-based experimental duo has performed as **Maninkari** since approximately 2006, producing ambient, drone, improvisational, and film music. Interviews emphasize fantasy, dreams, soundscapes, repetition, and spiritual healing. The name is a modern artistic adoption, not evidence of an Asháninka ritual ensemble. [MI]

Contemporary artists and researchers also invoke maninkari in ecological and decolonial performance work. These uses can foreground Indigenous ontology but sometimes universalize the beings into a generic “spirit of nature.”

### Neopaganism and occultism

The dedicated search found no established Golden Dawn, Theosophical, Renaissance hermetic, chaos-magical, or reconstructionist lineage centered on Maninkari. No historical grimoire is known to name them. Claims that Maninkari belong to an ancient worldwide occult order are unsupported.

There is likewise no documented large organized “Maninkari neopagan religion.” Individual New Age practice and psychedelic spirituality exist, but scale and continuity with Asháninka ritual cannot presently be established.

### Nationalist or extremist appropriation

No documented nationalist or modern extremist cult of Maninkari was found. The Shining Path’s violence affected the communities carrying these traditions, but the movement did not appropriate Maninkari as an ideological symbol in the sources reviewed.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### The Weiss baseline

Weiss supplied the first systematic reconstruction of Asháninka cosmology. Its strengths are extensive fieldwork, linguistic attention, and preservation of variant accounts. Its limitations include the historical term “Campa,” a tendency toward a systematized cosmological diagram, and the inherent problem of presenting multiple local accounts as one cultural structure.

Later scholarship increasingly emphasizes:

- variation between river systems and communities;
- persons rather than abstract “spirits”;
- perspectival transformation;
- the history of colonial contact;
- semantic change under Christianity;
- Indigenous authorship and contemporary testimony;
- gender change within shamanic practice.

### Benevolent or dangerous?

This is the dossier’s principal internal dispute.

**Benevolent model:** Weiss calls maninkari the Hidden Ones among good spirits; later authors describe them as luminous, healing, ancestral, or protective. [EA/ET]

**Ambivalent/perceptual model:** Bajo Perené speakers apply the word to beings who abduct, conceal, deceive, or cause people to become lost. One permits overlap with *kamári*. [RV]

Possible explanations include regional variation, semantic broadening from “good hidden persons” to “anything invisibly personal,” Christian demonization, decline of specialist knowledge, interviewer effects, and historical experiences of disappearance. The evidence does not justify choosing only one explanation.

### Spirits, ancestors, or owners?

Different scholars gloss maninkari as good spirits, ancestors, invisible beings, occult entities, divinities, or spirit-owners. Each captures part of the evidence but imports a different analytical vocabulary. “Ancestor” fits the claim that former Asháninka and good dead join them; “owner” fits some hunting narratives; “angel” fits Christian translation; “god” generally overstates their individual sovereignty.

### Etymology

The derivation from *man-aantsi*, “to hide oneself,” is well supported in later linguistic analysis and consistent with consultants’ explanations. Weiss’s “Hidden Ones” agrees. No serious alternative etymology was found. The principal uncertainty concerns morphological detail and whether the common English plural accurately represents Asháninka grammar, not the semantic root.

### Christian syncretism

Mission influence is certain, but one-way “replacement” is too simple. Asháninka translators and speakers actively use Indigenous vocabulary to make Christian scripture intelligible. The resulting identifications can operate in both directions: angels become maninkari, while maninkari acquire angelic and heavenly features.

The Ashéninka Revelation is especially important because it records a datable lexical decision rather than an ethnographer’s analogy.

### Comparative Southern Arawak claims

Matsigenka *saangaríite* and Nomatsiguenga *sáangatsíri* share invisibility, beauty, immortality, upper-world or remote settlements, and shamanic accessibility. This strongly supports a regional complex. It does not establish that the words are cognate without historical-linguistic demonstration, nor that every narrative can be transferred from one people to another.

Smith’s Yanesha, Santos-Granero’s Southern Arawak comparisons, Renard-Casevitz’s Matsigenka work, and Gow’s Yine history are best used to identify transformations and analogies—not to manufacture a unified “Southern Arawak pantheon.”

### Sources not securely applicable

The named Loreto authors and Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, and Pishtaco collections document the broader Peruvian selva imagination. No source located identifies those beings as Maninkari. The dossier therefore rejects a composite “Peruvian jungle pantheon” assembled by similarity alone.

### Modern pseudohistory

The following claims are unsupported or demonstrably recent:

- Maninkari are one singular creator god.
- They are extraterrestrials or DNA molecules according to ancient Asháninka teaching.
- They form a named hierarchy of crystal guardians led by Princess Lumira.
- They belong to an ancient global occult order.
- They have a canonical emblem, crystal, number, planet, or weekday.
- Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, Pishtaco, Christian angels, and maninkari are timelessly identical.
- A fixed “Maninkari mythology” was written down before European contact.

### State of the archive

The core evidence remains dispersed:

- Weiss’s dissertation and 1975 monograph;
- American Museum and anthropological publications;
- Franciscan printed chronicles and mission archives;
- linguistic dictionaries and translated scriptures;
- ethnographic recordings, field notes, and interviews;
- Indigenous publications and community cultural projects.

Much oral knowledge was never recorded. Epidemics, enslavement, mission concentration, rubber extraction, migration, and the internal armed conflict killed or displaced knowledge holders. “Absence from the archive” must consequently be handled as a documentary limit, not proof of absence from tradition.

### Final assessment

The fullest defensible reconstruction is not the biography of a god named “The Maninkari.” It is the history of an Asháninka category of hidden persons. In the earliest secure ethnography, they are principally benevolent, ancestral, socially organized spirits who occupy mountain and cosmic settlements, reveal themselves to trained shamans through tobacco and ayahuasca, and sometimes wear animal or celestial forms. Later evidence shows the category expanding or fragmenting: maninkari can be forest owners, protectors, postmortem ancestors, abductors, dangerous invisibles, devils by analogy, or Christian angels in translation.

That variation is not corruption around an recoverable single original. It is the documented history of the term. The secure record begins only in the 1960s, though the complex is almost certainly older; colonial sources establish the conditions of transformation but have not yet supplied the missing early word-attestation. The strongest modern discontinuities are equally clear: DNA beings, DMT entities, and named crystal guardians are twentieth- and twenty-first-century interpretations or inventions, not ancient doctrine.
