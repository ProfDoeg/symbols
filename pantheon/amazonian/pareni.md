# Pareni (the Salt Lady): Research Dossier

**Class:** In Matsigenka sources, a female *tasorintsi*—a primordial transforming divinity or culture heroine—and the once-human form of salt. “Goddess” is a serviceable English shorthand, but not an exact translation of an indigenous theological category. In Asháninka usage, the corresponding sacred salt may be called **Tsiwi/Tzivi**; Yanesha traditions do **not** identify the Cerro de la Sal with Pareni, but with the male salt-being **Posona’**.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest presently identifiable publication explicitly naming Pareni is Fidel Pereira’s Matsigenka account, published in **1987**; Renard-Casevitz and Olivier Dollfus published related material in **1988**. France-Marie Renard-Casevitz’s first full, source-transparent publication of the cycle—two tales recorded in 1991 and a longer text supplied by José Epifanio Pereira—appeared in French in **1992**, then in Spanish translation in **1994**. Colonial descriptions attest the Cerro de la Sal, its Indigenous ceremonial importance, and its trade network from **1635–1663 onward**, but the accessible colonial texts do not securely attest the personal name *Pareni*. Thus the mountain’s sacred status is documented centuries earlier than the named myth.

**Evidentiary caution:** No pre-Columbian inscription, manuscript, securely identified ancient image, or archaeological dedication names Pareni. Her recoverable record is oral, ethnographic, linguistic, geographic, and colonial-historical. The antiquity of individual narrative elements cannot be inferred simply from their description of primordial time.

## 1. Identification, names, and classifications

### Pareni / Pařeni

**[Secure modern ethnographic attestation]** *Pareni*, printed by Renard-Casevitz as **Pařeni**, is the Matsigenka name of the primordial woman who ultimately becomes the principal reddish salt body of the Cerro de la Sal. In the complete cycle she is not merely a personification of sodium chloride: she is a transforming being, mother of human and fish children, source of salt springs, and an ancestral body fixed in the landscape but regarded as inert rather than dead. Renard-Casevitz accordingly calls her *Dame Sel* or *Femme-Sel*, “Salt Lady” or “Salt Woman.” Her classification as *tasorintsi* places her among beings able to create or transform through extraordinary power, conventionally associated with potent speech or breath. [Renard-Casevitz, “Sel : femme gemme, femme condiment”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/jsa_0037-9174_1992_num_78_2_1461); [Santos-Granero, “Arawakan Sacred Landscapes”](https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bb72ea57-cccb-44a4-88d4-19be9413594c/content).

The recent Peruvian Ministry of Education Matsigenka vocabulary classifies **Pareni** explicitly as a *deidad femenina*, sister of Pachakama/Pachakamui and mother of fish. This is a contemporary pedagogical systematization, not an ancient canonical list. It credits her with creating the boquichico fish and changing troublesome husbands into the hummingbird, sweat bee, and dung beetle. [Matsigenka pedagogical vocabulary](https://repositorio.minedu.gob.pe/bitstream/handle/20.500.12799/11073/Okyarira%20niagantsipage%20ogotakenkanirira%20Matsigenka.%20Vocabulario%20pedag%C3%B3gico%20Matsigenka.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1).

### “Salt Lady,” “Salt Woman,” and “civilizing goddess”

**[Modern scholarly terminology]** “Salt Lady,” “Salt Woman,” *Dame-Sel*, and *déesse civilisatrice* are descriptive translations or anthropological classifications. They are not separately attested Indigenous epithets. “Civilizing goddess” emphasizes that Pareni produces useful species, salt, travel routes, and a geography of exchange; it should not be mistaken for the name of a priestly office or formal pantheon rank.

### Pareni and the Perené River

**[Linguistic evidence; etymology disputed or at least non-transparent]** Alto Perené speakers use **pareni** for “river”; *pareni-satzi* means approximately “riverine person/people,” and settlers rendered the Indigenous form as Spanish **Perené**. Younger speakers reportedly no longer know the toponym’s origin. The mythic claim that the river is named for the goddess is therefore a traditional etiological interpretation, but it does not establish whether the divine name derives from “river,” whether the river acquired the personal name, or whether myth later joined homophonous forms. [Mihas, “Landscape terms in Alto Perené Kampa”](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/03740463.2015.1096599).

The extinct Venezuelan Arawakan language also called *Pareni* is unrelated to this dossier’s divine figure except by accidental spelling. Humboldt explicitly located those Pareni speakers near the Orinoco, far from the Peruvian Perené and Matsigenka country. Treating the two as one tradition would be erroneous. [Humboldt’s discussion of the Orinoco Pareni language](https://www.arc-codex.com/library/7014).

### Tsiwi / Tzivi / Tivi

**[Regional Asháninka terminology]** Asháninka sources call salt **Tsiwi/Tzivi**; the Cerro can consequently be described by the same word, and modern comparative writing sometimes speaks of “Pareni or Tsiwi.” Santos-Granero gives *tivi* “salt” in his explanation of **Tiviari**, “Salt River.” Tsiwi is therefore best treated as the Asháninka salt-being/substance within a related sacred geography, not automatically as an exact personal-name translation of Matsigenka Pareni. [Santos-Granero, “Arawakan Sacred Landscapes”](https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bb72ea57-cccb-44a4-88d4-19be9413594c/content); [Rojas Zolezzi, “La moneda de sal como don divino”](https://es.scribd.com/document/987120057/1-Rojas).

### Posona’, Posapno, and Posopno

**[Regional variant—not an equation]** Yanesha tradition contradicts any pan-Arawak identification of the mountain as a woman named Pareni. For Yanesha narrators the Cerro is the transformed body of **Posona’**, Salt Man; the large hill is **Posapno**, and the river at its foot **Posopno**. Santos-Granero states this difference explicitly. Pareni and Posona’ occupy analogous places in neighboring salt etiologies, but the evidence does not make them spouses, aliases, or manifestations of a single canonical god. [Santos-Granero, “Arawakan Sacred Landscapes,” pp. 98–99](https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bb72ea57-cccb-44a4-88d4-19be9413594c/content).

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Colonial evidence for the place, not securely for the name

**1635. [Secure colonial attestation of place and sacred-economic importance]** Franciscan Jerónimo Jiménez reached the Cerro de la Sal through Huancabamba and Chorobamba, recognized that many Indigenous groups gathered there, founded San Francisco de las Salinas and nearby San Buenaventura de Quimiri, and attempted to use the concentration of visitors as the basis for conversion and reduction. The records establish the salt center and Indigenous control of it; they do not, in the accessible passages, name Pareni. [History of the Franciscan missions](https://pruebacerrolasaljsa.wordpress.com/juan-santos-atahualpa/ii-las-misiones-franciscanas/).

**1663. [Secure colonial report, interpretively limited]** A *Memorial* describes three salt hills: a major deposit and smaller hills yielding crystalline and white salt, with a saline river emerging from them. Another summary says rites at two hills were described with the Spanish verb *idolatrar*. Such wording demonstrates that missionaries perceived non-Christian ritual there. It does not securely disclose the worshippers’ theology, nor prove that the rites addressed Pareni personally. [Santos-Granero and Barclay ethnographic guide](https://biblio.flacsoandes.edu.ec/libros/digital/50105.pdf).

Franciscan accounts subsequently assembled by San Antonio, Biedma, Amich, Izaguirre and others document missions, salt extraction, attempted controls, epidemics, rebellions, and Indigenous ceremonial places. They are indispensable for the Cerro’s history but cannot be treated as verbatim witnesses to the twentieth-century Pareni narrative.

### Modern recordings and editions

**1987: Fidel Pereira. [Earliest presently identified printed naming]** Rojas Zolezzi reports that the myth was first registered by Fidel Pereira in 1987, alongside the later publications of Renard-Casevitz and Dollfus. The edition has not been located in an independently searchable full-text copy for this dossier, so its wording and collection date remain to be checked against a physical copy.

**1988: Renard-Casevitz and Dollfus. [Secure scholarly publication]** Their published Matsigenka material included “Pachakamui,” based on Fidel Pereira’s version. It introduced the larger cycle in which Pareni’s brother and the carried transformer are punished. The bibliographic date is secure from later citations, but the surviving online references sometimes conflate the 1988 publication with the 1994 Spanish translation.

**1991/1992: Renard-Casevitz, “Sel : femme gemme, femme condiment.” [Principal primary-text edition]** Two shorter Matsigenka narratives were collected in 1991:

- Jorge Martin wrote one in Matsigenka in September 1991.
- María Luisa Ríos’s Matsigenka narration was recorded at Kirigueti on 28 July 1991.
- José Epifanio Pereira supplied the longer Spanish text that joins Pareni’s marriages, the Pachakamui cycle, transformation of her children, and journey to the salt mountain.

Renard-Casevitz published these in the *Journal de la Société des Américanistes* in 1992, pp. 133–149. Her article is the essential primary edition because it identifies narrators, language, date, and editorial interventions. [Renard-Casevitz 1992](https://www.persee.fr/doc/jsa_0037-9174_1992_num_78_2_1461).

**1994: Spanish translation. [Later publication, often misidentified as the original transcription]** “La sal: mujer gema, mujer condimento,” *Revista de Antropología* 1(1), pp. 88–95, is the Spanish form cited by Santos-Granero. The frequent claim that “Renard-Casevitz’s transcription (1994)” is the first appearance needs qualification: the French original appeared in 1992, while the Spanish translation appeared in 1994.

**1994: Gerhard Baer. [Independent ethnographic synthesis]** *Cosmología y shamanismo de los Matsiguenga*, pp. 253–258, supplies another version used by Santos-Granero. Its agreement with the Renard-Casevitz corpus supports broad circulation of the cycle, but the variations must not be collapsed into a single canonical text.

**2004: Santos-Granero. [Comparative scholarly reconstruction]** “Arawakan Sacred Landscapes” synthesizes three versions and sets Pareni beside the traveling creator figures of other southern Arawak peoples. This is a comparative reconstruction, not another Indigenous narration. [Full chapter](https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bb72ea57-cccb-44a4-88d4-19be9413594c/content).

**2011 and later pedagogical collections. [Contemporary preservation/systematization]** Matsigenka-language collections and state educational publications now summarize Pareni as a female deity, Pachakama’s sister and mother of fish. These demonstrate continued cultural transmission, although the textbook category “deity” regularizes a more fluid oral ontology. [Ministry vocabulary](https://repositorio.minedu.gob.pe/bitstream/handle/20.500.12799/11073/Okyarira%20niagantsipage%20ogotakenkanirira%20Matsigenka.%20Vocabulario%20pedag%C3%B3gico%20Matsigenka.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=1).

## 3. Domain, powers, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**[Secure ethnographic attestation]**

Pareni’s documented domains are:

- rock salt and saline water;
- the creation or differentiation of certain animals;
- the origin of fish, including boquichico in the pedagogical summary;
- transformation through effective speech or will;
- geographic movement and the production of named places;
- long-distance circulation, trade, alliance, and interethnic peace at the Cerro;
- the permanent presence of ancestral time in the landscape.

Renard-Casevitz’s structural reading contrasts Pareni with **Kashiri**, Moon: Kashiri is an itinerant male who gives women manioc and cooking, fixing households to cultivated land; Pareni becomes an immobile female body that gives men meat and salt, encouraging hunting, travel, and exchange. This is an anthropological interpretation of complementary mythic functions, not a narrated meeting between the two beings. [Renard-Casevitz 1992, p. 147](https://www.persee.fr/doc/jsa_0037-9174_1992_num_78_2_1461?pageId=T1_147).

### Physical form and transformations

**[Attested narrative]** In primordial time Pareni has human female form. She has bodily appetites, marries, bears children, travels, urinates, grieves, and acts through anger or deliberation. Her final form is the great hill crossed by a reddish salt vein. Her surviving daughter becomes the adjacent smaller hill with white salt. Saline springs, clays and deposits along their route issue from the women’s urine.

Renard-Casevitz interprets the salt body as crystallized and immobile but not dead. Within the Matsigenka opposition she analyzes, decomposition is a kind of cooking; Pareni remains “raw,” outside ordinary mortality and time. That interpretation should be distinguished from the narrators’ reported actions.

### Material attributes

Pareni has no securely documented conventional costume, weapon, color, number, weekday, celestial body, sacred plant, or freestanding cult statue. Her securely attested material signs are geographic and alimentary:

- red rock salt in the principal hill;
- white rock salt in the daughter’s hill;
- salt water and springs;
- salty clay licks visited by parrots and macaws;
- fish;
- the Cerro and the river system connecting Urubamba, Tambo and Perené.

A story warns that improperly taking uncooked salt can cause the fragment of Pareni to consume the thief’s skin and flesh. This is evidence for the salt’s continuing personhood and danger, but not proof of a formal taboo universally enforced among all Arawak groups. [Renard-Casevitz, “Guerriers du sel, sauniers de la paix”](https://www.persee.fr/doc/hom_0439-4216_1993_num_33_126_369627).

### Iconography

**[Absence of evidence]** No ancient or early-modern image is securely identified as Pareni. The hill itself is her principal “image” in the sense of a transformed body or topogram. Colonial reports of sacred hills, stones, temples, or solar rites must not be retroactively relabeled depictions of Pareni without an identifying name.

## 4. Genealogy and divine relations

### Family

**[Attested Matsigenka narrative]**

- **Brother:** Pachakamui/Pachakama.
- **Husbands:** Tsonkiri, Shitati, Shiiro, and finally Kinteroni.
- **Children:** several sons transformed into titi monkeys; daughters transformed into deer and tapir; one surviving daughter transformed with Pareni into the smaller salt hill; fish children whose rearing place she seeks.
- **Nephew or quasi-nephew:** Iguiane, “the Carried One,” the small transforming being attached to Pachakamui. Sources differ or remain unstable over whether he is found by, carried by, empowers, or is described as a son of Pachakamui.

No parents, primordial birth, or securely attested divine grandparents are reported in the examined corpus. Pareni already exists in the presocial age when beings are immortal and human-shaped.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Tsonkiri — marriage and transformation.** Pareni supplies him with fish secretly removed from her own body. When he spies on their source and reacts with revulsion, she directs him to feed on flowers and makes him the hummingbird. **Source:** Renard-Casevitz 1992, pp. 138–144. **Tier:** securely attested recorded Matsigenka myth.

- **Shitati — marriage and transformation.** Her dependent, complaining husband refuses ordinary manioc and consumes her excrement; Pareni makes him the dung beetle. **Source:** Renard-Casevitz 1992. **Tier:** securely attested recorded Matsigenka myth.

- **Shiiro — marriage and transformation.** He sucks Pareni’s sweat and becomes the sweat bee. **Source:** Renard-Casevitz 1992; summarized in the Ministry vocabulary. **Tier:** securely attested recorded Matsigenka myth.

- **Kinteroni — marriage, alliance and loss.** The primordial giant armadillo, nearly Pareni’s equal in power, becomes her final husband. They act together against those who transformed their children; in one synthesis Kinteroni catches Pachakamui in a hole and pins Iguiane, but Iguiane transforms Kinteroni into the present giant armadillo before being fixed as the kamona palm. **Sources:** Renard-Casevitz 1992; Baer 1994; Santos-Granero 2004. **Tier:** securely attested cycle with variant allocation of actions.

- **Pachakamui/Pachakama — sibling conflict.** Pareni invites her brother to an initiation celebration; his carried companion transforms her children. She traps or casts Pachakamui into a chasm and prevents his escape, after which Kinteroni or another helper carries and pins him in the upper river country. In another compressed account, Pareni herself causes him to be nailed to a tree with chonta. **Sources:** Pereira/Renard-Casevitz and Dollfus 1988; Renard-Casevitz 1992; later summaries. **Tier:** attested Matsigenka myth, but textually variable.

- **Iguiane (“the Carried One”) — conflict over her children.** Iguiane, attached to and empowering Pachakamui, changes Pareni’s sons and daughters into animals. Pareni and Kinteroni retaliate. Iguiane then tries to dam rivers and unleash a flood, is pursued, and is pinned to the ground or changed into the kamona palm. **Sources:** Renard-Casevitz 1992; Santos-Granero 2004. **Tier:** attested in the published Matsigenka cycle; probable borrowed or reworked component.

- **Kashiri — no narrated interaction securely attested.** Renard-Casevitz compares their complementary gifts and gendered movements, but comparison is not an episode. **Tier:** modern scholarly structural comparison.

- **Avíreri/Avireri and Kíri — no direct meeting securely attested.** Their Asháninka cycle resembles the Pachakamui–Iguiane portion of Pareni’s story; Renard-Casevitz proposes borrowing through exchange relations. They should not be inserted as characters whom Pareni personally met. **Tier:** modern historical-comparative reconstruction.

- **Ayots and Poporrona’ — no direct meeting securely attested.** These Yanesha figures belong to a structurally related giant-and-dangerous-child complex discussed by Santos-Granero. Their relationship to the Pareni text is comparative, not episodic. **Tier:** modern scholarly comparison.

- **Posona’ — no interaction securely attested.** Posona’ is the Yanesha male salt-being occupying the analogous landscape role. **Tier:** regional alternative, not a divine encounter.

- **Kipatsi — no direct interaction securely attested.** Kipatsi is the female living Earth on which the primordial events occur. The sources place Pareni within her world but do not narrate a discrete encounter. **Tier:** shared cosmological setting only.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The marriages and creation of animal species

**[Securely attested 1991 oral texts, published 1992]** Pareni marries repeatedly. Each husband violates, caricatures, or fails the expected reciprocity of domestic life.

Tsonkiri receives fish from Pareni, who says she has already eaten. He spies and discovers that she draws the fish from her anus or body. His disgust and vomiting expose the secret. Pareni sends him to live from flowers, turning him into the hummingbird.

Shitati does not hunt and cries for food. He refuses proper manioc but consumes Pareni’s excrement; he becomes the dung beetle. Shiiro feeds on her sweat and becomes a sweat bee. The transformations make present animal diet and behavior legible as the enduring result of primordial social failures.

The item-specific description “the mother who fed her sons-in-law and turned them into animals” is **not supported in that form by the examined Pareni texts**. The full 1992 narrative describes Pareni feeding and transforming her **husbands**. Matsigenka domestic expectations do require hunters to supply affinal households, which may have encouraged later paraphrastic confusion between husband and son-in-law. Unless another independently documented variant can be produced, “sons-in-law” should be marked unverified.

### Kinteroni, Pachakamui and Iguiane

**[Attested cycle; disputed historical layering]** Pareni finally marries Kinteroni, the primordial armadillo, and has many children. Her brother Pachakamui travels with a small male figure clinging to his back. The companion—called Iguiane, “the Carried One”—gives Pachakamui his power and recklessly changes people into animals, threatening to transform Pachakamui himself if opposed.

During preparations for an initiation celebration, the transformer turns Pareni’s sons into titi monkeys and her daughters into deer and tapir; one daughter is transformed after leaving ritual seclusion. Pareni retaliates against her brother, casting or confining him in a chasm. His attempts to escape as an ant, bamboo or vine fail. Kinteroni or another relative then carries him upriver and pins or crucifies him with chonta. The pinning explains earthquakes and, in some versions, mortality: movement by the bound transformer threatens cosmic collapse.

Iguiane seeks his father or bearer, threatens people, and attempts to block rivers and cause a flood. Kinteroni pursues him through a sequence of progressively younger gardens that function as temporal tracks. He catches and carries him, then fixes him to the ground with a peach-palm stake. The closing transformations leave Kinteroni as armadillo and Iguiane as, or associated with, the kamona palm.

### Is this an Indigenous old core or a colonial borrowing?

Two distinct propositions must be separated.

1. **[Modern comparative reconstruction] Asháninka borrowing:** Renard-Casevitz argues that the Pachakamui–Iguiane section belongs to a “different tradition” and entered the Matsigenka Pareni cycle through trade with Asháninka neighbors. The close resemblance to **Avíreri and Kíri** supports diffusion or shared development.

2. **[Modern colonial-mimesis hypothesis] Christian Saint Christopher borrowing:** Santos-Granero argues that the related Yanesha/Asháninka/Ashéninka/Nomatsiguenga giant-and-child cycle derives from a colonial transformation of Saint Christopher carrying the Christ Child. In the transformed cycle the giant and child become antisocial, cannibalistic transformers; the myth subsequently supports beliefs about child sorcery. Crucially, Santos-Granero notes that the cycle is otherwise found among the four Arawak groups with historically documented child-sorcery accusations, but not traditionally among Yine or Matsigenka; its insertion in one Pareni version therefore plausibly reflects secondary borrowing. [Santos-Granero, “Saint Christopher in the Amazon”](https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/ec1a0175-8552-44f3-a358-bb24cb826716/content); [Spanish text](https://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?pid=S0254-92122005000100002&script=sci_arttext).

Neither hypothesis demonstrates that every transformer motif in the episode is Christian. Nor does it establish a precise date or single moment of transmission. The evidence supports a colonial or post-contact reconfiguration more securely than a wholly pre-Columbian, pan-Arawak canonical myth.

### The journey and becoming Cerro de la Sal

**[Securely attested Matsigenka narrative]** Bereft of husbands and most human children, Pareni travels with her remaining daughter to find a place suitable for raising her fish children. They descend the Urubamba, then ascend the Tambo and Perené, leaving Matsigenka territory and entering Asháninka and Yanesha lands.

Where either woman urinates, salt springs, deposits and salty clay licks arise. Names such as **Chimiato** (“water of the salt pit”), **Tiviha** (“salt spring”) and **Potiarihi** (“salty water”) map their route. In the upper Perené, Pareni becomes the large reddish salt hill; her daughter becomes the smaller white deposit; Tiviari, “Salt River,” runs below. The Perené itself is traditionally explained through her name. [Santos-Granero, “Arawakan Sacred Landscapes,” pp. 97–99](https://repository.si.edu/server/api/core/bitstreams/bb72ea57-cccb-44a4-88d4-19be9413594c/content).

The myth makes a political-geographic argument as well as an origin story: a Matsigenka ancestress travels across the territories of related peoples and becomes their common strategic resource. Renard-Casevitz reads this as a model of a dispersed Arawak network whose center was constituted by journeys and exchange rather than centralized government.

## 6. Cult, Cerro de la Sal, and ritual practice

### The physical site

The Cerro de la Sal is in the modern Villa Rica district of Oxapampa Province, Pasco. “Cerro” can refer to the deposit, the chain of hills, or the larger mission region. A 2023 geological study dates the great evaporite deposit itself to approximately **215–210 million years ago** and proposes “Pareni Formation” or “Pareni Salt” as its geological name. That nomenclature is a modern scientific commemoration of the myth, not evidence that the mythology is Triassic or prehistoric. [Geological study](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/368390221_An_overlooked_giant_salt_basin_in_Peru).

### Extraction and exchange

**[Colonial-historical and ethnographic evidence]** During the dry season, hundreds of Asháninka, Yanesha, Matsigenka, Yine/Piro, Conibo and other visitors gathered to extract, cook or process, carry and exchange salt. Approximately twenty-kilogram blocks were moved to waterways and transported by raft. Salt functioned as food, preservative, gift, and general equivalent in exchange; reciprocal trading partnerships extended the Cerro’s influence across the central forest and toward the Ucayali. [Cerro history and trade summary](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cerro_de_la_Sal); [Rojas Zolezzi 2025](https://es.scribd.com/document/987120057/1-Rojas).

The Cerro was therefore not a “temple of Pareni” in the familiar architectural sense. It was simultaneously ancestral body, mine, seasonal meeting ground, political center, peace zone and market. Renard-Casevitz’s formulation “warriors of salt, salt-workers of peace” captures the evidence that peoples hostile elsewhere could meet under regulated conditions there. No durable centralized Arawak state or universally recognized high priesthood has been demonstrated.

### Ritual precautions

**[Ethnographic and missionary reports]**

- Salt had to be cooked or properly processed; improperly taking a raw piece of Pareni could cause supernatural injury.
- Some outsiders were warned that a jaguar or dragon would devour unauthorized entrants. Izaguirre records the jaguar warning; Álvarez Lobo gives the dragon version. These may encode territorial restriction as supernatural danger.
- A 1663 missionary report understood rites on two salt hills as “idolatry.”
- Later sources report veneration of the sun at two Cerro hills, but this should not automatically be called worship of Pareni.
- The salt and landscape could be approached as living or agentive rather than inert property.

No securely attributable hymn, prayer formula, festival name, priestly costume, initiation exclusively for Pareni, temple prostitution, or human sacrifice to Pareni has been found.

### Franciscan appropriation

The Franciscans understood that controlling the Cerro could concentrate otherwise dispersed populations. Jiménez’s 1635 chapel attempted to superimpose Christian worship on a sacred Indigenous place. Missionaries repeatedly proposed regulating access to salt, requiring visitors to carry certificates of mission attendance or good conduct, and placing extraction under colonial administration. The policy joined conversion to economic coercion.

Jiménez was killed in 1637 in an Indigenous ambush attributed to the chief Zampati. Further Franciscan deaths and expulsions followed in the 1640s and 1687. In 1709 the mission was re-established as **Cristo Crucificado del Cerro de la Sal**, supported by armed guards and linked to Quimiri, Eneno, Nijandaris and Metraro. The mission’s Christian name is not evidence that Pareni was identified with Christ; it records attempted replacement and institutional occupation. [Franciscan mission narrative](https://pruebacerrolasaljsa.wordpress.com/juan-santos-atahualpa/ii-las-misiones-franciscanas/); [IHEAL history](https://books.openedition.org/iheal/13067).

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before European documentation

**[Reconstruction]** Archaeology and ethnohistory support a substantial pre-Columbian exchange network around the deposit, connecting eastern Andean and Amazonian societies. The precise date at which the hill became Pareni in Matsigenka narration cannot be established. Claims that the complete published cycle is pre-Inca or immemorial exceed the evidence.

### 1635–1708: first Franciscan penetration

The Cerro was already an Indigenous gathering and extraction center when Jiménez arrived in 1635. Mission building, attempted surveillance, and demands for military backing provoked recurring conflict. Pedro Bohórquez’s mid-seventeenth-century expedition added abuse and treasure-seeking to the instability. The documentary record concerns control of land, bodies and salt; it does not yet securely record the name Pareni.

### 1709–1742: mission system and coercive concentration

Francisco de San Joseph and the College of Santa Rosa de Ocopa developed a chain of missions. Armed personnel, including enslaved Africans in some establishments, supported mission discipline. Epidemics devastated concentrated settlements: Eneno reportedly fell from roughly 800 inhabitants to about 220 in 1722–1723. Mission residence consequently became associated with disease and death.

### 1742–1752: Juan Santos Atahualpa rebellion

Juan Santos Atahualpa united primarily Asháninka and Yanesha forces against the missions. Cerro access and its communication network were strategically important. Most missions collapsed; Spanish punitive expeditions failed to recapture the forest interior. The uprising restored approximately a century of effective Indigenous autonomy. It should not be called a “cult of Pareni”: surviving evidence ties the rebellion to a broader messianic, Inca-Christian and anti-colonial field, not to a documented oracle or command from Pareni. [Jones, “Evolution of Spanish Governance”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/americas/article/abs/evolution-of-spanish-governance-during-the-early-bourbon-period-in-peru-the-juan-santos-atahualpa-rebellion-and-the-missionaries-of-ocopa/4827334EA1AE1CCFA4D3C78DC5C70DE8).

After the expulsion of the missions, salt exchange continued and was augmented by temple-forges producing and repairing iron tools. Sacred sites included Cerro hills, Yanesha stone divinities at Palmaso, a Gran Pajonal solar-festival site and, later, Juan Santos’s tomb at Metraro. These were linked in a regional sacred economy but were not a single Pareni temple system. [Santos-Granero, “The Arawakan Matrix”](https://stri-apps.si.edu/docs/publications/pdfs/2002_SGranero_TheArawakan_Matrix.pdf).

### 1842–1930: republican reconquest and dispossession

Peru founded the San Ramón fort in 1842. Renewed military campaigns between 1847 and 1880, epidemics and settlement pushed Asháninka and Yanesha communities away from the Andean piedmont. European colonization expanded after 1873; the Peruvian Corporation later acquired a huge regional concession.

Gabriel Sala reported in 1896–1897 that settlers were blocking Indigenous extraction and compelling Indigenous labor for commercial exploitation. Asháninka attacks on settler estates followed. Villa Rica was founded in 1928 by German-descended settlers from Pozuzo. These events altered control of Pareni’s material body even when the name is absent from official records.

### Twentieth century: Christianization and ethnographic documentation

Catholic and especially Adventist missions altered settlement, ritual and education. An Adventist-inspired movement in the Perené region gathered hundreds into mission settlements in 1928; severe measles epidemics followed. None of this is securely documented as reinterpretation of Pareni herself.

The named Pareni cycle first entered the scholarly archive in the late twentieth century through Matsigenka narrators and bilingual intermediaries. The delay between colonial documentation of the Cerro and modern documentation of the name is a central fact, not evidence that the modern narrators invented her.

### 1980s–1990s: Shining Path and MRTA violence

Asháninka and Yanesha communities suffered forced recruitment, displacement, killings and military violence during the conflicts involving Sendero Luminoso, MRTA and the Peruvian armed forces. From 1989 communities formed armed self-defense groups, and insurgent forces were largely expelled from their territories by about 1995. Santos-Granero connects the upheaval to a resurgence of child-sorcery accusations in related Arawak communities. There is, however, **no located evidence that Sendero, MRTA, the army, or Indigenous defense organizations mobilized Pareni by name**. [Santos-Granero, historical appendices](https://www.scielo.org.pe/scielo.php?pid=S0254-92122005000100002&script=sci_arttext).

### Present

The Cerro remains a significant Indigenous sacred landscape and historical symbol. Traditional salt extraction reportedly persisted into the late twentieth century, though industrial goods and loss of territorial control reduced its old economic centrality. Pareni now also appears in educational resources, cultural-history publications and scholarly discussions of landscape personhood. The evidence supports continued remembrance; it does not establish the size, organization, or continuity of any formal present-day “Pareni cult.”

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Anthropology and Indigenous education

Pareni’s modern public identity was principally shaped by Renard-Casevitz’s phrase **“Salt Lady”**, her structural comparison with Kashiri, and Santos-Granero’s presentation of her as a traveling creator whose journey writes history into the landscape. Contemporary educational publications classify her alongside other “terrestrial deities.” This makes the mythology accessible but may impose a bounded, Greco-Roman-like pantheon on fluid Matsigenka categories.

### Geology

**[Modern commemorative naming]** Geologists have proposed **Pareni Formation/Pareni Salt** for the Late Triassic evaporite unit. This is a documented twenty-first-century scientific coinage based on Renard-Casevitz’s account.

### Popular mythology sites

At least one modern “pantheon registry” calls Pareni a “demigod,” assigns her a parrot familiar, and presents a neat portfolio of transformation and species creation. “Demigod” is not supported by the primary ethnography, and the familiar should not be accepted without checking the cited modern Matsigenka collection. Such registry pages are derivative reception, not evidence of ancient classification. [Modern registry entry](https://www.listofgods.com/registry/matsigenka_pareni.html).

### Occultism, neopaganism, fiction and politics

The dedicated search found no credible evidence for:

- Renaissance hermetic or ceremonial-magical use;
- Theosophical, Golden Dawn, chaos-magical or grimoire reception;
- an organized neopagan Pareni movement;
- Jungian literature of consequence;
- major appearances in film, novels, comics or games;
- nationalist, fascist or other extremist appropriation;
- a commercial cult product industry;
- identification with Lot’s wife, a European salt goddess, Pachamama, the Virgin Mary or another global figure.

Any modern webpage presenting such associations as ancient should be treated as modern invention unless it supplies a traceable Indigenous or historical source.

## 9. Historiography and contested questions

### Is Pareni Matsigenka, Asháninka, or pan-Arawak?

Renard-Casevitz presents Pareni as a unifying figure of the “Campa” or pre-Andean Arawak complex. Santos-Granero’s comparison supports a shared regional sacred geography but explicitly warns that Yanesha people tell a different, male-centered Posona’ story. The best conclusion is:

- the fully narrated Pareni cycle is securely Matsigenka;
- Pareni/Tsiwi language and female salt imagery occur in Matsigenka–Asháninka comparison;
- the Cerro was multiethnic;
- no single pan-Arawak canonical theology existed.

### Was the Pachakamui episode borrowed from the Avíreri cycle?

**Renard-Casevitz:** yes, probably; its different narrative character and resemblance to Asháninka Avíreri–Kíri suggest incorporation along trade routes.

**Santos-Granero:** agrees that the insertion is anomalous and places the wider giant-and-child story in a colonial history ultimately involving Saint Christopher.

**Caution:** similarity demonstrates relationship, not direction by itself. Trade could transmit narratives in several directions, and an earlier shared Arawak substrate cannot be categorically excluded. The distribution and the Christian parallels make post-contact borrowing plausible, not proved beyond dispute.

### Did Pareni’s name give rise to Perené?

The myth says the river followed by Pareni bears her name. Linguistics records *pareni* as “river” and notes that the toponym’s etymology is opaque to younger speakers. Mythic eponymy and lexical derivation remain competing explanations; neither should be announced as settled.

### Is Cerro de la Sal itself a temple?

Colonial and ethnographic sources document ceremonies, sacred hills, restrictions, seasonal congregation and—in the post-1742 period—temple-forges in the wider upper Perené. Calling the whole Cerro “Pareni’s temple” can therefore be a useful metaphor but overstates the architectural and institutional evidence.

### Human sacrifice, sexual rites, and “temple prostitution”

No evidence located in the reviewed primary or scholarly sources attributes human sacrifice, coerced sacred sex, or “temple prostitution” to Pareni. Colonial writers’ word *idolatría* cannot establish any of these practices. Reports of killings in the region concern missionary warfare, rebellion, child-sorcery accusations, settler violence and twentieth-century armed conflict; they must not be reassigned to Pareni’s cult.

### Confusion with Peruvian selva folklore

The Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna and Pishtaco cycles documented in Loreto and Ucayali compilations are important Amazonian traditions but are not episodes of Pareni’s narrative. Regan, Galeano, Cabrejos, Chirif, Dean, Gow and the Pishtaco literature provide comparative contexts for landscape beings, predation, trade, colonial violence and regional folklore. No securely attested meeting, genealogy or syncretism between Pareni and those beings was found. Their mere coexistence within “Peruvian Amazonian mythology” is not evidence of a shared pantheon.

## 10. Chronology

- **Pre-Columbian period — [historical reconstruction]:** Cerro de la Sal operates as a major exchange focus joining eastern Andean and Amazonian networks. No surviving text names Pareni.

- **1635 — [secure colonial documentation]:** Jerónimo Jiménez reaches the Cerro, recognizes its strategic congregation of Indigenous peoples, and establishes Franciscan foundations.

- **1637 — [secure colonial history]:** Jiménez and companions are killed during Indigenous resistance associated with Zampati.

- **1640s–1650s — [secure colonial history]:** Further mission failures and the abusive expedition of Pedro Bohórquez.

- **1663 — [secure colonial description]:** A memorial describes three salt hills, differently colored salt and a saline river; rites are characterized by missionaries as “idolatry.” The personal name Pareni is not securely present.

- **1682 — [primary Franciscan source]:** Manuel Biedma’s account advocates administrative control of the Cerro and conditional Indigenous access.

- **1687 — [colonial history]:** Renewed mission occupation and revolt.

- **1709 — [secure colonial history]:** Mission system re-established; mass celebrated at Quimiri on 4 December; the salt-center mission becomes Cristo Crucificado del Cerro de la Sal.

- **1722–1723 — [colonial demographic record]:** Epidemics devastate mission populations, including Eneno.

- **1742 — [secure colonial history]:** Juan Santos Atahualpa’s rebellion destroys or empties most missions; colonial access to the Cerro collapses.

- **1742–1752 — [secure colonial history]:** Rebellion withstands Spanish campaigns; Indigenous regional autonomy is restored.

- **Late eighteenth–early nineteenth centuries — [regional history]:** Salt exchange continues; upper Perené temples/forges and pilgrimage places integrate trade, ritual and metallurgy.

- **1842 — [republican documentation]:** Fort founded at San Ramón as Peru begins reconquest of the region.

- **1847–1880 — [secure historical record]:** Military advance, colonization and epidemics displace Arawak communities from the piedmont.

- **1885 — [published travel testimony]:** Ordinaire documents regional traditions and sacred sites; this is evidence for late nineteenth-century belief, not direct pre-contact observation.

- **1891 — [secure political history]:** Large land concession granted to the Peruvian Corporation.

- **1896–1897 — [missionary testimony]:** Gabriel Sala reports settler obstruction of Indigenous salt access and coerced labor; anti-settler resistance follows.

- **1921–1933 — [secure religious history]:** Adventist missions and millenarian expectations reshape upper Perené settlement; epidemics produce crisis and resistance.

- **1928 — [settlement history]:** Villa Rica founded near the salt region.

- **1953–1975 — [ethnographic background]:** Pérez Marcio, Weiss and other writers publish important material on central-forest mythology and cosmology, but no earlier full Pareni text has been verified in them.

- **1987 — [earliest presently identified named publication]:** Fidel Pereira publishes or supplies a recorded Pareni version.

- **1988 — [critical publication]:** Renard-Casevitz and Dollfus publish related Matsigenka material, including the Pachakamui narrative.

- **1989–1995 — [secure modern history]:** Asháninka and Yanesha communities endure Sendero Luminoso/MRTA conflict and form self-defense forces; child-sorcery accusations revive in some areas. No Pareni-specific role is established.

- **1991 — [primary oral documentation]:** Jorge Martin and María Luisa Ríos supply Matsigenka salt narratives; José Epifanio Pereira supplies the longer cycle used by Renard-Casevitz.

- **1992 — [principal edition]:** Renard-Casevitz publishes “Sel : femme gemme, femme condiment.”

- **1993 — [historical synthesis]:** “Guerriers du sel, sauniers de la paix” interprets the Cerro as the crystallized body of an ancestral woman and as the center of a decentralized exchange network.

- **1994 — [translation and parallel synthesis]:** Spanish translation of Renard-Casevitz appears; Baer publishes *Cosmología y shamanismo de los Matsiguenga*.

- **2002/2005 — [historiographic debate]:** Santos-Granero’s “Saint Christopher in the Amazon” develops the colonial-borrowing interpretation of the related giant-and-child myth; the Spanish version appears in 2005.

- **2004 — [comparative synthesis]:** Santos-Granero publishes “Arawakan Sacred Landscapes,” placing Pareni within a comparative southern Arawak geography while preserving the Yanesha Posona’ distinction.

- **2011–present — [living transmission]:** Matsigenka collections and educational materials preserve and classify the narrative.

- **2023 — [modern scientific reception]:** Geologists propose “Pareni Formation/Pareni Salt” for the Cerro’s Late Triassic evaporite unit.

- **2025 — [current scholarship]:** Rojas Zolezzi publishes a detailed study of salt as divine gift, currency and exchange medium among Asháninka communities.

## 11. Sources and assessment

The central primary-text source is Renard-Casevitz’s 1992 edition because it names narrators, languages and collection dates. Santos-Granero’s landscape study is the clearest accessible synthesis of three versions and explicitly records the Yanesha contradiction. His Saint Christopher study is essential for the disputed colonial layering of the Pachakamui/Iguiane material.

Franciscan chronicles—Biedma, San Antonio, Amich and Izaguirre—are primary sources for colonial intervention but hostile or institutionally interested witnesses to Indigenous religion. Their descriptions of *idolatry*, savagery or mission “discovery” must be read as colonial classifications. The Cerro was not literally discovered in 1635; Indigenous people guided Jiménez to an already functioning interregional center.

Weiss’s *Campa Cosmology*, Smith’s Yanesha work, Shaver and Dodd’s Nomatsiguenga research, Brown and Fernández’s *War of Shadows*, and Santos-Granero’s *The Power of Love* illuminate neighboring transformer cycles, cosmology, child-sorcery beliefs and political violence. They cannot automatically supply details about Pareni. Likewise, Gow’s Yine/Piro studies, Loreto folklore collections, Urarina ethnography and Pishtaco studies concern distinct narrative systems unless an actual textual connection is demonstrated.

The chief archival deficiency is chronological: the named cycle is documented only in late twentieth-century transcription, while the sacred and political importance of its landscape is documented from the seventeenth century. That gap permits responsible historical hypotheses but no confident reconstruction of a single “original” Pareni myth.
