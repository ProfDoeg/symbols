# The Pishtaco (Ñak'aq, Kharisiri): Research Dossier

> **Class:** A human or para-human predatory figure, “bogeyman,” occult assailant, and accusation category—not a god and not securely a member of any bounded “Amazonian pantheon.” Depending on the community and period, a pishtaco may be described as a living professional killer, a white or mestizo outsider, a friar, landowner, soldier, engineer, government agent, medical worker, tourist, sorcerer, or technologically equipped nonhuman stranger.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** A report written about 1575 by Cristóbal de Molina, recalling a rumor circulating around 1560 that Spaniards had been sent to Peru to kill Indigenous people and obtain their *untu*, human fat, as medicine for an otherwise incurable Spanish disease. Molina does **not** use the subsequently documented name *pishtaco*, and his passage does not establish a pre-Hispanic fat-stealing being.  
> **Evidentiary caution:** *Pishtaco*, Quechua *ñak’aq/naqaq*, and Aymara *kharisiri/lik’ichiri* belong to a historically related complex, but their actions, victims, methods, and ontological status vary. “Pishtaco” is used below as a comparative heading, not as proof that every regional figure is one timeless being.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** colonial documentary evidence recording a contemporary or recent belief.
- **[LE] Later elaboration:** a motif documented after the earliest colonial report.
- **[RV] Regional variant:** a locally attested form that should not automatically be generalized.
- **[F] Folklore/ethnography:** an oral claim documented by a named collector or fieldworker.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an interpretation proposed by researchers rather than stated by narrators.
- **[MI] Modern invention or adaptation:** demonstrably recent narrative, artwork, entertainment, or institutional claim.
- **[U] Unestablished:** repeated assertion for which adequate primary documentation has not been located.

## 1. Identification, names, and classifications

### Pishtaco

**[F/RV]** The central- and north-Peruvian form is written *pishtaco*, *pistaco*, *pishtako*, or *pishtaku*. It is generally connected with Quechua *pishtay*—to cut, slaughter, cut into pieces, or cut a throat—and more precisely with an agentive form such as *pishtakuq*, “one who cuts/slaughters.” Modern Spanish usage often treats *pishtaco* as a noun independent of Quechua morphology.

The name does not itself mean “fat sucker.” Cutting is primary in its etymology; fat extraction is the figure’s defining narrative action.

### Ñak’aq / naqaq

**[F/RV]** In southern Quechua regions, especially Ayacucho, Apurímac, Cuzco, and adjoining areas, forms include *ñak’aq*, *ñaqaq*, *naq’aq*, and Spanish spellings such as *nakaq*. They derive from a Quechua verb associated with slaughtering, cutting the throat, or butchering.

Older ethnographies sometimes gloss *ñak’aq* as a ritual slaughterer. That lexical meaning does not prove that the colonial fat-stealer directly descends from an identifiable pre-Hispanic priestly office.

### Kharisiri, kharikhari, lik’ichiri

**[F/RV]** Aymara communities use *kharisiri*, *karisiri*, or *kharikhari*, associated with *khariña*, “to cut.” *Lik’ichiri/llik’ichiri* relates to *lik’i/llik’i*, fat or grease. The ordinary glosses are therefore “cutter” and “fat-taker/fat-maker.”

The kharisiri is frequently less visibly violent than the classic knife-bearing pishtaco. In Altiplano accounts he can incapacitate a sleeping traveler with powder, prayer, a small device, needle, or magical act, remove fat without an obvious wound, and leave the victim to weaken and die later. Treatment may require replacing the lost vitality with fat from a black llama. These are **regional ethnographic forms**, not details securely present in Molina’s sixteenth-century report.

### Sacamantecas, degollador, cortacuellos, sacaojos, robaniños, pelacaras

These Spanish terms mean, respectively, “fat-taker,” “throat-cutter,” “neck-cutter,” “eye-remover,” “child-stealer,” and “face-peeler.”

**[LE/RV]** They are not simple lexical translations of a single being. They form an expandable family of accusations concerning the commercial extraction of Indigenous bodies. Juan Ansión’s 1989 collection deliberately traced a transition “from executioners to eye-stealers”: late-twentieth-century rumors about stolen eyes or organs replicated the older logic of fat removal while changing the commodity.

**[RV]** In Loreto and Ucayali, *pelacara/sacacara* can denote a face-stealer rather than a fat-remover. Peter Gow records Piro/Yine children calling him a *sacacara* or pishtaco: it cut off the face and removed the heart. The two categories overlapped locally but were not identical.

### Classification

No primary evidence establishes the figure as a god, goddess, angel, or worshipped demon.

- **[EA]** Molina’s agents are Spaniards allegedly collecting fat.
- **[F]** Highland narrators often describe an actual human killer with occult techniques and protection from powerful institutions.
- **[F/RV]** Some accounts make the figure a sorcerer, shape-shifter, invulnerable stranger, or member of a secret organization.
- **[F/RV]** Amazonian versions can approach the category of malignant forest or technological being.
- **[MSR]** Anthropologists classify the complex as rumor, legend, an image of unequal exchange, institutionalized fear, or an Indigenous theory of colonial and capitalist extraction.

“Demon” is serviceable only as a loose English description of conduct. It is not a stable traditional taxon.

## 2. Earliest attestation and documentary corpus

### The report of Cristóbal de Molina

**[EA, report c.1575 concerning c.1560]** In a discussion connected with the Taki Onqoy crisis, Molina says Indigenous people had believed that people in Spain sent to Peru for Indigenous *untu* because no other medicine could cure a certain disease. Fear became so intense that people avoided carrying firewood, grass, and other goods into Spaniards’ houses lest they be killed inside and their fat extracted.

The passage establishes:

1. a fat-taking rumor existed very early in colonial Peru;
2. the supposed beneficiaries were Spaniards;
3. the commodity was medicine;
4. the rumor disrupted daily relations between Indigenous people and colonists.

It does **not** establish:

1. the word *pishtaco*;
2. a pre-Conquest deity;
3. a standardized physical appearance or killing method;
4. church bells, cannon grease, machinery, airplanes, cosmetics, or organ trafficking;
5. that Spaniards actually operated a systematic fat trade.

Molina attributed the rumor polemically to the insurgent refuge at Vilcabamba and treated it as deliberate agitation. That is a colonial clerical interpretation, not independent proof of the rumor’s origin.

### Antonio de Herrera

**[LE, 1601]** Antonio de Herrera y Tordesillas subsequently reported related beliefs that Spaniards used Indigenous fat medicinally, including on wounds or sores. Because Herrera wrote an imperial history rather than recording an independent local oral performance, his testimony confirms early circulation but must not automatically be counted as a separate origin.

### Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century clerical associations

**[LE/F]** Traditions later attached the fat-taker to friars—especially, in some Cuzco narratives, Bethlehemites—and to the belief that human grease improved church bells, polished saints, or served ecclesiastical medicine. Ricardo Palma’s “Los barbones,” in *Tradiciones peruanas*, literaryized the accusation against friars.

No evidence located shows that human fat was materially required for bell casting. The motif instead associates a newly dominant sound and substance—European metal bells—with violent extraction. It is historically revealing as belief, not as metallurgy.

### Twentieth-century ethnographic corpus

- **Anthony Oliver-Smith, 1969:** “The Pishtaco: Institutionalized Fear in Highland Peru,” based on highland ethnography, established the figure as a serious social fear rather than merely a tale.
- **Gerald Weiss, 1975:** *Campa Cosmology*, documenting Asháninka/Campa cosmology and the incorporation of whites and missionaries into Indigenous historical mythology.
- **Juan Ansión and collaborators, 1989:** *Pishtacos: de verdugos a sacaojos*, a collection on slaughterers, eye-stealers, ethnic consciousness, rumor, and political violence.
- **Mary Weismantel, 2001:** *Cholas and Pishtacos*, interpreting the white male predator through race, sex, exchange, and accumulation.
- **Andrew Canessa:** work on the Aymara kharisiri, criticizing explanations that reduce it simply to fear of outsiders and emphasizing shifting boundaries of identity.
- **Fernando Santos-Granero, 1998:** detailed Yanesha evidence linking pishtaco rumors to road construction and the physical destruction of a sacred/historic landscape.
- **Peter Gow, 2001:** Piro/Yine evidence for the *sacacara/pishtaco*, gringo suspicion, and the historical transformation of myth.
- **Regan, 1983:** Loreto evidence for whites becoming nocturnal pishtacos and for the *pelacaras* rumor.

### Sources named in the brief but not evidentially interchangeable

Santos-Granero’s *The Power of Love* and his 2002 article on Saint Christopher concern Yanesha and wider southern-Arawak transformations, but the Saint Christopher/Christ-child story is principally evidence for a distinct child-sorcery complex. It should not be silently converted into a pishtaco myth.

Likewise, the Franciscan chronicles of Biedma, Amich, Izaguirre, Sala, Ordinaire, Navarro, and San Antonio; Smith’s Yanesha work; Shaver and Dodds on the Nomatsigenga; Renard-Casevitz and Rojas Zolezzi on Matsigenka peoples; Brown and Fernández on Asháninka political history; Dean on Urarina society; and Loreto/Ucayali collections concerning the Bufeo, Chullachaqui, and Yacuruna are essential contextual corpora. Their inclusion in a bibliography does not demonstrate that each contains a pishtaco episode. No such episode should be attributed without a page or identifiable narrative.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain

The being has no divinely governed domain. Its recurring field is **extraction**:

- **[EA]** Indigenous fat as medicine for Spaniards.
- **[LE]** Fat for treating wounds.
- **[LE/F]** Fat for church bells or saints’ images.
- **[F]** Grease for guns, cannon, sugar mills, railways, roads, mines, automobiles, factory machinery, aircraft, rockets, medicines, cosmetics, and foreign technology.
- **[F/MI]** Eyes, faces, blood, hearts, kidneys, sexual organs, fetuses, or transplantable organs.
- **[F]** Bodies buried beneath bridges, roads, dams, or buildings to make construction succeed.
- **[F]** Victims killed to pay the national foreign debt or enrich foreign institutions.

The commodity changes with political economy. The stable relation is that a powerful outsider converts an Indigenous body into value that leaves the community.

### Appearance

**[F]** The recurrent type is an adult man marked as socially foreign: pale or “gringo,” bearded or unusually hairy, physically large, well-equipped, and traveling alone or with institutional backing. He may dress as:

- a friar or priest;
- a mounted hacendado;
- a soldier, policeman, or civil guard;
- a road worker, engineer, miner, or surveyor;
- a doctor, nurse, aid worker, or organ trafficker;
- a missionary, anthropologist, photographer, tourist, or ayahuasca visitor;
- a technologically protected raider.

The whiteness is not absolute. Some accounts admit mestizo, Indigenous, Black, female, or coerced pishtacos. What matters is often the predatory relation to the victim and to institutions, not phenotype alone.

### Implements and methods

**[F/RV]**

- long knife, machete, hook, rope, or axe;
- powder that causes magical sleep;
- needle, tube, bottle, or small extracting apparatus;
- a cave or isolated workshop;
- hanging the victim upside down and collecting dripping fat;
- removing fat through a cut, navel, anus, back, or imperceptible aperture;
- cutting the throat and dismembering the body;
- draining the victim without immediate death;
- electric or laser-like weapons in recent Amazonian accounts.

Some narrators describe a visibly butchered corpse; others insist that no wound remains and diagnosis depends on progressive weakness, wasting, fever, or altered behavior.

### Fat as substance

**[F/MSR]** In many Andean ritual systems, fat—especially llama fat—is a concentrated source of vitality and an important offering. Human fat forcibly removed for private sale reverses sacrificial reciprocity: instead of fat circulating between community and sacred powers, it leaves through an unequal transaction.

This is a persuasive interpretation but not proof that the pishtaco began as a pre-Hispanic sacrificer. De Pribyl’s medical-anthropological review notes that securely described ritual fat offerings are normally animal, especially llama, fat. Molinié and Rivière therefore reject a straightforward equation between traditional sacrificer and pishtaco: the latter’s extraction serves foreign or private enrichment, not a reciprocal offering to known gods.

### Iconography

There is no ancient canonical iconography.

**[MI, twentieth century]** Ayacucho retablo artists, notably Nicario Jiménez Quispe, created influential visual histories showing friars extracting fat for bells and later pishtacos attached to modern political and technological power. These are modern Indigenous interpretations of history, not surviving colonial cult images.

Popular illustrations, films, television, and games increasingly depict a monstrous vampire or cryptid. That visual standardization is modern.

No sacred colors, calendrical days, numbers, animals, plants, celestial bodies, canonical weapons, or regalia are securely attested.

## 4. Genealogy and relations with other beings

The pishtaco has no generally attested parents, consort, offspring, siblings, servants, mount, pantheon rank, or divine genealogy. It is commonly reproduced socially—by recruitment, profession, foreign origin, secret initiation, or institutional employment—rather than through mythic descent.

### Every documented interaction with a named divine or sacred being

- **Viracocha / the *viracochas*** — **[RV/F; Asháninka material documented in twentieth-century ethnography]** In one Asháninka historical-cosmological complex, primordial whites identified as *viracochas* are drawn from a lake through the disobedience of a shaman-Inca’s son; whites later associated with Franciscans and pishtaco-like predators belong to this account. The term *viracocha* also historically became an Indigenous designation for Spaniards/whites, so the episode must not be reduced to “the god Viracocha fathered the pishtaco.”
- **Unnamed mountain beings/hills** — **[RV/F; Yanesha, 1983, Santos-Granero]** Hills damaged by road blasting become angry and demand human feeding; contractors are believed to hire pishtacos to kill and bury victims at landslide sites. Because the beings are not individually named in the documented passage, this is not a checkable interaction with a named god.
- **Catholic God, saints, and church bells** — **[LE/F]** Fat is said to serve churches, bells, saints’ images, or priests. These are institutional associations, not narrative meetings between Pishtaco and a named deity.
- **Saint Christopher and the Christ Child** — **No securely demonstrated pishtaco interaction.** Santos-Granero’s 2002 study documents a southern-Arawak transformation of Saint Christopher and the Child Christ into a cannibalistic giant and dangerous child-sorcery complex. It is historically comparable colonial mimesis, but the article does not warrant merging those beings with Pishtaco.
- **Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, Tunche, or other named Amazonian figures** — **No specific interaction securely established in the sources examined.** Co-presence in Loreto or Ucayali folklore collections is not an interaction.

## 5. Major narrative cycles

### The Spanish medicinal-fat rumor

**[EA, c.1560/c.1575]** Spain suffers an otherwise incurable disease. Spaniards in Peru require Indigenous human fat as medicine. Indigenous people consequently avoid entering Spaniards’ houses.

This is the earliest recoverable narrative core: foreign disease is cured by consuming colonized bodies.

### The clerical slaughterer

**[LE/F, colonial-to-republican transmission]** A friar or priest travels with a blade and soporific powder. He incapacitates a solitary traveler, cuts or drains the body, and sends the fat to ecclesiastical consumers. The substance makes bells ring beautifully, treats patients, or polishes saints.

The “friar with sleeping powder” is especially prominent in ñak’aq/kharisiri variants. Its precise first date remains uncertain; it should not be projected back into Molina.

### The industrial pishtaco

**[F, nineteenth and twentieth centuries]** A road, railway, mine, bridge, sugar mill, or other major work cannot function through ordinary means. Engineers employ pishtacos to obtain fat as lubricant or bodies as foundation offerings. The technology of the dominant society literally runs on Indigenous vitality.

The motif updates readily: guns and cannon give way to mills, locomotives, automobiles, electrical systems, aircraft, rockets, pharmaceuticals, and cosmetics.

### The Yanesha road rumors of 1983

**[RV/F, directly documented by Santos-Granero]** When a new road damaged hills and older landscape markers near Yanesha settlements, residents reported that pishtacos arrived with the construction crews. Angry hill beings caused landslides; the Villasol company supposedly hired pishtacos to kill civilians and soldiers and bury them where failures occurred. Downriver, state officials could themselves be identified as pishtacos.

This is one of the strongest documented Amazonian cases because the fieldworker recorded where, when, and under what material conditions the rumor emerged.

### Sacacara and pelacaras

**[RV/F, late twentieth century onward]** In Loreto and Ucayali the extracted commodity changes from fat to face, skin, heart, eyes, or organs. Regan recorded a Loreto belief that whites became pishtacos at night and made people waste away by consuming their fat; he also noted *pelacaras* removing faces to pay Peru’s foreign debt.

During Gow’s Piro fieldwork, children suspected him of being a *sacacara*: a gringo face-taker who removed the heart. The accusation demonstrates that an anthropologist’s benign self-description did not control local interpretations of his foreignness, observation, and access to bodies and information.

### Shining Path and counterinsurgency

**[F, 1980s–1990s]** During the internal armed conflict, pishtaco rumors returned forcefully in Ayacucho and the central forest. Depending on locality and narrator, pishtacos could be Sendero Luminoso guerrillas, soldiers, police, civil guards, government agents, missionaries, road workers, or unidentified outsiders.

Ansión’s 1989 volume records the return of the figure amid “the fires of Sendero and the repression.” The historical killings, disappearances, forced displacement, and secrecy of the conflict supplied conditions in which mutilated or missing bodies could be interpreted through an already available extraction legend. The dossier must not reverse that statement: the legend did not make documented state or insurgent violence unreal, nor does the violence prove each pishtaco accusation factually correct.

### The gringo pishtaco

**[F, continuing]** Foreigners carrying cameras, measuring devices, medicine, recording equipment, maps, or sample containers may be suspected of harvesting something—fat, blood, organs, images, knowledge, plants, or shamanic power. Contemporary ayahuasca tourism has generated accounts of “pishtaco tourists” and capitalist witchcraft around Shipibo communities. The category remains socially consequential even where narrators disagree about literal existence.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

There is no documented Pishtaco cult.

- No temple, shrine, priesthood, festival, liturgy, hymn, votive corpus, pilgrimage, sacred calendar, or theophoric name has been identified.
- No evidence shows communities offering sacrifice **to** Pishtaco.
- Accused pishtacos may be said to sacrifice victims **for** a road, bridge, hill, government, church, or commercial customer, but this makes them killers or intermediaries, not recipients of worship.
- Protective and curative practices are directed against attack. In kharisiri cases, treatment may involve llama fat, herbal preparations, ritual specialists, confession of the attack, or restoration of the lost bodily substance.
- Avoidance is central: people refrain from traveling alone, entering strangers’ houses, accepting food or medicine, or cooperating with suspect outsiders.
- Accusations can provoke flight, assault, or killing of suspected strangers. These outcomes belong to the historical record and should not be romanticized as harmless storytelling.

Calling this material a “cult” would impose a category contradicted by the evidence.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 1532

**[U/MSR]** Pre-Hispanic Andes possessed ritual slaughter, decapitation imagery, human sacrifice, and offerings of animal fat. None by itself securely attests the colonial pishtaco complex. Archaeological images of sacrificers cannot be identified as pishtacos merely because both cut bodies.

### Conquest to c.1560

European conquest produced warfare, epidemics, forced labor, violent medical encounters, and unfamiliar European uses of animal and sometimes human bodily substances. These conditions form a plausible background. A continuous pre-Conquest genealogy remains unproven.

### c.1560–1575: first documentary horizon

The medicinal-fat rumor appears in Molina: Spanish survival depends on Indigenous fat. The adversary is collective and colonial rather than a named supernatural individual.

### Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries

The motif attaches increasingly to soldiers, missionaries, hospitals, and religious orders. Fat serves wounds, medicine, bells, and saints. The killer develops recognizable equipment and occupational disguises.

### Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries

The figure follows hacienda power, mining, roads, railways, plantations, and industrial machinery. Owners and engineers replace or join friars as likely employers.

### Mid-twentieth century

Ethnographers document a widespread highland figure rather than an isolated story. Oliver-Smith describes “institutionalized fear”; Weiss records transformations among the Asháninka/Campa; regional forms preserve different attack techniques.

### 1980s–1990s

Political war, development projects, missionary activity, road penetration, and the expansion of state and extractive institutions accelerate the figure’s movement through the eastern-Andean and Amazonian frontier. Yanesha road rumors are documented in 1983. Loreto *pelacaras*, Ayacucho *sacaojos*, and organ-theft rumors recast the extracted commodity.

### Twenty-first century

Organ trafficking, medical markets, biotechnology, tourism, oil, mining, photography, electricity, and external debt become explanatory settings. Shipibo-Konibo accounts describe high-technology assailants using electricity or laser-like weapons.

This persistence is not unchanged survival. The legend survives precisely by replacing its clients, technologies, and commodities.

## 8. The 2009 Huánuco “human-fat gang”

In November 2009, Peruvian police publicly announced that a gang in Huánuco had murdered people, rendered their fat, and sold it for enormous sums to European cosmetics manufacturers. Suspects and containers of yellowish material were displayed to the press; the case was labeled the work of “pishtacos.”

The story rapidly collapsed:

- medical and cosmetics specialists said there was no plausible luxury market for clandestine human fat;
- local police indicated there was only one demonstrated victim, reportedly in a case connected with cocaine trafficking;
- authorities could not confirm the claimed international buyers, dozens of victims, or a fat-export network;
- Interior Ministry officials distanced themselves from the initial announcement;
- the police chief responsible for presenting the story was removed.

**Finding:** the case is **[MI/institutional rumor, 2009]**, not proof of traditional pishtacos or an international human-fat trade. At least one homicide allegation may have had an independent criminal basis, but the spectacular industrial-fat narrative was unsupported.

The event is nevertheless historically important: state police did not merely react to folklore; they mobilized its vocabulary to make a criminal investigation publicly intelligible. Reports that the announcement was designed to distract from allegations of a police death squad circulated at the time, but motive was disputed and should not be stated as established fact.

## 9. Modern reception and reinvention

### Literature

José María Arguedas incorporated ñak’aq/pishtaco imagery into literary representations of Andean social relations. Mario Vargas Llosa’s *Death in the Andes* uses pishtaco suspicion, mountain sacrifice, Sendero violence, and failed modernization. Literary treatments influence international perceptions but are not primary ethnographic transcripts.

### Retablos and visual history

Nicario Jiménez’s retablos sequence colonial friars, modern authorities, and machinery as changing embodiments of one extractive relation. This is modern historical art with its own argument, not a reproduction of ancient iconography.

### Screen and games

*Supernatural* presented pishtacos as literal fat-eating monsters in “The Purge” (season 9, episode 13, 2014). *Shadow of the Tomb Raider* (2018) drew on Andean and Amazonian legend in a commercial adventure setting. Such works detach the figure from specific Indigenous political histories and standardize it as a cryptid.

### Ayahuasca tourism

**[F/MSR, twenty-first century]** Recent Shipibo research records tourists and foreign shamans being interpreted through pishtaco-like suspicions: outsiders purchase, appropriate, or export therapeutic and spiritual power while local communities bear the risks. This is not Western neopagan worship of Pishtaco; it is a new social setting for the old extraction relation.

### Occultism and neopaganism

No reliable evidence was located for a historical Pishtaco current in Renaissance magic, Hermeticism, Theosophy, the Golden Dawn, ceremonial magic, Wicca, or an organized reconstructionist religion.

Internet bestiaries and eclectic occult pages sometimes recast the figure as an ancient demon, vampire, deity, or cryptid. Without community history or primary evidence, those presentations are **[MI]**. Pishtaco is feared or accused, not traditionally invoked as a patron.

### Recent pelacaras and “aliens”

Reports from Amazonian Peru in the 2010s and 2020s describe tall, armored, flying, electric, or laser-equipped *pelacaras*. Officials and journalists have proposed illegal miners or criminal intimidation; residents have sometimes rejected those explanations. Viral “alien” retellings are a further transformation.

A prosecutor’s claim that illegal miners used jetpacks should itself be treated as a claim, not a settled forensic conclusion. The secure evidence is that communities reported attacks and interpreted them through an available face-stealer/high-technology predator complex.

## 10. Historiography and contested questions

### Was the Pishtaco pre-Hispanic?

**Continuity position:** Quechua names connected with slaughter, archaeological sacrificer images, ritual uses of fat, and traditions of foundation sacrifice suggest an Indigenous substrate later redirected toward Spaniards.

**Colonial-formation position:** Molina’s earliest evidence specifically concerns Spaniards, foreign medicine, and colonial fear. Human fat in the legend leaves the reciprocal sacred economy for alien accumulation. The absence of an identifiable pre-Conquest pishtaco favors formation through colonial encounter.

**Assessment:** pre-Hispanic concepts of cutting, sacrifice, fat, and vitality probably supplied semantic materials, but the first securely documented pishtaco-type relation is colonial. Claims for an already complete pre-Conquest fat-stealing deity exceed the evidence.

### Did Spaniards really use human fat?

European early-modern medicine did employ substances derived from human bodies, and battlefield lore included fat-based wound treatments. That context made the rumor conceivable. It does not prove an organized Spanish program to murder Indigenous Peruvians for fat. Molina documents the belief and its consequences, not the alleged procurement system.

### Is the Pishtaco simply “the white man”?

Oliver-Smith, Ansión, and Weismantel emphasize institutional fear, colonial inequality, racialized masculinity, and accumulation. The interpretation explains the persistent white/gringo type.

Canessa and other scholars caution that the kharisiri also polices unstable boundaries within Indigenous society. Local people, neighbors, ritual specialists, or socially ambiguous persons may become suspects. “White exploiter” is a strong recurrent pattern, not an exhaustive definition.

### Andes-to-Amazon diffusion

Weiss regarded the Asháninka pishtaco as a relatively recent borrowing from the highlands. Later work proposes several routes: Quechua-Amazonian trade, missions, migrant labor, roads, military conflict, guerrillas, state development, and rumor networks.

The best-dated Amazonian evidence is largely late twentieth century: Regan in Loreto, Santos-Granero among Yanesha, Gow among Piro/Yine, and Brown and Fernández’s Asháninka historical setting. Assertions that the exact modern form has been continuously Amazonian since pre-Conquest times remain unproven.

### Sacrifice or anti-sacrifice?

The victim is sometimes buried under infrastructure, resembling foundation sacrifice. But no divine recipient may be named, and the killer’s profit remains central. Molinié and Rivière therefore read the act as an inversion or destruction of reciprocity rather than ordinary sacrifice.

### A legend versus a “false belief”

The factual existence of a secret fat-export industry is not established. The documented existence of rumors, avoidance, accusations, medical interpretations, community mobilization, violence, and altered relations with outsiders is beyond dispute. Treating the narrative as “only superstition” would discard its principal historical evidence.

## 12. Sources and source-critical conclusions

The most secure reconstruction is not the history of a single immortal monster. It is the history of a durable relation:

> outsiders convert Indigenous bodily vitality into medicine, sacred prestige, machinery, infrastructure, wealth, knowledge, or foreign power.

Its earliest surviving documentary form is a colonial medicinal-fat rumor. Friars, bells, knives, sleeping powder, hacendados, factories, roads, soldiers, organ markets, anthropologists, tourists, lasers, and “aliens” are successive historical embodiments. The changes are not corruption of an original canonical myth; they are the principal evidence of how the complex works.

The record supports neither an ancient Pishtaco god nor a continuous Amazonian cult. It supports a colonial-era fat-taking belief that combined with regional Quechua and Aymara slaughterer traditions, then traveled and transformed through eastern Peru’s missions, labor routes, roads, wars, extractive projects, medical encounters, and tourism.
