# Pavá (the Sun): Research Dossier

**Class:** Male solar deity, supreme *tasórentsi* (“transformer/divinity”) and divine father in documented Asháninka and Ashéninka traditions. In Christianized usage, *Pavá* can also mean God the Father or form part of the expression *Pavá Peróni*, “true Father/God.”

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest securely dated, explicit historical occurrence located is an **1888 Asháninka prophetic movement** in the upper Ucayali–Pichis–Palcazu region. A white or light-skinned messenger wearing Indigenous clothing reportedly spoke Asháninka and announced that “Father Sun” had sent him. The episode was recorded retrospectively from historical sources by Brown and Fernández (1991:62) and Santos-Granero (2002:515–16). Earlier seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Franciscan records document the peoples and missions of the region, but the materials examined do **not** securely attest the name *Pavá* as a solar deity. Oral tradition is necessarily older than its surviving transcription, but its pre-1888 date cannot be fixed.

---

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Pavá / Pává / Pawa / Pabá

**[Securely attested ethnographic usage]** Gerald Weiss recorded *Pává* as the theological name of the sun among people then called “Campa,” principally River Asháninka, during twentieth-century field research published in 1969, 1972, and in full in *Campa Cosmology* (1975). Orthographic variation reflects differing conventions rather than demonstrably different gods:

- **Pává / Pavá:** Weiss’s and much Peruvian scholarship’s spelling.
- **Pawa:** frequent in Brazilian Asháninka writing and in recent Indigenous-authored presentations.
- **Pabá:** a phonetic or orthographic variant found in some historical and linguistic discussions.
- **Meaning:** “our Father” or “Father.” Weiss glosses the theological expression as “our Father,” whereas Spanish summaries frequently give simply *Padre*. It is therefore safer to understand this as a kinship/divine title rather than an immutable personal name.
- The Spanish word *pava*, “guan/hen turkey,” is unrelated.

No traditional Asháninka script existed in the period from which the myths were first collected. The name is therefore written in modern Latin orthographies; claims for an ancient written form are unsupported.

### Katsirinkáiteri / Katserinkáiteri

**[Securely attested ethnographic usage]** Weiss translates this ordinary solar designation as “he who is hot.” It describes the perceptible sun and its heat. In a specifically theological register the same solar being was more often called *Pavá*. This register distinction matters: neither word need have been an exclusive personal name.

### Oriátsiri / Oriya / Oriaa

**[Regional variant]** “He who illuminates” or “the one who gives light,” recorded as another name for the sun in Asháninka-language and regional sources. Recent Brazilian Asháninka testimony collected from Moisés Piyãko distinguishes **Pawa, the Creator**, from **Oriya, Sun**, while also connecting them: Pawa gives Oriya a crown whose brilliance is sunlight. That formulation should not be silently harmonized with Weiss’s statement that Pavá “is” the sun. It is a contemporary regional theology from the Amônia/Apiwtxa context and may preserve a local distinction, a later elaboration, or both.

### Tasórentsi / Tasórenci / Tasorentsi

**[Securely attested category and title]** This word has overlapping uses:

1. a class of powerful benevolent divine transformers whose characteristic creative action is blowing or breathing upon things;
2. a singular title for the highest god;
3. in many contexts, a title for Pavá.

Weiss warns that when speakers use *Tasórentsi* to mean singular “God,” context usually shows that they mean Pavá, the sun. Modern Christian usage has widened or redirected the term toward the Christian God.

It is therefore misleading to list “Pavá” and “Tasórentsi” as two allied gods. *Tasórentsi* is a title and class term which can designate Pavá but is not consistently a separate named being.

### Pavá Peróni / Pava Peroni

**[Modern Christian–Asháninka syncretism]** Literally “true Father/God,” according to Enrique Casanto Shingari’s 2005 testimony published by Juan Carlos La Serna. Casanto explicitly said that this expression arose after evangelization: the older sun and moon were subordinated as false or “second-stage” gods, while *Pavá Peróni* designated the Christian God. This is unusually clear Indigenous testimony identifying a datable theological innovation.

### Itómi Pavá / Itomi Pawa

**[Securely attested relational title; later messianic elaboration]** “Child/son of Pavá” or “Son of the Sun.” The singular expression denotes a returning divine or civilizing messenger in Asháninka messianic thought. The plural can designate good spirits, sacred birds and animals, culture-bearing beings, or human-form emissaries of Pavá. Colonial rebels, missionaries, political leaders, and outsiders have at times been identified retrospectively or expectantly as *Itómi Pavá*.

### Inti and Dios

**[Syncretistic or translated identifications]** Weiss reports that some Asháninka speakers used Quechua *Inti* and Spanish *Dios* for the sun. This demonstrates contact and translation, not proof that Pavá originated as the Inca Inti or Christian God. Proposed Inca influence on solar vocabulary and theology remains a scholarly reconstruction; the available evidence establishes long Andean-Amazonian interaction but not simple derivation.

### Geographic and ethnic limits

Pavá is principally documented among the **Asháninka and Ashéninka**, including River, Pichis, Perené, Tambo, Ene, Gran Pajonal, upper Ucayali, and Amônia regional traditions. Older literature’s “Campa” often bundled related but distinct Southern Arawak peoples.

The following must not be treated as mere aliases:

- **Yanesha Yompor Ror:** an independent solar divinity with a separate genealogy and narrative cycle.
- **Nomatsiguenga solar or creator beings:** related through linguistic and regional history but not automatically Pavá.
- **Matsigenka beings discussed by Renard-Casevitz:** parallels and borrowings require case-by-case proof.
- **Loreto beings such as Bufeo, Chullachaqui, and Yacuruna:** broader Peruvian-selva folklore, not documented forms or epithets of Pavá.
- **Pachakamáite/Pachakamaite:** a downstream culture hero or producer of manufactured goods, sometimes called Pavá in loose speech or made a son of the sun. He is not securely identical with the solar Pavá.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Limits of the evidence

There is no ancient inscription, temple archive, manuscript scripture, or securely identified pre-Columbian image of Pavá. The corpus consists chiefly of:

- oral narratives recorded by outsiders or Indigenous educators;
- ethnographic fieldnotes and monographs;
- missionary accounts;
- historical reports of prophetic movements;
- modern Indigenous testimony, performance, and educational publications.

Dates below are dates of documentation or publication, not dates when a myth supposedly occurred.

### Franciscan documentary background, seventeenth–nineteenth centuries

**[Early-modern colonial record; no secure named Pavá attestation located]** Franciscan mission narratives assembled by Amich, San Antonio, Biedma, Izaguirre, Sala, and others document mission foundations, expeditions, violence, conversion attempts, Indigenous resistance, and the Juan Santos Atahualpa revolt. These records are indispensable for the historical setting, but their Christian categories—“God,” “devil,” “idolatry,” “false Inca”—cannot automatically be translated back into Pavá theology.

The absence of an explicit *Pavá* in the early records searched is meaningful but not proof that the being was absent. Missionaries often failed to record Indigenous vocabularies accurately, and repeated destruction or abandonment of missions interrupted observation.

### 1888 Father-Sun messenger

**[Earliest secure historical attestation]** Following the devastating 1885 upper-Ucayali smallpox epidemic, reports spread in 1888 of a divine messenger—described as white but dressed in Indigenous clothing—who spoke Asháninka. People from the upper Ucayali, Pichis, and Palcazu reportedly traveled to hear him. His message was that “Father Sun” had sent him and that scattered groups should form villages, each with a church.

This is not a pristine precontact myth. Its church-building program displays a Christianized prophetic idiom, while the authorizing Father Sun belongs to Asháninka religious language. It is early evidence precisely because it shows an already entangled Indigenous-Christian formation.

### Sala and Izaguirre, late nineteenth to early twentieth century

**[Historical ethnographic evidence]** Padre Gabriel Sala’s reports, later incorporated into Izaguirre’s multivolume history, describe divine messengers appearing in human form among Asháninka communities. Weiss cited Sala’s account as an instance of the belief that good spirits could materialize as humans to guide a group. The exact identification of every such emissary as Pavá or *Itómi Pavá* must not be assumed without the original wording.

### Pérez Marcio, *Los hijos de la selva* (1953)

**[Missionary ethnography]** An Adventist account of work among Amazonian peoples. It is valuable testimony to twentieth-century religious change, child-sorcery accusations, and mission life, but is confessional literature, not a neutral record. Its descriptions must be read against Bodley, Elick, Weiss, and later Indigenous testimony.

### Elick (1969) and Bodley (1970)

**[Ethnographic and mission-era evidence]** Elick documented Pichis Valley healing and the statement of a steam-bath healer that Pavá/God granted his power. Bodley documented the 1928–33 Adventist-associated transformative movement in the Perené basin, including expectations of resurrection, destruction of evil, and ascent to God’s house in the sky.

### Weiss, 1969–83

**[Core ethnographic corpus]**

- *The Cosmology of the Campa Indians of Eastern Peru* (1969 dissertation).
- “Campa Cosmology” (1972).
- *Campa Cosmology: The World of a Forest Tribe in South America* (1975).
- “The Sun God and the Jaguars: A Campa Case of the Incomplete Assimilation of Myth” (1983).

Weiss’s 1975 monograph is the principal documentary source for Pavá’s names, solar birth, ascent variants, place in the cosmic hierarchy, festivals, spirit children, and cosmography. Its broad label “Campa” and synthesis of multiple narrators require caution: it is not a single canonical Asháninka scripture.

### Brown and Fernández, *War of Shadows* (1991)

**[Historical-ethnographic synthesis]** Reconstructs the 1965 guerrilla episode through archives, journalism, and competing oral memories. It documents Asháninka identification of Guillermo Lobatón and other outsiders with *Itómi Pavá*, as well as the recurrent political power of solar-messianic expectations.

### La Serna, 2011

**[Modern syncretism study]** “Viviendo con el diablo en casa” documents Adventist-Asháninka interaction and publishes Casanto’s explicit account of the post-evangelization creation of *Pavá Peróni*.

### Contemporary Indigenous testimony

**[Living regional tradition]** Moisés Piyãko’s *Pawa: The Ashaninka Creator* presents an Apiwtxa/Amônia theology in which Pawa is Creator and Oriya is the crowned Sun. This is primary contemporary testimony, not evidence that every historical Asháninka community held the same distinction.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

**[Securely attested ethnographic core]** Pavá is associated with:

- sunlight and heat;
- life, fertility, and the earth’s useful products;
- cosmic order;
- divine fatherhood;
- the origin or transformation of living beings;
- benevolent spirits;
- periodic or future restoration of a disordered world.

Weiss states that from Pavá come “warmth, light, the useful products of the earth, [and] the good order of the universe.” “Creator” is accurate in some sources, but *transformer* is often more exact: Asháninka cosmogony commonly begins with beings in human form who are changed into animals, plants, rocks, stars, or other entities.

### Physical form

**[Mythic narrative]** Pavá was born on earth in human, Asháninka form. His growing heat killed his mother and threatened to burn the world; he therefore had to be transported upward. In his present condition he is the visible or animating solar being.

**[Regional contemporary variant]** In the Apiwtxa account, Pawa and Oriya can be distinguished: Pawa gives Sun a radiant crown. This resists a simple “Pavá equals the physical solar disc” formula.

### Celestial movement

Weiss records Pavá rising at **Otisiríko**, the eastern end of the world, and setting at **Irimáka**, its western end. Informants reportedly admitted they did not know how he returned from west to east. This explicit non-explanation should be preserved; no inferred underworld night journey belongs in the dossier.

Another recorded account says Pavá rises from the mythical downriver end, grows as he ascends and travels all day, and then leaves; Kashíri follows. Because people ceased singing thanks to him, Pavá became angry and ceased shining as continuously as before.

### Symbols and material associations

- **Light and heat:** primary perceptible attributes.
- **Panpipes/flutes and dance:** Weiss records festival dancing with panpipes in Pavá’s honor. Later summaries mention flute music.
- **Crown:** important in contemporary Apiwtxa teaching; Oriya’s light issues from the crown Pawa bestowed.
- **Vine or liana joining earth and sky:** instrument of an ascent version, not a permanent emblem.
- **Cushma:** flying carriers wrap the dangerously hot child in successive tunics.
- **Hummingbird and kentíparo bird:** agents in variant ascent narratives.
- **Sacred birds and animals:** numerous benevolent beings are *itómi Pavá*. Weiss names condor-like and other birds, otters, game birds, and animals maintained by mountain spirits, but this does not make each an exclusive solar cult animal.
- **Trees and flowers:** one comparative summary based on Weiss describes certain seasonal trees and flowers as Pavá’s daughters, their vegetation the clothing they shed while ascending.

No canonical color, sacred number, weekday, standardized weapon, permanent temple image, or anthropomorphic cult statue is securely documented.

### Iconography

**[Absence of evidence]** No precolonial image has been securely identified by inscription or continuous provenance as Pavá. Asháninka material culture is rich in textiles, body painting, featherwork, ceramics, music, and landscape associations, but attaching every sun-like motif to Pavá would be speculative.

Recent illustrations, paintings, school materials, and performances are modern representations. They may be authentic contemporary Asháninka expressions, but they are not archaeological evidence for an ancient standardized iconography.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Parentage and birth

**[Securely attested ethnographic myth]** Kashíri, the male Moon, visits or marries a mortal Asháninka woman and introduces cultivated manioc and other foods. She bears the solar child Pavá. The child’s heat burns his mother to death.

Some recent versions give Kashíri and the woman several children from one pregnancy; only one is identified as the sun. Details of conception vary, including conception through semen carried by river water. These are regional narrative variants, not a single fixed genealogy.

### Consorts and offspring

No securely documented, universally recognized consort of Pavá appears in the core Weiss corpus.

*Itómi Pavá* includes divine children or emissaries collectively. Some good spirits, sacred birds, and animals are called Pavá’s children without implying sexual procreation. *Pachakamáite* is sometimes presented as a son of the sun or “Pavá,” but the terminology varies and may reflect the wide honorific use of Father/God.

### Every documented named divine interaction

- **Kashíri (Moon):** Kashíri fathers the solar child Pavá with a mortal woman; source: oral myths synthesized by Weiss (1969/1975). **[Securely attested ethnographic core]**
- **Unnamed mortal mother:** she gives birth to Pavá and is burned to death by his heat; Weiss (1975). **[Securely attested ethnographic core]**
- **Unnamed maternal grandfather:** in one version he carries Pavá with tongs to the western end of the world and places him on his solar course; Weiss (1975). **[Regional variant]**
- **Kentíparo:** after other flying beings fail, this bird and/or its kind wraps Pavá in layers of new *cushmas* and carries him upward; Weiss (1975). **[Regional variant]**
- **Neorónke, the hummingbird:** carries one end of a vine to the sky so that Pavá and his entourage can ascend; Weiss (1975). **[Regional variant]**
- **Pavá’s unnamed terrestrial entourage:** ascends with him on the vine; Weiss (1975). **[Regional variant]**
- **Pavá’s daughters:** certain calendrically significant trees and flowers are described as daughters whose discarded clothing became the plants visible below; Weiss-based comparative account. **[Regional mythic elaboration]**
- **Good spirits / amachénga / other tasórentsi:** described collectively as Pavá’s children or as beings subordinate to him; Weiss (1975), Brown and Fernández (1991), Instituto Socioambiental. **[Securely attested class relation]**
- **Pachakamáite:** in traditions published by Varese and later compilers, a downstream maker/provider of metal goods is called a son of the Sun; his wife is Mamántziki. **[Regional syncretistic cycle]**
- **Inka:** some Asháninka historical myths call Inka *Itómi Pavá*, son of the Sun, whose living severed head gives manufactured wealth to whites; Brown and Fernández (1991) and later legal-cultural documentation. **[Post-contact mythic history]**
- **Sacramentaro:** a Perené narrative collected by Rojas Zolezzi calls this anti-colonial transformer a *Pavá* or god; he turns priests and soldiers into white stone but refuses to exterminate all outsiders because they too are his children. This is use of *Pavá* as a divine title, not secure identity with the sun. **[Regional historical mythology]**
- **Pavá Peróni / Christian God:** Christianization makes the “true Father” superior to the older sun and moon; Casanto testimony, 2005. **[Modern syncretistic identification]**
- **Christ:** Adventist eschatology’s returning Christ overlaps structurally with *Itómi Pavá*, but no source establishes a timeless identity. **[Twentieth-century syncretism]**
- **Korioshipíri:** ruler of the lowest demonic region in Weiss’s cosmology. Pavá and Korioshipíri occupy opposed moral-cosmic poles, but no direct encounter between them is recorded in the sources examined. **[Absence of documented interaction]**
- **Avíreri:** both are major transformers, yet no specific direct Pavá–Avíreri episode was securely located. **[Absence of documented interaction]**
- **Yompor Ror:** neighboring Yanesha solar divinity; no mythic meeting with Pavá was located. Their equation is comparative, not traditional. **[Absence of documented interaction]**
- **Yompor Rret:** rival predecessor of Yompor Ror in Yanesha myth, not an opponent of Pavá. **[Corrective finding]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The birth and raising of the Sun

**[Securely attested ethnographic core with variants]** Kashíri, the male Moon, impregnates an Asháninka woman after bringing cultivated plants. The newborn Pavá emits deadly heat, burning his mother. As he matures rapidly, his heat threatens the earth.

Three ascent solutions were recorded by Weiss:

1. the maternal grandfather uses tongs to carry him to the western edge and place him in his trajectory;
2. flying creatures attempt to lift him, with kentíparo finally succeeding after wrapping him in many *cushmas*;
3. hummingbird fixes a vine to the sky, permitting Pavá and companions to climb.

The variants agree on a terrestrial solar infancy, dangerous heat, and necessary elevation, but disagree on the agent and mechanism. No source warrants combining all three into one sequence.

### The severed earth–sky connection

**[Regional variant]** Other Asháninka/Ashéninka accounts connect Pavá’s ascent with a vine or former pathway between earth and the upper world. Human misconduct—often the behavior of warriors—causes Pavá to cut or remove the connection, leaving humans bound to the terrestrial level. Creatures on the rope may fall and become earthly species.

This explains both the remoteness of divinities and present human mortality or limitation. Its exact wording and cast vary by community.

### Pavá’s departure after human ingratitude

**[Regional oral tradition]** Pavá and Kashíri once lived among humans and taught proper life. People were instructed to sing joyfully and thank Pavá at dawn. Some forgot; angered, he withdrew upward or ceased to appear with former constancy. The present alternation of light and darkness is thus tied to divine departure and failed reciprocity.

### Creation through transformation

**[Securely attested general theology; attribution varies]** Contemporary Brazilian Asháninka teaching credits Pawa, directly or through his children, with earth, forest, rivers, animals, humans, sky, stars, wind, and rain. Many “creations” are transformations of primordial people through divine breath.

Older Peruvian ethnography distributes transformative acts among Pavá, Avíreri, and numerous *tasórentsi*. Treating Pavá as the sole creator of every species is therefore a regional or modern systematization.

### The return of a son of the Sun

**[Post-contact messianic cycle]** Asháninka history repeatedly recasts disruptive outsiders as possible *Itómi Pavá*:

- the 1888 messenger of Father Sun;
- Juan Santos Atahualpa in later historical memory;
- Adventist missionary figures and the 1928 movement;
- Werner Büttner/Bulner around 1960;
- guerrilla leader Guillermo Lobatón in 1965;
- other expected civilizers or liberators.

Recognition can be reversed. Brown and Fernández preserve memories equivalent to “We thought he was *Itómi Pavá*—he was not.” The cycle is thus not merely credulous messianism; it is a historical language for testing outsiders who promise cosmic, material, or political redress.

### Yanesha Yompor Ror: comparison without conflation

**[Independent neighboring tradition]** Yompor Ror (“Our Father Sun” or, in another literal analysis, “Our Father Flower”) and his sister-wife Yachor Arrorr are born into a world ruled by the murderous first sun Yompor Rret. Ror travels through Yanesha territory, transforming beings into stones, animals, and plants and inscribing the landscape with remembered events. At Eneñas he discovers a sexual betrayal involving Yompor Huar and Yachor Coc; he dismembers Coc, whose body gives rise to coca. He defeats or transforms divine brothers, reaches Cheporepen, ascends amid music and celebration, and becomes the present sun.

This cycle, documented by Smith (1977) and Santos-Granero (1983–98), resembles Pavá’s in solar ascent, transformation, divine kinship, and landscape-making. Yet its named characters, territorial itinerary, priestly cult, and narrative structure are Yanesha. Shared Southern Arawak history and regional exchange are reasonable contexts; identity is not demonstrated.

### Broader selva folklore

Bufeo, Chullachaqui, Yacuruna, Pishtaco, and Loreto/Ucayali aquatic or forest beings belong to intersecting modern Amazonian folklore. No reliable source found makes them episodes in Pavá’s myths. Their inclusion as Pavá’s servants, enemies, or avatars would be a modern synthetic invention.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Worship

Weiss states that no formal ceremony was associated exclusively with the sun beyond festival dancing with panpipes in his honor, and that other named gods lacked distinct ceremonies. This makes “sun cult” potentially misleading if it implies a centralized priesthood, permanent sanctuary, or sacrificial liturgy.

Documented practices include:

- dawn gratitude and singing in oral tradition;
- dancing and panpipe or flute music;
- invoking Pavá/God as the source of healing power;
- shamanic contact with benevolent spirits identified as his children;
- messianic assemblies around alleged emissaries;
- Christian worship translated through Pavá vocabulary.

### Priests and shamans

The **sheripiari**, a healer-shaman, mediates with spirit worlds; a **satantinkari** heals using steam baths and may avoid psychoactive plants. Neither office is securely a priesthood dedicated only to Pavá.

Good spirits’ true human form is ordinarily invisible, but shamans can perceive them. Lightning may mark their passage or the travel of a shaman’s soul.

### Temples

**[Absence of evidence]** No archaeological Pavá temple or precolonial solar sanctuary is securely documented. Christian churches and Adventist mission settlements became sites of hybrid worship, especially after 1921, but they were not indigenous temples to Pavá.

By contrast, Yanesha historically maintained temples and a more articulated priestly-ritual complex surrounding solar and other divinities. Transferring that institutional structure to the Asháninka would be erroneous.

### Sacrifice and contested practices

The dedicated search found:

- no secure evidence of human sacrifice offered to Pavá;
- no evidence of temple prostitution;
- no established Pavá oracle;
- no documented cult statue receiving blood sacrifice;
- no evidence of a modern occult or ceremonial-magic tradition centered on Pavá.

Child killings documented among some nineteenth- and twentieth-century Southern Arawak communities concerned accusations of lethal child sorcery, not sacrifices to Pavá. Santos-Granero argues that the practice developed after colonial contact amid epidemics, violence, and transformed Saint Christopher traditions. It must not be relabeled “sun sacrifice.”

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure and Cult

### Before European documentation

**[Reconstruction only]** Southern Arawak languages and religious complexes predate colonization, and solar traditions almost certainly circulated orally before transcription. No presently located source dates the name Pavá archaeologically or epigraphically. Claims of a millennia-old unchanged Pavá cult exceed the evidence.

### Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries: missions and anti-colonial transformation

Franciscan penetration beginning in the seventeenth century introduced Christian Father-God, saints, churches, mission settlements, epidemic disease, and coercive discipline. Indigenous communities also adopted, rejected, and transformed these elements.

Juan Santos Atahualpa’s rebellion from 1742 expelled missionaries from much of the central selva. Later Asháninka memory placed him within a solar-messianic pattern, but surviving contemporary documents do not securely show that he called himself *Itómi Pavá*. The solar identification is strongest as later oral-historical interpretation.

### 1880s–1910s: epidemic and prophetic movements

Smallpox and other epidemics, colonization, the Perené concession, mission expansion, and military pressure destabilized the region. The 1888 Father-Sun messenger is the earliest explicit attestation. Similar divine messengers appeared in the 1890s. These movements joined Indigenous divine authorization to imported forms such as churches and nucleated villages.

### 1921–39: Adventist transformation

Ferdinand Stahl reached the Perené area in 1921. In 1928 hundreds gathered in mission-modeled settlements anticipating resurrection, the destruction of evil, and ascent to God’s sky-house. Epidemics killed many participants. In 1933 approximately 120 of 300 converts at Sutziki reportedly died in a measles outbreak, prompting accusations that outsiders had brought death and attacks on the mission.

Adventism resonated with Asháninka concepts of cosmic opposition, impending transformation, heavenly beings, and returning messengers. It also reclassified the traditional sun and moon as subordinate or false divinities. *Pavá Peróni* emerged as the “true” Christian God.

### 1950s–60s: missionaries and revolutionary messiahs

Mission schools, biblical translation, and Christian settlements deepened lexical fusion between Pavá and God. Around 1960 some Asháninka identified the evangelical missionary Werner Bulner/Büttner as *Itómi Pavá*.

In 1965 Asháninka and Nomatsiguenga participants joined the Movimiento de Izquierda Revolucionaria. Some regarded Guillermo Lobatón as the returning Son of the Sun. The disastrous outcome generated skeptical counter-memories. *War of Shadows* demonstrates that mythic identification could mobilize politics but did not erase pragmatic judgments about leaders.

### 1980–2000: Shining Path and armed conflict

Sendero Luminoso and the MRTA entered Asháninka territories; guerrillas used forced recruitment, captivity, displacement, and killing. Asháninka communities organized armed self-defense forces from about 1989. The Peruvian Truth and Reconciliation Commission estimated that of approximately 55,000 Asháninka, about 10,000 were displaced, 5,000 held captive, and 6,000 killed.

Pavá was not documented as a Shining Path deity. The relevant history is the assault on communities in which solar-messianic and Christian traditions continued, not an appropriation of Pavá by Maoism. Any claim that Abimael Guzmán was widely identified as *Itómi Pavá* is unsupported by the sources examined.

### Twenty-first century

Traditional, Christian, and hybrid understandings coexist. Some speakers distinguish Pawa the Creator from Oriya the visible Sun; some identify Pavá with the Christian Father; some preserve Pavá as the pre-Christian solar divinity; some treat the old stories as ancestral narratives rather than present objects of worship.

The Apiwtxa movement in Brazil explicitly revitalizes Asháninka environmental, ritual, linguistic, and cosmological knowledge. This is living Indigenous practice, not a Western neopagan reconstruction.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Indigenous revitalization

Contemporary Asháninka educators, artists, activists, and shamans use Pawa/Pavá as a language of kinship among humans, animals, plants, waters, and celestial beings. Environmental presentations may foreground Pawa as Creator and the source of relational obligations to the forest. These are modern formulations rooted in community traditions, not necessarily verbatim survivals of Weiss’s River Asháninka corpus.

### State educational presentation

Peru’s official Indigenous Peoples Database summarizes Pavá as the solar god, child of male Moon Kashíri and a woman, provider of heat, and recipient of music and dance. This is a useful public synthesis based principally on Weiss, not a newly collected canonical statement.

### Popular mythography and online registries

Recent “pantheon” sites commonly present Pavá as a neatly bounded supreme god with a fixed family tree, allies, and enemies. Such catalogues often:

- turn *Tasórentsi* into a separate being;
- conflate divine categories with proper names;
- make regional variants universal;
- assign direct rivalries not found in a narrative;
- equate Pavá, Yompor Ror, Inti, and the Christian God timelessly.

These are **modern systematizations**, not primary evidence.

### Occultism and neopaganism

The dedicated search found no documented Renaissance hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, chaos-magic, grimoire, or established Western neopagan tradition centered on Pavá. Isolated online invocations may exist, but no historically significant current could be established. Claims that Pavá belongs to an ancient universal “solar mystery school” are modern inventions unless specific documentation is supplied.

### Literature, games, and commerce

No major film, international literary work, comic franchise, or game was found to have materially standardized Pavá’s modern image. Modern visibility comes mainly through ethnography, Indigenous cultural advocacy, school resources, museum and sound archives, and environmental journalism.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### “Supreme God” versus “the sun”

Weiss describes Pavá as both the paramount god and the sun. Later evidence complicates this:

- some speakers distinguish ordinary solar names from the theological title Pavá;
- Christianized speakers use Pavá for God beyond the visible sun;
- contemporary Apiwtxa teaching distinguishes Pawa the Creator from Oriya the crowned Sun.

The safest conclusion is that Pavá is a relational divine title whose referential range has varied. A rigid Western distinction between “sun itself,” “sun god,” and “creator God” may misrepresent Asháninka categories.

### Creator versus transformer

Popular summaries say Pavá created everything. Asháninka cosmology more often describes primordial persons being transformed by divine breath. Pavá may be ultimate source, supreme transformer, or father of transformer beings, depending on region and narrator. “Creator” is attested but should not erase transformation ontology.

### Indigenous core versus Christian influence

“Father,” heavenly residence, divine sons, messengers, creator language, churches, resurrection, and final renovation all resemble Christianity. Yet resemblance does not prove wholesale missionary invention. The 1888 messenger already joins Father Sun to churches, showing early entanglement. The strongest documented Christian innovation is *Pavá Peróni*, explicitly dated by Casanto to evangelization.

### Inca influence

The use of *Inti*, myths of Inka as a son of the Sun, manufactured wealth controlled by a captive Inka, and long trade with Andean peoples show sustained interaction. They do not prove Pavá was borrowed intact from Inca religion. Solar paternity could have been indigenous, borrowed, or repeatedly reworked; current evidence cannot choose conclusively.

### Yanesha comparison

Santos-Granero and Smith document the much fuller Yompor Ror saga. Similarities may reflect shared Southern Arawak inheritance, areal circulation, or parallel development. The systems also differ substantially. “Yompor Ror is the Yanesha name for Pavá” is an overstatement.

### Terminology and colonial bias

“Campa” is now widely rejected as an external and often pejorative label. Weiss’s work remains indispensable, but its ethnic aggregation and masculine English pronouns should be checked against local terminology. Some modern summaries contradict each other about the gender of the sun; the Pavá narratives examined normally treat Pavá as male, while confusion may arise from grammatical gender, regional cosmologies, or faulty secondary synthesis.

### State of the evidence

The principal record is oral and ethnographic. It is held in published monographs, dissertations, sound archives, missionary collections, Indigenous educational materials, and field recordings. No single archive contains a canonical corpus. Audio such as the CNRS/Musée de l’Homme recording labeled “Pava” preserves performance evidence but does not establish an ancient musical form without provenance analysis.

### Discredited or unsupported claims

The following could not be established:

- a pre-Columbian Pavá temple;
- a standardized ancient idol;
- human sacrifice to Pavá;
- temple prostitution;
- a direct Pavá–Avíreri combat;
- a direct Pavá–Yompor Ror meeting;
- a Pavá cult among all peoples of Loreto and Ucayali;
- an ancient written scripture;
- a continuous occult order;
- a fixed universal pantheon with Pavá at its head;
- an exact equivalence with Inti or the Christian God from time immemorial.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before the seventeenth century:** Solar and transformer traditions likely circulate orally among Southern Arawak-speaking peoples. **[Modern reconstruction; undatable]**
- **1635 onward:** Franciscan missions establish intermittent contact with Yanesha and Asháninka-related communities. No secure named Pavá occurrence has been located in the earliest mission record. **[Colonial documentation]**
- **1742–c.1756:** Juan Santos Atahualpa’s uprising expels missions from much of the central selva. Later oral memory places him in a Son-of-the-Sun complex. **[Historical event; later mythicization]**
- **1879–85:** Yellow fever, measles, and smallpox devastate Yanesha and Asháninka regions. **[Historical documentation]**
- **1888:** A messenger claims that Father Sun sent him to tell dispersed Asháninka to form church-centered villages. **[Earliest secure explicit attestation]**
- **1896–98:** Asháninka, Ashéninka, Nomatsiguenga, and Yanesha resistance movements accompany renewed appearances of divine messengers. **[Historical documentation]**
- **1921:** Ferdinand Stahl begins major Adventist work in the Perené region. **[Historical documentation]**
- **1928:** Hundreds join an Adventist-influenced transformative movement promising resurrection and ascent to God’s celestial house. **[Historical documentation]**
- **1933:** Measles kills roughly 120 of 300 Sutziki converts; anti-mission reaction follows. **[Historical documentation]**
- **1953:** Pérez Marcio publishes *Los hijos de la selva*. **[Missionary publication]**
- **c.1960:** Some Asháninka identify Werner Bulner/Büttner as *Itómi Pavá*. **[Modern messianic identification]**
- **1965:** Some participants interpret MIR leader Guillermo Lobatón through the Son-of-the-Sun expectation; the guerrilla project collapses. **[Political-messianic history]**
- **1969–75:** Elick, Bodley, and especially Weiss publish the foundational modern ethnographic documentation; Weiss’s full *Campa Cosmology* appears in 1975. **[Scholarly documentation]**
- **1977:** Richard Chase Smith publishes the Yanesha Yompor Ror cycle in *Deliverance from Chaos for a Song*. **[Comparative neighboring corpus]**
- **1983:** Weiss publishes “The Sun God and the Jaguars.” **[Scholarly interpretation]**
- **1980–2000:** Internal armed conflict devastates Asháninka communities; self-defense forces emerge from about 1989. **[Documented history]**
- **1991:** Brown and Fernández publish *War of Shadows*; Santos-Granero publishes *The Power of Love*. **[Major scholarly publications]**
- **1998:** Santos-Granero publishes the landscape-history analysis of the Yompor Ror saga. **[Major scholarly publication]**
- **2002:** Santos-Granero’s “Saint Christopher in the Amazon” places the 1888 Father-Sun episode within epidemic, colonial, and child-sorcery history. **[Major scholarly publication]**
- **2005:** Enrique Casanto explains *Pavá Peróni* as a product of evangelization. **[Primary modern testimony; published 2011]**
- **2020s:** Indigenous cultural and environmental revitalization presents Pawa as Creator and, in Apiwtxa teaching, distinguishes him from Oriya/Sun while retaining their intimate relation. **[Living regional tradition]**
- **2026:** No centralized Pavá church, standardized pan-Asháninka creed, archaeological temple, or well-documented Western neopagan movement is established. Traditional, Christianized, revitalized, and secular-cultural understandings coexist. **[Present evidentiary assessment]**
