# The Shapshico (Shapingo): Research Dossier

> **Class:** Forest spirit, “little devil,” or demon in Peruvian Amazonian and adjoining Andean vernacular traditions. In some Loreto, San Martín, Ucayali, and Huánuco sources *Shapshico* is another name for the Chullachaqui/Sacharuna; in other tellings it is a more overtly malignant, Christianized devil-form or a distinct forest demon. It is not securely documented as a god of a unified “Southern Arawak pantheon.”
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The oldest directly verifiable occurrence located in the available documentary record is a twentieth-century Huánuco folklore vocabulary defining *Shapshico* as “Diablillo” (“little devil”). Pérez Marcio’s *Los hijos de la selva* (1953) may constitute an equally early or earlier printed narrative source, but its relevant text could not be inspected and therefore cannot be claimed as the first attestation. No occurrence was found in the searched seventeenth- through nineteenth-century Franciscan chronicles.
>
> **Central finding:** “The Shapshico” is not one stable, ancient divine individual with a recoverable genealogy and cult. The evidence instead reveals an overlapping family of names and conceptions produced in Quechua, Amazonian Spanish, Kichwa-Lamista, Yine, Cocama, and other intercultural settings. *Shapshico*, *Shapishico*, and *Shapingo* can denote: (1) the Christian Devil; (2) a small devil or generic malign spirit; (3) a forest owner or guardian; (4) the Chullachaqui, recognizable by an anomalous foot; or (5) a locally differentiated, more monstrous relative of the Chullachaqui. These usages must not be collapsed into a single reconstructed pre-Columbian god.

## Evidentiary labels

The following labels are used throughout:

- **[Early attested]**: the earliest directly verifiable documentary material presently located.
- **[Folklore]**: an oral account, vernacular belief, or published collection of such material.
- **[Regional variant]**: a form restricted, so far as the evidence shows, to a particular locality or population.
- **[Syncretistic identification]**: a historically produced equation involving Christian, Indigenous, or neighboring regional categories.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**: an analytical interpretation rather than an informant’s explicit statement.
- **[Modern retelling]**: a literary, educational, touristic, internet, or entertainment adaptation.
- **[Modern invention/unverified]**: a recent assertion for which no older supporting testimony was located.
- **[Negative finding]**: a requested feature or association not established in the inspected evidence.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

In the most conservative definition, the *shapshico* is “a demon or devil that inhabits the forest,” the formulation adopted by the Asociación de Academias de la Lengua Española. **[Modern lexicographic synthesis]** In Huánuco material it can be simply a *diablillo*. In Loreto and other lowland collections it overlaps with the *Chullachaqui*, a short forest being with asymmetrical or nonhuman feet who imitates relatives, leads travelers astray, owns or protects game, and punishes improper forest use. In some contemporary narratives, however, the Shapshico has acquired horns, red or green skin, a goat’s tail, fangs, and an explicitly infernal appetite. Those conspicuously European diabolic traits are late, variably attested, and should not be projected backward.

The brief’s description of the Shapshico as the Chullachaqui’s “darker relative” is therefore defensible only as a description of one branch of recent folklore. **[Regional variant/modern systematization]** Some sources distinguish a violently demonic Shapshico from the ambivalent guardian Chullachaqui; other sources state outright that they are the same being.

### Documented names

- **Shapshico** — Amazonian Peruvian Spanish. ASALE defines it as a “demonio o diablo que habita en la selva.” **[Modern lexicographic synthesis]**
- **Shapshicu / shapshico / shapshi ku**, phonemic form `/šapši-ku/` — Huallaga Quechua. David Weber’s dictionary gives *dyablu*, “the devil,” and illustrates it with the statement: “If we do something bad, they say the devil becomes very happy.” **[Documented language usage; Christianized semantic frame]**
- **Shapishico / Shapyshico** — common variant in San Martín and popular publications. Frequently identified with the Chullachaqui. **[Folklore/regional spelling]**
- **Shapingo** — northern Peruvian and Amazonian Spanish, derived in modern lexicography from Quechua *shapinku*. Meanings include “devil,” “demon,” “evil spirit,” and figuratively a mischievous, daring, or incorrigible person. **[Documented regional lexeme]**
- **Shapinku** — cited in Cajamarca lexicographic material as “diablo, demonio, ser diabólico.” **[Regional lexicography]**
- **Shapi** — shortened northern Andean form of *Shapingo*, applied to the Devil and sometimes to a mischievous person. **[Regional vernacular]**
- **Chapingo** — Bagua/Amazonas spelling variant glossed “devil or demon.” **[Regional variant]**
- **Yashingo / Ñashingo / Ishingo** — names associated with the Chullachaqui or a bearded forest devil in Nauta, Loreto, and Ucayali materials. Their precise linguistic relationship to *Shapingo* remains unestablished. **[Regional variant; etymology uncertain]**
- **Chullachaqui / Chullachaki** — from Quechua *ch’ulla*, “single, unpaired, unequal,” and *chaki*, “foot/leg”: “the one with unequal feet.” Some sources treat this as a synonym of Shapshico; others preserve distinct descriptions. **[Folklore/syncretistic identification]**
- **Sacharuna** — Quechua *sacha runa*, “forest person.” Jaime Regan reports that the being called Sacharuna across the forest is commonly known as Chullachaqui or Shapshico. **[Folklore/ethnographic classification]**
- **Supay** — an Indigenous Andean category whose colonial and postcolonial meanings were increasingly translated as Christian “devil.” In Alto Pucalpillo, animals of the forest are said to be raised by the *supay or shapingo*. The equation is historically important but does not prove that every pre-Christian *supay* was identical to the Shapshico. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Diablo, demonio, diablillo, maligno** — Spanish interpretive terms pervasive in the documentary record. **[Colonial/postcolonial Christian vocabulary]**

### Etymology

No critical historical etymology of *Shapshico* has been established.

The Huallaga form *shapshicu* may be segmented in contemporary lexicography as *shapshi-ku*, but the dictionary does not demonstrate a recoverable literal meaning predating “devil.” A 2022 Quechua–Spanish dictionary proposes *chhapchikuk* as a Quechua equivalent for Spanish regional *shapshico*; that is a modern normative equivalence, not proof of the word’s historical derivation.

For *Shapingo*, modern Cajamarca studies derive it from Quechua *shapinku*. Popular sources sometimes claim a root *chapi* meaning “devil,” attributed to Federico Kauffmann Doig, but the searched material does not supply a historical linguistic demonstration. **[Disputed/unverified etymology]**

A separate popular explanation connects *shap-* with Quechua *sapra/shapra*, “body hair” or “beard,” associating the “hairy devil” with Spanish conquerors. This is suggestive because hairy or bearded strangers figure prominently in post-Conquest demon imagery, but the phonological and historical derivation has not been established in the sources inspected. **[Modern scholarly/popular reconstruction]**

### Classification problem

Calling the being “Amazonian (Southern Arawak)” is too narrow. The best-documented usages cross several linguistic and social fields:

- Huallaga and northern Quechua;
- Amazonian regional Spanish;
- Kichwa-Lamista communities;
- Loreto riverine and Cocama informants;
- a Yine narrative from Puija near Sepahua;
- Ucayali and San Martín folklore.

Gerald Weiss’s Asháninka/Campa demonology supplies essential comparative context, but his *Campa Cosmology* does not establish *Shapshico* as the name of an Asháninka deity. Likewise, Yanesha, Matsigenka, Nomatsiguenga, and Yine cosmologies contain forest masters, transformers, dangerous spirits, and deceptive beings, but resemblance is not identity. **[Negative finding]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest directly verifiable evidence

A “Notas del folklore peruano” vocabulary from Huánuco contains the compact entry:

> “Shapshico.—Diablillo.”

**[Early attested]** This establishes the word as a Huánuco vernacular supernatural term, but by itself says nothing about unequal feet, a goat foot, forest ownership, genealogy, or narrative history. The web-accessible copy does not expose sufficiently clear bibliographic metadata to assign its original print year securely; it belongs to the mid-twentieth-century folklore-collection milieu.

Pérez Marcio’s *Los hijos de la selva* (1953) is repeatedly associated with mid-century Peruvian Amazonian folklore and deserves inspection in a physical or fully digitized copy. The relevant passage could not be independently verified here. It therefore remains a **[probable early source, unverified at passage level]**, not a secure terminus ante quem for any particular trait.

### Huallaga Quechua lexical evidence

David J. Weber’s *Diccionario del quechua del Huallaga* records *shapshicu ~ shapshico ~ shapshi ku* as “the devil.” Its example sentence embeds the term in Christian moral language: wrongdoing pleases the Shapshico. **[Documented Christianized vernacular usage]** It does not depict a separate forest creature or provide a myth.

### Regan’s Loreto corpus

Jaime Regan’s *Hacia la tierra sin mal* (first published in the early 1980s; later digital copies circulate) is the most substantial accessible comparative folklore corpus found. It reports that throughout the forest the *sacharuna* is better known as Chullachaqui or Shapshico and preserves several localized accounts:

- a being about 1.5 meters tall, dark-skinned, with one normal foot and one deformed like a closed fist, from a Cocama informant at Nauta;
- a Chullachaqui with an animal or childlike foot that abducts or misleads people;
- a Kichwa-Lamista account in which it imitates a family member, takes unbaptized children, and has a deer foot;
- a forest protector who helps disciplined hunters obtain game but punishes excessive hunting;
- a short Shapshico/Chullachaqui with one large and one small foot.

These are discrete testimonies, not parts of one canonical biography. **[Folklore/regional variants]**

### Yine narrative from Puija

A narrative credited to Segundo Espinoza Sandoval, a former leader of the Native Community of Puija, calls the Shapshico a small, deformed, hairy forest dwarf with unequal legs. It appears as a known person to solitary travelers and causes them to become lost.

The attached Don Preshico story makes the being owner of the game animals. When a hunter’s dogs pursue a peccary into a hole, the Shapshico emerges and protests: “What are you doing bothering my animals?” He challenges the hunter to feats of strength. The hunter ultimately shoots it, later acquires extraordinary strength through the encounter, and is haunted and eventually killed by its spirit. **[Folklore; Yine-attributed regional narrative; modern web transcription]**

This account is important because it combines three otherwise separable roles: deceiver, animal master, and transferable source of dangerous power.

### Contemporary Napo/Loreto testimony

An ethnographic essay on beings in Napo oral tradition reports *yashingo* and *shapshico* as names for the Chullachaki. It describes the being as short, dark, and large-headed, with unequal feet; it may appear as a well-dressed lame gentleman or as a relative and particularly enjoys misleading children. The essay cites Jaime Regan, Ministry of Education collections, José María Arguedas-related material, and informant testimony. **[Folklore/modern ethnographic synthesis]**

### Kichwa-Lamista testimony

In published discussion of Kichwa-Lamista medicinal knowledge, *shapshico* is not necessarily an individual monster. It can name the *ánima*, owner, guardian, spirit, *yachay*, projectile power, or “devil” of a plant. The ayahuasca plant’s spirit may appear as the Chullachaqui, an insect, or a bird. Such beings guard the forest, teach medicinal knowledge, heal, and assist hunting. **[Regional animist usage]**

This material is decisive against defining Shapshico exclusively as evil. The same term can designate relational forest agency that becomes dangerous or beneficial according to context, preparation, diet, and conduct.

### Asháninka/Campa comparative corpus

Gerald Weiss’s *Campa Cosmology* documents a dense Asháninka spirit taxonomy:

- dead persons’ souls may become harmful ghosts;
- ghosts may join classes of demons;
- spirits can take deceptive animal form;
- visible abnormality, emaciation, decay-colored appearance, disguise, excessive sexuality, and bad social conduct characterize various demons;
- malignant beings steal souls and cause illness, madness, and death.

This helps explain the broader regional logic in which visual anomaly and deceptive appearance signify dangerous nonhuman agency. But Weiss does not identify these beings collectively as Shapshico. **[Comparative context; negative name finding]**

### Corpus audit of sources named in the brief

The following works are crucial for comparative Amazonian religious history but cannot automatically be treated as Shapshico texts:

- Fernando Santos-Granero, *The Power of Love* and “Saint Christopher in the Amazon” — Yanesha myth, history, Christian transformation, and messianism; no directly verified Shapshico passage located.
- Gerald Weiss, *Campa Cosmology* — extensive Asháninka demonology; no directly verified Shapshico name.
- Richard Chase Smith, *Deliverance from Chaos for a Song* — Yanesha musical and ritual order; no directly verified Shapshico occurrence located.
- Shaver and Dodd on Nomatsiguenga cosmology — relevant comparative material, but no verified Shapshico occurrence located.
- Michael Brown and Eduardo Fernández, *War of Shadows* — Asháninka sorcery, violence, and the Shining Path period; historically essential but no securely located Shapshico episode.
- France-Marie Renard-Casevitz and Enrique Rojas Zolezzi on Matsigenka worlds — relevant to forest masters and dangerous beings; no verified equation with Shapshico.
- Peter Gow on the Yine/Piro — highly relevant to mythic historicity and transformation; the Puija narrative shows contemporary Yine use of the name, but a passage in Gow directly naming Shapshico was not located.
- Bartholomew Dean on Urarina society — no verified Shapshico occurrence.
- Weiss and Ansión on the Pishtaco — relevant to colonial violence and monstrous outsiders, but the Pishtaco is not thereby a Shapshico.
- Franciscan chronicles by Amich, Biedma, Izaguirre, San Antonio, Sala, Ordinaire, and Navarro — no indexed or searchable occurrence of the terms *Shapshico*, *Shapishico*, or *Shapingo* was located.

These negative findings suggest that the named being crystallized in print comparatively late, or that earlier observers translated cognate ideas under broad labels such as *demonio*, *diablo*, *supay*, “forest spirit,” or “owner.” They do not prove that the oral traditions themselves began in the twentieth century.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains and functions

#### Forest and game ownership

In the Puija narrative, the Shapshico calls peccaries “my animals.” In Kichwa-Lamista testimony, *supay or shapingo* raises the wild animals. Regan’s Loreto material describes the Chullachaqui/Shapshico as protector of forest animals and plants, rewarding properly prepared hunters and punishing overhunting. **[Folklore/regional variants]**

The title “owner of the forest” is therefore documented, but it is relational rather than monarchic: ownership means guardianship, husbandry, control of availability, and the capacity to hide or release game.

#### Deception and disorientation

The most stable narrative action is imitation. The being takes the appearance or voice of a relative, friend, or familiar animal and induces a person to follow it away from the human path. Victims are lost, abducted, or returned only through ritual intervention. **[Widely distributed folklore]**

#### Child abduction

Several Chullachaqui/Shapshico stories describe the imitation of a parent and the removal of a child. A Napo testimony published by Rocchietti and Cárdenas recounts a child following what appeared to be her father although her real father was present elsewhere; the child never returned. Regan records a Kichwa-Lamista belief that unbaptized children are especially vulnerable. The baptismal condition is plainly Christian. **[Folklore plus Christian accretion]**

#### Illness and shock

Modern definitions attribute spasms, vomiting, fainting, and terror to its presence. In the Puija story the hunter loses consciousness after the encounter. These bodily effects fit broader Amazonian understandings of fright and spirit attack, but highly specific symptom lists in internet sources cannot be projected backward without corroboration. **[Folklore; some modern elaboration]**

#### Medicinal and shamanic agency

Among Kichwa-Lamista speakers, *shapshico* may name the spirit or owner of a medicinal plant and may teach, heal, or aid hunting. **[Regional variant]** This is incompatible with a simple Satanic identity and demonstrates the semantic layering produced by translation as *diablo*.

### Physical appearance

No single authoritative image exists. The recorded forms include:

- short, approximately 1.5 meters tall;
- small or dwarf-like;
- dark-skinned;
- elongated but diminutive;
- hairy or bearded;
- large-headed;
- deformed;
- one normal foot and one small, malformed, backward-facing, fist-shaped, childlike, or animal foot;
- a deer, peccary, dog, turtle, rooster, or other animal foot in Chullachaqui-linked variants;
- reddish body, small horns, and goat tail;
- greenish body, pointed ears, prominent nose, and fangs in recent horror-oriented retellings;
- a well-dressed lame gentleman;
- the exact appearance of a relative, friend, or animal.

The “goat foot” belongs to the Christianized branch of the imagery. Goats are not indigenous Amazonian fauna, and the horned, goat-tailed devil is a European Christian visual complex. Its appearance in Shapshico descriptions is strong evidence of post-Conquest iconographic absorption. **[Syncretistic identification]**

The “hairy body” is attested in the Puija account and in wider descriptions of bearded *yashingo*-type forest devils. Connecting it specifically to Spanish conquerors is an interpretive hypothesis, not an informant-independent historical fact. **[Folklore plus modern reconstruction]**

### Transformative capacity

Shapshico/Chullachaqui can appear as:

- a victim’s father, mother, sibling, spouse, uncle, or friend;
- a child;
- a forest animal;
- a shadow or presence;
- a human stranger, including a lame gentleman.

Its actual anomaly survives the transformation in many tellings: the feet reveal the disguise. **[Folklore]**

### Symbols and sacred associations

Securely documented associations include:

- dense old-growth forest;
- game animals;
- tree hollows and underground holes;
- renaco trees with large aerial roots;
- paths and crossroads within the forest;
- medicinal plants and ayahuasca;
- abnormal footprints;
- darkness and solitude.

No securely documented sacred color, calendrical day, celestial body, canonical number, formal weapon, regalia, or heraldic emblem was located. **[Negative finding]**

A contemporary Kokama artist, Alejandro Canayo, has produced a small ceramic representation labeled—cautiously by its publishers—“¿Shapshico?” This is valuable evidence of modern Indigenous Amazonian art, but it is not ancient iconography. **[Modern artistic reception]**

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No securely attested parentage, birth narrative, spouse, offspring, sibling group, or pantheon hierarchy exists for the Shapshico as a named individual. **[Negative finding]**

Recent summaries sometimes call it a hybrid offspring of a goblin and a demon or the Chullachaqui’s sinister relative. No early or ethnographically strong source for that genealogy was located. It should be classified as **[modern popular systematization]**.

The idea of a “Southern Arawak pantheon” containing the Shapshico as a discrete member is likewise not supported. Southern Arawak-speaking peoples possess richly documented but distinct cosmological systems; the vernacular name travels across these systems rather than defining a single inherited divine person.

### Identifications and conceptual relations

- **Chullachaqui** — often identical by name and function; sometimes differentiated as a more ambivalent forest guardian versus a more demonic Shapshico. **[Folklore/syncretistic identification]**
- **Sacharuna** — Regan reports this as a wider category or equivalent: forest person/owner. **[Folklore identification]**
- **Supay** — Alto Pucalpillo speakers use “supay or shapingo” for the being that raises game animals. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Christian Devil/Satan** — Huallaga and northern Peruvian lexicography directly glosses Shapshico/Shapingo as the Devil. **[Christian syncretism]**
- **Plant ánimas or madres** — in Kichwa-Lamista discourse *shapshico* may be one situational name for the animate owner or teacher of a plant. **[Regional animist classification]**
- **Yashingo/Ñashingo** — Loreto and Ucayali names for closely comparable or identical bearded forest devils. **[Regional identification]**

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

The surviving corpus is unusually poor in named supernatural-to-supernatural narrative episodes. Most tales concern the Shapshico interacting with humans or animals.

| Other being | Interaction | Source and date | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Chullachaqui** | No narrative meeting is documented. Sources identify Shapshico as the Chullachaqui or place the names in apposition. | Regan, *Hacia la tierra sin mal*, early 1980s; later folklore syntheses | **[Folklore/syncretistic identification]** |
| **Supay** | No narrative meeting. In Alto Pucalpillo the owner who raises forest animals is called “the supay or shapingo.” | Panduro and Rengifo testimony cited in a 2021 anthropological study | **[Regional identification]** |

| **Plant ánima/chullachaqui of ayahuasca** | Not a separate encounter; the plant’s spirit may manifest as Chullachaqui and may also be called Shapshico according to context. | Kichwa-Lamista medicinal-plant testimony, published in modern ethnographic literature | **[Regional variant]** |

**Absence finding:** No securely attested combat, marriage, seduction, alliance, judgment, theft, rescue, or joint adventure between Shapshico and another independently named god or spirit was located.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The familiar who leads a victim away

**[Folklore; broad Amazonian cycle]**

The being approaches a solitary adult or unattended child in the form of somebody trusted. It acts and speaks sufficiently like that person to induce the victim to follow. The path leads away from settlement and garden into increasingly deep forest. Only an anomalous foot, footprint, silence, or behavioral inconsistency reveals the imitation.

Outcomes vary:

- the victim is abandoned but eventually recovered;
- a shaman calls or blows tobacco toward the forest until the victim returns;
- the child is never recovered;
- prayer causes the being to disappear;
- the victim is fed strange or excellent forest food;
- the victim returns disoriented or ill.

The unequal foot is central to Chullachaqui variants but not to every bare lexical use of *Shapshico*. Baptism and Christian prayer are later religious elements, while imitation, forest displacement, and anomalous embodiment likely belong to the broader Indigenous narrative structure. That historical layering is plausible but cannot be dated precisely.

### Don Preshico and the owner of the animals

**[Regional Yine-attributed folklore; modern transcription]**

A hunter named Preshico follows his dogs as they pursue a peccary into a hole. The Shapshico emerges and asks why the man is molesting “my animals.” Rather than immediately attacking, it imposes tests of strength. Preshico uproots a plant and pulls down a hanging *itininga* vine or rope. When the being becomes threatening, Preshico shoots it.

Afterward, Preshico faints. On waking, he swallows and rubs himself with moss or lichen according to ancestral knowledge. He acquires extraordinary strength, but the spirit of the killed Shapshico accompanies and torments him. Eventually another terrifying encounter causes his death.

The story links hunting ethics, forest ownership, ordeal, dangerous acquisition of power, killing, and spiritual retaliation. It is the closest thing in the located corpus to a full Shapshico heroic narrative. No evidence dates this specific plot earlier than its recent web documentation.

### The abducted child

**[Loreto/Napo folklore]**

A forest-dwelling family prepares to travel. A small girl apparently leaves with her father. When another adult asks about her, it becomes clear that the father is still present: the departing figure was the Shapshico. The family searches unsuccessfully; the child is never found.

The story dramatizes the instability of appearance and the danger of imperfect attention at the boundary between house and forest. It also acts as a practical warning concerning child supervision without being reducible to that function.

### The nocturnal imitator

**[Loreto/Napo folklore]**

A woman whose husband has gone out at night hears or senses his return. A shadow beside the bed scratches its legs in the same manner as her husband. She addresses it while reaching for a knife. The emphasis is not an unequal foot but exact imitation through remembered bodily habit. The surviving extract does not provide a complete resolution.

### The two siblings and the Shapshico

**[Modern literary/educational retelling]**

A Ucayali educational study summarizes a story in which two children endure successive dangers while trying to reach their sister’s house. The Shapshico embodies the forest’s danger; perseverance, sibling solidarity, and aid from young people enable their survival. This moral framing belongs to modern educational literature.

### The fisherman at Cocha Tipishca

**[Modern literary retelling]**

In *The Mayantu and Other Tales*, a fisherman encounters an elongated, crimson, horned, goat-tailed demon at a lake. It demands his blood and threatens to eat him. Dawn forces the demon to withdraw toward hell, though it promises future revenge.

This is not an early Indigenous myth text. Its hell, horned demon, goat tail, red coloration, cannibalistic speech, and dawn-closing infernal portal form an overt Christian and horror-literary synthesis. It is nevertheless significant evidence for the twenty-first-century image of Shapshico.

### The drunken Shapshico and the loggers

**[Modern Ucayali folklore collection]**

Loggers discover that their liquor is disappearing. They pretend to leave, catch the Shapshico drinking, surround it, and beat it. The being vanishes and announces revenge through their dreams. The men return to Pucallpa without cutting any trees.

This story preserves the forest-sanction function in a comic modern setting involving commercial logging and alcohol. It does not require the being to be morally benevolent: fear of retaliation produces conservation.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### No institutional cult

No temple, shrine, priesthood, festival calendar, sacrificial office, pilgrimage, canonical hymn, idol inventory, or dedicated oracle of the Shapshico has been documented. **[Negative finding]**

Accordingly, calling it a “god” should not imply an institutional cult comparable to Greco-Roman, Mesopotamian, or state-level Andean religion. Its documentary setting is vernacular cosmology, oral narrative, hunting practice, healing, and language.

### Forest etiquette

The strongest practice-related evidence concerns proper relations with the forest owner:

- hunters undertake dietary preparation;
- humans request or negotiate access to game;
- hunters should not take excessive numbers;
- animals may be fed salt at mineral licks;
- fruit trees may be planted along paths;
- forest beings can be “tamed” or familiarized through feeding rather than domesticated;
- the owner may reward disciplined hunters and punish exploiters.

Not every one of these practices is addressed uniquely to a being called Shapshico. The Alto Pucalpillo evidence instead situates “supay or shapingo” within a larger relational system. **[Ethnographic context]**

### Protective responses

Reported protections include:

- recognizing the anomalous foot;
- refusing to follow an apparent relative without verification;
- shouting near or into the being’s ear;
- Christian prayer;
- baptism as supposed protection for children;
- shamanic tobacco blowing and calling the lost person by name;
- dogs, which some *yashingo* traditions say the being fears;
- inherited plant, moss, or lichen remedies after an encounter.

These measures belong to different communities and narratives and should not be synthesized into one ritual manual.

### Sacrifice and offerings

No human sacrifice, animal sacrifice, temple offering, or sacramental offering made specifically to Shapshico was located. **[Negative finding]**

The broader practice of respecting, feeding, or negotiating with forest owners should not be redescribed as “sacrifice” without an informant or source using an equivalent category.

### Sexual rites and “temple prostitution”

No evidence whatsoever connects Shapshico with institutional sexuality or “temple prostitution.” **[Negative finding]**

### Material and visual record

No archaeological image can be securely identified as Shapshico. Modern ceramics, book illustrations, educational art, and internet images are reception evidence only.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Possible precolonial substrate

Forest owners, animal masters, deceptive nonhuman persons, transformative spirits, and dangerous doubles are widely documented in Amazonian cosmologies. It is reasonable to infer that some narrative structures now expressed through the name *Shapshico* draw on precolonial concepts. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**

It is not possible, however, to date the name itself to the precolonial period or assign it to a single Proto-Arawak being. **[Negative finding]**

### Sixteenth to eighteenth centuries: Christian demonological translation

Missionaries habitually rendered Indigenous nonhuman beings as *demonios*, *diablos*, or deceptive spirits, while *supay* increasingly became a Quechua equivalent of the Christian Devil. The Shapshico’s later semantic field is consistent with that transformation.

No inspected missionary chronicle explicitly names Shapshico, however. Thus the colonial transformation is historically well grounded at the level of categories but not directly documented for this particular name. **[Contextual reconstruction]**

### Nineteenth century

Ordinaire and other travelers and missionaries documented Indigenous societies, ritual specialists, “superstitions,” spirits, and mission conflict. No searchable occurrence of the name was located. **[Negative finding]**

### Early to mid-twentieth century: lexical and folklore visibility

By the mid-twentieth century the word appears in Huánuco folklore as *diablillo*. Pérez Marcio’s 1953 title belongs to the same period of intensified literary collection and representation of Amazonian worlds. The being’s early printed profile is therefore lexical and folkloric rather than scriptural or archaeological.

### Late twentieth century: ethnographic contextualization

Regan’s Loreto corpus firmly situates Shapshico within the Sacharuna/Chullachaqui complex. Regional ethnography clarifies its relation to hunting, forest ownership, imitation, and Christian ideas such as baptism.

At the same time, Asháninka, Yanesha, Matsigenka, Nomatsiguenga, Yine, and Urarina ethnographies showed why a pan-Amazonian “demon” category is inadequate. Each society maintains its own classifications, and apparent equivalents cannot be merged merely because missionaries or Spanish speakers call them *diablos*.

### Shining Path and civil-war years

The internal armed conflict devastated Asháninka and other central-selva communities through forced recruitment, massacre, displacement, enslavement, disease, and militarization. Brown and Fernández’s *War of Shadows* is fundamental documentation of that historical setting.

No reliable evidence was found that the Shapshico itself became a named Shining Path symbol, combatant allegory, or organized cult. **[Negative finding]** More generally, older idioms of predation, sorcery, outsiders, and forest danger could provide language through which unprecedented violence was understood, but any specific Shapshico connection requires testimony that has not been located.

The Shapshico must also not be conflated with the Pishtaco. The latter is a humanlike fat-stealing killer strongly associated with colonial outsiders, road building, mines, state projects, and political terror. Functional resemblance does not establish identity.

### Twenty-first century

The being now circulates in:

- folklore websites and podcasts;
- bilingual story collections;
- regional dictionaries;
- school and university research;
- tourism promotion;
- museum and government educational material;
- contemporary Indigenous art;
- gastronomy and branding;
- online horror writing.

In this process the name increasingly oscillates between ecological “guardian of the forest” and visually standardized fantasy demon.

### Continuity and revival

Belief and storytelling remain alive in Amazonian communities, but no organized “Shapshico religion,” reconstructionist denomination, or documented neopagan revival was located. **[Negative finding]**

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Ecological guardian

Government and educational presentations commonly treat Shapshico as another name for Chullachaqui and emphasize its role as protector of the forest against excessive hunting and logging. This interpretation has genuine ethnographic support, but modern ecological messaging often regularizes a far more ambivalent body of traditions. **[Modern pedagogical reframing]**

### Horror demon

Recent web retellings depict a nearly zoological monster with green skin, pointed ears, fangs, nauseating odor, predation on children, and supposed historical attacks on Spanish colonists. One site even claims that dogs enabled intensive hunts that made Shapshicos nearly extinct.

No historical documentation for Shapshico hunts, population decline, or near extinction was found. Those assertions are **[modern invention/unverified cryptidization]**.

### Literature

Arnaldo Panaifo Texeira’s *Shamiro* contains the exclamation “the damned Shapshico got his way,” demonstrating literary use of the name for a malignant agent. Modern educational and bilingual storybooks have expanded the being into a conventional fantasy antagonist.

The English-language *Mayantu and Other Tales* fixes a horned, red, goat-tailed, flesh-eating Shapshico in a recognizable international demon idiom. **[Modern retelling]**

### Visual art

Alejandro Canayo’s ceramic work, tentatively captioned “¿Shapshico?”, is modern Indigenous art rather than an archaeological cult image. Its uncertainty is important: modern artists may materialize orally transmitted beings without claiming a single canonical form.

### Gastronomy and commerce

A Lima restaurant named a pisco-based cocktail “Shapshico,” using Amazonian fruits such as cocona and camu camu. Reporting from 2014 explicitly described its namesake as a “little devil who makes you lose yourself in the forest.” This is a documented commercial appropriation that turns danger into regional exoticism.

“Shapshico” also named an egalitarian Amazonian community established by painter César Calvo de Araújo near the end of his life. The project ended when illness forced his return to Lima; he died in 1970. Here the name appears to evoke Amazonian locality and alterity rather than worship.

Huánuco’s university-sponsored “Mikunki Shapshico” gastronomic festival had reached its fourteenth edition by 2023, showing the term’s domestication as a marker of regional identity.

### Film and performance

A 2025 Peruvian Ministry of Culture eligibility document lists a proposed project titled *Un Shapshico en el Parque Kennedy*. A listing proves the project’s administrative existence, not completion or cultural impact. **[Contemporary reception]**

### Occultism, esotericism, and neopaganism

Dedicated searches found no established Shapshico tradition in Renaissance occultism, Theosophy, the Golden Dawn, ceremonial magic, chaos magic, New Age angelology, Jungian literature, modern grimoires, or organized neopagan reconstructionism. **[Negative finding]**

Claims placing it in an ancient universal demonology would therefore be modern invention unless supported by new evidence.

### Political and extremist appropriation

No nationalist, fascist, revolutionary, Shining Path, or other extremist movement was found using Shapshico as a formal symbol. **[Negative finding]**

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Is Shapshico identical to Chullachaqui?

This is the central classificatory dispute.

**Evidence for identity:**

- Regan treats Sacharuna, Chullachaqui, and Shapshico as overlapping names.
- Napo and San Martín sources call Chullachaqui “also” Shapshico or Shapishico.
- Government educational material explicitly presents Chullachaqui as known by the names Shapshico and Shapingo.
- Shared traits include small size, imitation of relatives, forest residence, unequal feet, game ownership, and abduction.

**Evidence for differentiation:**

- Lexicographic sources may define Shapshico only as “devil” or “little devil.”
- The horned, goat-tailed, red monster is more explicitly Christian-demonic than many Chullachaqui accounts.
- The Puija story makes the Shapshico killable but spiritually retaliatory, while other Chullachaqui traditions present a continuing forest-owner category.
- Contemporary speakers and authors sometimes describe them as relatives rather than identical names.

The safest conclusion is historical polysemy: identity in some communities and texts, differentiation in others. **[Documented variation]**

### Indigenous being or Spanish Devil?

The alternatives are misleading if posed absolutely.

The forest-owner, animal-master, transformative-double, and unequal-foot motifs fit Indigenous Amazonian and Andean narrative worlds. Horns, goat anatomy, hell, Satanic moral polarity, danger to the unbaptized, and defeat through Christian prayer are post-Conquest Christian elements. The documented being is therefore a layered product of long translation and exchange, not simply an untouched pre-Columbian god or a wholesale European import. **[Modern historical reconstruction]**

### Goat foot versus unequal foot

The goat foot is not the earliest securely attested defining feature. Older and ethnographically stronger Chullachaqui-linked accounts give one malformed foot or the foot of locally familiar fauna, particularly deer. The goat belongs to European demon iconography and represents a regional or late Christianized elaboration. **[Syncretistic identification]**

### “Darker relative”

This formulation describes a modern attempt to systematize inconsistent folklore. It works where storytellers distinguish the protective Chullachaqui from a predatory Shapshico, but fails where *Shapshico* is simply the local name for the same forest owner. **[Modern systematization]**

### Arawak attribution

A Yine story and the geographic presence of Asháninka and Yanesha communities in Pasco, Huánuco, Junín, and Ucayali explain why the Shapshico may be associated with Southern Arawak peoples. Yet the strongest lexical evidence is Quechua and Amazonian Spanish, and the folklore extends through multilingual riverine populations. Designating it exclusively “Southern Arawak” obscures this history. **[Historiographic correction]**

### State of the evidence

The primary record is fragile:

- oral narratives were often collected late;
- informants’ community, language, and precise words are inconsistently supplied;
- many publications translate distinct categories as “devil”;
- local books are difficult to access or incompletely digitized;
- internet reproductions often omit original publication details;
- modern summaries copy one another and erase regional attribution;
- illustrations are routinely presented without provenance.

The most urgent research need is a passage-level survey of regional archives and physical editions, particularly Pérez Marcio, Arguedas-era folklore files, Loreto and San Martín education collections, and Franciscan lexical materials.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

The following were not substantiated:

- that Shapshico was an ancient, unitary god of all Southern Arawak peoples;
- that it has a canonical genealogy;
- that every Chullachaqui is a Shapshico or vice versa;
- that the goat foot is its original pre-Columbian form;
- that Spanish colonists historically hunted it nearly to extinction;
- that it belongs to a formal occult hierarchy;
- that it received human sacrifice;
- that it had temples or an organized priesthood;
- that it was appropriated institutionally by the Shining Path.

## 11. Sources

### Primary and near-primary records

- The Huánuco entry “Shapshico.—Diablillo” in *Notas del folklore peruano* is the earliest directly verifiable lexical testimony located.
- Weber’s *Diccionario del quechua del Huallaga* provides the principal linguistic documentation for *shapshicu* as “devil.”
- Regan’s *Hacia la tierra sin mal* preserves named and localized informant accounts relating Shapshico, Sacharuna, and Chullachaqui.
- The Puija/Sepahua narrative attributed to Segundo Espinoza Sandoval is the fullest accessible story naming Shapshico directly.
- Kichwa-Lamista testimony supplies the essential evidence for Shapshico/Shapingo as a situational name for plant or forest agency rather than an exclusively evil demon.
- Rocchietti and Cárdenas preserve recent Loreto testimony and document modern artistic representation.
- *The Mayantu and Other Tales* is a primary source for modern literary Christianization and horror imagery, not ancient belief.

### Critical and comparative scholarship

- Weiss is indispensable for Asháninka categories of soul, ghost, demon, transformation, abnormality, and illness.
- Santos-Granero, Smith, Renard-Casevitz, Rojas Zolezzi, Gow, Shaver and Dodd, Brown and Fernández, and Dean provide the necessary warning against merging Yanesha, Asháninka, Matsigenka, Nomatsiguenga, Yine, and Urarina cosmologies.
- Modern lexical studies of Cajamarca demonstrate that *Shapingo* can mean the Devil, a malicious person, or a mischievous child, documenting semantic domestication.
- ASALE and the Academia Peruana de la Lengua establish present-day regional definitions but cannot by themselves date the mythic complex.

### Final assessment

The Shapshico is best reconstructed not as an ancient god with a biography but as a historically layered name moving among several kinds of nonhuman person. Its most securely supported profile is a small or anomalous forest being that deceives through resemblance, owns or guards animals, and can make people lost, ill, empowered, or dead. Its relation to the Chullachaqui is locally variable. Its goat foot, horns, tail, infernal home, and Satanic moral identity mark the incorporation of the Spanish Christian Devil. Its role as medicinal teacher or ecological guardian preserves other relational possibilities that the word “demon” obscures.

The documentary silence before the twentieth century is a fact about the surviving name-record, not proof that every associated story is recent. Conversely, the antiquity of Amazonian forest-master concepts does not license calling every modern Shapshico detail pre-Columbian. The being’s actual history lies precisely in that unresolved movement between Indigenous forest person, translated *supay*, Christian devil, regional folktale character, and modern cultural emblem.
