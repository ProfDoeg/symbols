# Hermod: Research Dossier

**Class:** Hermóðr is variously classified in the surviving evidence as a heroic inhabitant of Valhöll, an Odinic retainer, a legendary human king or warrior, and—only unequivocally in medieval Icelandic systematization—one of the Æsir and a son or servant of Óðinn. “Messenger-god” is a useful modern shorthand, but no medieval source gives him that standing title or makes messaging his permanent divine office.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest firmly datable occurrence of Old Norse **Hermóðr** is Eyvindr skáldaspillir’s *Hákonarmál*, stanza 14, composed about **961**, where Óðinn orders Hermóðr and Bragi to welcome the dead King Hákon to Valhöll. The cognate Old English **Heremōd** occurs twice in *Beowulf*. That poem may preserve substantially older material and is often dated much earlier than its sole manuscript, but its composition remains disputed; the surviving witness, British Library Cotton MS Vitellius A XV, dates from the end of the tenth or early eleventh century. Thus *Beowulf* may contain the historically earliest tradition, while *Hákonarmál* supplies the earliest narrowly secure, approximately contemporary attestation.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identity

Hermóðr is a marginal but revealing figure at the boundary between Germanic heroic legend and Norse divine mythology. Three principal configurations survive:

1. **Hero or Valhöll-retainer:** In tenth-century *Hákonarmál*, Hermóðr stands with Bragi in Valhöll and obeys Óðinn. Nothing calls him a god or Óðinn’s son.
2. **Favored warrior:** In *Hyndluljóð* 2, Óðinn gives Hermóðr a helmet and mail-coat, just as he gives Sigmundr a sword. The parallel strongly resembles Óðinn’s patronage of mortal heroes.
3. **Æsir-related underworld rider:** In Snorri’s thirteenth-century *Gylfaginning* 49, Hermóðr rides Sleipnir to Hel to negotiate for Baldr’s release and is called either Óðinn’s **sveinn** or **sonr**, depending on manuscript tradition. Here alone his familiar modern identity is fully narrated.

The Old English Heremod is a Danish or Anglian king whose great strength and initial eminence become tyranny, isolation, and exile. Whether he is historically the same legendary figure as the Norse Hermóðr is disputed rather than established.

### Name forms

- **Old Norse:** *Hermóðr*, occasionally normalized *Hermoðr*; genitive *Hermóðs*.
- **Old English:** *Heremōd*, with long *ō* in normalized spelling.
- **Reconstructed Proto-Germanic personal name:** commonly ***Harjamōdaz** or ***Harimōdaz**.
- **Medieval and antiquarian Latinized forms:** *Heremodius*, *Hærmodus* and comparable orthographies.
- **Older English renderings:** Hermod, Hermoth, Hermód.
- **Modern Scandinavian:** Icelandic *Hermóður* or the preserved Old Norse form *Hermóðr*; Norwegian/Danish/Swedish usually *Hermod*.

The compound is transparent at the Germanic level:

- Old Norse *herr*, Old English *here*: “army, host.”
- Old Norse *móðr*, Old English *mōd*: “mind, spirit, courage, temper,” with semantic extensions toward anger or battle-fury.

Accordingly, responsible glosses include **“army-spirit,” “war-mind,” “battle-courage,”** or **“war-fury.”** “Spirit of war” is conventional but can mislead if taken as proof that Hermóðr began as a personified abstraction. The etymology establishes the semantics of the name, not the ontological origin of its bearer.

Medieval Scandinavian personal-name evidence shows that *Hermóðr/Hermoth* existed as a human name, though sparsely. Norwegian place-name evidence is uncertain because forms in *Hermóðr*, *Hermundr*, and later *Herman* can converge. No place-name securely proves a sanctuary or cult of the mythic Hermóðr.

### Titles and epithets

There is no securely attested cult epithet. Common modern expressions require qualification:

- **“Hermóðr the Brave”** — an English explanatory epithet, not a stable medieval Old Norse title.
- **“Messenger of the gods”** — **modern systematization**, inferred principally from the single Hel-ride.
- **“God of speed”** — **modern invention/popular-culture development**, especially prominent in Marvel-derived material.
- **“Son of Óðinn”** — **later canonical elaboration**, supported by some manuscripts of *Gylfaginning* and by a later *þula*, but not by the earliest attestations.
- **“Brother of Baldr”** — explicit in Snorri’s narrative; unattested in earlier verse.

### Classification through time

| Source | Date | Classification implied |
|---|---:|---|
| *Beowulf* | composition disputed; manuscript c. 1000 | Human legendary king/hero |
| *Hákonarmál* 14 | c. 961 | Valhöll inhabitant or Odinic retainer; divinity unstated |
| *Hyndluljóð* 2 | probably twelfth–thirteenth century in preserved form | Recipient of Óðinn’s martial patronage; likely heroic |
| *Málsháttakvæði* 9 | thirteenth century | Figure who tried to prolong Baldr’s life |
| *Gylfaginning* 49 | c. 1220 | Æsir-associated rider; Óðinn’s “boy/servant” or son; Baldr’s brother |
| *Nafnaþulur* | medieval, transmitted with Snorri’s *Edda* | Listed among Óðinn’s sons |
| Modern handbooks and retellings | nineteenth century onward | Minor god and habitual divine messenger |

There is no ancient or medieval equation of Hermóðr with Hermes or Mercury. Roman *interpretatio* equated Mercury principally with Wodan/Óðinn, not Hermóðr. Their messenger and underworld-travel functions invite modern comparison, but a historical syncretism is unattested.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### *Hákonarmál*: earliest secure Old Norse evidence

Eyvindr skáldaspillir composed *Hákonarmál* after Hákon the Good’s death around 961. Its account of Hákon’s reception in Valhöll imitates the older *Eiríksmál*. In stanza 14 Óðinn/Hroptatýr says:

> “Hermóðr and Bragi, go to meet the prince, for a king is coming hither to the hall.”

This is **securely attested early material**. Hermóðr speaks no surviving line. Bragi, not Hermóðr, subsequently reassures Hákon. Hermóðr’s presence establishes association with Óðinn, Valhöll, and the reception of a royal dead man, but not genealogy, divine rank, speed, Sleipnir, or a general messenger-office. The Skaldic Project and critical editions treat the poem as tenth-century court poetry; Snorri later quotes this stanza in *Skáldskaparmál*.

### *Beowulf*: Heremod

The unique manuscript is the Nowell Codex portion of Cotton MS Vitellius A XV, copied around 1000. The poem’s date is contested: proposals range from the eighth century to near the manuscript date. A claim that Heremod is “attested about 700” therefore depends on an early dating of the poem; the physical witness itself is later.

Heremod appears in two passages:

- **Lines 898–915:** following the Sigemund song, Heremod’s courage or conflict ebbs; he goes alone from human joys. The passage contrasts Sigemund’s glory with Heremod’s failure, although exact syntax and narrative implications have long been debated.
- **Lines 1709b–1724a:** Hrothgar warns Beowulf through Heremod’s example. God had raised Heremod above all men, but a blood-fierce disposition grew in him; he ceased giving rings to the Danes, became joyless, and brought prolonged affliction upon his people.

This Heremod is unambiguously human within the poem: an exceptionally powerful, failed ruler. He is called an *eafora Ecgwelan*, “offspring of Ecgwela,” and associated with the Scyldings. Anglo-Saxon royal genealogies place Heremod in an ancestral sequence, commonly as son of Itermon and father of Scyld. The genealogical position is not identical to the poem’s presentation and cannot simply be imposed upon it.

Recent scholarship argues that the legend concerned popular rejection, royal tyranny, exile, and regime change more than mere avarice. Leonard Neidorf has also proposed that the Anglo-Saxon and Norse evidence preserves an older relationship between Heremod/Hermóðr and Óðinn that the Christian *Beowulf* poet obscured. This is an ingenious **modern scholarly reconstruction**, not a fact stated in *Beowulf*.

### *Hyndluljóð* 2

*Hyndluljóð* survives not in the Poetic Edda’s Codex Regius, but in *Flateyjarbók* (GKS 1005 fol.), compiled in the late fourteenth century. The poem is usually considered older than that manuscript, but its layers and date are disputed. Stanza 2 says of Óðinn:

> *gaf hann Hermóði hjalm ok brynju,  
> en Sigmundi sverð at þiggja*

“He gave Hermóðr a helmet and mail-coat, and Sigmundr a sword to receive.”

This does not narrate the Hel-ride or call Hermóðr divine. Pairing him with the mortal Völsung hero Sigmundr favors the interpretation of Hermóðr as an Odinic hero. Some commentators nevertheless read Snorri’s divine Hermóðr back into the stanza. Both possibilities must remain visible.

### *Málsháttakvæði* 9

This thirteenth-century poem, preserved in the Codex Regius of Snorri’s *Edda* (GKS 2367 4to), says:

> *Hermóðr vildi auka aldr,  
> Éljúðnir vann sǫlginn Baldr.*

“Hermóðr wished to lengthen life; Éljúðnir possessed/swallowed Baldr.”

The wording alludes compactly to an attempt to restore or extend Baldr’s life and to Hel’s hall Éljúðnir. It independently shows that the rescue attempt circulated in verse, although it is not necessarily older than Snorri’s prose. It does not mention Sleipnir, Móðguðr, the gate, or the universal-weeping condition.

### Snorri’s *Edda*

Snorri Sturluson probably composed or organized the *Edda* around 1220. Its four principal witnesses are:

- **DG 11 4to, Codex Upsaliensis:** first quarter of the fourteenth century.
- **GKS 2367 4to, Codex Regius:** first half of the fourteenth century.
- **AM 242 fol., Codex Wormianus:** around 1350.
- **Utrecht MS 1374, Codex Trajectinus:** c. 1600 copy of a lost medieval exemplar.

Their versions differ. In the commonly edited Regius-version of *Gylfaginning* 49, Hermóðr is *sveinn Óðins*. *Sveinn* can mean boy, young man, page, servant, or retainer; “son” is possible contextually but is not the word’s necessary meaning. Other manuscripts explicitly read *sonr Óðins*. Later in the same episode Baldr is called Hermóðr’s brother. The variation is central to the classification dispute.

A *Nafnaþulur* list of Óðinn’s sons includes:

> *Hermóðr, Sigi, Skjöldr …*

Such lists are learned, fluid, and absent from some manuscripts and editions. They demonstrate medieval systematization, not necessarily Viking-Age genealogy.

### Other named corpora: negative findings

A dedicated check of the tradition’s major corpora produces an important negative result:

- **Tacitus, *Germania*:** no Hermóðr/Heremod.
- **Merseburg Charms:** no Hermóðr.
- **Adam of Bremen:** no Hermóðr.
- **Runic inscriptions:** no securely identified divine Hermóðr; personal-name-like readings do not establish the god.
- **Völsunga saga:** Sigmundr appears, but Hermóðr does not participate in its narrative.
- **Nibelungenlied:** no Hermóðr.
- **Poetic Edda’s Codex Regius:** no surviving Hermóðr passage; *Hyndluljóð* is transmitted elsewhere.
- **Heimskringla:** preserves and quotes *Hákonarmál*, but adds no independent Hermóðr myth.
- **Saxo Grammaticus:** no character securely named Hermóðr in the pertinent legendary sequence.
- **Wagner’s *Ring* libretti:** no Hermod character.

Saxo’s tyrant Lotherus and another legendary figure, Olo, have been compared with Heremod because of narrative structures involving Odinic favor, royal misconduct, overthrow, or death. These are **scholarly analogues**, not textual attestations of Hermóðr under an agreed alternate name.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Attested functions

Hermóðr has no securely documented cultic domain. His source-based functions are narrower:

- welcoming an honored royal dead man to Valhöll;
- receiving martial equipment from Óðinn;
- undertaking the journey to Hel;
- negotiating with Hel;
- carrying messages and gifts between the living gods and the dead.

“Messenger” is therefore an inference from action. Unlike Hermes, Iris, or the Christian Gabriel, Hermóðr is never formally installed as herald, patron of travelers, commerce, communication, or eloquence.

### Characteristic equipment

- **Helmet and mail-coat:** given by Óðinn in *Hyndluljóð* 2.
- **Sleipnir:** borrowed for the Hel-ride in *Gylfaginning* 49; the horse remains Óðinn’s.
- **Draupnir:** carried back from Baldr to Óðinn, not owned by Hermóðr.
- **Nanna’s gifts:** a linen garment for Frigg and a gold ring or finger-ring for Fulla, again carried rather than possessed.

No source gives Hermóðr a permanent weapon, emblem, sacred animal, color, number, weekday, celestial body, plant, rune, or festival. Modern associations with ravens, winged helmets, caducei, the color blue, Mercury, or a special “Hermod rune” are unattested inventions or free devotional symbolism.

### Powers and limitations

Snorri does not say that Hermóðr himself possesses supernatural speed or flight. His successful passage depends on Sleipnir. He rides for nine nights through deep, dark valleys, crosses Gjallarbrú, and has the horse leap Hel’s gate. He can enter the realm of the dead while alive and return, but he cannot compel Hel or revive Baldr. His agency is diplomatic and courageous, not sovereign.

### The gate-leap

The often-illustrated leap is explicit in *Gylfaginning*: Hermóðr dismounts, tightens the girth, remounts, spurs Sleipnir, and the horse clears the gate without touching it. Thus “Hermóðr leapt the gate” is shorthand; the text attributes the physical leap to Sleipnir.

### Historical iconography

No Viking-Age image is securely identifiable as Hermóðr. Riders on eight-legged horses, most famously on Gotland picture stones, are conventionally associated with Óðinn and the journey to the dead; none bears an identifying inscription naming Hermóðr.

The earliest unambiguous labeled or text-dependent depictions are post-medieval:

- **NKS 1867 4to, fol. 96r (1760):** an Icelandic paper manuscript written and illustrated by the priest Ólafur Brynjúlfsson, now in the Royal Danish Library. It shows Hermóðr on Sleipnir with Baldr and Hel. This is the best-known historical image, but it is eighteenth-century antiquarian manuscript art, not pre-Christian cult iconography.
- **Lorenz Frølich, nineteenth-century “Hermod’s Ride to Hel”:** part of the Romantic-national visual recasting of Eddic mythology.
- **Louis Moe, *Ragnarok: En Billeddigtning* (1929):** depicts Hermóðr at Hel’s gates.

The recognizable modern type—armed young rider on an eight-legged horse before a barred underworld gate—is consequently a post-medieval construction based closely on Snorri’s prose.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical traditions

| Tradition | Relationship | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|
| *Gylfaginning* Regius reading | *sveinn Óðins* | Ambiguous: Óðinn’s boy, page, servant, or possibly son |
| Other *Gylfaginning* manuscripts | son of Óðinn | Later canonical elaboration |
| *Gylfaginning* narrative | Baldr’s brother | Later canonical elaboration |
| *Nafnaþulur* | one of Óðinn’s sons | Learned medieval systematization |
| Modern genealogies | son of Óðinn and Frigg | Partly modern inference: Frigg is Baldr’s mother, but no source explicitly names her as Hermóðr’s mother |
| *Beowulf* | offspring of Ecgwela | Old English heroic genealogy |
| Anglo-Saxon genealogies | son of Itermon, father of Scyld | Royal-genealogical tradition; human ancestor |
| Modern Marvel continuity | son of Odin and often Frigga | Modern invention/adaptation |

No spouse, child, or mother is securely attributed to the Norse Hermóðr. Calling Thor, Váli, Víðarr, Höðr, or the many figures listed as Óðinn’s sons his brothers produces a systematized family tree not actually narrated in Hermóðr’s sources.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Óðinn — ordered reception:** Óðinn/Hroptatýr directs Hermóðr and Bragi to greet Hákon in Valhöll; *Hákonarmál* 14, c. 961. **Securely attested early material.**
- **Bragi — joint reception duty:** Hermóðr and Bragi are jointly ordered to welcome Hákon; *Hákonarmál* 14. **Securely attested early material.**
- **Óðinn — gift of arms:** Óðinn gives Hermóðr helmet and mail-coat; *Hyndluljóð* 2, medieval poem in fourteenth-century *Flateyjarbók*. **Later poetic attestation; heroic/divine status disputed.**
- **Sigmundr — parallel beneficiary, not a narrated meeting:** Hermóðr and Sigmundr receive different martial gifts from Óðinn in the same stanza; *Hyndluljóð* 2. **Joint textual appearance, but no direct interaction between them.**
- **Frigg — commission:** Frigg asks who among the Æsir will ride to Hel and offers all her favor; Hermóðr accepts; *Gylfaginning* 49, c. 1220. **Later canonical elaboration.**
- **Óðinn — use of Sleipnir:** Óðinn’s horse is fetched for Hermóðr; *Gylfaginning* 49. No spoken exchange with Óðinn is recorded. **Later canonical elaboration.**
- **Sleipnir — mount and partner in passage:** Hermóðr rides Sleipnir to Hel, tightens his girth, and spurs him over the gate; *Gylfaginning* 49. Sleipnir is a named mythic animal rather than a god. **Later canonical elaboration.**
- **Móðguðr — encounter at Gjallarbrú:** the bridge-guardian questions Hermóðr, observes that he lacks the color of the dead, and directs him down and north toward Hel; *Gylfaginning* 49. **Later canonical elaboration.**
- **Baldr — meeting in Hel:** Hermóðr finds his brother Baldr seated in the high seat, stays overnight, receives Draupnir from him, and later carries Hel’s terms concerning his return; *Gylfaginning* 49. **Later canonical elaboration.**
- **Nanna — transmission of gifts:** Nanna sends Hermóðr back with a linen garment for Frigg and a ring for Fulla; *Gylfaginning* 49. **Later canonical elaboration.**
- **Hel — negotiation:** Hermóðr petitions Hel to let Baldr return; she makes release conditional upon all things, living and dead, weeping for him; *Gylfaginning* 49. **Later canonical elaboration.**
- **Frigg — delivery of Nanna’s gift and Hel’s condition:** implied by Hermóðr’s return and Nanna’s instruction; *Gylfaginning* 49. **Later canonical elaboration.**
- **Fulla — delivery of Nanna’s ring:** carried by Hermóðr; *Gylfaginning* 49. No direct conversation is narrated. **Later canonical elaboration.**
- **Loki/Þökk — no direct interaction:** Loki is responsible for Baldr’s death in Snorri and is commonly identified by Snorri as the probable disguised Þökk who refuses to weep, but Hermóðr never meets, addresses, or confronts Loki/Þökk. **Absence of documented interaction.**
- **Höðr — no direct interaction:** although Höðr kills Baldr in Snorri’s narrative, no meeting with Hermóðr is recorded. **Absence of documented interaction.**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The reception of Hákon in Valhöll

In *Hákonarmál*, valkyries select Hákon the Good for death. The king questions why victory was taken from him; the valkyrie Skǫgul announces that they must ride to tell Óðinn a king is coming. Óðinn orders Hermóðr and Bragi to meet him. Hákon arrives blood-covered and suspicious of Óðinn, but Bragi promises him peace among the *einherjar* and ale with the Æsir.

Hermóðr’s role is small but structurally important: he belongs to Valhöll’s receiving company. Some scholars see an analogy with *Eiríksmál*, where Sigmundr and Sinfjötli welcome Eiríkr Bloodaxe. That parallel may explain why *Hyndluljóð* later places Hermóðr beside Sigmundr among Óðinn’s favored warriors. It does not prove they were interchangeable gods.

### Óðinn’s gift to Hermóðr

*Hyndluljóð* opens with Freyja’s appeal to Hyndla and an invocation of Óðinn’s patronage. Óðinn pays some warriors with gold, gives Hermóðr helmet and mail, and gives Sigmundr a sword. The stanza’s primary theme is selective Odinic favor. It contains no messenger mythology.

The figure may be:

- the same heroic Valhöll inhabitant as in *Hákonarmál*;
- Snorri’s Hermóðr understood retrospectively;
- or an older heroic figure later divinized in learned Icelandic tradition.

Simek’s formulation that Hermóðr “is a god only in Snorri’s version” captures the skeptical position. Other scholars argue that hero and god cannot be separated so neatly, especially within an Odinic heroic afterlife where dead kings, legendary champions, and divine retainers overlap.

### Hermóðr’s ride to Hel

The fullest account occurs in *Gylfaginning* 49.

After Baldr’s death, the Æsir are overcome by grief. Frigg asks which of them will win all her love and favor by riding the Hel-road and offering Hel a ransom for Baldr. Hermóðr accepts. Óðinn’s horse Sleipnir is brought to him.

Hermóðr rides for nine nights through valleys so deep and dark that he sees nothing. He reaches the river Gjöll and the gold-roofed Gjallarbrú. Its guardian, Móðguðr, says five companies of dead men crossed the previous day, yet the bridge resounds no less beneath Hermóðr alone. He does not have the color of a dead person. After he identifies his mission, she tells him Baldr has crossed and directs him downward and northward.

At Hel’s gate, Hermóðr dismounts and tightens Sleipnir’s girth. He remounts and spurs him; Sleipnir jumps entirely over the gate. Hermóðr rides to the hall and finds Baldr, his brother, in the high seat. He remains overnight.

In the morning he asks Hel to let Baldr ride home, explaining the gods’ grief. Hel answers that this will test whether Baldr is as universally loved as reported: if all things in the world, living and dead, weep for him, he may return; if anyone refuses, he remains with her.

Baldr escorts Hermóðr from the hall and returns Draupnir, the ring Óðinn placed on his funeral pyre. Nanna gives Hermóðr a linen garment for Frigg and other gifts, including a ring for Fulla. Hermóðr returns and reports what he has seen and heard.

The Æsir send messengers throughout the world, and all beings and things weep except the cave-dwelling giantess Þökk, who says Hel should keep what she has. Snorri comments that people suppose Þökk was Loki in disguise. Baldr consequently remains in Hel until after Ragnarök.

#### Development and variants

- *Málsháttakvæði* confirms that a Hermóðr–Baldr–Éljúðnir rescue tradition existed in medieval verse.
- No extant pre-Christian poem narrates the ride.
- *Baldrs draumar* gives the journey to the underworld to Óðinn himself, not Hermóðr, although its purpose is prophecy before Baldr’s death rather than attempted recovery afterward.
- The descent resembles international return-from-death and visionary-journey narratives. Similarity alone does not establish Christian borrowing.
- Snorri’s account may preserve older oral material, may synthesize divergent poems, or may substantially literary-shape the episode. Its precise pre-Christian depth cannot be established.

### Heremod’s fall in *Beowulf*

Old English Heremod begins with extraordinary divinely permitted power but becomes a destructive ruler. He ceases the expected royal circulation of treasure, becomes murderous or blood-fierce, loses communal joy, and brings suffering on his people. He departs alone from human happiness; exile or expulsion is implied.

The poem uses him twice as a negative royal exemplum. He is not a failed underworld messenger and has no relationship to Baldr in the surviving Old English text.

Possible connections with Norse Hermóðr include:

- identical cognate name;
- association with Sigemund/Sigmundr in both *Beowulf* and *Hyndluljóð*;
- extraordinary martial standing;
- possible Odinic patronage reconstructed from later analogues;
- traditions of fall, removal, or residence among the dead.

Against identification:

- Old English Heremod is explicitly a human king;
- the Norse sources never narrate his tyranny;
- Snorri’s Hermóðr serves Baldr rather than opposing or replacing him;
- shared Germanic personal names do not require a single character;
- several proposed bridges depend on much later and transformed material.

The prudent conclusion is **probable traditional relationship but unreconstructable identity-history**.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

No temple, sanctuary, priesthood, festival, sacrifice, votive object, prayer, charm, oath, or ritual text is securely dedicated to Hermóðr.

There are likewise:

- no securely Hermóðr-derived pre-Christian cult place-names;
- no rune-stone invoking him;
- no theophoric name that demonstrably denotes devotion rather than ordinary personal naming;
- no image bearing his name;
- no report by Tacitus, Adam of Bremen, or a saga author of sacrifice to him;
- no evidence for a distinct “messenger cult.”

This absence is unusually decisive. Hermóðr is a literary and legendary figure in the evidence currently available, not a demonstrable recipient of institutional worship.

General evidence for sacrifice, including human sacrifice, in Germanic or Viking-Age religion must not be transferred to him. Texts and archaeology make ritual killing a legitimate wider subject of debate, but there is **no documented human sacrifice to Hermóðr**, no association with temple prostitution, and no cult violence committed in his name.

The same caution applies to conversion. Scandinavian Christianization suppressed or displaced pre-Christian religious practices generally, but no source records destruction of a Hermóðr temple, forced abandonment of his cult, or a missionary denunciation aimed at him.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Germanic heroic background: uncertain prehistory

The cognate Old English and Old Norse names imply a Germanic naming tradition older than the surviving manuscripts. Whether one mythic person lay behind all later forms cannot be demonstrated. The recoverable early profile is heroic and martial, not chiefly messenger-like.

### Anglo-Saxon record: perhaps eighth century, manuscript c. 1000

*Beowulf* makes Heremod a failed Danish ruler and royal warning. Genealogies absorb a Heremod into dynastic ancestry. This is a process of heroic-historical and genealogical preservation, not evidence of worship.

### Norwegian court poetry: c. 961

*Hákonarmál* locates Hermóðr in Valhöll. He has access to Óðinn and performs a reception duty with Bragi. This is the oldest firmly dated Norse evidence and the strongest reason to treat him as an Odinic afterlife figure before Snorri.

### High-medieval Icelandic poetic tradition

*Hyndluljóð* casts him as the recipient of Óðinn’s martial gifts. *Málsháttakvæði* associates him with Baldr’s attempted recovery. These strands bridge heroic Hermóðr and Snorri’s underworld rider without clearly establishing divine sonship.

### Snorri’s systematization, c. 1220

In *Gylfaginning*, Hermóðr acquires his lasting narrative identity. The prose joins together:

- membership among the Æsir;
- kinship or service to Óðinn;
- brotherhood with Baldr;
- courage to travel alive to Hel;
- Sleipnir;
- negotiation with Hel;
- return as messenger and gift-bearer.

Manuscript variation between “Óðinn’s servant/boy” and “Óðinn’s son” shows the classification still moving during transmission.

### Later medieval and early-modern antiquarian preservation

Hermóðr remained marginal. He generated no major saga cycle. Genealogists and mythographers discussed links between the Old English king and the Norse rider. The eighteenth-century NKS 1867 4to illustration gave the episode a durable visual form.

### Nineteenth-century comparative mythology

Romantic-era writers regularized the Norse gods into a Greek-style pantheon and promoted Hermóðr to “messenger of the gods.” Popular manuals added habitual speed, bravery, and a standing divine office. These claims usually amplify one journey into a permanent cosmic occupation.

### Twentieth- and twenty-first-century scholarship

Simek, Lindow, Orchard and others emphasize the small and heterogeneous corpus. Current debate increasingly resists treating the *Beowulf*, skaldic, Eddic, and Snorrian figures as either obviously identical or wholly unrelated.

### Present-day worship

Individual Heathens sometimes venerate Hermóðr as a god of messages, courageous journeys, grief, funerary passage, mediation with ancestors, or carrying prayers. Online testimony documents such worship, including explicitly labeled **UPG**—“unverified personal gnosis.” No reliable population figure exists, and Hermóðr remains peripheral even within modern Heathenry. This is a modern religious development, not demonstrable cult continuity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literary retelling

Nineteenth-century mythology books transformed Snorri’s episode into a self-contained adventure and often called Hermod “the swift” or the gods’ regular messenger. Such presentations influenced children’s literature and reference works more than medieval evidence warrants.

Matthew Arnold’s narrative poem **“Balder Dead”** (1855) gives Hermod an expanded dramatic role in the embassy to Hela. Later popular retellings, including Neil Gaiman’s *Norse Mythology* (2017), repeat the Hel-ride for general readers.

Wagner’s *Der Ring des Nibelungen* does **not** include Hermod. Claims that he is a Ring character confuse the wider Germanic source-world with Wagner’s selected dramatis personae.

### Visual art

Romantic and antiquarian artists established the familiar visual formula:

- mounted youthful warrior;
- Sleipnir as fantastic or eight-legged steed;
- dark descent or monumental gate;
- enthroned Baldr and Hel.

This iconography derives from literary reconstruction. It is not a recovered Viking-Age Hermóðr type.

### Comics

Marvel introduced Hermod in the late 1970s, adapted by Roy Thomas and John Buscema. The comics make him an Asgardian son of Odin, exceptionally fast, and a recurrent messenger. His “god of speed” quality is substantially a **modern comic-book elaboration**. It should not be back-projected into Snorri, where Sleipnir supplies the extraordinary locomotion.

### Games

A character named Hermod appears in *Kingdom Hearts: Dark Road*, but this is a loose name-borrowing rather than an adaptation of the Hel-rider. Online lists also attach the name to other games and mobile characters, often without any narrative relationship to the medieval figure. Hermóðr does not appear as a character in the Norse-era *God of War* games; claims that he does are erroneous.

### Occult and esoteric use

Hermóðr has no meaningful role in Renaissance Hermetism, ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or the classical grimoire tradition. Search results that connect “Hermod” to Hermeticism commonly arise from superficial resemblance to **Hermes**, not historical transmission.

Modern magical practitioners may invoke him for communication, spirit journeying, or contact with the dead. These are contemporary analogical constructions based on the Hel-ride. No medieval Hermóðr spell, sigil, planetary correspondence, or rune is known.

### Nationalism and extremism

Germanic mythology as a whole has been appropriated by racial-nationalist and extremist movements, and parts of modern Heathenry have documented racialist branches. No evidence, however, shows Hermóðr as a significant extremist emblem comparable to Óðinn, Thor, Týr, runes, or the Valknut. Assigning generic far-right Norse appropriation specifically to Hermóðr would overstate the record.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### God, hero, or promoted dead man?

This is the principal debate.

- **Hero-first model:** *Hákonarmál* places Hermóðr among Valhöll’s inhabitants, while *Hyndluljóð* treats him like Sigmundr, a hero favored by Óðinn. Snorri or the tradition before him subsequently made him an Æsir and son of Óðinn.
- **Divine-retainer model:** service in Valhöll, mythic gift reception, and the superhuman Hel journey may reflect a minor divine being whose cult left no trace.
- **Fluid-category model:** the sharp modern distinction between god, dead hero, ancestral king, and divine retainer may misrepresent Old Norse mythic classification.

The evidence cannot decisively select one.

### *Sveinn Óðins* versus *sonr Óðins*

The Regius wording *sveinn Óðins* need not mean “son.” Translating it automatically as such conceals manuscript and semantic uncertainty. Other witnesses and the *Nafnaþulur* explicitly support sonship, indicating either a variant older reading or later genealogical clarification.

### Heremod and Hermóðr

Earlier scholarship often assumed simple identity. Later work became more cautious because the narratives diverge sharply. Recent articles by Leonard Neidorf have revived a historically connected model: an early hero favored and then ruined by Óðinn, with separate versions preserved in *Beowulf*, Norse texts, and Saxo’s analogues. The proposal explains several otherwise isolated parallels, particularly the association with Sigemund, but depends on reconstruction across centuries and languages.

### Lotherus and Olo

Saxo’s Lotherus is an oppressive ruler overthrown by his people, making him a strong analogue for *Beowulf*’s Heremod. Saxo does not name him Heremod. Olo likewise participates in an Odinic royal legend compared with Heremod. Treating either as “Hermod under another name” exceeds the explicit record.

### Christian influence on the Hel-ride

Possible comparanda include Christian descents to hell, visionary journeys, ransom or intercession narratives, and return-from-death stories. Conversely, the episode contains indigenous-looking elements: Sleipnir, Gjöll, Gjallarbrú, the living rider’s distinct color, and Hel’s conditional legalistic response. No extant source allows the passage to be cleanly divided into pagan core and Christian overlay.

### Messenger-god comparison

Dumézilian or comparativist approaches might place Hermóðr beside Hermes or other Indo-European messengers. The corpus is too small to establish inherited functional equivalence. Óðinn, not Hermóðr, is the historically attested Germanic equivalent of Mercury under Roman and later weekday interpretation.

### Source survival

The evidence is constrained by severe asymmetry:

- only one manuscript of *Beowulf*;
- no Viking-Age manuscript of an Eddic poem;
- *Hyndluljóð* in a late-fourteenth-century manuscript;
- Snorri’s *Edda* in divergent post-authorial witnesses;
- almost no material, epigraphic, or cultic evidence;
- no securely pre-Christian image of Hermóðr.

Consequently, apparent changes may sometimes reflect accidents of preservation rather than real historical innovation.

### Evidentiary conclusion

The reconstructable history is not that of a well-attested “Norse messenger god” with a known cult. It is the history of a Germanic heroic name appearing in divergent traditions: a disastrous king in Old English poetry; a prestigious Valhöll figure in tenth-century Norwegian verse; a warrior favored by Óðinn in an Eddic stanza; and finally the courageous rider to Hel in Snorri’s medieval synthesis. Sonship, permanent messenger-office, speed-god status, fixed iconography, occult correspondences, and most present-day devotional functions are progressively later constructions. The Hel-ride is authentic medieval evidence, but its status as a Viking-Age belief and its relationship to the human Heremod remain unresolved.
