# Brynhild: Research Dossier

> **Class:** In the earliest securely preserved legendary witness, the *Nibelungenlied* (composed in Middle High German about 1200), Brünhild is a superhumanly strong human queen, not a goddess. In the Old Norse tradition preserved principally in the thirteenth-century *Poetic Edda*, Snorri’s *Edda*, and *Völsunga saga*, Brynhildr is variously a valkyrie, shield-maiden, king’s daughter, and heroic woman; whether the valkyrie Sigrdrífa was always Brynhildr is disputed. Wagner’s nineteenth-century Brünnhilde is explicitly a divine valkyrie and daughter of Wotan.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation of the legendary figure:** The *Nibelungenlied*, composed probably in the Passau region around 1200 and preserved in early-thirteenth-century manuscripts. The personal name itself is much older: Latinized *Brunichildis* and variants name the historical Visigothic–Frankish queen Brunhild of Austrasia in sixth- and seventh-century sources. That historical queen’s relationship to the legendary Brünhild is possible but unproved.
>
> **Principal evidentiary warning:** No pre-Christian inscription, temple, cult image, prayer, sacrifice, theophoric place-name, or securely Viking-Age artifact identifies Brynhild. She belongs to Germanic heroic legend and medieval literature, not to the securely documented cultic pantheon. Calling her an ancient “Norse goddess” is a modern conflation, encouraged especially by Wagner’s divine Brünnhilde.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### The figure

Brynhild is the principal tragic woman of the Sigurd/Siegfried heroic complex. Across its surviving branches she is:

- **[Secure medieval German attestation]** Brünhild or Prünhilt, queen of Îsenstein in Iceland, possessed of extraordinary strength and compelled to marry the man who defeats her athletic trials, in the *Nibelungenlied*.
- **[Secure medieval Norse attestation]** Brynhildr Buðladóttir, daughter of Buðli and sister of Atli, a king’s daughter and shield-maiden in the Eddic heroic poems and *Völsunga saga*.
- **[Later canonical Norse synthesis]** A valkyrie punished by Óðinn, enclosed in sleep and awakened by Sigurðr.
- **[Medieval variant]** A daughter of King Heimir rather than Buðli in *Þiðreks saga*.
- **[Modern invention and synthesis]** Brünnhilde, daughter of Wotan and Erda, favorite of nine valkyrie sisters, in Wagner’s *Ring*.

She has no fixed “domain” comparable to Þórr’s thunder or Freyja’s fertility. Her recurrent narrative functions are the dangerous bride, wronged oath-partner, martial woman subjected to dynastic marriage, instigator of the hero’s death, mourner, suicide, and—in Wagner—the compassionate rebel whose final act ends the gods’ order.

### Names

- **Old Norse:** *Brynhildr*. The normal etymology joins *brynja*, “mail-coat, armor,” and *hildr*, “battle”: approximately “armor-battle” or “armored battle.” *Hildr* is also independently the name of a valkyrie.
- **Middle High German:** commonly *Brünhilt*, *Prünhilt*, or *Prunhilt* in the principal manuscripts.
- **Modern German:** *Brünhild*; Wagner’s spelling is *Brünnhilde*. The final *-e* is not the medieval nominative form, and the doubled *nn* makes the association with modern German *Brünne*, “coat of mail,” visually conspicuous.
- **Latin historical forms:** *Brunichildis*, *Brunechildis*, and related spellings for the historical queen Brunhild of Austrasia.
- **Modern English:** Brynhild, Brunhild, Brunhilda, Brunhilde, or Brünnhilde, depending on whether the Norse, German medieval, or Wagnerian figure is intended.
- **Sigrdrífa:** Old Norse *Sigrdrífa*, conventionally interpreted as “victory-driver” or “inciter to victory,” from *sigr*, “victory,” and *drífa*, “to drive/urge.” In the Codex Regius prose introducing *Sigrdrífumál*, the awakened valkyrie gives this name. *Völsunga saga* identifies her as Brynhild. Whether *Sigrdrífa* is a personal name, a valkyrie-title, or evidence for an originally independent heroine remains disputed.

### Titles and descriptions

Brynhild has no securely attested cult epithet. Literary designations include:

- *valkyrja*, “valkyrie,” in Snorri’s *Skáldskaparmál* and the Norse prose synthesis.
- *mær und hjalmi*, broadly “maiden beneath a helmet,” in the awakening tradition.
- Buðli’s daughter, Atli’s sister, Heimir’s foster-daughter, or Heimir’s daughter, depending on source.
- Queen of Iceland/Îsenstein in the *Nibelungenlied*.
- *Wotans Tochter*, Wotan’s daughter, in Wagner.
- *Wunschmädchen* or “wish-maiden” is sometimes used in later Wagnerian commentary, but it is not a medieval title of Brynhild.

There is no ancient *interpretatio romana* or comparable classical equation for her. Identifications with Athena, Minerva, Freyja, or Bellona in popular occult lists are modern analogies, not inherited traditions.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Personal name versus legendary character

**[Secure historical attestation]** The name is borne by Brunhild of Austrasia, born about 543 and killed in 613. Gregory of Tours’ late-sixth-century *Historiae* and the seventh-century *Chronicle of Fredegar* document her political career. Her feud with Fredegund, dynastic marriages, and violent execution have long invited comparison with the feud-centered heroic legend.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** The historical queen may have contributed her name, royal status, feud, or death to an evolving heroic figure. No surviving early source says that the queen became the legendary heroine, however. The legendary Burgundian setting also preserves remoter fifth-century memories—the destruction of a Burgundian kingdom and traditions about Attila—so a simple one-person historical key is inadequate.

A Latin place-name recorded as *lectulus Brunihildae*, “Brunhild’s bed,” in the Taunus has sometimes been recruited as evidence for the sleeping-woman story. Its connection to the legend is insecure; reference to the historical queen or later place-lore is at least as plausible.

### The *Nibelungenlied*

**[Earliest secure legendary attestation; later medieval canonical German version]** The anonymous Middle High German epic was composed about 1200, probably in an Austrian/Bavarian courtly environment often associated with Passau. An apparent allusion in Wolfram von Eschenbach’s *Parzival*, about 1204–1205, supplies an approximate upper bound.

The three principal codices are:

- Manuscript A, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek Cgm 34, later thirteenth century.
- Manuscript B, Stiftsbibliothek St. Gallen Cod. Sang. 857, mid-thirteenth century.
- Manuscript C, Badische Landesbibliothek Donaueschingen 63, about 1225–1250.

They represent materially different redactions; C frequently reshapes and moralizes the poem. The labels A, B, and C derive from Karl Lachmann’s now-obsolete hierarchy and do not rank the manuscripts by simple textual quality. All three entered UNESCO’s Memory of the World Register in 2009.

Here Brünhild rules Îsenstein in Iceland. A suitor must defeat her in throwing a spear, casting a stone, and leaping; failure means death. Siegfried, invisible under the *tarnkappe*, secretly performs Gunther’s feats. After marriage she binds Gunther and suspends him from a wall; Siegfried again subdues her invisibly in the bedchamber and takes her ring and girdle. When Kriemhild later displays these objects and calls Brünhild Siegfried’s concubine, Brünhild’s humiliation leads Hagen and Gunther toward Siegfried’s murder. The poem gives her no suicide or pyre and largely removes her after Siegfried’s death.

### The *Poetic Edda*

**[Secure manuscript evidence; uncertain earlier oral composition]** The principal witness is Codex Regius GKS 2365 4to, written in Iceland in the later thirteenth century, commonly around 1270. Its poems are older than the manuscript in varying and contested degrees; they cannot all be assigned a single Viking-Age date.

The relevant corpus includes:

- ** *Grípisspá*:** Grípir prophesies that Sigurðr will awaken a sleeping warrior-woman, learn wisdom from her, be deceived into betraying her, and participate in winning her for Gunnarr. The poem is often judged a relatively late synopsis designed to reconcile existing lays.
- ** *Sigrdrífumál*:** Sigurðr crosses a luminous barrier, cuts armor from a sleeping figure, and awakens a valkyrie who identifies herself as Sigrdrífa. She says Óðinn struck her with a sleep-thorn because she gave victory to the king he had condemned. She teaches Sigurðr victory-runes, ale-runes, birth-runes, sea-runes, branch-runes, speech-runes, mind-runes, and ethical counsel. The poem breaks off in the Codex Regius “Great Lacuna”; later paper manuscripts supply additional matter.
- ** *Brot af Sigurðarkviðu*:** The fragment presupposes the fire-ride and broken oaths. Brynhild urges the Gjúkung brothers against Sigurðr, then laughs at news of his death but predicts disaster.
- ** *Guðrúnarkviða I*:** Brynhild is associated with Sigurðr’s death and grief. Guðrún’s lament dominates.
- ** *Sigurðarkviða hin skamma*:** A full tragic treatment: Brynhild demands Sigurðr’s death, subsequently reveals that she did not truly desire Gunnarr, kills herself, and directs the arrangement of the funeral pyre so that she and Sigurðr may be burned together with a sword between them.
- ** *Guðrúnarkviða II*:** Guðrún recounts the disaster and Brynhild’s role.
- ** *Helreið Brynhildar*:** A prose introduction and short poem describe Brynhild’s wagon journey toward Hel after her cremation. An unnamed giantess accuses her of ruining another woman’s marriage and causing deaths; Brynhild answers with her own history, including Óðinn’s punishment and Sigurðr’s awakening of her.

The surviving arrangement is not a seamless canonical biography. Contradictions—especially over the identity of the awakened woman, the number of meetings, Sigurðr’s death-place, and Brynhild’s family—remain visible.

### Snorri’s *Edda*

**[Thirteenth-century learned systematization]** Snorri Sturluson composed the *Edda* about 1220–1230 as a handbook of traditional poetry and mythic diction. In *Skáldskaparmál* he states that Sigurðr found a woman sleeping in helmet and mail, cut off the armor, and woke her; “she gave her name as Hildr; she is called Brynhildr, and was a valkyrie.” Snorri thereby supplies a threefold equation: Hildr = Brynhildr = valkyrie.

His compressed Niflung narrative has Sigurðr exchange shape with Gunnarr, ride through the fire, lie beside Brynhild with the sword Gramr between them, exchange rings, and later become the object of Brynhild and Guðrún’s river quarrel. Brynhild incites Gunnarr and Hǫgni, who use Gotthormr to kill Sigurðr because their own oaths bind them.

This is a learned reconciliation of poetic material, not proof that every surviving lay originally told the same story.

### *Völsunga saga*

**[Later canonical Norse synthesis]** The saga was probably compiled in Iceland in the later thirteenth century. Its sole complete medieval witness is NKS 1824 b 4to, written around 1400, where it is followed by *Ragnars saga loðbrókar*. The linkage makes Áslaug, daughter of Sigurðr and Brynhild, ancestress of Ragnar’s line.

The saga explicitly identifies the awakened woman as Brynhild. It integrates Eddic verse and prose into a continuous biography:

1. Brynhild is a valkyrie and daughter of Buðli, fostered by Heimir.
2. She disobeys Óðinn by granting victory to Hjálmgunnarr’s opponent, Agnarr.
3. Óðinn pricks her with a sleep-thorn and declares she will marry.
4. Sigurðr crosses the shield-wall or flame-like light, cuts off her armor, and wakes her.
5. They pledge themselves.
6. They meet again at Heimir’s home and renew their vows.
7. Grímhild gives Sigurðr a forgetfulness drink; he marries Guðrún.
8. Sigurðr, in Gunnarr’s shape, crosses the fire and wins Brynhild for Gunnarr, placing Gramr between them.
9. Guðrún reveals the deception during a dispute over precedence in the river.
10. Brynhild demands Sigurðr’s death, afterwards laments him, kills Sigurðr’s young son, and stabs herself.
11. She orders a joint pyre with Sigurðr and the sword placed between them.

The saga’s smoothness is secondary: it bridges gaps and discrepancies in its poetic sources.

### *Þiðreks saga*

**[Regional continental tradition preserved in Old Norse]** Compiled in Norway about the mid-thirteenth century from German narrative material, *Þiðreks saga af Bern* makes Brynhild the daughter of King Heimir, living at Sægard in Swabia. Sigurðr has previous contact with her, but the narrative does not reproduce the full Óðinn–sleep-thorn initiation. Gunnarr’s wooing involves deception and Sigurðr’s assistance; the saga preserves continental configurations distinct from both the *Nibelungenlied* and *Völsunga saga*.

### Other medieval and later witnesses

- **[Medieval derivative]** *Norna-Gests þáttr* identifies the awakened valkyrie with Brynhild, showing that the identification was established in thirteenth-century Icelandic prose.
- **[Medieval learned prose]** Snorri’s account contains the figure; *Heimskringla* does not supply independent evidence for a Brynhild cult.
- **[Absence of evidence]** No securely attributed skaldic poem from a datable Viking-Age court gives an independent Brynhild narrative.
- **[Absence of evidence]** Tacitus, Adam of Bremen, Saxo Grammaticus, and the Merseburg Charms do not name Brynhild. They illuminate Germanic religion or heroic tradition generally but cannot attest her personally.
- **[Early-modern folklore transmission]** Faroese heroic ballads, including *Brynhildar táttur* in the Sigurd cycle, preserve substantially reshaped oral versions recorded mainly in eighteenth- and nineteenth-century manuscripts. Their late recording does not establish that every detail is medieval.
- **[Nineteenth-century reception]** William Morris and Eiríkr Magnússon translated *Völsunga saga* in 1870; Morris reworked it as *Sigurd the Volsung* in 1876.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Functions and powers

Brynhild has no documented sphere of worship. Her textual attributes vary:

- **Battle allocation:** Sigrdrífa/Brynhild can determine victory, though Óðinn can punish her for contradicting his decision.
- **Valkyrie knowledge:** She knows runic and ethical lore in *Sigrdrífumál*.
- **Prophecy:** The Eddic Brynhild foretells the Gjúkungs’ destruction and Guðrún’s later marriages.
- **Martial identity:** Helmet, mail, shield, and participation in battle.
- **Superhuman strength:** The German Brünhild hurls a massive spear and stone, leaps prodigiously, and physically overcomes Gunther.
- **Conditional invulnerability or strength:** In the *Nibelungenlied*, her extraordinary power disappears after she is defeated and sexually subordinated. The poem does not explain this through a named magical belt in the manner of some modern retellings; Siegfried takes a girdle and ring as trophies.
- **Fire boundary:** In Norse sources, the marriageable Brynhild is enclosed by flame or a radiant shield-wall passable only by a fearless hero.
- **Sleep enchantment:** The sleep-thorn is Óðinn’s punishment in the Norse synthesis.
- **Limitations:** She can be deceived by shape-changing, forgetfulness magic, invisibility, and male oath-politics. She cannot reverse Sigurðr’s murder after demanding it.

### Symbols and objects

Securely literary objects include armor, helmet, shield, sword, runes, a drinking horn or cup, sleep-thorn, ring, fire-wall, funeral wagon, and pyre. The sword placed between Brynhild and Sigurðr signifies abstinence in the bridal deception and is placed between them again on the funeral pyre.

No medieval source assigns her sacred colors, weekdays, planets, numbers, plants, incense, or ritual offerings. Such correspondences in modern occult tables are modern inventions unless a particular modern author explicitly identifies them as personal or system-specific symbolism.

### Iconography

**[Absence of secure identification]** No Viking-Age image can presently be identified as Brynhild with confidence. Female figures with horns, cups, weapons, or horses may represent valkyries, elite women, or mythic scenes, but naming one Brynhild requires circular dependence on later texts.

**[Medieval manuscript culture]** The principal *Nibelungenlied* codices are important textual artifacts, but they do not establish a stable ancient Brynhild iconographic type.

**[Modern canonical image]** Wagnerian stage design fixed the best-known visual vocabulary: winged or horned helmet, breastplate, spear, shield, horse, and a fire-ringed rock. The stereotyped horned helmet is not derived from a secure medieval Brynhild image.

Important modern representations include Arthur Rackham’s illustrations for Wagner’s *Ring* (1910–1911), Fritz Lang’s film *Die Nibelungen: Siegfried* (1924), and innumerable opera photographs and posters. These illustrate modern receptions, not ancient cult art.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations

### Competing genealogies

| Source layer | Parentage and kinship |
|---|---|
| Eddic/Norse dominant version | Daughter of Buðli; sister of Atli and, in some arrangements, Oddrún; foster-daughter of Heimir |
| *Völsunga saga* | Buðli’s daughter, Heimir’s foster-daughter; mother by Sigurðr of Áslaug |
| *Þiðreks saga* | Daughter of King Heimir |
| *Nibelungenlied* | Family origin is not developed; autonomous queen of Îsenstein before marriage to Gunther |
| Wagner | Daughter of Wotan and Erda; sister or half-sister within Wotan’s divine family and leader among the valkyries |

### Partners and offspring

- **Sigurðr:** Pledged lover in the Norse tradition; never her publicly acknowledged husband.
- **Gunnarr/Gunther:** Husband obtained through deception.
- **Áslaug:** Daughter of Sigurðr and Brynhild in *Völsunga saga*’s dynastic continuation. This descent is not common to all Brynhild traditions.
- **No children in the *Nibelungenlied*:** None are assigned to Brünhild and Gunther.
- **Wagner:** Brünnhilde has no child.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

The medieval corpus contains remarkably few such interactions because Brynhild’s principal associates are heroic humans.

- **Óðinn — punishment after disputed battle-victory:** Sigrdrífa/Brynhild grants Agnarr victory contrary to Óðinn’s decision; Óðinn strikes her with a sleep-thorn and says she must marry. *Sigrdrífumál*, *Helreið Brynhildar*, and *Völsunga saga*, preserved thirteenth century. **[Secure medieval Norse attestation; exact early form disputed]**
- **Óðinn — protective invocation taught to Sigurðr:** In the rune-teaching complex, she invokes or describes divine powers associated with carved runes; the precise textual boundaries and attribution vary because of the manuscript lacuna. *Sigrdrífumál*. **[Secure medieval text; interpretation disputed]**
- **Wotan — commander, father, judge, and reconciled antagonist:** Brünnhilde disobeys his command by protecting Siegmund; Wotan strips her divinity, places her in sleep, and surrounds her with fire, but accedes to her request that only a fearless hero reach her. Wagner, *Die Walküre*, text completed in the 1850s, premiered 1870. **[Modern mythopoetic invention]**
- **Wotan — awakening-era confrontation:** In *Siegfried*, Wotan does not personally awaken Brünnhilde; Siegfried breaks the Wanderer’s spear and crosses the fire. Brünnhilde’s prior relationship with Wotan governs the scene but there is no new meeting. **[Modern invention; absence of direct interaction noted]**
- **Waltraute — warning about Valhalla:** Waltraute visits Brünnhilde and begs her to return the ring to the Rhinemaidens so that Wotan and the gods may be saved; Brünnhilde refuses because she treats it as Siegfried’s pledge. Wagner, *Götterdämmerung*, Act I. **[Modern invention]**
- **Gerhilde — joint appearance and appeal:** Fellow valkyrie; participates in the Valkyries’ gathering and the dispute over sheltering Sieglinde. Wagner, *Die Walküre*, Act III. **[Modern invention]**
- **Ortlinde — same episode:** Fellow valkyrie in the “Ride” and confrontation with Wotan. **[Modern invention]**
- **Waltraute — same episode, before her later solo visit:** Fellow valkyrie. **[Modern invention]**
- **Schwertleite — same episode:** Fellow valkyrie. **[Modern invention]**
- **Helmwige — same episode:** Fellow valkyrie. **[Modern invention]**
- **Siegrune — same episode:** Fellow valkyrie. **[Modern invention]**
- **Grimgerde — same episode:** Fellow valkyrie. **[Modern invention]**
- **Rossweisse — same episode:** Fellow valkyrie. **[Modern invention]**
- **Erda — no direct onstage meeting:** Wagner makes Erda Brünnhilde’s mother, but the two never converse in the cycle. **[Modern genealogy; absence of interaction]**
- **Fricka — no direct substantive encounter:** Both appear in *Die Walküre*’s dramatic world, but Fricka argues her case to Wotan, not Brünnhilde. **[Modern invention; no discrete interaction]**
- **Hel — no interaction:** *Helreið Brynhildar* takes Brynhild toward Hel, the destination or realm. The poem does not stage a meeting with a named goddess Hel. **[Absence of evidence]**

The giantess who challenges Brynhild during the Hel-ride is unnamed and therefore cannot be entered as an interaction with a named divinity.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The valkyrie’s disobedience and awakening

**[Secure medieval Norse attestation]** Sigrdrífa tells Sigurðr that two kings fought. Óðinn had promised victory to Hjálmgunnarr, but she felled him and favored Agnarr. Óðinn pricked her with a sleep-thorn and declared that she would never again win victory in battle and would marry.

Sigurðr finds a luminous place on Hindarfjall. Inside the shield-wall lies an armored sleeper. Thinking the armor has grown into the flesh, he cuts it with Gramr. She awakens, greets day, night, gods, and goddesses, and offers Sigurðr a memory-drink. She teaches runes and counsel.

**Identity dispute:** Codex Regius calls her Sigrdrífa in prose; *Völsunga saga* calls her Brynhild. Snorri says she named herself Hildr and was called Brynhildr. Some scholars infer two originally distinct figures—a supernatural victory-woman and a Burgundian tragic queen—later fused. Others take *Sigrdrífa* as a functional valkyrie-name or epithet and see no need for an independent heroine. The evidence cannot decide the characters’ prehistoric identity.

### The pledges and the second meeting

**[Later canonical synthesis]** *Völsunga saga* has Sigurðr and Brynhild exchange pledges after the awakening, then meet again at Heimir’s residence. She warns that he will marry Guðrún; he insists that he desires her alone and gives her a ring.

The double meeting may be the compiler’s attempt to preserve divergent poetic accounts. It is not securely recoverable from the damaged Eddic sequence.

### The forgetfulness drink

**[Medieval Norse elaboration]** Grímhild gives Sigurðr a drink that makes him forget Brynhild; he marries Guðrún. This preserves Sigurðr’s heroic honor by transferring much responsibility to magic and dynastic manipulation.

The *Nibelungenlied* has no equivalent prior betrothal or amnesia. Its Siegfried knowingly helps Gunther obtain Brünhild in exchange for Kriemhild.

### Winning Brynhild through fire

**[Norse canonical version]** Brynhild has vowed to marry only the man who crosses her encircling fire. Gunnarr’s horse refuses. Sigurðr changes shape with Gunnarr, rides Grani through the flames, and spends three nights beside Brynhild with Gramr between them. He gives her a ring from Andvari’s treasure and takes another ring from her.

**[Continental variant]** In the *Nibelungenlied*, the test is not a fire-wall but three athletic contests. Invisible Siegfried supplies Gunther’s strength. The poem’s Brünhild is an autonomous queen whose marriage condition is defeated through fraud.

### The wedding-night subjugation

**[Continental medieval variant]** Brünhild resists consummation, binds Gunther, and hangs him from a nail or hook. On a subsequent night invisible Siegfried wrestles her into submission, taking her ring and girdle, after which Gunther consummates the marriage.

The episode is coercive and violent. Whether the poem implies that Siegfried himself had intercourse with her is deliberately unresolved; Kriemhild later claims that he did, but the narrator does not establish that claim. Modern summaries that treat Kriemhild’s accusation as fact erase the poem’s ambiguity.

### The queens’ quarrel

**[Shared narrative core with divergent settings]**

- In *Völsunga saga* and Snorri, Brynhild and Guðrún quarrel while bathing over whose husband deserves the higher place in the water. Guðrún reveals that Sigurðr, not Gunnarr, crossed the fire and shows the ring.
- In the *Nibelungenlied*, Brünhild and Kriemhild quarrel over precedence before the cathedral. Kriemhild calls Brünhild a concubine and produces the ring and girdle taken during the bedroom struggle.

The quarrel exposes not merely rivalry but the men’s marriage fraud. In both traditions the public proof destroys Brünhild’s status and helps initiate Siegfried/Sigurðr’s death.

### Vengeance and Sigurðr’s death

**[Variant-rich medieval tradition]** Brynhild demands that Gunnarr choose between her and Sigurðr. Because Gunnarr and Hǫgni are oath-bound to Sigurðr, Gotthormr is induced to kill him.

The sources disagree about the killing:

- Sigurðr is murdered in bed beside Guðrún in *Sigurðarkviða hin skamma* and *Völsunga saga*.
- The prose attached to *Brot af Sigurðarkviðu* acknowledges a German version in which he is killed outdoors in a forest.
- The *Nibelungenlied* has Hagen spear Siegfried at a spring during a hunt, with Gunther’s complicity.
- Wagner follows the hunting version but embeds it in his ring-curse and potion plot.

Brynhild’s reaction is equally divided: triumph, terrible laughter, prophetic speech, grief, confession of love, and determination to die coexist across the Norse poems. These are preserved tensions, not stages in an ordinary psychological biography.

### Suicide and the pyre

**[Norse canonical ending]** After Sigurðr’s murder, Brynhild stabs herself. She requests a great pyre: Sigurðr beside her, the sword between them, attendants and valuables accompanying them. The placing of the sword repeats the marriage-bed separation but now joins them in death.

*Völsunga saga* adds the killing of Sigurðr’s young son before Brynhild’s suicide. The *Nibelungenlied* supplies neither suicide nor pyre; Brünhild simply disappears from the foreground after Siegfried’s death.

### *Helreið Brynhildar*

**[Late or composite Eddic memorial poem]** Brynhild’s body is burned in a richly hung wagon. On the road to Hel an unnamed giantess bars her passage and accuses her of bloodshed and visiting another woman’s husband. Brynhild answers by narrating Óðinn’s punishment, her enclosure, Sigurðr’s arrival, and her oath that only Sigurðr would possess her. The poem turns her journey into self-defense and closes with anticipated union with Sigurðr.

Its reconciliation of valkyrie, king’s daughter, and wronged lover may be secondary. It remains an essential medieval witness even if its internally harmonizing form is late.

### The *Nibelungenlied* Brünhild and Kriemhild

**[Canonical Middle High German version]** Brünhild is Kriemhild’s rival rather than Gudrun’s supernatural counterpart. Her insistence that Siegfried is Gunther’s vassal creates the status dispute. The church-door quarrel exposes the sexual and political fraud practiced against her. Hagen offers revenge; Gunther assents. Brünhild is not the second half’s avenger—Kriemhild becomes that figure after Siegfried’s death.

### Wagner’s Brünnhilde

**[Modern mythopoetic synthesis, 1848–1874]** Wagner fused Norse material, the *Nibelungenlied*, *Þiðreks saga*, Jacob Grimm, modern philology, Greek tragedy, and his own politics and philosophy.

- In *Die Walküre*, Brünnhilde is Wotan and Erda’s daughter. She joyfully executes Wotan’s will until compassion for Siegmund and the pregnant Sieglinde makes her disobey. Wotan removes her divinity and surrounds her with fire.
- In *Siegfried*, Siegfried awakens her. Fear enters him for the first time; she relinquishes divine knowledge and embraces mortal love.
- In *Götterdämmerung*, Siegfried is drugged into forgetting her and, disguised through the Tarnhelm, wins her for Gunther. She joins the conspiracy against him, believing herself betrayed.
- After recovering the truth, she orders Siegfried’s pyre, returns the ring to the Rhinemaidens through her self-immolation, rides Grane into the fire, and precipitates the destruction of Valhalla.

Wagner’s Brünnhilde is therefore neither the *Nibelungenlied* queen nor simply the Eddic Brynhild. Her compassion toward Siegmund, divine parentage, relationship with Waltraute, and world-redemptive ending are Wagner’s.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Historical cult

No Brynhild cult is documented.

- No temple, sanctuary, altar, priesthood, festival, pilgrimage, sacrifice, votive object, or cult image is securely dedicated to her.
- No known runic inscription invokes Brynhild as a supernatural helper.
- No theophoric personal name establishes worship: *Brynhildr* is a Germanic dithematic human name, and bearing it does not imply devotion to a goddess.
- Tacitus’s *Germania*, Adam of Bremen’s Uppsala description, Saxo, the Merseburg Charms, and the archaeological cult sites at Uppåkra, Tissø, and elsewhere provide no Brynhild-specific evidence.
- There is no evidence connecting Brynhild to human sacrifice or “temple prostitution.”

The rune lore in *Sigrdrífumál* is textual evidence for medieval ideas about magical inscription; it is not a surviving Brynhild ritual manual. Modern attempts to map its “rune categories” directly onto the Elder Futhark’s individual letter names generally exceed what the poem says.

### Funerary ritual in the story

Brynhild’s pyre, wagon, grave-goods, attendants, and proposed human deaths are narrative funerary imagery. They may echo Scandinavian elite-funeral concepts, but the poem is not an archaeological report and cannot prove that a historical Brynhild was burned.

### Modern practice

Individual Heathens, occultists, or eclectic pagans may honor Brynhild as a valkyrie, heroic ancestor, or goddess. Such practice is a modern religious choice, not evidence of an unbroken cult. No reliable census isolates Brynhild worship as a separate organized movement.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Sixth–seventh centuries: the historical name

Brunhild of Austrasia makes the name historically visible. Hostile historiography—especially Fredegar—helped turn a powerful queen into a violent moral exemplum. Whether this influenced the legendary heroine is unresolved.

### Early medieval oral heroic tradition

The Burgundians, Attila/Atli, and dragon-slayer narrative circulated across Germanic-language regions. The surviving evidence does not allow reconstruction of a single original “Brynhild myth.” A likely shared nucleus consists of a formidable bride, Gunther’s dependence on Sigurd/Siegfried to win her, deception, a quarrel with the hero’s wife, exposure of the deception, and the hero’s death.

### About 1200: courtly German Brünhild

The *Nibelungenlied* adapts heroic material to Christian aristocratic culture. Brünhild’s strength is marvelous, but she is not presented as a pagan deity or valkyrie. Her defeat domesticates a sovereign woman into Gunther’s queen; the unresolved violence of that transition powers the catastrophe.

### Thirteenth century: Norse poetic and prose systematization

The Codex Regius poems preserve multiple layers. Snorri, *Völsunga saga*, *Norna-Gests þáttr*, and *Þiðreks saga* increasingly organize fragmentary legends into prose sequences. Brynhild becomes firmly identified with the punished valkyrie, although contradictions remain.

### Late medieval and early-modern survival

The story persisted through manuscripts, Scandinavian legendary sagas, German heroic literature, print antiquarianism, and Faroese ballads. The *Nibelungenlied* ceased to be widely copied after the late medieval period and was effectively rediscovered for modern scholarship in 1755.

### Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries: national epic and reconstructed mythology

German antiquarians and Romantics elevated the *Nibelungenlied* as a national epic. Jacob Grimm’s comparative mythology and nineteenth-century editions made Norse witnesses central to German discussions. This often blurred “Norse,” “German,” and pan-Germanic into a supposedly unitary mythology.

Wagner began his Nibelung project in 1848 with *Der Nibelungen-Mythus* and *Siegfrieds Tod*. He composed the cycle’s dramatic text largely backward, then the music forward. The completed tetralogy premiered at Bayreuth, 13–17 August 1876. Wagner’s synthesis decisively recast Brynhild as a goddess-like valkyrie whose ethical awakening surpasses Wotan’s law.

### Twentieth century to present

Wagnerian stage tradition, Fritz Lang, fantasy illustration, comics, games, and film made the armored valkyrie the dominant international image. Medieval Brünhild’s athletic contests, political sovereignty, and absence from the story’s second half are much less familiar.

There is no evidence of uninterrupted worship between the Middle Ages and modern paganism. Contemporary devotion is revival or innovation.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and music

- Wagner’s *Ring* is the single most influential reinvention.
- William Morris’s *Sigurd the Volsung* (1876) re-poeticized the Norse material.
- Friedrich Hebbel’s dramatic trilogy *Die Nibelungen* (1861) offered another major German theatrical treatment.
- J. R. R. Tolkien’s posthumously published *Legend of Sigurd and Gudrún* reworked Eddic tradition; comparisons between Brynhild and Éowyn are interpretive rather than direct identity.

### Film and visual culture

Fritz Lang’s two-part *Die Nibelungen* (1924), especially *Siegfried*, monumentalized the German epic in Weimar cinema. Brünhild appears as an exotic, formidable northern queen. The film’s later nationalist appropriation should not be collapsed into Lang’s every intention, but its monumental visual language was readily politicized.

Opera illustration—especially Arthur Rackham—popularized fire, armor, spear, shield, horse, and flowing hair. The “horned Valkyrie” caricature belongs to modern stage and popular culture, not secure medieval iconography.

### Comics, games, and commercial culture

Marvel’s Valkyrie/Brunnhilde, *Fate/Grand Order*, *Record of Ragnarok*, and numerous role-playing games borrow the name and selected motifs. These characters frequently combine Wagner, generic valkyrie lore, modern fantasy armor, romance, and newly invented powers. They are not evidence for medieval tradition.

“Brünnhilde” is also commercial shorthand for a powerful operatic soprano or stereotypically martial woman. Bugs Bunny’s “Brünnhilde” performance in *What’s Opera, Doc?* is a parody of Wagner, not of the Eddas.

### Occult and neopagan reception

Unlike Óðinn, Þórr, or Freyja, Brynhild had little independent role in Renaissance ceremonial magic, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, or the classical grimoires. Claims that she possessed an ancient planetary seal, prescribed incense, feast day, or initiation rite are modern constructions.

Modern rune manuals sometimes place *Sigrdrífumál*’s teachings in Brynhild’s mouth and treat her as a rune-mistress. That has a medieval textual basis at the level of the episode, but fixed mappings to modern rune spreads, chakras, planets, or Jungian categories are recent systems.

Psychological readings cast her as anima, warrior woman, wounded feminine, or sacrificial redeemer. These are twentieth- and twenty-first-century interpretations, not recoveries of a documented ancient theology.

### Nationalism and extremist appropriation

The Nibelung material was used in nineteenth- and twentieth-century German nationalism, and Wagner’s work became entangled with racial nationalism, antisemitism, Bayreuth politics, and Nazi cultural appropriation. Brünnhilde’s immolation and the destruction of Valhalla have consequently carried political associations far beyond the medieval figure.

Care is required: Wagner’s politics, the semantic content of the *Ring*, its Nazi reception, and later productions are related but not identical questions. Scholarship disputes how much antisemitic or racial coding is internal to particular characters and musical structures. It is nevertheless documented that Nazi cultural policy appropriated Wagner and “Germanic” heroic material. Modern racialist or “folkish” treatments that present Brynhild as an ancestral Aryan goddess are modern ideological constructions unsupported by medieval cult evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Brynhild based on Brunhild of Austrasia?

The name and some broad motifs invite the comparison: a sixth-century royal woman, dynastic conflict, feud, hostility in later historiography, and a spectacular violent death. Earlier scholarship often sought direct historical originals for heroic characters.

The objection is methodological: the legend combines historical periods, places, and persons; its surviving forms are many centuries later; and no transmission document connects the queen to the heroine. The responsible conclusion is “possible contribution, unproved,” not identity.

### Was Sigrdrífa originally Brynhild?

The principal positions are:

1. **Original distinction:** Sigrdrífa was a valkyrie wisdom-figure; Brynhild was a human or heroic queen. Later Norse compilers fused them.
2. **Epithet theory:** *Sigrdrífa* is a descriptive valkyrie-name, so the awakened woman can always have been Brynhild.
3. **Early fusion, unrecoverable origins:** Whatever the prehistory, *Helreið Brynhildar*, Snorri, *Völsunga saga*, and *Norna-Gests þáttr* attest a medieval identity that cannot be dismissed merely because it may be composite.

The Codex Regius lacuna prevents recovery of a complete poetic transition from Sigrdrífa to Brynhild.

### Is the valkyrie identity ancient or Scandinavian?

The *Nibelungenlied* does not call Brünhild a valkyrie, and its narrative needs no Óðinn, sleep-thorn, or rune-teaching. This supports the view that valkyrie identity is specifically Norse or a Norse elaboration. Conversely, the superhuman bride may preserve older mythic traits that the courtly German poem rationalized. No surviving continental pagan text decides the matter.

### Fire-wall versus athletic trials

The fire-surrounded sleeper and bride belong to the Norse record; the athletic queen belongs to the German epic. Attempts to reconstruct one as necessarily original depend on comparative models rather than direct evidence. The common recoverable fact is deception in the wooing, not the exact test.

### Sex, consent, and the girdle

The *Nibelungenlied* depicts force and fraud. Kriemhild says Siegfried first “loved” Brünhild, but this is part of an insult; the narrator leaves intercourse unresolved. Modern retellings often turn the girdle into an explicit magical source of strength or describe rape as unambiguous textual fact. Both require qualification: coercive assault is explicit, while intercourse and magical mechanics are not stated with equal certainty.

### Dating the Eddic poems

The Codex Regius is a later-thirteenth-century manuscript; individual poems may be substantially older. Linguistic form, meter, intertextuality, Christian influence, and narrative dependence supply relative arguments, but precise dates such as “ninth century” are rarely secure. “Viking-Age poem” should not be treated as a manuscript date.

### Textual state and archives

- Codex Regius GKS 2365 4to is preserved in Reykjavík and has a modern diplomatic electronic edition.
- The *Völsunga saga*’s complete medieval witness NKS 1824 b 4to is in Copenhagen.
- *Nibelungenlied* A is in Munich, B in St. Gallen, and C in Karlsruhe.
- The Great Lacuna in Codex Regius removed part of the Sigurd cycle. *Völsunga saga* and later paper manuscripts help reconstruct narrative content, but they cannot reproduce a lost exemplar word for word.
- The three chief *Nibelungenlied* manuscripts preserve different redactions, not transparent copies of a single accessible original.

Foundational modern reference works include Jan de Vries’s *Altgermanische Religionsgeschichte*, Gabriel Turville-Petre’s *Myth and Religion of the North*, Theodore M. Andersson’s work on the heroic legend, Rudolf Simek’s *Dictionary of Northern Mythology*, John Lindow’s *Norse Mythology*, Andy Orchard’s handbooks, and the *Kulturhistorisk leksikon for nordisk middelalder*. Carolyne Larrington’s translation is especially useful for literary framing; Henry Adams Bellows remains accessible but reflects older dating and reconstruction; Anthony Faulkes’s editions and translations are standard for Snorri.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Brynhild was an independently worshipped pan-Germanic goddess.
- A particular Viking-Age “valkyrie” figurine certainly depicts Brynhild.
- Her ancient cult used a fixed feast day, color, planet, rune, or sacrificial rite.
- The *Nibelungenlied* tells the same story as *Völsunga saga* with merely altered names.
- Wagner faithfully dramatized one medieval source.
- Sigrdrífa and Brynhild are demonstrably either always identical or certainly unrelated.
- The historical queen Brunhild is conclusively the legendary figure.
- Kriemhild’s claim that Siegfried had intercourse with Brünhild is an uncontested narratorial fact.
