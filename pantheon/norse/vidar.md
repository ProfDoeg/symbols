# Vidar: Research Dossier

**Class:** male god, specifically one of the **Æsir** (*áss*) in medieval Old Norse classification.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the name and figure are securely preserved in the mythological poems *Grímnismál*, *Vafþrúðnismál*, *Vǫluspá*, and *Lokasenna*, but their earliest surviving physical witness is the Icelandic vellum manuscript **GKS 2365 4to, the Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda, copied in the second half of the thirteenth century, conventionally about 1270**. The poems themselves are generally considered older—often substantially older—but their individual composition dates remain disputed. A carving on the tenth-century Gosforth Cross in Cumbria may depict Vidar fighting Fenrir and would, if the identification is correct, constitute earlier material evidence; the identification is influential, not secure. No unquestionably identifiable pre-Christian inscription, runic dedication, theophoric place-name, cult image, temple, priesthood, or offering to Vidar is presently known.

## Evidentiary labels

- **Securely attested:** stated or shown by an identifiable primary source.
- **Early poetic tradition:** preserved in a medieval manuscript but probably composed or transmitted before Snorri.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** Snorri Sturluson’s thirteenth-century synthesis or another medieval expansion.
- **Regional or textual variant:** a competing form confined to a source, manuscript, or locality.
- **Learned systematization:** a medieval or modern attempt to organize fragmentary traditions.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference rather than an ancient statement.
- **Modern invention or reception:** post-medieval art, occult doctrine, popular mythology, fiction, or revived religion.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

Vidar is a sparsely documented Æsir god whose surviving mythology is overwhelmingly eschatological. In the Eddic poems he is Odin’s son, occupies a grassy or wooded land, avenges Odin by killing the wolf Fenrir, and survives the cosmic destruction usually called Ragnarök. In *Lokasenna* he silently obeys Odin by yielding his seat and pouring Loki a drink. Snorri turns these scattered notices into a compact divine profile: Vidar is “the silent Áss,” possesses a thick shoe, is nearly as strong as Thor, and is a source of support to the gods in danger. Snorri also identifies his mother as the jötunn woman Gríðr and gives an etiological explanation of the thick shoe.

Calling Vidar simply “the god of vengeance” is serviceable but potentially misleading. Medieval sources make vengeance for Odin his defining action, and *Skáldskaparmál* calls him the gods’ “avenging Áss”; they do not establish a general cultic jurisdiction over every kind of revenge, justice, forests, survival, environmentalism, silence, or footwear.

### Name forms

- **Old Norse:** **Víðarr**, also normalized **Viðarr** in editions employing different orthographic conventions.
- **Icelandic:** **Víðarr** in normalized Old Norse; modern personal-name forms include **Víðar**.
- **Norwegian, Swedish, Danish, German, English, and French reception:** **Vidar**.
- **Older English antiquarian spellings:** **Vithar**, **Vitharr**, **Vidarr**, **Vídar**.
- **Runic form:** no securely identified contemporary runic inscription naming the god survives. A reconstructed younger-futhark spelling should not be presented as an attested divine inscription.

The first vowel is long: *Ví-*. The doubled final *-r* is the Old Norse masculine nominative ending as represented in normalized spelling.

### Etymology

The etymology is unresolved.

1. **“Wide ruler,” “wide warrior,” or “widely ruling one.”** The first element is commonly connected with Old Norse *víðr*, “wide, broad.” The second has been associated with an agent or warrior/ruler element ultimately related to a host or army. Simek and Orchard accordingly print cautious meanings such as “wide ruler?” The question mark matters.

2. **A wood or forest compound.** Some interpreters connect the first element with Germanic words for “wood,” encouraged by *Grímnismál*’s description of his overgrown home, Víði. This may be etymology influencing interpretation or interpretation influencing etymology; it is not settled.

3. **Dumézil’s comparison with Viṣṇu.** Georges Dumézil emphasized a “wide” root and compared Vidar’s decisive auxiliary role beside Thor with Viṣṇu’s relationship to Indra. This is **modern comparative reconstruction**, not an attested Norse identification and not a demonstrated direct historical borrowing. Dumézil’s full Vidar study appeared in 1965.

No medieval source glosses the name. Popular translations such as “forest warrior,” “silent forest god,” and “wide ruler of the renewed world” should therefore be labeled modern interpretations.

### Epithets and poetic designations

The following are found in, or generated by, Snorri’s *Edda*:

- **inn þǫgli áss / hinn þögli áss** — “the silent Áss.”  
  **Later canonical elaboration:** *Gylfaginning* and *Skáldskaparmál*. The Eddic poems do not explicitly call him mute or silent.

- **eigandi járnskós** — “possessor of the iron shoe.”  
  **Later learned systematization:** *Skáldskaparmál*. *Gylfaginning* initially calls the shoe thick, then explains it as assembled from leather scraps; manuscript and editorial traditions have consequently produced both “iron shoe” and “thick shoe” in later retellings.

- **óvinr ok bani Fenrisúlfs** — “enemy and slayer of Fenrir/Fenris-wolf.”  
  **Securely attested narrative role:** summarized by *Skáldskaparmál* from the Ragnarök tradition.

- **hefniáss goðanna** — approximately “the gods’ avenging Áss.”  
  **Later canonical characterization:** *Skáldskaparmál*.

- **byggviáss föður-tópta** — “the Áss who inhabits his father’s homesteads/estates.”  
  **Later canonical characterization:** *Skáldskaparmál*. This may relate to the post-Ragnarök inheritance but does not prove that Vidar becomes king of the renewed cosmos.

- **sonr Óðins; bróðir Ása** — “son of Odin; brother of the Æsir.”  
  Genealogical formulas in *Skáldskaparmál*.

### Silence

Vidar’s silence is **Snorrian**, not universal across the corpus. *Lokasenna* narrates him acting without preserving a speech by him, but absence of dialogue is not itself an epithet. Snorri explicitly calls him silent.

Modern explanations include ritual silence before vengeance, a vow comparable to Váli’s abstentions before avenging Baldr, the stillness of wilderness, reserve before eschatological action, and a structural opposition to noisy Thor. None is stated by a medieval source. Vidar should not be called congenitally mute: the texts never say he cannot speak.

### Cross-cultural equations

No ancient or medieval *interpretatio romana*, *interpretatio graeca*, Christian demonization, or securely documented Germanic equation identifies Vidar with another god. Dumézil’s comparison with Viṣṇu is typological, not a historical syncretism. Rudolf Steiner’s equation of Vidar’s future activity with renewed perception of the etheric Christ is a twentieth-century Anthroposophical construction.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The Codex Regius and the problem of dating

GKS 2365 4to is a small Icelandic vellum codex now held by the Árni Magnússon Institute in Reykjavík. Palaeography, orthography, and language—not a scribal colophon—place it in the second half of the thirteenth century; circa 1270 is conventional. The manuscript was in the Danish Royal Library from 1662 until its return to Iceland in 1971. Its modern electronic edition provides facsimile, diplomatic, normalized, and linguistically annotated texts. The manuscript’s date is not the date of every poem: the lays preserve traditional material of different ages, but assigning them precise Viking-Age dates is often impossible. [Codex Regius electronic edition](https://eae.ku.dk/q?p=eae%2Fvols%2Ftext%2F1)

### Primary poetic texts

#### *Grímnismál* 17

**Early poetic tradition; physical witness circa 1270.**

Odin, speaking as Grímnir, describes the dwellings of the gods. Vidar’s country, **Víði**, is said to be filled with brushwood and tall grass. The stanza’s difficult final lines present “the son” as prepared to avenge his father, apparently from horseback or after descending from a horse depending on the reading and translation.

What it establishes:

- Vidar has a named divine dwelling.
- Its salient landscape is uncultivated or overgrown.
- He is Odin’s son and future avenger.

What it does not establish:

- A historical forest cult.
- That he is a god of ecology, hunting, or agriculture.
- That Víði was an earthly sanctuary.

#### *Vafþrúðnismál* 51 and 53

**Early poetic tradition; physical witness circa 1270, with partial preservation elsewhere.**

During Odin’s wisdom contest with Vafþrúðnir, the giant states that **Vidar and Váli will inhabit the gods’ sanctuaries after Surtr’s fire has subsided**. He also predicts that Vidar will avenge Odin by **cleaving or splitting Fenrir’s cold jaws** in combat.

This is the clearest early poetic witness for Vidar as both avenger and survivor. It provides the jaw-breaking version but says nothing about a special shoe or leather scraps.

#### *Vǫluspá* 54–55 in conventional numbering

**Early poetic tradition; surviving Codex Regius text circa 1270, with a variant version in Hauksbók.**

After Fenrir kills Odin, the “great son of the Victory-Father” comes against the beast. Vidar causes a sword to stand in or penetrate the heart of Hveðrungr’s son—Fenrir—and thereby avenges his father.

This version is materially different from the jaw-rending story:

- *Vǫluspá*: a weapon penetrates the heart.
- *Vafþrúðnismál*: Vidar cleaves the jaws.
- Snorri: he pins and tears open the jaws, followed in some readings by a sword thrust.

The variations must not be harmonized into a single original scene. The Old Norse stanza and manuscript variants also create difficulties over whether the action is performed “with his hand” or “with his hands.” A comparative presentation of the manuscripts is available in the [Comparative *Vǫluspá*](https://www.mimisbrunnr.info/cv-st-55-57).

#### *Lokasenna* 9–11

**Early poetic tradition; physical witness circa 1270.**

At Ægir’s feast Loki accuses the assembled gods of denying him hospitality. Odin orders Vidar to rise and let “the wolf’s father”—Loki—have a seat, lest Loki speak hostile words. Vidar does so and pours Loki a drink. Loki then greets or toasts the Æsir before beginning his sustained abuse.

This is Vidar’s only surviving non-eschatological action. It does not show him as mute; it shows him acting without quoted speech. Dumézil made much of the scene, interpreting Vidar as controlling or creating ritual-social space, but that is a structural reading, not the poem’s explicit explanation.

### Snorri Sturluson’s *Edda*

Composed in Iceland around **1220**, the *Edda* survives in manuscripts copied later in the thirteenth and fourteenth centuries. It is indispensable but not a transparent transcript of pre-Christian religion: Snorri was a Christian antiquarian, poet, mythographer, and systematizer explaining traditional poetic diction.

#### *Gylfaginning* 29

**Later canonical elaboration.**

Vidar is introduced as the silent Áss. He has a thick shoe, is nearly as strong as Thor, and is said to provide great support to the gods in every danger. The last claim is not illustrated by surviving myths outside Ragnarök.

#### *Gylfaginning* 51

**Later canonical elaboration of Ragnarök.**

After Fenrir swallows Odin, Vidar advances. He places the foot wearing the thick shoe on Fenrir’s lower jaw, grasps the upper jaw, and tears the mouth apart. Snorri explains that the shoe is assembled from the triangular scraps people cut from the toe and heel of shoes. He adds that anyone concerned to assist the Æsir should throw such scraps away so that they accrue to Vidar’s shoe.

This is the only source for the leather-scrap explanation. It is evidence for a medieval story about an accumulation of scraps; it is not independent archaeological evidence of an actual pre-Christian cobblers’ rite.

#### *Gylfaginning* 53

**Later canonical elaboration.**

Vidar and Váli survive because neither sea nor Surtr’s fire harms them. They dwell on Iðavöllr after Ragnarök. Móði and Magni join them and inherit Mjǫllnir; Baldr and Höðr return from Hel. Snorri has thus created a more fully populated post-apocalyptic divine society than *Vafþrúðnismál* supplies.

#### *Skáldskaparmál*

**Later canonical and learned systematization.**

The work supplies Vidar’s kennings, calls his shoe iron, and identifies Gríðr as “mother of Vidar the Silent” when she equips Thor for his journey to Geirrøðr. Its lists establish medieval knowledge of the figure but cannot automatically be treated as independent pagan-era poems. The standard critical edition is Anthony Faulkes’s [*Skáldskaparmál*](https://vsnr.org/editions/snorri-sturluson-edda-skaldskaparmal/); Faulkes’s edition of [*Prologue and Gylfaginning*](https://vsnr.org/editions/snorri-sturluson-edda-prologue-and-gylfaginning/) is likewise foundational.

### Nafnaþulur and skaldic corpus

Vidar occurs among divine names in the *þulur* transmitted with *Skáldskaparmál*. These are learned name lists, useful for medieval nomenclature but not proof of an active cult.

The surviving independent skaldic corpus provides no clear early poem narrating Vidar’s deeds. Apparent forms of *viðarr* may instead be ordinary vocabulary or scribal confusion with the god’s name; the Skaldic Project explicitly notes one such textual problem. [Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages](https://skaldic.org/m.php?i=1093&p=verse&v=t&x=0)

### Sources that do not attest Vidar

A deliberate negative corpus check is important:

- **Tacitus, *Germania* (c. 98 CE):** no Vidar.
- **The Merseburg charms (tenth-century manuscript):** no Vidar.
- **Adam of Bremen, *Gesta Hammaburgensis* (c. 1070s):** no Vidar.
- **Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum* (c. 1200):** no recognizable Vidar narrative.
- **Snorri, *Heimskringla*:** no securely identifiable Vidar cult or independent Vidar myth.
- ***Vǫlsunga saga*, the *Nibelungenlied*, and Wagner’s *Ring*:** no Vidar character.
- **Runic inscriptions:** no secure invocation or dedication.
- **Theophoric place-names:** none generally accepted.

Accordingly, Vidar cannot securely be projected back into the pan-Germanic pantheon of the Roman Iron Age. He may be older than the surviving Icelandic evidence, but that remains an inference.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Secure attributes

- Male Æsir.
- Son and avenger of Odin.
- Killer of Fenrir.
- Survivor of Ragnarök.
- Occupant of Víði.
- Exceptional strength—“next to Thor”—according to Snorri.
- Thick or iron shoe in Snorri.
- Silence as a Snorrian epithet.

### Domains claimed in later literature

“Vengeance” is well grounded as a narrative specialization. “Strength” is Snorrian. “Silence,” “resilience,” “survival,” “forests,” “regeneration,” “justice,” “boundaries,” and “environmental protection” are common modern abstractions from his story, not documented ancient cult portfolios.

No medieval evidence associates him with:

- a planet, constellation, weekday, month, number, or color;
- a sacred animal distinct from his enemy Fenrir;
- a sacred plant or tree;
- a festival or calendar date;
- a named sword;
- runes peculiar to him;
- judicial oaths or legal assemblies.

### The shoe

The sources produce three related but not identical descriptions:

1. **Thick shoe:** *Gylfaginning*’s introductory notice.
2. **Scrap-built shoe:** Snorri’s Ragnarök narrative.
3. **Iron shoe:** *Skáldskaparmál*’s kenning list.

Nothing in *Vǫluspá* or *Vafþrúðnismál* mentions a shoe. The common modern image of a colossal boot made over the entire duration of world history comes from Snorri and later illustration.

Dumézil argued that discarding scraps for Vidar’s shoe may preserve ritual practice and compared it with other ritual uses of footwear and silence. Yet Snorri’s wording can equally be read as mythic etiquette or an antiquarian explanation. No leather deposit, workshop practice, or ritual text independently confirms it.

### Gosforth Cross

The Gosforth Cross stands in St Mary’s churchyard, Gosforth, Cumbria. It is usually dated to the **first half of the tenth century** and combines Christian and Scandinavian visual vocabularies. One carving shows a human confronting a great open-mouthed beast, with a foot placed at or within its jaws.

A long-established reading identifies the figure as Vidar defeating Fenrir. If correct, it predates the surviving Eddic manuscripts by roughly three centuries and demonstrates circulation of the jaw-combat story in Viking-settled northern England.

The identification remains contested:

- The monument supplies no identifying inscription.
- Beast-combat and mouth-of-hell imagery also occur in Christian art.
- The whole cross is a Christian monument capable of assimilating pagan-looking motifs.
- Scholars disagree whether the scene represents Vidar and Fenrir, Odin and Fenrir, Christ defeating Satan or Death, or a deliberately multivalent monster-conquest image.

Anders Hultgård’s recent review stresses that proposed Fenrir identifications in early imagery are often less secure than summaries imply. Victoria Thompson’s Christian reading similarly cautions against automatically assigning every ambiguous beast to Norse mythology. The responsible label is therefore **probable or possible regional iconographic reception**, not certain portraiture. [Oxford study of Ragnarök iconography](https://academic.oup.com/book/44543/chapter-abstract/376520861) [Study emphasizing the cross’s Christian setting](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/12/4/228/htm)

### Later images

- **Lorenz Frølich, late nineteenth century:** Romantic-national visualizations helped standardize the powerful booted avenger.
- **W. G. Collingwood, 1908:** depicted Vidar standing between Fenrir’s jaws and swinging a sword, explicitly inspired by the Gosforth monument. This hybridizes textual variants and archaeological interpretation. [Collingwood image record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AVidar_by_Collingwood.jpg)
- Modern fantasy art commonly adds armor, forest symbolism, axes, green coloring, or magical runes. These are modern inventions unless tied to a particular work.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

| Relationship | Evidence | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| Father: **Odin** | *Grímnismál*, *Vǫluspá*, *Vafþrúðnismál*; Snorri | Securely attested medieval tradition |
| Mother: **Gríðr** | *Skáldskaparmál* | Snorrian elaboration; no Eddic poem names his mother |
| Brother/companion survivor: **Váli** | *Vafþrúðnismál* 51; *Gylfaginning* 53 | Securely attested pairing |
| Broad kinship: “brother of the Æsir” | *Skáldskaparmál* | Learned poetic formula |
| Consort | None | No medieval evidence |
| Children | None | No medieval evidence |
| Mount | A horse may be implied in *Grímnismál* 17 | Textually uncertain; no named mount |

Gríðr is a jötunn woman who separately aids Thor by lending him a belt, iron gloves, and the staff Gríðarvǫlr. The text does not narrate Vidar meeting her or Thor.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Odin — obeys Odin’s instruction at Ægir’s feast.** Odin tells Vidar to rise, make room for Loki, and pour him a drink; Vidar complies. *Lokasenna* 9–11, early poetic tradition preserved circa 1270.

- **Odin — avenges his death.** Fenrir kills Odin; Vidar immediately attacks and kills Fenrir. *Vǫluspá* 54–55 and *Vafþrúðnismál* 53, early poetic tradition; expanded in *Gylfaginning* 51, circa 1220.

- **Fenrir — combat and killing.** Vidar pierces the wolf’s heart in *Vǫluspá*, cleaves its jaws in *Vafþrúðnismál*, and pins and tears open its mouth in Snorri. Early poetic variants and later canonical elaboration.

- **Loki — ritual hospitality.** Vidar yields his seat and pours Loki a drink after Loki demands admission to Ægir’s feast. *Lokasenna* 9–11. The poem preserves no reply by Vidar.

- **Ægir — appears as an attendant or guest in Ægir’s hall.** Vidar participates in the feast hosted by Ægir but no direct dialogue between them survives. *Lokasenna* prose frame and stanzas 9–11. Shared scene, not a developed relationship.

- **Váli — jointly survives Ragnarök.** The brothers inhabit the gods’ sanctuaries after Surtr’s fire. *Vafþrúðnismál* 51; elaborated in *Gylfaginning* 53. No conversation or joint combat is recorded.

- **Móði and Magni — shares the renewed divine world.** Snorri places them with Vidar and Váli on Iðavöllr after Ragnarök. *Gylfaginning* 53. Joint appearance only.

- **Baldr and Höðr — shares the renewed world after their return.** *Gylfaginning* 53. Joint appearance only; no direct exchange.

- **Thor — compared in strength.** Snorri says Vidar is nearly as strong as Thor. *Gylfaginning* 29. This is comparison, not an episode.

- **The Æsir collectively — described as their reserve support and avenger.** *Gylfaginning* 29 and *Skáldskaparmál*. No separate pre-Ragnarök rescue story survives.

No source records Vidar interacting with Frigg, Týr, Heimdallr, Freyr, Freyja, Njǫrðr, Hel, Surtr, Jǫrmungandr, or Gríðr. Genealogy and co-membership in a pantheon do not constitute meetings.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The avenging of Odin

The common core is concise: Fenrir kills Odin during Ragnarök; Odin’s son Vidar kills Fenrir and avenges him.

#### Version A: the heart-thrust

In *Vǫluspá*, Vidar advances against Fenrir and makes a blade stand in the wolf’s heart. No special shoe appears.

**Tier:** early poetic tradition.

#### Version B: the cleft jaws

In *Vafþrúðnismál*, Vidar cleaves Fenrir’s cold jaws. The exact mechanics are not developed.

**Tier:** early poetic variant.

#### Version C: the thick shoe and torn mouth

Snorri combines the jaw motif with a physical explanation. Vidar steps on the lower jaw with his thick shoe, grasps the upper, and tears the wolf’s mouth apart. The shoe consists of toe-and-heel scraps collected whenever people cut shoes. Snorri also knows the sword tradition, producing later hybrid retellings in which Vidar first holds the jaws apart and then stabs the wolf.

**Tier:** later canonical elaboration.

The variants may reflect separate oral forms, differing poetic compression, or Snorri’s synthesis. There is insufficient evidence to identify one as the original.

### Survival and the renewed world

*Vafþrúðnismál* says Vidar and Váli live in the gods’ sanctuaries after Surtr’s fire. Snorri expands this into a renewed gathering at Iðavöllr with Móði, Magni, Baldr, and Höðr.

Vidar’s survival is therefore old within the extant poetic record. Claims that he becomes the new All-Father, king of the surviving gods, or ruler of a new pantheon are **modern inference or invention**. No medieval text crowns him or gives him legislative or creative activity after the catastrophe.

### The seat and cup at Ægir’s feast

Loki returns to Ægir’s hall after killing the servant Fimafeng and being driven away. He demands recognition of Odin’s sworn brotherhood with him. Odin directs Vidar to yield a seat and pour Loki ale. Vidar’s act restores formal hospitality long enough for Loki to begin insulting the divine company.

Dumézil interpreted the episode together with Vidar’s foot placement at Ragnarök: in one scene he creates room in ordered social space; in the other his foot fixes a chaotic monster. This is an ingenious **modern structural interpretation**, not an explanation supplied by *Lokasenna*.

### The leather scraps

Snorri’s instruction that people should discard cut-off shoe scraps because they contribute to Vidar’s eschatological shoe resembles an etiological custom. Whether Snorri reports an actual practice, a storyteller’s explanation of waste disposal, or his own mythographic elaboration cannot be established.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Material and institutional evidence

There is no secure archaeological or epigraphic evidence for an organized Vidar cult:

- no named temple;
- no altar or votive inscription;
- no cult statue securely identified;
- no priesthood;
- no recorded festival;
- no sacrificial prescription;
- no oracle;
- no securely theophoric settlement name;
- no runic prayer;
- no demonstrable personal names invoking the god during the pagan period.

This absence is significant. It distinguishes Vidar from figures such as Thor, Odin, Freyr, Ullr, and Njǫrðr, who have varying combinations of place-name, literary, iconographic, amuletic, or cult-site evidence.

### The shoe-scrap custom

The one quasi-ritual notice is Snorri’s statement that people who wish to aid the Æsir should throw away the scraps cut from shoes so that they become material for Vidar’s thick shoe.

Possible readings:

- **Textually attested medieval custom claim:** Snorri knew a practice accompanied by a mythic explanation.
- **Folkloric etiological rule:** the tale explains what to do with shoe scraps.
- **Mythographic elaboration:** Snorri converted the idea of a special shoe into collective human assistance.
- **Modern ritual reconstruction:** present-day Heathens may deliberately save or dedicate leather scraps, but this does not prove continuity.

### Sacrifice and contested practices

No source associates Vidar with human sacrifice, animal sacrifice, sexual rites, temple prostitution, ritual violence, or sacred kingship. General evidence about sacrifices to Norse gods cannot be reassigned to him without a source. No documented medieval suppression campaign singles out his worship.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Possible pre-Christian formation

The poetic economy of *Vǫluspá*, *Vafþrúðnismál*, and *Grímnismál* suggests that audiences already knew Vidar’s identity and revenge role. This supports an oral prehistory, probably within the late pre-Christian or conversion-period Scandinavian tradition. Its depth cannot be measured. There is no secure Proto-Germanic cognate or Roman-period witness.

### Tenth-century Anglo-Scandinavian possibility

If the Gosforth beast-combat scene is Vidar and Fenrir, the myth was visually adapted in tenth-century Cumbria and placed in dialogue with Christian victory over death or Satan. Because the identification is disputed, it cannot carry the entire argument for early dating.

### Thirteenth-century textual consolidation

Around 1220 Snorri systematized the god:

- silent;
- nearly as strong as Thor;
- owner of a thick shoe;
- reserve defender of the Æsir;
- son of Gríðr;
- slayer of Fenrir by jaw-rending;
- survivor of Ragnarök.

The Codex Regius, copied later in the thirteenth century, preserved the principal poetic witnesses. Snorri’s profile subsequently became more influential in handbooks than the poems’ inconsistencies.

### Christian transmission

Vidar’s record survives because Christian Icelandic scribes copied mythological poetry and Snorri composed a guide to traditional poetics. There is no evidence that Christian authorities conducted a campaign specifically against Vidar. His cult—if a distinct cult existed—cannot be traced through conversion, clandestine survival, or folklore.

### Antiquarian rediscovery

Early-modern Scandinavian antiquarians copied, edited, and translated the Eddas. As printed mythology became available, Vidar moved from a small medieval textual dossier into systematic national mythologies.

### Nineteenth-century mythography

Romantic and nationalist artists made him visually concrete: an armored, muscular avenger with a colossal shoe. These pictures often combined *Vǫluspá*, Snorri, and the Gosforth interpretation. Unlike Wagner’s transformed Odin/Wotan, Thor/Donner, and other figures, Vidar does **not** appear as a character in *Der Ring des Nibelungen*. Assigning a Wagnerian Vidar role to Siegfried or another character is comparative interpretation, not textual presence.

### Twentieth- and twenty-first-century revival

Vidar entered modern Heathen devotional writing as a patron of endurance, quiet strength, forests, vengeance, recovery from trauma, boundary-setting, or service performed without recognition. These functions are generally extrapolated from the medieval stories. No continuous initiatory lineage connects them to Viking-Age practice.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Anthroposophy

In lectures delivered in Oslo in June **1910**, Rudolf Steiner transformed Vidar into a prophetic spiritual power. Steiner said that Vidar would overcome the Fenris Wolf understood as decadent or confused ancient clairvoyance and would enable future perception of Christ in the etheric world. He further asserted that Vidar had been concealed in mysteries and occult schools and referred to an unidentified image near Cologne.

These claims are **modern occult revelation**, not medieval tradition or archaeology. They remain influential in Anthroposophical circles. [Steiner’s 1910 lecture](https://rsarchive.org/Lectures/GA121/English/RSP1970/19100617p02.html)

### Comparative mythology

Dumézil’s 1965 “Le dieu Scandinave Vidarr” remains the most concentrated scholarly attempt to interpret the whole dossier. He linked:

- silence and ritual abstention;
- the act of yielding spatial place in *Lokasenna*;
- the foot’s spatial control over Fenrir;
- the preserved shoe-scrap instruction;
- Vidar’s relation to Thor;
- comparison with Viṣṇu and Indra.

The article openly begins from the fact that Vidar’s evidence is “thin but precise.” Its structural unity is a scholarly construction, though one built from real textual details. [Dumézil’s article](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rhr_0035-1423_1965_num_168_1_8204)

### Reconstructionist and neopagan religion

Modern Ásatrú and Heathen communities sometimes honor Vidar through offerings, prayers, environmental work, vows of silence, leather objects, boots, or meditations on endurance and revenge. These are living religious practices and should be reported as such. They are not archaeologically demonstrated continuations of a historical Vidar cult.

Modern Heathenry includes universalist, inclusive, ethnic, folkish, and openly racist movements. Germanic divine names have been appropriated by white-nationalist and neo-Nazi currents, but no evidence located makes Vidar, as distinct from Odin, Thor, runes, or generalized “Aryan” symbolism, a major historic extremist emblem. A dossier should therefore neither omit the broader political setting nor falsely assign Vidar a specific extremist cult unsupported by documentation.

### Literature and visual culture

- **W. G. Collingwood, 1908:** the best-known English illustration, based on the Gosforth reading.
- Modern retellings commonly call him the “Silent Avenger,” a convenient post-medieval title.
- Retellings frequently make him ruler of the post-Ragnarök world, although the Eddas only say he survives and inhabits the renewed divine realm.
- Fantasy works often recast Víði as an enchanted forest kingdom and the shoe as an indestructible magical artifact.

### Comics and games

Marvel’s Vidar is an adaptation rather than a faithful dossier of the medieval god. Appearances or cameos are catalogued from *Thor* no. 293 (1980), while *Thor Annual* no. 12 (1984) is sometimes treated as his first full appearance. Modern comics give him family relations, appearance, biography, and adventures unattested in medieval sources. [Marvel appearance catalogue](https://marvelreading.com/character/vidar)

In *God of War Ragnarök* (2022), Vidar is not an embodied narrative character; his name appears in the “Vidar’s Might” equipment set. Claims that Kratos replaces Vidar in the game’s theology are fan interpretation. [Game-related Vidar entry](https://godofwar.fandom.com/wiki/Vidar)

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Dating the poems

Older scholarship often assigned Eddic lays confident Viking-Age dates. Current practice is more cautious: language, meter, intertextuality, and religious content can suggest relative age, but oral transmission and thirteenth-century manuscripts prevent exact dating. “Tenth-century poem” is an estimate, not a physical attestation.

### Snorri’s reliability

Two extremes should be avoided:

- treating Snorri as a direct ethnographic reporter of pagan worship;
- dismissing every detail absent from surviving verse as his invention.

Snorri had access to poems and traditions now lost, but he also reconciled contradictions and created teachable systems. The thick shoe’s construction, Vidar’s near equality to Thor, Gríðr’s maternity, and the expanded survivor assembly are therefore historically attested **in thirteenth-century Iceland**, while their pre-Christian age remains uncertain.

### The two deaths of Fenrir

The sword-in-heart and split-jaw traditions are the central textual dispute. Possible explanations include:

- independent oral variants;
- metaphorical equivalence between “cleaving” jaws and killing with a sword;
- Snorri’s harmonization of more than one tradition;
- scribal or editorial instability in *Vǫluspá*.

There is no warrant for silently combining every detail.

### Gosforth

The Gosforth identification has shaped almost every modern visualization of Vidar. Yet an uninscribed human-and-beast carving cannot be identified solely because it resembles Snorri’s much later prose. Its Christian monumental setting and the availability of Christian monster-conquest imagery remain essential counter-evidence.

### Ritual silence

Jan de Vries and especially Dumézil connected Vidar’s silence with ritual preparation for vengeance, comparing Váli’s refusal to wash or comb his hair until Baldr is avenged. The comparison is suggestive, but the texts never say Vidar took a vow. “Silent” may instead express restraint, narrative inactivity, or an epithet whose explanatory myth is lost.

### The “forest god”

Víði’s overgrown landscape and a possible wood-etymology generated the theory that Vidar was originally a forest deity. Against it:

- no cult text calls him protector of forests;
- no sacred grove is dedicated to him;
- the etymology is disputed;
- divine dwellings often have descriptive landscapes without defining a god’s cultic function.

“Forest god” is consequently a reconstruction, not a securely attested classification.

### Indo-European comparison

Dumézil’s Vidar–Viṣṇu comparison belongs to twentieth-century structural Indo-European mythology. Similarity of a “wide” name, an auxiliary relationship to a stronger warrior, and decisive eschatological action is not proof of identity or direct descent. Later popular works sometimes promote this comparison as settled Proto-Indo-European religion; that overstates the evidence.

### Evidence that does not exist

The following frequently circulating claims cannot presently be established:

- Vidar was worshipped across all Germanic Europe.
- He had a Viking temple or priestly order.
- his rune was *Eihwaz*, *Isa*, *Tiwaz*, or another named rune;
- his sacred color was green, black, or brown;
- his holy day was 29 December or any weekday;
- he ruled justice, ecological balance, or all vengeance;
- his shoe had a proper name;
- he married or fathered a new divine race;
- he became king after Ragnarök;
- the Gosforth Cross unquestionably portrays him;
- shoe scraps were universally collected in historical Scandinavian worship.

## 10. Chronology

| Date or period | Attestation or development |
|---|---|
| Before c. 800 | No secure evidence. A Proto-Germanic Vidar is possible but unproved. |
| Viking Age, broadly c. 800–1050 | Probable oral development or circulation of the poetic Ragnarök traditions later preserved in Eddic verse. Exact dating is disputed. |
| First half of tenth century | Gosforth Cross erected in Cumbria. One beast-combat scene may depict Vidar splitting Fenrir’s jaws; identification contested. |
| Tenth–twelfth centuries | Proposed composition periods for *Vǫluspá*, *Vafþrúðnismál*, *Grímnismál*, and *Lokasenna* vary among scholars. These are reconstructed dates, not surviving manuscripts. |
| c. 1220 | Snorri composes the *Edda*, consolidating Vidar as the silent, strong god of the thick shoe and elaborating his killing of Fenrir. |
| Second half of thirteenth century, conventionally c. 1270 | GKS 2365 4to, Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda, is copied: earliest secure physical textual witness for the principal Vidar poems. |
| c. 1300–1350 | Major surviving manuscripts of Snorri’s *Edda* transmit the prose dossier and variant readings. |
| Seventeenth century | Eddic manuscripts enter learned Scandinavian collections; Codex Regius is given to the Danish king in 1662. |
| Eighteenth–nineteenth centuries | Printed editions and translations spread the name Vidar through European comparative mythology and Romantic art. |
| 1886 and after | Antiquarian interpretation of the Gosforth Cross develops; Ragnarök identifications become increasingly influential. |
| 1895 | Lorenz Frølich’s Eddic illustrations help establish the modern visual Vidar. |
| 1908 | W. G. Collingwood publishes his Gosforth-inspired image of Vidar fighting Fenrir. |
| 17 June 1910 | Rudolf Steiner lectures on Vidar as the future conqueror of corrupt clairvoyance and herald of etheric perception of Christ. |
| 1957 | Second edition of Jan de Vries’s *Altgermanische Religionsgeschichte* synthesizes the older religious-historical debate. |
| 1959 | Dumézil’s *Les dieux des Germains* develops the structural Indo-European framework. |
| 1965 | Dumézil publishes “Le dieu Scandinave Vidarr,” the classic dedicated comparative study. |
| 1980–1984 | Marvel Comics introduces and develops its adapted Vidar. |
| Late twentieth century | Vidar enters modern Heathen and neopagan devotional practice, with silence, resilience, vengeance, and environmental meanings increasingly emphasized. |
| 2005 | Anthony Faulkes’s second critical edition of *Edda: Prologue and Gylfaginning* appears. |
| 2007 | English translation of Rudolf Simek’s *Dictionary of Northern Mythology* provides a standard concise scholarly reference. |
| 2022 | Anders Hultgård’s *The End of the World in Scandinavian Mythology* re-examines Ragnarök imagery, including the uncertainty surrounding Fenrir identifications. *God of War Ragnarök* uses Vidar’s name for equipment rather than presenting him as a character. |
| 2024 | The scholarly electronic edition of Codex Regius makes facsimile, diplomatic, and normalized texts available together. |
| Present | Vidar remains a documented medieval literary god and a living object of modern Heathen devotion, occult reinterpretation, scholarship, art, and popular entertainment; no evidence demonstrates unbroken cult continuity. |

### Final assessment

Vidar’s securely reconstructable medieval identity is small but coherent: he is an Æsir, Odin’s son, associated with an overgrown dwelling, destined to avenge Odin by killing Fenrir, and destined to survive the world’s destruction alongside Váli. The poetic sources disagree over whether he pierces Fenrir’s heart or cleaves its jaws. Snorri makes him silent, nearly as strong as Thor, the owner of a thick shoe built from leather scraps, and the son of Gríðr. No independent evidence establishes a dedicated historical cult.

Almost everything beyond that core—forest lordship, a universal jurisdiction over vengeance or justice, ritual vows of silence, Indo-European equivalence with Viṣṇu, kingship after Ragnarök, esoteric guardianship of future Christianity, ecological patronage, named runes, colors, feast days, spouses, and descendants—belongs to scholarly reconstruction or modern religious and artistic reception. The history of Vidar is therefore not one of a richly documented public cult but of a compact poetic figure progressively expanded by Snorri, antiquarians, comparative mythologists, occultists, artists, contemporary Heathens, and popular media.
