# Audhumla: Research Dossier

**Class:** Primeval mythic cow and cosmogonic being. Snorri’s text does not call her a goddess, giantess, or object of worship.

**Earliest secure attestation:** *Gylfaginning*, chapter 6, in Snorri Sturluson’s *Edda*, composed in Iceland about 1220. The earliest surviving physical witness is probably *Codex Upsaliensis*, Uppsala University Library DG 11 4to, first quarter of the fourteenth century. No securely dated Viking-Age inscription, skaldic stanza, image, or cultic evidence names her.

**Central evidentiary finding:** The narrative of the primeval cow, her four milk-rivers, her feeding of Ymir, and her three-day licking of Búri from salty rime-stones is carried by *Gylfaginning* alone. A second medieval textual notice—the verse name-list *Nafnaþulur*, transmitted with some manuscripts of Snorri’s *Edda*—merely calls her the foremost or noblest of cows. Everything beyond those notices is interpretation, comparison, reception, or modern invention.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Medieval attestation]** Directly present in a medieval textual witness.
- **[Later canonical elaboration]** A later source that systematizes or expands inherited Norse material.
- **[Regional variant]** A geographically distinct historical version.
- **[Learned systematization]** An interpretation produced through medieval scholarship or ordering.
- **[Comparative reconstruction]** A modern scholarly inference from related traditions.
- **[Early-modern/modern reception]** Post-medieval art, literature, scholarship, or religious use.
- **[Modern invention]** A claim or attribute unsupported by medieval evidence and first documented in recent reception.
- **[Absence of evidence]** Not attested in the surveyed primary corpus.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

**[Medieval attestation]** In *Gylfaginning* 6, Auðhumla is a cow formed when primeval rime drips in Ginnungagap. Four streams or “milk-rivers” flow from her teats and feed Ymir. She feeds herself by licking salty rime-stones. Over three days this licking exposes a human-shaped being, Búri, ancestor of Borr and thus of Óðinn, Vili, and Vé. She is consequently a mediator between the two opposed divine genealogies: she sustains the progenitor of the frost-giants while uncovering the ancestor of the Æsir.

The text calls her simply *kýr sú*—“that cow”—not *áss*, *ásynja*, *goð*, *jötunn*, or another divine class. “Primeval cow” or “cosmogonic cow” is therefore more accurate than “goddess.” John Lindow’s useful description of her as joining the future opposing races is a modern structural interpretation, not a medieval title.

### Name forms

The principal forms transmitted or used in editions are:

- **Auðhumla** — the normalized form printed in Anthony Faulkes’s edition and in many Icelandic texts.
- **Auðumbla** — a widespread manuscript/editorial variant and the most common form in modern English reference works.
- **Auðumla** — a further reduced variant.
- **Audhumla, Audhumbla, Audumbla, Audumla** — modern forms replacing Old Norse **ð** with *d* or *dh*.
- Occasionally **Authumbla/Authumla** appears in Anglicized popular writing, reflecting an attempt to represent **ð** by *th*.

The Old Norse initial element **auð-** means “wealth, riches, abundance” and occurs in names and compounds. The second element has commonly been connected with a Germanic adjective reconstructed as something like **\*humala-**, “hornless, polled.” Thus the conventional gloss is “rich hornless cow,” “hornless cow rich in milk,” or, more loosely, “abundant hornless cow.” Rudolf Simek and John Lindow accept versions of this explanation, ultimately associated with Adolf Noreen’s 1918 study “Urkon Audhumla och några hennes språkliga släktingar.”

**[Scholarly interpretation]** The phrase “rich in milk” is partly contextual: *auðr* signifies wealth or abundance, while the extraordinary milk supply in the story makes dairy abundance an attractive implication. It is not a literal word-for-word rendering of an element meaning “milk.”

**[Disputed]** The precise historical formation of the second element and the relationship among *-humla*, *-umbla*, and *-umla* are not completely certain. Manuscript variation may reflect unfamiliarity with an already opaque name. “Hornless” is the prevailing explanation, not an independently supplied gloss in *Gylfaginning*.

Tacitus, *Germania* 5, says German cattle lacked their usual “forehead ornament,” normally understood as prominent horns. This makes a name referring to a polled cow locally intelligible, but Tacitus neither names Auðhumla nor records a primeval-cow myth. The connection is contextual evidence, not an attestation of her.

### Epithets and titles

**[Medieval attestation]** In the *Nafnaþulur* cow-list she is described as:

> *Auðhumla er œzt kúa*  
> “Auðhumla is foremost/noblest of cows.”

This is the only medieval epithet or ranking securely associated with her. “Cosmic cow,” “Great Mother Cow,” “cow of creation,” “ice cow,” “mother of manifestation,” and “goddess of nourishment” are modern descriptive or religious titles.

There is no attested Old Norse cult title, byname tied to a locality, or interpretatio identifying her with a foreign goddess.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### *Gylfaginning*

Snorri Sturluson’s *Edda* was probably assembled around 1220 as a work on poetry, mythological language, and traditional narrative. Its attribution to Snorri is stated explicitly only in the Uppsala manuscript, although the attribution is broadly accepted. The principal medieval witnesses preserve divergent compilations rather than a single authorial copy:

- **U — DG 11 4to, Codex Upsaliensis:** first quarter of the fourteenth century; probably the oldest substantially complete manuscript. Its text is shorter and textually independent in important respects.
- **R — GKS 2367 4to, Codex Regius of Snorri’s *Edda*:** first half of the fourteenth century; the usual basis of modern critical editions. This is not GKS 2365 4to, the different *Codex Regius* containing most of the Poetic Edda.
- **W — AM 242 fol., Codex Wormianus:** approximately the middle of the fourteenth century.
- **T — Utrecht University Library MS 1374, Codex Trajectinus:** a paper manuscript from about 1600, generally regarded as a copy of a lost medieval exemplar related to R.
- Fragmentary witnesses A, B, C, and AM 756 4to preserve other portions of the work.

The Viking Society’s edition describes DG 11 as probably the earliest surviving witness and emphasizes that its shorter version differs markedly from the other redactions. GKS 2367 is catalogued as an early-fourteenth-century Icelandic manuscript containing *Gylfaginning*, *Skáldskaparmál*, þulur, and *Háttatal*.

### The complete narrative

**[Medieval attestation: *Gylfaginning* 6]** Gangleri asks where Ymir lived and what sustained him. High replies:

> *Næst var þat, þá er hrímit draup, at þar varð af kýr sú, er Auðhumla hét, en fjórar mjólkár runnu ór spenum hennar, ok fæddi hon Ymi.*

“Next, when the rime dripped, from it came the cow called Auðhumla; four rivers of milk ran from her teats, and she fed Ymir.”

Asked what sustained the cow, High continues:

> *Hon sleikði hrímsteinana, er saltir váru …*

“She licked the rime-stones, which were salty.”

On the first evening human hair emerged from the stone; on the second day a human head; on the third day a complete man. He was named Búri and described as handsome, large, and powerful. Búri begot Borr; Borr and the giant’s daughter Bestla produced Óðinn, Vili, and Vé.

This is all that the medieval narrative says about Auðhumla. It does not state:

- that she has parents, mate, or offspring;
- that Búri is her biological son;
- that she consciously creates him;
- that she is a goddess, an incarnation of Earth, or the Milky Way;
- that she is hornless in appearance;
- that the four streams flow to four cardinal directions;
- what becomes of her after Búri emerges;
- that she dies, is sacrificed, or is transformed into part of the cosmos.

### *Nafnaþulur*

**[Medieval attestation, textually secondary]** The *Nafnaþulur*, versified lists of poetic terms transmitted in association with *Skáldskaparmál*, include Auðhumla as the only individually named cow and call her *œzt kúa*. Faulkes prints the notice in his edition of *Skáldskaparmál*. The þulur are valuable evidence for medieval naming and poetic classification, but their relationship to Snorri’s original project is complicated, and they should not automatically be treated as authorial commentary by Snorri.

### Negative corpus audit

**[Absence of evidence]**

- **Poetic Edda:** Auðhumla is absent from the poems in GKS 2365 4to and related Eddic witnesses. Neither Carolyne Larrington’s nor Henry Adams Bellows’s translation supplies an independent Auðhumla passage.
- **Vafþrúðnismál:** stanza 31 supplies the older poetic statement that poisonous or icy drops from Élivágar grew until a giant arose. Snorri quotes this material near his account of Ymir, but the poem contains no cow, milk-rivers, salt-lick, or Búri episode.
- **Vǫluspá:** contains cosmogonic material and the making of the world from Ymir, but no Auðhumla.
- **Skaldic corpus:** no securely dated independent Viking-Age or medieval skaldic stanza naming Auðhumla has been identified. Her *Nafnaþulur* listing is not evidence of a pre-Snorrian skaldic poem about her.
- **Heimskringla:** no independent Auðhumla narrative.
- **Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum*:** no Auðhumla.
- **Tacitus, *Germania*:** hornless-looking German cattle and the Nerthus procession are relevant comparative data, but Auðhumla is not named.
- **Adam of Bremen:** no Auðhumla.
- **Merseburg charms:** no Auðhumla.
- **Runic inscriptions:** no accepted inscription in the Scandinavian Runic Text Database securely names her.
- **Vǫlsunga saga, Nibelungenlied, and Wagner’s *Ring* texts:** no Auðhumla episode.
- No inscription, theophoric personal name, place-name, law, prayer, charm, or saga testimony demonstrates a cult of Auðhumla.

Thus “the *Gylfaginning* account alone” is substantially correct for the story. The *Nafnaþulur* notice proves circulation of her name within the wider manuscript tradition but adds no narrative.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain and functions

**[Medieval attestation]** Auðhumla performs two functions:

1. **Nourishment:** Four milk-streams sustain Ymir when no ordinary ecosystem yet exists.
2. **Disclosure or generation:** By licking the salty rime-stones she gradually exposes Búri.

The text does not explain whether Búri already existed inside the stone or was physically generated through her licking. Translations such as “licked Búri out,” “licked him free,” and “brought him forth” encode different interpretations of the same terse description.

Her other attributes are minimal:

- sex: grammatically and biologically female, a milk-giving cow;
- environment: dripping rime and salty stones in Ginnungagap;
- action: lactation and licking;
- temporal number: three days of progressive emergence;
- productive number: four milk-rivers.

No color, size, horns, markings, celestial association, feast day, sacred plant, weapon, tool, dwelling, personality, speech, or moral character is supplied.

### The four milk-rivers

**[Medieval attestation]** *Fjórar mjólkár* means four milk-rivers or four streams of milk. The passage does not name them or say where they flow.

**[Learned-systematization hypothesis]** Scholars have compared the four streams with the four rivers of biblical Paradise in Genesis 2:10–14. Simek considers the numerical resemblance potentially attributable to Snorri’s clerical education rather than proof of a shared ancient Near Eastern “Great Mother” complex. This is plausible but unprovable: four also naturally expresses spatial completeness.

**[Modern interpretation]** Claims that the streams explicitly establish the four cardinal directions, become terrestrial rivers, represent the four elements, or form the Milky Way do not occur in the medieval text.

### Salt

Salt is necessary to the story because Auðhumla feeds on salty rime-stones. It also makes practical sense in relation to cattle seeking mineral salt. No medieval source, however, turns salt into her sacred substance or prescribes salt offerings to her.

### Historical iconography

**[Absence of evidence]** No Viking-Age carving, picture-stone, pendant, bracteate, tapestry, or runestone has been securely identified as Auðhumla. An isolated bovine image cannot be given her name without narrative identifiers.

The earliest securely identifiable surviving image presently documented is post-medieval:

- **Jakob Sigurðsson, “Auðhumbla and Búri,” 1760**, ink and colored wash, NKS 1867 4to, fol. 95r, Royal Danish Library. The cow licks a partly emerging Búri from a block. The manuscript was begun by the priest Ólafur Brynjúlfsson; the illustration is eighteenth-century reception, not Viking-Age iconography.
- A related image survives in the Icelandic manuscript **SÁM 66**, likewise eighteenth century.
- **Nicolai Abraham Abildgaard, *Ymer og Audhumbla***, approximately 1790, belongs to the Scandinavian neoclassical recovery of Norse myth. It pictures Ymir drinking while the cow exposes Búri. Its naturalistic bovine appearance is the artist’s design, not a recovered ancient iconographic canon.

Modern images commonly add immense size, white hide, horns, an icy landscape, cosmic light, or four visible streams. None is textually mandatory.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Origin and status

**[Medieval attestation]** Auðhumla comes into being from the dripping rime: *þá er hrímit draup, at þar varð af kýr sú*. No father, mother, maker, or birth process is identified. She appears after or alongside Ymir within Snorri’s sequence.

She has no recorded mate, offspring, siblings, or descendants. Calling Búri her “son” is an interpretive modernization. Snorri says he comes from the stone as she licks it; he does not say that she conceives or bears him.

### Genealogical consequences

Although she is not inserted into the Æsir’s biological genealogy, her action enables it:

> Auðhumla’s licking → Búri → Borr + Bestla → Óðinn, Vili, Vé.

Her milk separately preserves:

> Auðhumla’s milk → Ymir → frost-giant line.

Lindow therefore observes that she links the two future enemy populations. This is a structural relation, not a claim that she is their common biological mother.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Ymir — nourishes him with four streams of milk.** *Gylfaginning* 6, composed about 1220; earliest manuscripts early fourteenth century. **[Medieval attestation]**
- **Búri — licks the salty rime-stones for three days, during which his hair, head, and whole body emerge.** *Gylfaginning* 6, about 1220. **[Medieval attestation]**

### Named beings with no direct interaction

- **Óðinn, Vili, and Vé:** Auðhumla enables the emergence of their grandfather Búri, but she never meets or speaks with them.
- **Borr and Bestla:** genealogically downstream; no scene with Auðhumla.
- **Nerthus, Freyja, Frigg, Jörð, Hathor, Isis, Hera, Gāw ī Ēwdād/Gavaevodata, and Vedic cows:** later comparison only; no traditional encounter or ancient equation.
- **Bergelmir and the later giants:** no interaction.
- **Surtr or personified Muspell/Niflheim:** no interaction; the environmental forces surrounding her are not presented as a meeting between named persons.

There is no combat, seduction, judgment, covenant, theft, punishment, or joint cult involving Auðhumla in the surviving medieval record.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The formation of Auðhumla and nourishment of Ymir

**[Medieval attestation]** In Snorri’s sequence, icy Élivágar produce layers of rime in Ginnungagap. Heat from Muspell causes the rime to melt and drip. The quickened drops produce Ymir; the next dripping produces Auðhumla. Four milk-rivers flow from her teats, feeding him.

Snorri supports the emergence of the giant with quotations from *Vafþrúðnismál* and a short *Vǫluspá*, but he quotes no verse for Auðhumla. That asymmetry matters. It may mean Snorri knew a prose tradition, a lost verse, or fashioned an explanation for how Ymir survived. The evidence does not decide among those possibilities.

Margaret Clunies Ross notes the episode’s narrative utility: it answers how the first living being could be nourished when no mothers or ordinary food system existed. Utility does not prove invention, but it helps explain why the cow occupies this exact point in Snorri’s cosmology.

### Licking Búri from the rime-stones

**[Medieval attestation]** Auðhumla eats by licking salty stones. The action creates a measured progression:

- evening of day one: human hair appears;
- day two: a human head;
- day three: a whole man.

Búri is beautiful, great, and strong. He begets Borr, whose marriage to Bestla unites the new paternal line with giant ancestry. Their sons become the gods who kill Ymir and make the world from his body.

Auðhumla herself disappears from the narrative after Búri’s emergence. No source records her fate during Ymir’s killing or the flood of his blood.

### Developmental interpretation

Three positions should be kept distinct:

1. **Inherited pre-Christian tradition.** The unusual proper name, the pastoral logic of milk and salt, and broad Indo-European bovine parallels make it possible that Snorri preserved an older myth otherwise lost.
2. **Snorrian organization or elaboration.** Auðhumla lacks independent poetic support, while Snorri embeds her inside a learned, coherent cosmogony and may have supplied or reshaped details.
3. **Christian or encyclopedic influence.** The four streams invite comparison with Paradise’s four rivers, and the orderly three-day emergence resembles learned narrative patterning. Such influence would not necessarily make the entire cow episode Snorri’s invention.

No surviving evidence establishes any one position conclusively.

### The “Indo-European cow of creation”

**[Comparative reconstruction]** Iranian tradition supplies the strongest substantial comparison. Avestan litanies name a uniquely created primordial bovine; the later Middle Persian *Bundahišn* and *Wizīdagīhā ī Zādspram* narrate Gāw ī Ēwdād, the primordial bovine whose death ultimately generates beneficial animals, plants, and foods. But this bovine is a creation of Ohrmazd, is killed in the cosmic conflict, and becomes the ancestor of animal species—features absent from Auðhumla’s story.

Vedic tradition richly associates cows with nourishment, wealth, speech, dawn, sacrifice, and cosmic production, but no surviving Vedic passage reproduces the exact complex “cow formed from rime, feeding a giant, licking a divine ancestor from salt.” Comparison demonstrates a shared pastoral symbolic field, not direct textual descent.

Other suggested comparisons include:

- Hathor and Isis as cow-associated Egyptian divinities;
- “cow-eyed” Hera;
- Nerthus’s cattle-drawn procession in Tacitus;
- the animal nurse as a widespread mythic motif;
- sacrificial creation from a primordial human or bovine body.

These analogues are typological. No historical chain of borrowing to Snorri has been demonstrated. The phrase “the Proto-Indo-European primeval cow” can misleadingly turn a scholarly model into an attested deity. There is no reconstructed Proto-Indo-European name securely underlying *Auðhumla*.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Direct evidence

**[Absence of evidence]** There is no identified:

- temple, shrine, cult-house, sacred grove, spring, or altar dedicated to Auðhumla;
- priesthood or religious office serving her;
- festival or calendar date;
- sacrifice, libation, prayer, hymn, charm, oath, divination, or pilgrimage addressed to her;
- votive image or inscription;
- theophoric personal or dynastic name;
- place-name securely formed from her name;
- account of suppression, conversion, desecration, or temple destruction connected specifically with her.

No evidence links her to human sacrifice, temple prostitution, cult violence, or a political priesthood.

### Cattle cult and cattle sacrifice

Germanic and Scandinavian sources provide extensive evidence for the economic and ritual importance of cattle. Tacitus describes Nerthus’s wagon as drawn by cows; archaeological deposits and textual accounts attest cattle sacrifice in multiple contexts. None of this is automatically evidence for worship of Auðhumla. “Cattle mattered ritually” and “this named cow had a cult” are different propositions.

Medieval saga stories about individuals venerating or depending upon exceptional cattle are likewise not attestations of Auðhumla unless she is named or the distinctive licking-and-Búri narrative appears. It does not.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Before approximately 1200

**[Absence of direct evidence]** No securely datable pre-Christian document names Auðhumla. A prehistoric or Viking-Age form of the story is possible but unreconstructable. General cattle symbolism and distant comparative parallels cannot supply the missing Scandinavian evidence.

### Approximately 1220: Snorri’s synthesis

Auðhumla enters the documented record in *Gylfaginning*. The work organizes poetic fragments, oral or prose traditions, and medieval learning into a question-and-answer cosmology. This is the decisive attestation.

### Early fourteenth century: manuscript transmission

The episode survives in the principal *Edda* redactions, including the short Uppsala version and the R/W/T family. Variant spellings show that the name was not perfectly stable. The independent redactions confirm medieval transmission but do not push the attestation back before Snorri.

### Medieval *Nafnaþulur*

The cow-list preserves her name and the status “foremost of cows.” This converts a narrative individual into a lexical-poetic item but supplies no cult or additional legend.

### Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries: antiquarian recovery

Printed editions and antiquarian manuscripts returned the *Edda* to wider Scandinavian and European readerships. Eighteenth-century Icelandic manuscript illustrators supplied the first securely identifiable surviving pictures, including Jakob Sigurðsson’s 1760 scene. Paul-Henri Mallet’s writings helped popularize “Celtic” and Northern antiquity in Europe, though the period’s category “Celtic mythology” often conflated distinct traditions.

### Late eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: national-romantic art

Abildgaard’s *Ymer og Audhumbla* translated the terse medieval account into monumental visual form. Philologists standardized Old Norse texts and increasingly discussed the cow through comparative mythology. Wagner did not incorporate her into the *Ring*, so claims that she belongs to Wagner’s plot are false.

### Twentieth-century scholarship

Jan de Vries, Georges Dumézil, H. R. Ellis Davidson, E. O. G. Turville-Petre, Rudolf Simek, and later John Lindow situated her within Germanic religion or comparative Indo-European mythology. Scholarly confidence has varied: some presentations treat her as a genuine inherited fragment; more source-critical treatments foreground the absence of any independent pre-Snorrian attestation.

### Late twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Auðhumla entered modern Heathen, esoteric-runic, fantasy, comics, gaming, and commercial culture. These receptions greatly enlarge her attributes but do not enlarge the medieval corpus.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Esoteric runology

**[Modern invention]** Edred Thorsson’s *Futhark: A Handbook of Rune Magic* associates Auðhumla with **uruz**, calling her an expression of the “mother of manifestation” and a primal formative force. It interprets her in a modern magical cosmology involving energized essence, archetypal patterning, and psychic practice.

No medieval rune poem connects the U-rune with Auðhumla. No inscription gives her a rune, bind-rune, galdr, or magical function. The association combines the rune-name’s bovine or aurochs meanings with Snorri’s cow and is a twentieth-century esoteric synthesis.

Later grimoires and online occult systems repeat or expand this equation, sometimes associating her with Fehu, Uruz, wealth, fertility, purification, the element of ice, or a supposed ancient sigil. These assignments conflict because they are modern constructions, not variants preserved from antiquity.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist reception

**[Modern religious reception]** Contemporary Heathen and Ásatrú writers may honor Auðhumla as a spirit or goddess of nourishment, patience, motherhood, cattle, land, or communal support. Milk, cream, salt, or water are sometimes proposed as offerings.

These are legitimate documented modern religious practices where communities perform them, but they are revivals or innovations without an attested medieval ritual template. “Reconstructionist” should not be taken to mean that a historical Auðhumla cult has been reconstructed from direct evidence.

The Hrafnar organization’s “Auntie Auðumla’s Guide to Norse Feasting,” for example, uses her name as an emblem of hospitality and abundance. It does not establish an ancient feast in her honor.

### Visual art

The 1760 manuscript illustration and Abildgaard’s painting fixed the now-familiar composition: a monumental cow simultaneously nursing Ymir and freeing Búri. Modern illustrators often make her white, luminous, horned, or mountain-sized. Ironically, if the “hornless” etymology is correct, horned depictions contradict the probable name, though the medieval narrative itself never describes her head.

### Comics and games

- **Marvel Comics:** Audumbla first appears in *Journey into Mystery* no. 97 (1963) in a retelling of Norse creation. Later Marvel continuity makes Tiwaz rather than Búri the being in the ice, sometimes connects the cow with Gaea, gives her wild-cattle offspring, and invents a “Ragnahorn.” These are comic-book elaborations.
- **Fire Emblem: The Sacred Stones** (2004): “Audhulma” is an ice sword and one of Jehanna’s Sacred Twin treasures. The name and ice association derive from the myth; Auðhumla is not a weapon in Norse tradition.

### Commercial appropriation

Auðhumla is the name of a modern Icelandic dairy cooperative owned by approximately 500 milk producers and serving as the majority parent of Mjólkursamsalan. The cooperative explicitly explains that its name comes from the primeval milk-giving cow. This is an unusually direct commercial adaptation of her medieval function.

### Political and extremist appropriation

The dedicated search found no well-documented Auðhumla-specific political cult, nationalist emblem, or extremist organization comparable to modern appropriations of Óðinn, Thor’s hammer, or certain runes. General völkisch and racialized uses of “Germanic origins” can absorb cosmogonic material, but assigning a specific extremist history to Auðhumla without documentation would be misleading.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Auðhumla an old myth or Snorri’s creation?

This is the central debate.

**Arguments for inherited tradition:**

- her unusual and apparently meaningful Old Norse name;
- practical pastoral features—milk and a mineral lick;
- her placement in a cosmogony partly anchored by old Eddic verses;
- Iranian and other Indo-European primordial-bovine parallels;
- her inclusion in a traditional-style poetic name-list.

**Arguments for caution or Snorrian elaboration:**

- total absence from surviving Eddic poems;
- no independent skaldic citation;
- no inscription, image, place-name, or cultic evidence;
- her narrative solves a conspicuous logical problem in Snorri’s prose: how Ymir was fed;
- the four streams may reflect biblical or learned cosmography;
- the *Nafnaþulur* notice may depend on the prose narrative rather than represent an independent tradition.

The most defensible conclusion is limited: the story may preserve inherited material, but its only recoverable medieval form is Snorrian.

### Etymology

Noreen’s “rich/abundant hornless cow” analysis remains influential. Tacitus’s notice of inconspicuously horned German cattle supplies cultural plausibility. Yet the variant forms and rarity of the second element prevent absolute certainty. Etymology cannot prove either cult or prehistoric antiquity.

### Comparative mythology

Turville-Petre and Ellis Davidson emphasized broad primordial-cow parallels; Simek lists Hathor, Isis, Hera, Nerthus, and Indo-Iranian material but cautions against deriving the four milk-streams from a universal Great Mother. Dumézilian and later Indo-European models invite structural comparison among primordial bovines, first beings, sacrifice, and world-making.

A methodological problem recurs: comparativists sometimes combine separate Norse roles. Ymir, not Auðhumla, is killed and dismembered to form the world. Auðhumla, unlike the Iranian primordial bovine, neither dies nor generates animal species. A reconstructed “Indo-European creation myth” should not erase those differences.

### Christian influence

Snorri was a Christian Icelandic author writing centuries after conversion. Christian learning inevitably formed part of his intellectual world. Possible echoes of Paradise’s four rivers are therefore worth considering. But resemblance alone cannot divide the episode mechanically into “pagan cow” plus “Christian number four.” The relevant pre-Christian evidence is missing.

### State of the evidence

The important physical witnesses are preserved in:

- Uppsala University Library: DG 11 4to;
- Árni Magnússon Institute for Icelandic Studies, Reykjavík: GKS 2367 4to and related manuscripts;
- Arnamagnæan Collection, Copenhagen: AM 242 fol. and related witnesses;
- Utrecht University Library: MS 1374;
- Royal Danish Library: NKS 1867 4to.

Faulkes’s editions of *Gylfaginning* and *Skáldskaparmál* remain standard accessible critical resources. Heimir Pálsson’s edition and translation of the Uppsala *Edda* is essential for its independent redaction.

### Claims that should be rejected or relabeled

- “Auðhumla was a widely worshipped Norse cow-goddess”: **unsupported**.
- “Archaeologists have found her temples”: **unsupported**.
- “Her ancient rune was Uruz/Fehu”: **modern esoteric invention**.
- “She was Nerthus/Hathor/Isis”: **modern comparison, not historical identification**.
- “She was sacrificed and the world was made from her body”: **conflation with other primordial-bovine traditions and with Ymir**.
- “She was mother of Búri”: **interpretive shorthand, not Snorri’s wording**.
- “Her four streams became the four world-rivers/cardinal directions”: **modern or learned interpretation**.
- “She appears in the Poetic Edda”: **false**.
- “Viking images depict her”: **unestablished**.
- “The Milky Way is explicitly her milk”: **unattested**.

| 98 CE | Tacitus’s *Germania* mentions unimpressive or absent-looking horns on German cattle and, separately, cows drawing Nerthus’s wagon. Comparative context only. |
| Viking Age, approximately 750–1050 | No securely identified inscription, verse, place-name, or image of Auðhumla. |
| Approximately 1000 | Iceland’s formal Christian conversion precedes the surviving written account by more than two centuries. |
| Late twelfth–thirteenth-century poetic transmission | *Vafþrúðnismál* and *Vǫluspá* preserve related cosmogonic material but omit Auðhumla. |
| Approximately 1220 | *Gylfaginning* is composed/compiled in Snorri’s *Edda*: first secure attestation of Auðhumla, the four milk-rivers, and the licking of Búri. |
| First quarter of fourteenth century | DG 11 4to, the Uppsala *Edda*, provides probably the earliest surviving substantial physical witness. |
| First half of fourteenth century | GKS 2367 4to, Codex Regius of Snorri’s *Edda*, preserves the version commonly used for modern editions. |
| Mid-fourteenth century | Codex Wormianus preserves another principal redaction. |
| Medieval manuscript period | *Nafnaþulur* calls Auðhumla *œzt kúa*, “foremost/noblest of cows.” |
| Approximately 1600 | Codex Trajectinus copies a lost medieval exemplar. |
| 1760 | Jakob Sigurðsson illustrates Auðhumla and Búri in NKS 1867 4to, fol. 95r—the earliest securely identifiable surviving depiction located in this investigation. |
| Approximately 1790 | Nicolai Abildgaard paints *Ymer og Audhumbla*, establishing an influential neoclassical visualization. |
| 1918 | Adolf Noreen publishes a focused etymological study of Auðhumla’s name. |
| 1956–57 | Jan de Vries’s *Altgermanische Religionsgeschichte* consolidates comparative study of Germanic religion. |
| 1964 | Turville-Petre and Ellis Davidson discuss primordial-cow parallels in major English-language syntheses. |
| 1984/1992 editions | Edred Thorsson’s *Futhark* makes Auðhumla a symbol of Uruz and “manifestation,” a modern occult construction. |
| 1987; revised printings thereafter | Anthony Faulkes’s translation of Snorri’s *Edda* becomes a standard English reference; his 2005 second-edition Old Norse text supplies the critical wording. |
| 1993 English edition | Rudolf Simek’s *Dictionary of Northern Mythology* summarizes the name, comparisons, and possible learned influence on the four streams. |
| 2001/2002 | John Lindow emphasizes Auðhumla’s structural mediation between giants and Æsir. |
| 2004 | *Fire Emblem: The Sacred Stones* reuses her name as the ice sword Audhulma. |
| 2012 | Heimir Pálsson publishes a modern edition and translation of the Uppsala *Edda*. |
| Twenty-first century | Heathen groups, occultists, artists, games, comics, and an Icelandic dairy cooperative use Auðhumla as a symbol of nourishment, abundance, creation, or ice. No evidence of continuous worship from the medieval period is established. |
| 2026 | The evidentiary position remains essentially unchanged: two medieval textual notices within the *Edda* tradition, no proven pre-Snorrian attestation or historical cult. |

### Contested-material audit

Explicit searches were conducted for Auðhumla in connection with etymology, origin, Indo-European reconstruction, Nerthus, syncretism, sacrifice, cult, human sacrifice, suppression, occultism, rune magic, neopagan practice, political appropriation, visual art, games, comics, and commercial use.
