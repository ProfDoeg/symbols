# The Valkyries: Research Dossier

> **Class:** A collective of supernatural female beings—Old Norse *valkyrjur*, singular *valkyrja*—variously classified in the sources as Óðinn’s battle-agents, choosers of the slain, distributors of victory, attendants in Valhǫll, fate-governing *dísir*, supernatural brides, or exceptional human princesses possessing valkyrie status. They are not securely classified as a race of goddesses with a fixed genealogy.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The cognate Old English noun *wælcyrge/wælcyrige*, “chooser of the slain,” survives in the eighth-century Anglo-Saxon glossary tradition, securely in Cambridge, Corpus Christi College MS 144, copied around 800, where it glosses classical beings including the Fury Tisiphone and the war-goddess Bellona. This proves an early Germanic word and a semantic association with female battle/death powers, but not the complete later Scandinavian system. The earliest reasonably datable Scandinavian narrative evidence is the skaldic memorial poem *Hákonarmál*, composed shortly after King Hákon the Good’s death in 961, in which Óðinn sends Gǫndul and Skǫgul to choose a king for Valhǫll.

## Evidence labels used

- **[EARLY–SECURE]** Contemporary or near-contemporary evidence, or an early manuscript whose wording is secure.
- **[EARLY POETIC]** Poetry plausibly originating before Scandinavian conversion but preserved only in later manuscripts.
- **[LATER CANONICAL]** The influential thirteenth-century Icelandic synthesis, especially the Eddas.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** A locally or textually restricted form.
- **[LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]** A medieval antiquarian’s ordering of heterogeneous traditions.
- **[COMPARATIVE RECONSTRUCTION]** A modern scholarly proposal based on cognates or structural parallels.
- **[LATE ACCRETION]** A medieval or early-modern development not securely recoverable for the pagan period.
- **[FOLKLORE]** Post-conversion vernacular tradition.
- **[MODERN REINVENTION]** A modern literary, religious, artistic, political, or commercial construction.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The collective name

**[EARLY–SECURE]** Old Norse *valkyrja*, plural *valkyrjur*, is conventionally parsed as *valr*, “the slain lying on a battlefield,” plus the agent noun related to *kjósa*, “choose”: “chooser of the slain.” The cognate Old English forms are *wælcyrge, wælcyrige,* and *wælcyrie*. The word is not simply “chooser of warriors”; its first element denotes battle-corpses or the battle-dead.

A recent etymological reconsideration accepts Proto-Germanic *\*wala-kuzjōn-* and the ordinary synchronic interpretation “slain-chooser,” while asking whether deeper Indo-European verbal history could once have supported a different nuance, including “taste/test the slain.” That is a linguistic hypothesis, not an attested Germanic definition. “Chooser of the slain” remains the explanation supported by the historical meanings of Old Norse *kjósa* and Old English *ċēosan* ([Ginevra, 2025](https://www.stockholmuniversitypress.se/en/books/121/files/75f04301-6be5-464c-ae67-8027c7d0a2a8.pdf)).

German *Walküre*, Scandinavian *valkyrie/valkyrja/valkyria*, French *walkyrie*, and English *Valkyrie* are later reflexes or borrowings. Wagner’s spelling and art made German *Walküre* internationally prominent.

### Other collective designations

- **Óðins meyjar**, “Óðinn’s maidens”: a descriptive category preserved in learned poetic-name traditions. **[LATER CANONICAL/LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **óskmeyjar**, usually “wish-maidens” or “chosen maidens”: found in *Oddrúnargrátr*. The first element is disputed and may suggest belonging to Óðinn as *Óski*, “Wish-granter,” rather than the modern sense of wish-fulfilling spirits. **[EARLY POETIC]**
- **dísir**, singular *dís*: a broad and unstable category of supernatural women, guardians, ancestral powers, fate-beings, or dangerous female spirits. Some valkyries can be called or understood as *dísir*, but *dís* is not an exact synonym for *valkyrja*. **[EARLY POETIC/LATER SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- “Battle-maidens,” “shield-maidens,” and “swan-maidens” are useful descriptions in particular narratives, not interchangeable indigenous class names.

### The principal names

Valkyrie names often look like battle vocabulary, personified weapons, noises, restraints, victory terms, or poetic abstractions. Translation is frequently uncertain because a name may preserve an obsolete noun, a pun, or a conventional poetic term.

#### The *Vǫluspá* group

**[EARLY POETIC]** *Vǫluspá* names:

- **Skuld** — “Debt,” “Obligation,” or “That-which-shall-be”; also the youngest named Norn.
- **Skǫgul** — possibly “Shaker” or “High-towering one”; strongly associated with battle.
- **Hildr** — “Battle.”
- **Gunnr** — “War/Battle.”
- **Gǫndul** — commonly “Wand-wielder,” perhaps referring to a magical staff.
- **Geirskǫgul** — “Spear-Skǫgul.”

The overlap between Skuld as Norn and valkyrie is direct textual evidence that medieval categories of fate-woman were permeable; it is not proof that every Norn was a valkyrie.

#### The *Grímnismál* group

**[EARLY POETIC]** *Grímnismál* 36 names women who bring ale to the einherjar:

- **Hrist** — “Shaker.”
- **Mist** — probably “Mist” or “Cloud,” though interpretation is uncertain.
- **Skeggjǫld** — perhaps “Axe-age,” “Bearing an axe,” or “War-age.”
- **Skǫgul** — “Shaker.”
- **Hildr** — “Battle.”
- **Þrúðr** — “Strength/Power”; elsewhere also the name of Þórr’s daughter, but identity is not assured.
- **Hlǫkk** — “Noise of battle.”
- **Herfjǫtur** — “Host-fetter/Army-fetter,” evoking battlefield paralysis.
- **Gǫll** — “Tumult/Noise.”
- **Geirǫnul** — uncertain, probably a spear-name compound.
- **Randgríðr** — “Shield-truce” or “Shield-destroyer,” uncertain.
- **Ráðgríðr** — “Council-truce” or “Plan-destroyer,” uncertain.
- **Reginleif** — “Inheritance/legacy of the powers.”

In this stanza their immediate function is service in Valhǫll, not choosing battlefield deaths. The famous division between terrifying battle spirits and gracious cup-bearers therefore exists within the medieval corpus itself.

#### Other narrative valkyries

- **Sigrún** — “Victory-rune” or “victory-secret”; valkyrie and human king’s daughter in the Helgi poems.
- **Sváva/Sváfa** — etymology disputed; possibly connected with “sleep,” the Suebi, or the legendary Sváfaland.
- **Kára** — perhaps “Wild/Stormy one” or “Curly-haired,” uncertain; appears only in later prose reporting a lost poem.
- **Sigrdrífa** — “Victory-driver” or “Urger to victory.” The name may function as a title rather than an original personal name.
- **Brynhildr** — “Armor-battle”; German **Brünhilt/Brünnhilde**.
- **Hlaðguðr svanhvít** — first name uncertain; epithet “Swan-white.”
- **Hervǫr alvitr** — “Army-wise” or “strange/otherworldly being”; interpretation of *alvitr* is disputed.
- **Ǫlrún** — often “Ale-rune”; possibly “foreign rune/secret,” uncertain.
- **Gunnr, Róta, Skuld** — Snorri’s named battle-riders.
- **Gǫndul and Skǫgul** — the agents in *Hákonarmál*.
- **Hildr** — both a named valkyrie and, in the Hjaðningavíg legend, a woman who renews dead warriors.
- **Mist, Hildr, Þrúðr, Hløkk, Herfjǫtur, Gǫll, Geirǫnul, Randgríðr, Ráðgríðr, Reginleif** and numerous further names recur in Snorri’s *Nafnaþulur*. **[LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]**

#### The *Darraðarljóð* twelve

**[MEDIEVAL TEXT PRESERVING POSSIBLY EARLIER POETRY]** The poem names Hildr, Hjǫrþrimul, Sanngríðr, Svipul, Guðr, and Gǫndul among twelve valkyries. Not all twelve are individually named. Their names again evoke battle, swords, change, violence, or supernatural movement. The text calls them *valkyrjur* explicitly.

### Identifications with other kinds of being

- **Norns:** There is functional overlap in determining fate, and Skuld belongs to both lists. Complete identity is not attested. **[ATTESTED OVERLAP; MODERN DEBATE]**
- **Dísir:** Probably the broadest indigenous category within which some valkyries could be situated. Nevertheless, sources also use *dísir* for family guardians and other powers having no battlefield function. **[ATTESTED CATEGORY OVERLAP]**
- **Fylgjur:** Modern scholars compare the protective valkyrie-brides of heroes with female guardian followers, but no source supplies a universal equation. **[COMPARATIVE RECONSTRUCTION]**
- **Swan-maidens:** *Vǫlundarkviða* calls the three swan-clad women valkyries, but swan form is not a normal attribute of every valkyrie. **[REGIONAL/NARRATIVE VARIANT]**
- **Furies, Bellona, Gorgons:** Old English glossators used *wælcyrge* to translate Latin or classical female agents of war and terror. This is a datable act of learned equivalence, not proof that Anglo-Saxons worshipped Bellona as a valkyrie ([Bosworth–Toller](https://bosworthtoller.com/34306)).
- **The Merseburg *idisi*:** The First Merseburg Charm, copied in the tenth century in Old High German, describes *idisi* who sit, bind armies, and release fetters. Their actions resemble Herfjǫtur and other battle-fate women. Identifying them specifically as valkyries is plausible but remains a modern classification; the charm never says *walküria*.
- **Tacitus’s prophetic Germanic women:** *Germania* 8 reports that Germans regarded women as possessing something sacred and prophetic and sometimes took their counsel. It does not attest valkyries.
- **The Alaisiagae and continental Matronae:** Equations with valkyries have been proposed from names and martial functions. No inscription calls them valkyries; these remain comparative hypotheses.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

1. **Old English glossary tradition, eighth–eleventh centuries. [EARLY–SECURE]**  
   *Wælcyrge* glosses Tisiphone, Alecto, Erinyes, Bellona, Venus, and Gorgon-related terms in several manuscripts. The Corpus Glossary manuscript, Cambridge Corpus Christi College 144, dates to about 800; its material descends partly from earlier glossary traditions. These entries establish a culturally intelligible Old English battle/death-woman term but preserve no native valkyrie narrative. The Dictionary of Old English Corpus provides the systematic corpus context ([DOE Corpus](https://varieng.helsinki.fi/CoRD/corpora/DOEC/)).

2. **Rök stone, Ög 136, early ninth century. [DISPUTED]**  
   One difficult passage has been interpreted through a kenning in which a woman or valkyrie rides a wolf, the carrion-beast being her “horse.” The Rök inscription is notoriously compressed and disputed. It is evidence for a martial-carrion image only under that reading, not a plain inscription saying “valkyrie.”

3. **Viking-Age armed-woman imagery, eighth–tenth centuries. [MATERIAL, IDENTIFICATION DISPUTED]**  
   Small metal figures and picture stones show armed women or women welcoming mounted warriors with horns. These may represent valkyries, ritual performers, noblewomen, mythic heroines, or deliberately multivalent figures. None bears a label.

4. ***Hákonarmál*, c. 961. [EARLY POETIC/NEAR-CONTEMPORARY]**  
   Eyvindr skáldaspillir composed this praise-poem after Hákon the Good fell at Fitjar. Óðinn sends Gǫndul and Skǫgul to choose who will enter his hall. It is preserved in later kings’ sagas, principally *Heimskringla* and *Fagrskinna*, but its linguistic form and political occasion support tenth-century composition.

5. **Karlevi stone, Öland, late tenth century. [EARLY–SECURE, INDIRECT]**  
   Its skaldic memorial stanza calls the dead chieftain a mighty “tree of Þrúðr of battles” or comparable woman-kenning. Þrúðr is a valkyrie name, but the inscription’s grammar uses it inside a poetic kenning; it does not narrate valkyrie belief.

### The Eddic corpus

The principal manuscript is Reykjavík, Árni Magnússon Institute, **GKS 2365 4to**, the *Codex Regius* of the Poetic Edda, copied around 1270. Many poems are linguistically older, but proposed composition dates range broadly, commonly from the ninth through thirteenth centuries. Manuscript date and poem date must not be conflated.

- ***Vǫluspá***: lists six valkyries as women “ready to ride” over the earth. **[EARLY POETIC]**
- ***Grímnismál* 36**: thirteen named women serve ale in Valhǫll. **[EARLY POETIC]**
- ***Vǫlundarkviða***: three king’s daughters described as valkyries arrive in swan garments, marry Vǫlundr and his brothers, then depart after seven or nine years. **[EARLY POETIC; SWAN-MAIDEN VARIANT]**
- ***Helgakviða Hjǫrvarðssonar***: Sváva, daughter of King Eylimi, names the silent hero Helgi, protects him in battle, and becomes his beloved.
- ***Helgakviða Hundingsbana I–II***: Sigrún, daughter of Hǫgni, rides with other valkyries, defies an arranged marriage, protects Helgi, and laments him after death.
- ***Sigrdrífumál***: Sigurðr awakens an armored woman on Hindarfjall; she says Óðinn struck her with a sleep-thorn because she gave victory contrary to his command. She teaches Sigurðr runes, ritual knowledge, and counsel. The conventional title is editorial; the Codex Regius textual sequence is damaged by a lost quire.
- ***Helreið Brynhildar***: Brynhildr, journeying toward Hel, narrates her past, including the sleep punishment. This poem helps identify her with the sleeping valkyrie.
- ***Oddrúnargrátr***: uses *óskmeyjar* in heroic context.
- ***Guðrúnarkviða*, *Atlakviða*, and associated heroic poems**: refer to Brynhildr and the Vǫlsung complex, though not every appearance classifies her as a valkyrie.

Carolyne Larrington’s translation is the most accessible modern English rendering named in the brief; Henry Adams Bellows is public-domain and useful but reflects older textual judgments. A searchable Bellows text is available through the [Poetic Edda PDF](https://www.sacred-texts.com/neu/poe/poe.pdf).

### Skaldic and saga corpus

- ***Hákonarmál***, c. 961: Gǫndul and Skǫgul choose Hákon for Óðinn.
- **Early battle kennings:** *valkyrie*, *Hildr*, *Gunnr*, *Skǫgul*, and other female battle names become components of kennings for battle, weapons, or warriors. Whether each kenning evokes a separately imagined person is uncertain. The [Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages database](https://skaldic.org/db.php?if=default&table=files&view=) is the critical digital corpus.
- ***Darraðarljóð***, transmitted in *Brennu-Njáls saga*, chapter 157: twelve valkyries weave the outcome of the Battle of Clontarf, 1014, with intestines, severed heads, arrows, and swords. The saga was composed around 1280; the poem may be eleventh-century, but its exact date and place of composition remain disputed ([Old Norse text](https://heimskringla.no/wiki/Darra%C3%B0arlj%C3%B3%C3%B0_%28Nj%C3%A1ls_saga%29)).
- ***Vǫlsunga saga***, late thirteenth century: synthesizes the Sigurðr–Brynhildr materials and explicitly makes the awakened woman Brynhildr.
- ***Hrómundar saga Gripssonar*** and later reports of *Káruljóð*: Kára protects Helgi Haddingjaskati by flying above battle in swan form; when he accidentally strikes her, her supernatural protection ends. The surviving prose is late and the poem is lost. **[LATE ACCRETION/LOST-POEM REPORT]**
- ***Hervarar saga*** and Hildr traditions: offer related supernatural-warrior women but should not automatically be made valkyries.
- ***Sörla þáttr* and the Hjaðningavíg complex:** Hildr repeatedly revives the dead so battle can continue. Versions differ about her intention and agency.
- ***Heimskringla***, c. 1230: preserves *Hákonarmál* in *Hákonar saga góða*.
- **Snorri Sturluson, *Edda*, c. 1220:** *Gylfaginning* 36 says Óðinn sends valkyries to every battle; they determine death and victory. Gunnr, Róta, and Skuld ride to choose the slain. *Skáldskaparmál* and *Nafnaþulur* supply further names and poetic usages. Anthony Faulkes’s edition and translation remain standard; an electronic text is available in [Faulkes’s *Edda*](https://is.cuni.cz/studium/predmety/index.php?did=62028&do=download&kod=ARL100252).
- **Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum*, c. 1200:** contains warrior women and versions of Germanic heroic traditions but no systematic valkyrie order comparable to Snorri’s.
- ***Nibelungenlied*, c. 1200:** Brünhild is an extraordinarily powerful queen, not explicitly a valkyrie. Treating the *Nibelungenlied* Brünhild as straightforwardly the same supernatural being as Snorri’s valkyries imports Norse evidence into a distinct German version.
- **Bryggen runic charm, Bergen, late fourteenth century:** invokes protection “against a harmful *skag-valkyrie*” or comparable threatening being. Its late date and Christian-era magical setting show that *valkyrie* could also denote a dangerous female supernatural attacker. **[LATE MEDIEVAL MAGIC]**

### Sources that do not directly attest Valkyries

- Tacitus’s *Germania* (98 CE) attests revered prophetic women, not *valkyrjur*.
- Adam of Bremen’s *Gesta Hammaburgensis* (c. 1070s) describes Scandinavian cult and alleged Uppsala sacrifices but does not document a valkyrie cult.
- The Merseburg Charms contain *idisi*, not the word “valkyrie.”
- No securely read Viking-Age runic text straightforwardly says “this image is a valkyrie.”

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Functions

**[EARLY POETIC]** The oldest recoverable Scandinavian function is selecting or causing battlefield death and allocating distinguished dead warriors to Óðinn. In *Hákonarmál*, choice is political as well as fatal: Óðinn increases his following by bringing a king into Valhǫll.

**[EARLY POETIC]** Valkyries can protect favored living heroes, alter battles, name heroes, arrange marriages, and become lovers or wives. Sváva and Sigrún are not impersonal corpse-collectors; they are royal women whose supernatural power and familial obligations drive heroic tragedy.

**[LATER CANONICAL]** Snorri regularizes them as Óðinn’s dispatched agents who “choose those who are to be slain” and determine victory. This is the most influential concise definition, but it is a thirteenth-century synthesis.

**[LATER CANONICAL]** In Valhǫll they serve drink to the einherjar. Modern art often separates the “cup-bearing maiden” from the “armored warrior,” but the medieval sources allow both roles.

**[REGIONAL VARIANT]** They may weave battle itself. In *Darraðarljóð*, the loom is a killing apparatus: intestines are warp and weft, heads are weights, arrows are heddle-rods, and swords beat the cloth. The valkyries rejoice in impending slaughter and decide where death will fall. This is not an eyewitness description of ritual but a visionary poem embedded in a later saga.

**[REGIONAL VARIANT]** They may be swan-maidens, as in *Vǫlundarkviða* and the late Kára tradition.

### Powers and limitations

They foreknow or determine battle outcomes, ride through air or over sea, protect warriors, paralyze armies, teach runes, and sometimes revive the dead. Their authority is not uniform:

- Gǫndul and Skǫgul obey Óðinn.
- Sigrdrífa/Brynhildr disobeys him by granting victory to the wrong combatant.
- Sigrún and Sváva act from personal desire and kinship.
- Hildr perpetuates battle through resurrection.
- Kára’s protection can be accidentally destroyed.

This variety undermines the modern idea of a fixed corps operating under an unbreakable celestial chain of command. A recent study argues that disobedient valkyries resemble Norns more than Óðinn’s servants and dramatize rivalry between sovereign divine will and impersonal fate ([Heide, 2022](https://tidsskrift.dk/rvt/article/view/132111)).

### Appearance and equipment

Secure literary attributes include:

- female form;
- armor, helmet, shield, spear, and sword;
- horses capable of supernatural travel;
- shining or atmospheric effects associated with their riding;
- swan-cloaks in one narrative family;
- drinking horns in Valhǫll;
- loom and weaving instruments in *Darraðarljóð*.

The modern winged helmet is not a defining medieval attribute. No source says all valkyries have wings. Winged or horned helmets arose from nineteenth-century theatrical and Romantic visual conventions. Horses are attested; **winged horses** are largely modern conflation with Pegasus-like imagery.

### Archaeological images

#### Hårby figure

**[VIKING-AGE MATERIAL; IDENTIFICATION PROBABLE BUT UNPROVED]** A small gilded-silver figure found near Hårby on Funen, Denmark, and dated broadly to the Viking Age, approximately the ninth century, depicts a long-haired or long-garbed figure holding a sword and a round shield. Because the figure is unambiguously armed and conforms to a wider armed-female motif, it is commonly called the “Hårby Valkyrie.” The object itself bears no identifying inscription. Museum Odense catalogs it as a silver valkyrie, while the National Museum preserves the official collection record ([Museum Odense](https://samlinger.museumodense.dk/OBM/9867x0307); [National Museum of Denmark](https://samlinger.natmus.dk/do/asset/12785)).

The image is especially important because it visibly carries sword and shield rather than merely a drinking horn. It does not prove that actual shieldmaidens formed a military institution.

#### Horn-bearing women and riders

Gotlandic picture stones, including the Tjängvide stone, and metal pendants show women presenting drinking horns to mounted men, sometimes beside an eight-legged horse. These are frequently interpreted as valkyries welcoming the dead to Valhǫll. The conjunction of rider, horse, hall, and horn supports that reading, but individual figures remain unlabeled.

#### Ribe moulds and pendants

Reconstructed mould fragments from Viking-Age Ribe include armed female figures. One interpretation sees them as ritual performers rather than literal portraits of supernatural beings. Some shields are held in impractical orientations, suggesting emblematic or ceremonial display. The opposition “valkyrie or real woman” may therefore be false: human performers could embody mythic roles ([Deckers, Croix, and Sindbæk, 2021](https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/full/10.1080/00766097.2021.1923893)).

A newer analysis warns that “valkyrie” has become an automatic archaeological label for diverse anthropomorphic objects whose bodies, uses, and depositional contexts vary substantially ([Antiquity, 2025](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/viking-bodymaking-new-evidence-for-intraaction-with-iconic-viking-anthropomorphic-art/9F917FD75D099ABF2278568ED9AE0999)).

### Unattested modern attributes

No medieval source establishes:

- a universal number of nine;
- a hereditary “race” of Valkyries;
- Freyja as their commander;
- color-coded Valkyrie orders;
- obligatory chastity;
- admission into the order through a standardized initiation;
- a fixed connection with ravens as personal familiars;
- a special “Valkyrie rune”;
- horned helmets;
- wings growing from their bodies;
- a pan-Germanic temple cult devoted specifically to them.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Collective genealogy

No premodern Norse source gives the collective a single origin, mother, or birth myth.

In the heroic lays, named valkyries may be human royal daughters:

- Sváva is daughter of Eylimi.
- Sigrún is daughter of Hǫgni.
- Brynhildr is daughter of Buðli in the heroic genealogy.
- Ǫlrún is daughter of Kjárr of Valland.
- Hlaðguðr and Hervǫr are daughters of King Hlǫðvér.

These genealogies make valkyrie status compatible with human or heroic aristocratic birth. They do not explain the collective’s cosmic origin.

**[MODERN REINVENTION]** Wagner makes his Walküren daughters of Wotan, usually by different mothers; Brünnhilde is specifically the daughter of Wotan and Erda in the *Ring*. This became enormously influential but is not the genealogy of the Eddic collective.

### Brynhildr and Sigrdrífa

The source history is genuinely inconsistent:

- In *Sigrdrífumál*, the awakened figure calls herself **Sigrdrífa**, “victory-driver,” or is introduced by prose under that name.
- In *Helreið Brynhildar*, Brynhildr’s retrospective story incorporates the supernatural sleep-punishment.
- *Vǫlsunga saga* identifies the sleeper unequivocally as Brynhildr and produces two meetings between her and Sigurðr.

One scholarly model treats Sigrdrífa as an originally separate valkyrie whom saga synthesis merged with Brynhildr. Another reads *sigrdrífa* as a functional title for Brynhildr. The damaged Codex Regius sequence prevents certainty. The saga’s identification is canonical for later Scandinavian reception but should not be projected without qualification into every Eddic layer. A modern edition of the saga notes that the Poetic Edda leaves the identity unclear while the saga resolves it ([Crawford translation](https://mimameidir.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/06/the-saga-of-the-volsungs-jackson-crawford.pdf)).

### Documented interactions with named divine beings

Each line below records an actual scene or explicit relationship. Mere membership in the same pantheon is excluded.

- **Óðinn — Gǫndul and Skǫgul:** Óðinn sends them to choose a king from the battlefield and bring Hákon to Valhǫll; *Hákonarmál*, c. 961, preserved in thirteenth-century kings’ sagas. **[EARLY POETIC]**
- **Óðinn — valkyries collectively:** He sends them to battles; they assign death and victory; Snorri, *Gylfaginning* 36, c. 1220. **[LATER CANONICAL/LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Óðinn — Gunnr, Róta, and Skuld:** They ride to battles to choose the slain and govern fighting; *Gylfaginning* 36. **[LATER CANONICAL]**
- **Óðinn — Sigrdrífa/Brynhildr:** She gives victory to Agnarr instead of Hjálmgunnarr, whom Óðinn favored; Óðinn pricks her with a sleep-thorn and declares she will no longer win victory in battle but will marry; *Sigrdrífumál* and *Vǫlsunga saga*. **[EARLY POETIC WITH LATER PROSE SYNTHESIS]**
- **Óðinn — Sigrún:** In *Helgakviða Hundingsbana II*, Sigrún says Óðinn has given Helgi’s spirit or victory-related destiny; the exact agency varies with translation. There is no face-to-face meeting. **[EARLY POETIC, INDIRECT]**
- **Skuld — valkyries/Norns:** Skuld is simultaneously named among Norns and among battle-riding valkyries in *Vǫluspá* and Snorri. This is categorical overlap rather than an episode between two beings. **[EARLY POETIC/LATER CANONICAL]**
- **Bragi — Gǫndul and Skǫgul in the Hákon cycle:** Bragi participates in Hákon’s reception in Valhǫll after the valkyries have selected him, but no direct exchange between Bragi and the valkyries survives. **[EARLY POETIC; JOINT EPISODE ONLY]**
- **Hermóðr — Gǫndul and Skǫgul in the Hákon cycle:** Hermóðr joins Bragi in welcoming Hákon; again, no direct valkyrie–Hermóðr dialogue is preserved. **[EARLY POETIC; JOINT EPISODE ONLY]**
- **Freyja — none securely attested:** *Grímnismál* says Freyja receives half the battle-dead in Fólkvangr and Óðinn the other half, but it does not say she commands valkyries or that they deliver her share. The common modern claim that Freyja is “queen of the Valkyries” is unestablished.
- **Þórr — none securely attested:** Þrúðr is both a valkyrie name and the name of Þórr’s daughter, but the sources do not securely identify them as the same individual.
- **Loki, Týr, Heimdallr, Baldr, Frigg, Njǫrðr, Freyr, Hel — no documented direct interaction** with the valkyrie collective in the surviving medieval corpus.
- **Erda — Brünnhilde:** Mother and daughter in Wagner’s *Ring*, especially through Wotan’s statements and Brünnhilde’s inherited wisdom. **[MODERN REINVENTION]**
- **Wotan — Wagner’s nine Walküren:** Their father and commander; he orders them to gather fallen heroes and punishes Brünnhilde for disobedience. Wagner, *Die Walküre*, libretto completed in the early 1850s, premiered 1870. **[MODERN REINVENTION]**
- **Fricka — Brünnhilde:** In *Die Walküre*, Fricka’s demand that Wotan punish Siegmund forces the command that Brünnhilde later disobeys. They share the dramatic conflict but have limited direct interaction. **[MODERN REINVENTION]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The choosing of Hákon

**Earliest form:** *Hákonarmál*, c. 961. **[EARLY POETIC]**

Óðinn sends Gǫndul and Skǫgul to decide which ruler of Yngvi’s lineage will come to him. At the Battle of Fitjar, Hákon sees the valkyries mounted, helmeted, and bearing shields. Gǫndul announces that the gods’ following is increasing because Hákon and his army have been invited home. Hákon protests that the valkyries have dealt with him harshly; Skǫgul answers that they caused him to hold the field and forced his enemies to flee. She then rides to tell Óðinn that the king is coming.

The poem is royal ideology as well as myth. Hákon, a Christian-associated king ruling a still substantially pagan constituency, is appropriated into Óðinn’s heroic afterlife. The valkyries validate both his battlefield courage and his place among sacral kings.

### Sváva and Helgi Hjörvarðsson

**Source:** *Helgakviða Hjǫrvarðssonar*, preserved in *Codex Regius*. **[EARLY POETIC]**

A nameless, silent prince sits on a burial mound and sees nine valkyries. Their leader Sváva names him Helgi and directs him toward a magnificent sword. She protects him in battle. Helgi later asks Sváva’s father for her hand; they love one another, though she continues riding to battles. Helgi is killed after accepting a duel prompted by his brother’s rash oath. As he dies, he asks Sváva to marry the brother; she refuses. The prose framework says Helgi and Sváva were reborn.

The poem combines initiation, naming, weapon-gift, supernatural guardianship, love, dynastic obligation, and reincarnation. Sváva is both valkyrie and royal daughter.

### Sigrún and Helgi Hundingsbani

**Sources:** *Helgakviða Hundingsbana I–II*. **[EARLY POETIC]**

Sigrún, daughter of Hǫgni, rides through the air and sea with other valkyries. She has been promised to Hǫðbroddr but chooses Helgi. She warns him of the marriage and helps precipitate a war in which Helgi kills Hǫðbroddr and members of Sigrún’s family. They marry.

Later, Sigrún’s brother Dagr, bound by oath, sacrifices to Óðinn or invokes him and receives a spear. Dagr kills Helgi. Sigrún curses Dagr and grieves with extreme intensity. Helgi temporarily returns from the burial mound; Sigrún spends a night with the corpse-like revenant, whose wounds are wet and cold. He returns to the world of the dead before dawn. The prose claims Sigrún died of sorrow and that she and Helgi were reborn as Kára and Helgi Haddingjaskati.

This is not a simple story of a Valkyrie transporting a dead man to Valhǫll. It presents a supernatural woman trapped between lover, natal family, vengeance, death, and recurring identity.

### Sigrdrífa/Brynhildr’s punishment and awakening

**Sources:** *Sigrdrífumál*, *Helreið Brynhildar*, related Eddic prose, and *Vǫlsunga saga*. **[EARLY POETIC/LATER CANONICAL SYNTHESIS]**

The valkyrie gives victory to Agnarr even though Óðinn has promised it to Hjálmgunnarr. Óðinn strikes her with a sleep-thorn, confines her behind a shield-wall or fire, and decrees marriage. Sigurðr reaches Hindarfjall, finds an armored figure whom he initially takes for a sleeping man, cuts away the armor, and discovers a woman. On waking, she gives him a drink of memory and teaches victory-runes, ale-runes, birth-runes, sea-runes, limb-runes, speech-runes, and mind-runes, followed by ethical counsel.

The *Vǫlsunga saga* identifies her as Brynhildr, gives her a fuller royal genealogy, and integrates the awakening into the tragic Burgundian cycle. Sigurðr later crosses the fire in Gunnarr’s shape, wins Brynhildr for Gunnarr, and places a sword between them. Recognition of the deception contributes to Sigurðr’s murder and Brynhildr’s suicide.

The widespread modern story of “a warrior goddess rescued from enchanted sleep by a destined hero” is therefore based on a real medieval complex but often suppresses the transmission problem, the coercive marriage sentence, the rune-teaching, the disguise, and the catastrophic second betrothal.

### The swan-valkyries and Vǫlundr

**Source:** *Vǫlundarkviða*. **[EARLY POETIC/REGIONAL VARIANT]**

Three brothers find three women spinning beside a lake, their swan garments nearby. The prose calls them valkyries and king’s daughters: Ǫlrún, Hlaðguðr Svanhvít, and Hervǫr Alvitr. They live with the brothers for seven winters; in the ninth they depart to fulfill battle-destiny and do not return. Two brothers search for them. Vǫlundr remains behind and is subsequently captured and maimed by King Níðuðr.

The women’s function here is neither selecting battlefield dead nor serving Óðinn. The passage links valkyries with migratory swan-maidens, textile work, autonomous departure, and the abandonment that begins Vǫlundr’s catastrophe.

### Hildr and the everlasting battle

**Sources:** Bragi Boddason’s *Ragnarsdrápa* fragments, Snorri’s *Skáldskaparmál*, *Sörla þáttr*, and related versions. **[EARLY SKALDIC CORE/LATER VARIANTS]**

Hildr stands between her father Hǫgni and her abductor or lover Heðinn. In the best-known form, the armies fight; Hildr revives the dead each night so battle resumes endlessly. Some versions make her a deliberate instigator, others a mediator whose actions fail, and later Christianized versions arrange an eventual ending.

Because *hildr* is also the common noun “battle” and a valkyrie name, poetic and personal meanings continually overlap. Whether the earliest Hildr was conceived specifically as a valkyrie cannot be settled in every passage.

### *Darraðarljóð*: the loom of battle

**Source:** *Brennu-Njáls saga* 157, composed c. 1280, describing a vision at Caithness on Good Friday during the Battle of Clontarf, 23 April 1014. The poem may be substantially older than the saga. **[MEDIEVAL TRANSMISSION/POSSIBLE ELEVENTH-CENTURY POETRY]**

A man named Dǫrruðr looks through an opening and sees twelve women at a loom. Their warp and weft are human entrails; severed heads are loom weights; a sword is the beater; arrows form the reels or heddles. They chant that the bloody web is stretched for the approaching battle. They name rulers and announce death across Ireland. After finishing, they tear the fabric apart; six ride north and six south.

The poem fuses female textile production with battlefield dismemberment, prophecy, and causation. It may encode a Norse perspective on Clontarf, but it is not neutral historical reporting. Nor does it prove that women enacted a comparable gut-weaving ritual. Its horror is poetic and visionary.

### Kára’s fatal protection

**Source:** later *Hrómundar saga Gripssonar*, reporting the lost *Káruljóð*. **[LATE/FRAGMENTARY]**

Kára flies above Helgi in swan form and magically protects him in battle. Helgi swings too high and cuts off her leg, destroying her power; he is then killed. The episode continues the Helgi–valkyrie reincarnation scheme but survives too late to reconstruct confidently as Viking-Age belief.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### What is securely absent

No temple, sanctuary, priesthood, festival, sacrificial tariff, dedicatory inscription, or unambiguous votive offering is securely identified as belonging specifically to “the Valkyries.” No surviving pre-Christian prayer addresses the collective. No place-name series demonstrates a territorial Valkyrie cult comparable to those argued for major gods.

Consequently, statements that “the Vikings worshipped the Valkyries at dedicated temples” are unsupported.

### Dísir and possible ritual context

The *dísablót*, sacrifice to the *dísir*, is textually attested in Old Norse literature, and the place-name Dísarsalr and the Disting at Uppsala have been connected with female powers. Because some valkyries can be understood as *dísir*, valkyrie ideas may have participated in this broader religious field. But the inference cannot be reversed: evidence for worship of *dísir* is not evidence for a distinct Valkyrie cult.

### Funeral and hall imagery

Horn-bearing women welcoming mounted warriors may express an aristocratic funerary ideology: the dead rider arrives at a hall and is ceremonially received. Jewelry bearing these motifs may have been protective, commemorative, status-bearing, or ritual. Contexts vary too much to assign one function.

### Human sacrifice

Human sacrifice is reported for parts of the Norse world by Adam of Bremen, saga texts, and Ibn Faḍlān’s account of a Rus funeral; archaeological interpretation is contested case by case. None of these securely documents a sacrifice **to the Valkyries**.

The elderly woman called the “Angel of Death” in Ibn Faḍlān’s Rus funeral account supervises the killing of an enslaved girl. Neil Price and others have compared her function with a valkyrie who transfers a person into death. That is a modern functional comparison, not Ibn Faḍlān’s identification. Recent work emphasizes the gendered agency of ritual execution while leaving the recipient deity unresolved ([Cambridge Archaeological Journal](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/cambridge-archaeological-journal/article/agents-of-death-reassessing-social-agency-and-gendered-narratives-of-human-sacrifice-in-the-viking-age/C98F99D68BCAA96CF83831D36BE15B19)).

The mutilated bodies in *Darraðarljóð* belong to poetic imagery, not evidence of a cult sacrifice performed by valkyrie priestesses.

### Magic

*Sigrdrífumál* associates its valkyrie with rune-knowledge and protective techniques. The late Bryggen charm instead treats a valkyrie-like being as something against which protection is required. These sources reveal magical valences at opposite poles—teacher and threat—but not an organized Valkyrie magical cult.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before c. 700: reconstructed background

**[COMPARATIVE RECONSTRUCTION]** The compound underlying Old English *wælcyrge* and Old Norse *valkyrja* probably belonged to Proto-Germanic or an early Northwest Germanic continuum. Female death, battle, and fate powers have analogues among Irish war-goddesses, Greek Keres and Erinyes, Roman Furies, and other Indo-European traditions. Parallel does not demonstrate descent from one reconstructed “Proto-Indo-European Valkyrie.”

### c. 700–900: lexical and visual emergence

The Old English glosses establish the word-family. Scandinavian armed-woman and horn-bearer images appear during the Viking Age. The Rök stone may employ a valkyrie-related wolf-rider image, though its interpretation remains disputed.

At this stage there is no surviving unified theology. The evidence points instead to a semantic complex joining female agency, battle, corpses, fate, terror, welcome, and elite death.

### c. 900–1050: skaldic and heroic prominence

*Hákonarmál* supplies the clearest early narrative of named valkyries operating for Óðinn. Battle-woman names flourish in skaldic diction. The Karlevi inscription shows such poetic language in public commemoration. The putative early form of *Darraðarljóð* belongs near the end of this period.

Valkyries serve aristocratic warrior ideology: death in battle can become selection, invitation, and incorporation into Óðinn’s host. Yet the Helgi and Vǫlundr materials show that heroic love, protection, swan transformation, and resistance to marriage were equally important.

### c. 1000–1300: conversion and manuscript preservation

Scandinavian conversion did not produce a documented decree specifically suppressing Valkyrie worship; no separate cult is known to have existed. Pagan ritual institutions and public sacrifices were displaced as Christianity became established. Valkyries survived primarily as poetic vocabulary, legendary characters, antiquarian mythology, and magical language.

Christian scribes and authors preserved most of the material now available. This does not make it wholly Christian invention, since older meters, language, and tenth-century poems are demonstrable. It does mean that selection, framing, and systematization belong to Christian-era manuscript culture.

Snorri’s *Edda* regularized diverse figures into Óðinn’s battle-service. The *Vǫlsunga saga* harmonized conflicting Brynhildr traditions. *Njáls saga* embedded *Darraðarljóð* in a Christian calendrical frame—Clontarf occurs on Good Friday.

### c. 1300–1700: learned survival

Valkyries persisted in manuscripts, antiquarian compilations, heroic ballads, German and Scandinavian legendary traditions, and learned treatments of Eddic poetry. The category could also degrade into a term for a witch-like or harmful woman, as Old English homiletic and later magical usage already anticipated.

There is no demonstrated continuous secret Valkyrie cult spanning conversion to modern Paganism.

### c. 1750–1870: Romantic-national reconstruction

The rediscovery, editing, and translation of Eddic and German heroic texts made Valkyries emblems of a supposedly recovered northern antiquity. Artists increasingly standardized them as armored maidens on horseback. National Romanticism fused Scandinavian, Icelandic, medieval German, and classical visual materials.

### 1870–1945: Wagnerian dominance and political appropriation

Wagner’s *Die Walküre*, premiered in Munich on 26 June 1870, transformed the international image. The Valkyries became nine mounted daughters of Wotan. The orchestral prelude to Act III—the “Ride of the Valkyries”—made furious aerial cavalry their defining scene ([Naxos synopsis and performance history](https://www.naxos.com/libretti/walkure.htm)).

Völkisch nationalism and later National Socialism appropriated Wagnerian and Nordic symbols in constructing a racialized Germanic past. Valkyries consequently entered nationalist monumental art and propaganda environments. This political use is modern; it should not be projected onto heterogeneous medieval Scandinavian religion.

“Operation Valkyrie” (*Unternehmen Walküre*) was originally a German military emergency plan and became associated with the 20 July 1944 attempt to overthrow Hitler. Its name reflects modern German heroic-military reception, not medieval ritual.

### 1945–present: global popular image and religious revival

The Wagnerian “Ride” became cinematic shorthand for aerial attack, most famously in *Apocalypse Now* (1979), where helicopter loudspeakers broadcast it during an assault. Comics, animation, fantasy novels, role-playing games, and video games have detached Valkyries from Óðinn and recast them as angelic warriors, resurrectors, psychopomps, alien soldiers, selectable character classes, or all-female military orders.

Modern Heathens sometimes honor individual valkyries, invoke them in memorial contexts, or understand them as *dísir*. Practices vary greatly. They are modern reconstructions from medieval sources and contemporary religious experience, not demonstrable continuations of an ancient Valkyrie liturgy.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Wagner’s *Die Walküre*

**[MODERN REINVENTION]** Wagner wrote both music and text. His nine are Brünnhilde, Gerhilde, Ortlinde, Waltraute, Schwertleite, Helmwige, Siegrune, Grimgerde, and Roßweiße. With partial exceptions such as Siegrune, their exact ensemble and genealogy are Wagnerian creations. The Act III libretto shows them fully armed, arriving on horseback with dead warriors across their saddles ([French historical libretto](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/La_Walkyrie/Acte_III)).

Brünnhilde initially obeys Wotan but chooses to protect Siegmund after understanding Wotan’s suppressed desire. Wotan strips her divinity, puts her to sleep, and surrounds her with fire. This transforms the Eddic punishment into a drama of compassion, law, paternal power, and individuation. Wagner’s Brünnhilde later becomes the redemptive center of the *Ring*.

The “Ride” is not a recovered Viking melody. It is a nineteenth-century orchestral composition whose motifs became more culturally influential than any medieval description.

### Visual arts

Peter Nicolai Arbo’s *Valkyrien* (1864/1869), Edward Robert Hughes’s *The Valkyrie’s Vigil* (1906), and Wagnerian stage design established flowing hair, breastplates, spears, shields, airborne horses, and theatrical helmets. The winged helmet became especially persistent despite lacking status as a standard Eddic attribute.

### Literature

Important modern transformations include:

- William Morris’s *The Story of Sigurd the Volsung* (1876), recasting the Vǫlsung material in Victorian epic.
- H. Rider Haggard’s *The Wanderer’s Necklace* and numerous Romantic or fantasy uses of warrior women.
- J. R. R. Tolkien’s posthumously published *The Legend of Sigurd and Gudrún*, which reconstructs the heroic cycle through scholarly and creative choices.
- Poul Anderson’s and other twentieth-century fantasy adaptations.
- Contemporary fantasy series that use “Valkyrie” for an autonomous warrior sisterhood: this is generally a modern genre convention.

### Comics, film, television, and games

- Marvel’s Valkyrie, principally Brunnhilde, debuted in stages around 1970 and developed into a superhero only loosely dependent on medieval tradition. Marvel’s Asgardians are a modern fictional cosmology.
- *Thor: Ragnarok* (2017) presents Valkyries as an elite Asgardian military order destroyed by Hela.
- *God of War* and related games turn Valkyries into bosses, imprisoned spirits, or hierarchical warriors.
- *Assassin’s Creed Valhalla*, *Valkyrie Profile*, *Record of Ragnarok*, and numerous Japanese manga, anime, and games radically reconfigure names and functions.
- “Valkyrie” is widely used for aircraft, military projects, vehicles, software, sports teams, and commercial products because it connotes speed, selection, female force, and glorious death.

These are legitimate parts of reception history but not evidence for ancient belief.

### Occultism and Paganism

Unlike Isis, Hecate, or the archangels, Valkyries did not acquire a major fixed role in Renaissance ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn, or classical grimoire hierarchies. Modern occult claims about ancient “Valkyrie initiations,” fixed sigils, chakras, planetary rulerships, or a ninefold esoteric order lack medieval documentation unless tied transparently to a modern author.

Germanic Heathen and Ásatrú communities may honor Valkyries alongside ancestors, *dísir*, and gods. Some practitioners treat named Valkyries as independent persons; others regard the names as poetic aspects of battle and fate. No reliable census isolates “Valkyrie worshippers,” and modern practice should not be homogenized.

### Nationalist and extremist appropriation

Norse and Germanic symbols have been used by inclusive Heathens, national heritage movements, neo-Nazis, white supremacists, militaries, feminists, and commercial entertainment. Context determines meaning. Nazi reception drew upon a nineteenth-century racialized “Nordic” imaginary and Wagner’s Germanic prestige, not a simple survival of Viking religion. Modern extremist deployment is documented reception, not the original meaning of every Valkyrie image.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Are Valkyries originally death-demons, fate-women, priestesses, or poetic inventions?

Older scholarship often proposed a linear evolution:

1. terrifying corpse-demons;
2. Óðinn’s battle servants;
3. beautiful heroic lovers;
4. courtly cup-bearers.

The sources do not date neatly enough to prove this sequence. Terrible and attractive aspects coexist. *Hákonarmál* already combines death-selection with prestigious reception; heroic poetry combines martial power with love; *Darraðarljóð* remains horrifying in a late manuscript.

Hilda Ellis Davidson connected Valkyries with death-belief and guardian women. Jan de Vries emphasized Germanic religious structures. Georges Dumézil sought Indo-European functional patterns. Later gender-focused scholarship treats them as figures through whom male-authored heroic literature negotiates female autonomy, marriage, kinship, and masculine death.

### Valkyries, Norns, and *dísir*

The strongest conclusion is overlap without complete equivalence. Skuld’s dual classification is explicit. Weaving, allotment, prophecy, and battlefield decision link the groups. Yet the terms remain distinguishable in many contexts. Karen Bek-Pedersen’s work stresses that supernatural-female classifications changed by period, place, and genre; rigid taxonomies can be artifacts of modern scholarship.

### The shieldmaiden question

A **valkyrie** is a supernatural or supernaturally empowered category; a **shieldmaiden** (*skjaldmær*) is a literary armed woman. Some characters, especially Brynhildr, blur the distinction. The existence of valkyrie stories neither proves nor disproves historical female warriors.

Birka grave Bj.581 contained a biologically female individual with a complete high-status weapons assemblage, two horses, and gaming pieces. Genomic analysis confirmed XX chromosomes and argued for identification as a high-ranking warrior ([Hedenstierna-Jonson et al., 2017](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/full/10.1002/ajpa.23308)). A later reassessment defended the grave’s integrity and warrior interpretation while acknowledging that social identity cannot be read mechanically from sex or grave goods ([Price et al., 2019](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/antiquity/article/viking-warrior-womenreassessing-birka-chamber-grave-bj581/7CC691F69FAE51DDE905D27E049FADCD)).

The cautious conclusion is:

- the occupant was biologically female;
- the grave was constructed with an exceptional martial assemblage;
- “warrior” is a plausible social interpretation;
- one grave does not prove widespread female combat units;
- it does not identify the deceased as a “Valkyrie”;
- literary shieldmaidens cannot simply be used as documentary reports.

### The armed-woman figurines

The traditional label “Valkyrie pendant” is often reasonable but not demonstrable. Alternatives include ritual performers, mythic heroines, human warriors, divine women, and deliberately ambiguous composites. Hårby strengthens the armed-female reading but does not settle the being’s precise identity.

### Brynhildr versus Sigrdrífa

The conflict arises from fragmentary manuscripts and saga synthesis, not mere scholarly pedantry. “Sigrdrífa” can be read either as a name or a title. *Vǫlsunga saga*’s unification became dominant because it offers a continuous narrative; it may preserve tradition, impose harmonization, or both.

### The Old English evidence

Old English *wælcyrge* proves that the compound and a female battle-terror concept were not exclusively Icelandic inventions. But glossary equivalences are not ethnographic descriptions. As Alaric Hall emphasizes, a gloss supplies a contextual translation; it cannot automatically be reversed into a complete definition of the native term ([Hall dissertation](https://www.alarichall.org.uk/ahphdful.pdf)).

### Discredited or unestablished popular claims

- “Freyja commanded all Valkyries”: unattested.
- “All Valkyries were Óðinn’s daughters”: Wagnerian, not medieval Norse doctrine.
- “There were always exactly nine”: Wagnerian standardization.
- “Valkyries had winged helmets or wings”: modern iconographic convention.
- “Every armed female pendant is a Valkyrie”: unproved.
- “Valkyries prove armies of Viking shieldmaidens”: invalid inference.
- “The Merseburg *idisi* are certainly Valkyries”: plausible comparison, not explicit identification.
- “Valkyries conducted human sacrifice”: no direct evidence.
- “An unbroken Valkyrie priesthood survived Christianization”: no documentary evidence.
- “Valkyrie” and “Norn” are synonyms: contradicted by the sources’ partial differentiation.
- “Sigrdrífa and Brynhildr were certainly always one person”: unprovable from the damaged and layered textual record.
