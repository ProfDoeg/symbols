# Bragi: Research Dossier

> **Class:** In Snorri Sturluson’s thirteenth-century system, Bragi is an **áss**, a male god of the Æsir, governing poetry, eloquence, and skaldic composition. Earlier poems place a Bragi in Valhǫll or call Bragi foremost among poets, but do not always establish unambiguously whether he is already a god, a supernatural court poet, or the remembered ninth-century skald Bragi Boddason.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest datable text containing the supernatural Bragi is probably the anonymous **Eiríksmál**, composed in Norway shortly after the death of King Eiríkr Bloodaxe, conventionally c. **954**. Bragi speaks with Óðinn in Valhǫll, but the poem never explicitly calls him a god. The first securely explicit classification as an **áss** is considerably later: *Lokasenna*, of disputed Viking-Age or early medieval composition but preserved only in the c. **1270** Codex Regius, addresses him as an áss; Snorri’s *Edda*, composed c. **1220**, systematically presents him as a god.
>
> **Central evidentiary conclusion:** Bragi has a substantial medieval literary existence but no presently identifiable archaeological cult. No securely attributed pre-Christian temple, dedication, cult image, runic invocation, festival, priesthood, sacrifice, place-name, or theophoric-name series demonstrates public worship of him. The evidence permits—but cannot prove—the influential hypothesis that the god developed from the posthumous mythologization of the ninth-century poet Bragi Boddason.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Bragi

The normalized Old Norse form is **Bragi**, genitive **Braga**. Medieval manuscripts employ ordinary Latin-script Old Norse spelling, not a distinctive divine logogram. Modern reflexes include Icelandic **Bragi**, Norwegian/Danish/Swedish **Brage**, German **Bragi/Brage**, and English **Bragi**.

The name is conventionally connected with Old Norse **bragr**, but that noun has overlapping semantic fields:

- *bragr*: poetry, poetic art, or a poem;
- *bragr*: the foremost or noblest member of a class;
- related poetic words include *bragnar*, “men, chiefs,” and *bragningr*, “king.”

Comparison has also been made with Old English **brego**, “prince, ruler,” and, more remotely, with Indo-European vocabulary for authoritative or ritually effective formulation, including Sanskrit *bráhman-* and Old Irish *bricht*. These comparisons are etymological proposals, not evidence of a common Indo-European “poetry god.”

**[Later canonical elaboration]** In *Gylfaginning* 26 Snorri reverses the likely direction of ordinary lexical derivation: poetry is called *bragr* from Bragi’s name, and an outstanding man or woman may be called the *bragr* of that class. Rudolf Simek and others regard that divine-name etymology as secondary systematization.

The similar **bragarfull/bragafull**, an oath or memorial cup mentioned in saga literature, cannot safely be translated “Bragi’s cup.” It is more commonly explained from *bragr* in the sense “foremost”: the principal or chieftain’s cup. No source says that it was offered to Bragi.

### Source-given descriptions and kennings

Snorri’s *Skáldskaparmál* gives the most compact medieval nomenclature:

- **Iðunnar verr** — “husband of Iðunn”;
- **frumsmiðr bragar** — “first maker/originator of poetry”;
- **hinn síðskeggja áss** — “the long-bearded god”;
- **sonr Óðins** — “son of Óðinn”;
- **skegg-Bragi** — “beard-Bragi,” Snorri’s explanation for calling a heavily bearded man a Bragi.

The Old Norse passage begins: *Hvernig skal kenna Braga? Svá at kalla hann Iðunnar ver, frumsmið bragar ok hinn síðskeggja Ás … ok sonr Óðins.* This is a thirteenth-century poetic taxonomy, not an independently documented cult-title list.

No ancient or medieval source gives Bragi a stable equivalence under *interpretatio Romana* or *interpretatio Graeca*. Modern comparisons with Apollo, Hermes, Orpheus, Ogma, the Muses, or Sarasvatī are comparative or devotional analogies, not historical syncretisms.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Bragi Boddason: the earlier human evidence

Before the supernatural Bragi appears, Old Norse tradition remembers **Bragi Boddason inn gamli**, “Bragi Boddason the Old.” He is conventionally placed in the ninth century and is normally treated as the earliest named skald from whom verse survives.

His reconstructed *Ragnarsdrápa* describes mythological scenes supposedly painted on a shield: Þórr fishing for the World Serpent, Gefjon ploughing Zealand from Sweden, Hamðir and Sǫrli’s attack on Jǫrmunrekkr, and the everlasting Hjaðningavíg. The surviving stanzas are quotations in much later works, particularly Snorri’s *Edda*; “*Ragnarsdrápa*” as a continuous poem is therefore an editorial reconstruction. *Skáldatal* associates the poet with Ragnarr loðbrók, Eysteinn beli, and Bjǫrn at Haugi, but those royal synchronisms are themselves learned medieval tradition. Recent metrical and linguistic research continues to debate how securely the fragments can be placed in the ninth century.

Nothing in Bragi Boddason’s surviving attributed verse calls him divine. His importance is that he predates every secure textual appearance of the supernatural Bragi and bears exactly the same name.

### *Eiríksmál*, c. 954

**[Securely attested early material]** This anonymous praise-poem was composed after Eiríkr Bloodaxe’s death, traditionally for Queen Gunnhildr. It dramatizes Valhǫll preparing to receive the dead king. Óðinn asks Bragi about a noise like the approach of a thousand men; Bragi answers and later participates in the dialogue surrounding Eiríkr’s arrival.

This is the earliest reasonably datable supernatural Bragi. Yet the text calls neither Bragi nor the heroic Sigmundr a god. Bragi may be:

1. an established divine court poet;
2. the human Bragi Boddason imagined among Óðinn’s honored dead;
3. an intermediate, already mythologized form from which Snorri’s god developed.

The poem’s very ambiguity is foundational to the deification debate.

### *Hákonarmál*, c. 961

**[Securely attested early material]** Eyvindr skáldaspillir composed this memorial poem after Hákon the Good’s death at Fitjar. It deliberately recalls *Eiríksmál*. Óðinn sends **Hermóðr and Bragi** to welcome Hákon into Valhǫll; Bragi reassures the suspicious king that he has the friendship of the gods.

Again, Bragi’s location and authority are supernatural, but the poem does not grammatically classify him as a god. He functions as Valhǫll’s praise-poet and ceremonial spokesman.

### *Grímnismál* 44

**[Early traditional material, date disputed]** In the catalogue of superlatives:

> *Óðinn ása … Bifrǫst brúa, en Bragi skálda*  
> “Óðinn [is foremost] of the Æsir … Bifrǫst of bridges, and Bragi of skalds.”

The stanza is preserved in Codex Regius, GKS 2365 4to, copied in Iceland around 1270, and in AM 748 I 4to. Its composition date is uncertain, and editors have sometimes treated it as an interpolation. Crucially, its grammar ranks Bragi among **skalds**, not among gods: Óðinn occupies the “Æsir” position. The line can consequently refer to the historical poet.

### *Lokasenna* 8–16

**[Early or high-medieval mythological poetry; exact date disputed]** Bragi attends Ægir’s feast with his wife Iðunn. When Loki returns after killing Ægir’s servant Fimafengr, Bragi offers him a horse, sword, and ring if he will refrain from provoking the gods. Loki accuses Bragi of cowardice and calls him a *bekkskrautuðr*, an “ornament of the bench.” Bragi threatens to take Loki’s head; Loki replies that Bragi is brave while seated. Iðunn restrains her husband. Loki then says she embraced the killer of her brother—apparently Bragi—though no surviving source names that brother or tells the killing.

The poem explicitly calls Bragi an **áss**, the best pre-Snorrian evidence that audiences knew a divine Bragi. Its dating, however, ranges widely; the physical witness remains c. 1270.

### *Sigrdrífumál* 15–17

**[Traditional rune-lore; transmitted in a thirteenth-century compilation]** The awakened valkyrie teaches Sigurðr where primordial runes were inscribed. Among a catalogue including Sleipnir’s teeth, a bear’s paw, an eagle’s beak, and a wolf’s claw is:

> *á Br […] tungu* / normalized **á Braga tungu**  
> “on Bragi’s tongue.”

The poem says that these runes were scraped away, mixed with sacred mead, and sent along many paths among Æsir, elves, Vanir, and humans.

This is the sole medieval basis for the popular formula “Bragi has runes carved on his tongue.” The text does **not** describe a permanent tattoo-like set of runes, specify their characters, or narrate Bragi acquiring them. Moreover, because Bragi is also a human personal name, the referent is not beyond dispute, though the divine Bragi is the conventional interpretation. The corresponding passage in *Vǫlsunga saga* has a significantly different catalogue and omits Bragi’s tongue.

### Snorri Sturluson’s *Edda*, c. 1220

**[Later canonical/learned systematization]**

In *Gylfaginning* 26, Bragi is one of the Æsir:

- renowned for wisdom;
- especially renowned for eloquence and command of language;
- most skilled in poetry;
- the supposed source of the noun *bragr*;
- husband of Iðunn.

In *Skáldskaparmál*, Bragi sits beside the visitor Ægir at an Æsir banquet and becomes the principal instructor in myth and poetic diction. He recounts:

- Þjazi’s seizure of Iðunn and Loki’s recovery of her;
- Skaði’s compensation and marriage;
- the origin and theft of the mead of poetry;
- numerous myths preserved to explain kennings.

Bragi is the narrator, not usually an actor in those embedded myths. His role makes poetry explain itself through its personified divine master. Elsewhere Snorri supplies his kennings, parentage, marriage, and beard.

### Other medieval notices

- **Grettis saga**, composed in substantially its present form in the fourteenth century, contains a verse using Bragi as Iðunn’s husband. **[Late medieval corroboration]**
- Lists of Óðinn’s sons sometimes include Bragi, but the relationship is otherwise poorly attested. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Saxo Grammaticus**, *Gesta Danorum* (c. 1200–1220), provides no clear independent Bragi dossier.
- **Tacitus**, *Germania* (98 CE), does not name or securely describe Bragi.
- **Adam of Bremen**, *Gesta Hammaburgensis* IV (c. 1070s), does not name Bragi among the Uppsala gods.
- The **Merseburg charms** (manuscript c. 900s), continental Germanic divine names, and the **Nibelungenlied** supply no Bragi evidence.
- No presently catalogued runic inscription securely names Bragi as a god.
- Wagner’s *Ring des Nibelungen* (librettos developed 1848–1874) contains no Bragi character. Wagner’s absence is important because modern summaries sometimes project a standardized “Norse pantheon” into works that actually select and transform a much narrower body of material.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain

Bragi’s securely sourced domain is not all “art” or even all music. Medieval sources specifically associate him with:

- skalds and skaldic poetry;
- eloquent or decorous speech;
- technical poetic diction;
- court praise;
- the reception and commemoration of kings.

Modern extensions to music generally, songwriting, storytelling, theatre, visual art, inspiration, education, or “creativity” are reasonable devotional expansions but are not medievally documented offices.

Óðinn, not Bragi, remains the central possessor and distributor of poetic inspiration in the myth of the mead of poetry. The traditions therefore overlap without making the gods interchangeable: Óðinn seizes the intoxicating substance of poetry; Bragi embodies cultivated poetic speech and the court poet’s social office.

### Attributes

- **Tongue:** *Sigrdrífumál* places runes “on Bragi’s tongue.” The familiar image of permanently rune-carved speech is a modern concretization of an obscure catalogue.
- **Long beard:** securely given only by Snorri’s kenning list. It became his chief visual marker in Romantic art.
- **Wisdom, eloquence, verbal command:** Snorri, *Gylfaginning* 26.
- **Poetic priority:** Snorri calls him *frumsmiðr bragar*.
- **Ceremonial welcome:** *Eiríksmál* and *Hákonarmál* place him in Valhǫll.
- **Warrior prowess:** conspicuously contested. Bragi threatens Loki, but Loki repeatedly calls him cowardly. No surviving narrative shows Bragi fighting.
- **Killing of Iðunn’s brother:** implied only by Loki’s accusation. The victim and circumstances are otherwise unknown.

There are no securely attested sacred animals, plants, colors, numbers, weekdays, planets, weapons, or special substances belonging to Bragi. Mead belongs broadly to feasting and poetic inspiration but is never documented as Bragi’s exclusive sacrament.

### Iconography

No Viking-Age or medieval image can be securely identified as Bragi. There is no surviving inscribed idol, cult statue, bracteate, picture stone, rune stone, or manuscript miniature naming him.

The recognizable modern iconography is Romantic:

- **Nils Jakob Blommér, *Idun and Brage* (1846):** Bragi appears as a bearded harpist beside apple-bearing Iðunn.
- **Harald Conradsen medal (1851):** Bragi and Iðunn in classicizing costume, again with harp and apple.
- **Carl Ehrenberg (1883):** paired illustrations of Bragi/Iðunn and Baldr/Nanna.
- **Lorenz Frølich (1895):** Bragi and Iðunn and scenes from *Lokasenna*.
- **Emil Doepler the Younger, *Walhall* (1901):** a further national-Romantic visualization.

The harp or lyre is therefore **modern iconographic shorthand**, not a medieval attribute. The ancient Scandinavian skald is not documented as a harp-playing minstrel.

## 4. Genealogy and divine relations

### Genealogy

| Relationship | Source | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|
| Son of Óðinn | *Skáldskaparmál* kenning list, c. 1220 | Later learned systematization; unattested in early poetry |
| Mother | No medieval source names her | Unknown |
| Husband of Iðunn | *Lokasenna* prose frame and dialogue; *Gylfaginning* 26; *Skáldskaparmál*; later saga verse | Well-attested medieval relationship |
| Children | None named | No evidence |
| Siblings | Only inferentially the other “sons of Óðinn” | No individual sibling bond is narrated |
| Iðunn’s brother | Implied by *Lokasenna* to have been killed by Bragi | Unnamed; otherwise unattested |

The modern claim that **Gunnlǫð is Bragi’s mother** is not found in the Eddas. It is produced by combining Snorri’s “son of Óðinn” with Óðinn’s sexual acquisition of the mead from Gunnlǫð. The texts never say that encounter conceived Bragi. It must be labeled **modern invention or unsourced modern inference**.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Óðinn — conversation in Valhǫll.** Óðinn asks Bragi about the tumult heralding Eiríkr Bloodaxe’s arrival; Bragi answers. *Eiríksmál*, c. 954. **[Securely attested early material; Bragi’s divine status ambiguous]**
- **Óðinn — receives a commission with Hermóðr.** Óðinn sends Bragi and Hermóðr to welcome Hákon. *Hákonarmál* 14–16, c. 961. **[Securely attested early material; divine status not explicit]**
- **Hermóðr — joint embassy.** Hermóðr and Bragi together meet the dead Hákon at Valhǫll. *Hákonarmál*, c. 961. **[Securely attested early material]**
- **Loki — attempted bribery, insult contest, and threat.** Bragi offers Loki horse, sword, and ring to keep peace; Loki calls him cowardly; Bragi threatens decapitation. *Lokasenna* 8–15, uncertain composition date, manuscript c. 1270. **[Medieval mythological attestation]**
- **Iðunn — marriage and intervention.** Iðunn identifies Bragi as her husband and restrains him during his quarrel with Loki. *Lokasenna* 16; confirmed by Snorri. **[Medieval attestation; later canonical elaboration]**
- **Iðunn — alleged killing of her brother.** Loki says Iðunn embraced her brother’s killer, contextually Bragi. No surviving account supplies the brother’s name or episode. *Lokasenna* 17. **[Isolated, unexplained attestation]**
- **Ægir — banquet conversation.** Bragi sits next to Ægir and answers his questions about gods, myths, kennings, and poetry. *Skáldskaparmál*, c. 1220. **[Later canonical framing narrative]**

Bragi recounts myths involving Loki, Iðunn, Þjazi, Skaði, Njǫrðr, Óðinn, Hœnir, Kvasir, the dwarfs Fjalarr and Galarr, Suttungr, Gunnlǫð, Þórr, and others. Narrating their acts is not itself a documented personal interaction; they should not be converted into invented Bragi relationships.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

Bragi has no surviving independent adventure-cycle comparable to those of Þórr, Óðinn, or Loki. His narrative record consists of three compact roles.

### The heavenly reception of dead kings

In *Eiríksmál*, Bragi is already in Valhǫll when Eiríkr arrives. He serves as Óðinn’s interlocutor and helps articulate the king’s entry into heroic immortality.

Eyvindr’s *Hákonarmál* adapts this scheme for Hákon the Good. Valkyries select the king; Óðinn orders Hermóðr and Bragi to meet him; Bragi assures Hákon of divine favor. The poem gives a Christian-associated king a traditional pagan reception and thereby turns poetic commemoration into political reconciliation.

**Development:** This is the strongest bridge between Bragi Boddason and the god. A famous skald imagined after death at Óðinn’s court could naturally become the paradigmatic heavenly skald. The texts, however, never narrate his apotheosis.

### The flyting with Loki

At Ægir’s feast, Bragi is the first god to engage the returning Loki. His offer of treasures performs a court poet’s peace-making and gift-distribution vocabulary. Loki attacks precisely the weakness of praise poetry: words unsupported by deeds. Bragi’s threat is answered with the charge that he is brave only on the bench.

Iðunn’s intervention prevents escalation, but Loki then darkens the marriage by alleging that Bragi killed her brother. Because the lost episode is nowhere recovered, attempts to reconstruct it as a complete myth are speculation.

The poem positions Bragi as the defender of socially regulated speech against destructive satire. Jan de Vries consequently interpreted him less as a generic “arts god” than as a personification of socially constructive praise.

### Bragi as teacher of poetic myth

In *Skáldskaparmál*, Bragi explains why kennings mean what they mean. He tells the myths behind “Þórr’s deliverance,” Iðunn’s abduction, Skaði’s compensation, the mead of poetry, gold-kennings, and heroic circumlocutions.

This is a **Snorrian framing construction**, not evidence that pre-Christian worshippers believed Bragi personally witnessed every narrated event. Its literary function is nevertheless important: the god of poetic competence becomes the internal authority for a Christian Icelandic handbook designed to preserve skaldic language.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Positive evidence

There is no secure positive cultic dossier. Medieval poetry shows knowledge of Bragi as a supernatural or divine person, but literary attestation alone does not demonstrate an organized cult.

### What is not attested

No evidence presently establishes:

- a Bragi temple, shrine, sacred grove, or named cult center;
- an image bearing his name;
- a priesthood or hereditary religious office;
- a festival or calendar day;
- animal or human sacrifice to him;
- an invocation in a runic inscription;
- a votive dedication;
- a securely Bragi-derived sacred place-name;
- a historical “Bragi cup” rite;
- divination, healing, initiation, or funerary rites under his patronage;
- temple prostitution or cult violence;
- state sponsorship or suppression of a distinct Bragi cult.

The absence is particularly notable because Tacitus, Adam of Bremen, archaeological evidence from major Scandinavian ritual sites, and the place-name record preserve information about other Germanic gods. It does not prove that nobody privately invoked Bragi; it means that such worship cannot currently be documented.

The personal name **Bragi** also cannot automatically be treated as theophoric. It may derive directly from *bragr* or from the prestige of the poet Bragi Boddason.

### Modern offerings

Contemporary Heathens sometimes offer mead, ale, poems, songs, recitations, written manuscripts, or acts of learning to Bragi. These are **modern reconstructionist or experiential practices**, not demonstrably continuous rites. No medieval source supplies a Bragi liturgy.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Ninth century: the poet

Bragi Boddason is remembered as an exceptionally early master of *dróttkvætt*. Whether every transmitted stanza is genuinely ninth-century remains debated, but the tradition of an ancestral “first skald” is older than Snorri’s divine taxonomy.

### Mid-tenth century: Valhǫll’s poet

*Eiríksmál* and *Hákonarmál* place Bragi beside Óðinn as the ceremonial voice receiving dead rulers. This is the earliest datable supernatural phase. The poems do not disclose whether audiences distinguished god and deified skald.

### Tenth to twelfth centuries: proposed apotheosis

Heinz Klingenberg and Margaret Clunies Ross have argued that the famous skald’s elevation may have occurred during the later tenth century. The model is:

1. historical poet;
2. idealized dead poet at Óðinn’s court;
3. heavenly prototype of praise-poetry;
4. independent god of poetry.

This is a persuasive reconstruction, not a recorded historical event. No source says, “Bragi Boddason became a god.”

An alternative model begins with an older divine or ritual title connected with *bragr*, “foremost one” or effective poetic formulation. On that view, the human poet bore an already meaningful name, and later transmission caused the figures to converge. The absence of independent cult evidence makes this difficult to prove.

### Conversion period

Norway and Iceland underwent political Christianization around the late tenth and early eleventh centuries. No source documents the closing of a Bragi sanctuary, destruction of his image, or forced abandonment of a Bragi-specific rite. His tradition survived primarily because Christian Icelandic authors and scribes preserved poetry and poetic technique.

### Thirteenth century: Snorrian consolidation

Snorri turns the fragmentary heavenly skald into a clearly classified áss with:

- a divine father;
- a divine wife;
- a defined occupational domain;
- physical characterization;
- an etymological explanation;
- a major role as the narrator of *Skáldskaparmál*.

This is the dominant “canonical” Bragi known today, but it is the endpoint of the medieval evidence rather than its uncontested beginning.

### Fourteenth to seventeenth centuries: manuscript afterlife

The Eddic and Snorrian texts circulated in Icelandic manuscripts. Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda was copied c. 1270; the principal *Edda* manuscripts are fourteenth-century or later. The mythological poems therefore survive across a significant temporal gap from their proposed composition.

Codex Regius came to Bishop Brynjólfur Sveinsson in 1643 and later entered the Danish royal collection. Antiquarian publication transformed Bragi from a manuscript figure into a European literary symbol.

### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries: Romantic reinvention

Bragi became a personification of northern poetry:

- Friedrich Gottlieb Klopstock invoked him in such works as *Wingolf* (1747), *Braga* (1771), and *Die Kunst Tialfs* (1771).
- Friedrich David Gräter edited *Bragur: Ein litterarisches Magazin der deutschen und nordischen Vorzeit* (1791–1812).
- Adam Oehlenschläger included “Bragis Sang om Gefion” in *Nordens Guder* (1819).
- Blommér’s 1846 painting and subsequent illustrations fixed the old, bearded harpist beside Iðunn.
- Marcellus Emants’s *Godenschemering* (1883) gave Bragi a small role indebted to *Lokasenna*.

This period invented much of his now-standard visual identity.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Modern Ásatrú and Heathenry revived worship of Norse gods from the 1970s onward. Bragi occupies a specialized rather than central place, principally among poets, musicians, writers, and performers. There is no reliable census of exclusive Bragi worshippers and no continuous institutional lineage from medieval cult.

Contemporary Heathenry includes inclusive, reconstructionist, eclectic, ethnic, and racist or “folkish” currents. Norse symbols and gods have been appropriated by nationalist and white-supremacist movements, but no documented extremist cult or ideology centers specifically on Bragi. Claims beyond that would overstate the evidence.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Occult and esoteric traditions

Bragi is not prominent in Renaissance planetary magic, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or the classic ceremonial grimoires. Modern tables assigning him a planet, zodiacal sign, tarot card, rune, chakra, color, crystal, incense, or weekday are modern correspondences. They have no basis in medieval Bragi sources unless their creators explicitly identify them as personal or system-specific innovations.

The idea of Bragi’s tongue bearing a permanent rune set has become attractive in rune magic and tattoo culture. Its ancient kernel is *Sigrdrífumál*’s single phrase; detailed designs and claims that the runes granted omniscience or magical singing are modern elaborations.

### Modern Heathenry

Common modern practices include:

- dedicating original verse;
- reciting poetry at a blót or sumbel;
- pouring mead;
- invoking Bragi before writing or performance;
- treating learning a traditional meter as an offering.

These practices are religiously real for their practitioners but historically modern reconstructions. “Unverified personal gnosis” concerning Bragi’s personality, preferred offerings, colors, or messages should not be backdated into pre-Christian religion.

### Literature, art, and popular media

Bragi’s modern public image derives disproportionately from nineteenth-century Romanticism. The harp, white or golden beard, flowing robes, and gentle elderly demeanor originate in modern art rather than identifiable medieval iconography.

In the strategy game **Age of Mythology** (2002), Bragi is a selectable Norse minor god, embedding him in a tidy hierarchical game-pantheon unlike the uncertain medieval record. Other games and fantasy works use “Bragi” for bards or skalds, sometimes as ordinary human names. Such characters must not automatically be treated as depictions of the god.

The historical poet and the god are also frequently fused in popular reference works, which may call him “a mortal poet elevated to godhood” as settled fact. The underlying scholarship regards that as likely or plausible, not demonstrated.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was the god originally the poet?

This is the principal controversy.

**Evidence favoring deification:**

- the human Bragi Boddason is chronologically earlier than secure divine attestations;
- *Eiríksmál* and *Hákonarmál* place Bragi in Valhǫll without calling him a god;
- *Grímnismál* calls Bragi best of skalds rather than placing him in the “gods” category;
- there is no archaeological or toponymic cult evidence;
- Snorri’s fully developed god is late;
- the role exactly reproduces the ideal court skald’s office.

**Evidence or arguments against treating the case as closed:**

- *Lokasenna* explicitly calls him an áss and may preserve pre-Snorrian material;
- a divine name related to *bragr* could precede the poet;
- the early poems may assume rather than explain Bragi’s divine identity;
- absence of cult evidence is not proof of nonexistence;
- oral tradition can long predate manuscripts.

The careful conclusion is that posthumous mythologization is a strong modern reconstruction, perhaps the prevailing one, but not a documented apotheosis.

### Bragi as personified praise

Jan de Vries emphasized the social opposition between praise and blame. The court poet confers durable reputation, while the satirist can destroy honor. Bragi’s effort to restrain Loki in *Lokasenna* dramatizes regulated praise confronting antisocial invective. This interpretation fits the evidence better than the broad modern title “god of all creativity,” although it remains structural interpretation.

### Fertility theory

De Vries also connected Bragi’s marriage to rejuvenating Iðunn with life-renewing speech and possibly fertility. Klingenberg objected that the evidence does not require a fertility cult: imperishable poetic fame and Iðunn’s renewal form a literary pairing without proving agricultural worship. No sacrifice, sanctuary, or ritual confirms Bragi as a fertility god.

### Óðinn hypothesis

Some writers have proposed that Bragi is an epithet, hypostasis, or poetic aspect of Óðinn. The shared poetry domain encourages this, but medieval sources treat them as separate speakers in *Eiríksmál* and as father and son in Snorri. “Bragi is simply Óðinn” is therefore a speculative reconstruction, not an attested identity.

### Textual limitations

The evidence is structurally late:

- ninth- and tenth-century skaldic poems survive by quotation in later manuscripts;
- Eddic poem composition dates are disputed;
- Codex Regius was copied c. 1270;
- Snorri wrote as a Christian antiquarian and poetic theorist;
- the main *Edda* manuscripts postdate the work’s composition;
- the sources preserve literary selection, not a complete census of pre-Christian belief.

The 2024 electronic edition of GKS 2365 4to supplies a diplomatic transcription and manuscript history. Modern standard translations include Carolyne Larrington’s *Poetic Edda*, Henry Adams Bellows’s older public-domain version, and Anthony Faulkes’s edition and translation of Snorri’s *Edda*. The Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages project is essential for Bragi Boddason and the royal memorial poems.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- **Gunnlǫð was Bragi’s mother:** unattested modern inference.
- **The bragarfull was necessarily drunk to Bragi:** etymologically and textually unproved.
- **Bragi had a known Viking temple or priesthood:** no evidence.
- **A particular rune was Bragi’s personal rune:** no medieval evidence.
- **Bragi’s harp is ancient iconography:** nineteenth-century convention.
- **He governed every art form:** modern expansion.
- **His tongue permanently bore a specified rune row:** elaboration of one obscure poetic line.
- **The mortal poet’s deification is recorded history:** scholarly reconstruction, not a recorded event.
- **Wagner gave Bragi a role in the *Ring*:** false; he is absent.

| c. ninth century | Traditional period of Bragi Boddason; poems attributed to him survive only through later quotation. |
| c. 954 | *Eiríksmál* places Bragi speaking with Óðinn in Valhǫll—the earliest datable supernatural attestation. |
| c. 961 | Eyvindr skáldaspillir’s *Hákonarmál* has Óðinn send Hermóðr and Bragi to welcome Hákon. |
| Later tenth century, hypothesized | Proposed period for Bragi Boddason’s mythologization or apotheosis; no direct account survives. |
| c. tenth–thirteenth centuries | Possible composition range assigned to relevant Eddic poems; individual datings remain disputed. |
| 98 CE, c. 970s, c. 1070s | Tacitus, the Merseburg manuscript, and Adam of Bremen respectively provide no Bragi attestation. |
| c. 1200–1220 | Saxo’s *Gesta Danorum* supplies no clear Bragi tradition. |
| c. 1220 | Snorri’s *Edda* explicitly systematizes Bragi as an áss, poet, eloquent speaker, Iðunn’s husband, Óðinn’s son, and long-bearded god. |
| c. 1270 | Codex Regius GKS 2365 4to preserves *Grímnismál*, *Lokasenna*, and *Sigrdrífumál*. |
| c. 1300–1350 | Principal surviving manuscripts of Snorri’s *Edda* are copied. |
| Fourteenth century | *Grettis saga* reaches substantially its surviving form and contains a poetic reference to Bragi as Iðunn’s husband. |
| 1643 | Brynjólfur Sveinsson obtains Codex Regius, initiating its early-modern scholarly history. |
| 1662 | Codex Regius is presented to King Frederik III of Denmark. |
| 1747–1771 | Klopstock uses Bragi/Braga as a symbol of northern poetic inspiration. |
| 1791–1812 | Gräter publishes the antiquarian literary periodical *Bragur*. |
| 1819 | Oehlenschläger publishes “Bragis Sang om Gefion.” |
| 1846 | Blommér paints *Idun and Brage*, influential in fixing harp-and-apple iconography. |
| 1851 | Conradsen’s medal depicts Bragi and Iðunn in classicizing form. |
| 1883 | Ehrenberg’s illustration and Emants’s *Godenschemering* contribute to Romantic literary reception. |
| 1895 | Lorenz Frølich illustrates Bragi/Iðunn and the *Lokasenna* quarrel. |
| 1901 | Doepler includes Bragi in *Walhall* imagery. |
| 1970s onward | Modern Ásatrú and Heathen movements establish new worship of Norse gods; Bragi receives specialized devotional attention. |
| 1985 | Codex Regius is returned from Denmark to Iceland. |
| 2002 | *Age of Mythology* presents Bragi as a selectable Norse minor god. |
| 2024 | The Arnamagnæan institutes publish a new electronic edition of GKS 2365 4to. |
| 2026 | Bragi remains a living figure in Heathen devotion, popular media, poetry, and online rune culture, but no evidence has established continuity with an ancient institutional cult. |
