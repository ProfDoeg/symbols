# Vali: Research Dossier

**Class:** Áss, a male god of the Æsir in the medieval Icelandic mythographic tradition; more specifically, the divinely generated avenger of Baldr. Saxo Grammaticus preserves a markedly different, euhemerized counterpart named **Bous/Boe**, a mortal or demigodlike warrior.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Váli is first securely recoverable in Eddic poetry, especially *Vǫluspá* 32–34 and *Vafþrúðnismál* 51. Their dates of composition are disputed, commonly placed around the tenth century or c. 1000, but the earliest surviving physical witness is the late-thirteenth-century **Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda**, GKS 2365 4to. No known Viking-Age inscription, image, or securely Váli-derived place-name predates that manuscript evidence. The distinction is essential: a poem may be centuries older than its manuscript, but that earlier dating is philological reconstruction, not a surviving contemporary document.

Throughout this dossier:

- **[EA]** securely attested early material
- **[LC]** later canonical elaboration
- **[RV]** regional or textual variant
- **[LS]** learned systematization
- **[SY]** syncretistic identification
- **[MA]** medieval or early-modern accretion
- **[F]** folklore
- **[MSR]** modern scholarly reconstruction
- **[MI]** modern invention or adaptation

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Orientation

Váli is an exceptionally concentrated mythic figure. In the principal Icelandic tradition, Óðinn fathers him on Rindr for the sole immediate purpose of avenging Baldr. He grows or becomes capable of combat with supernatural speed and kills Baldr’s slayer while only one night old, before washing his hands or combing his hair. In *Vafþrúðnismál*, he and Víðarr survive the cosmic destruction associated with Ragnarǫk and inhabit the gods’ sanctuaries after Surtr’s fire has gone out. Snorri Sturluson subsequently classifies Váli among the Æsir, calls him courageous and an excellent shot, and systematizes poetic descriptions of him as Baldr’s avenger and Hǫðr’s enemy and killer.

The tradition does **not** securely establish Váli as a general “god of vengeance,” “god of justice,” “god of rebirth,” solar god, spring god, or patron of Valentine’s Day. Those are extrapolations from his single mythic function or modern constructions.

### Names and forms

#### Váli

**Old Norse:** *Váli*, conventionally normalized with a long á.

This is the standard name in *Vǫluspá*, *Vafþrúðnismál*, *Baldrs draumar*, *Hyndluljóð*, and Snorri’s *Edda*. The name is not preserved in an authenticated pre-Christian runic inscription referring to the god.

Its etymology is unsettled:

- **[MSR] “Little Vanir-god.”** Detter and Neckel connected the name with a reconstructed Germanic diminutive such as *\*Wanilōn*, “little Van” or “little member of the Vanir.” This is formally ingenious but mythologically awkward because every surviving classification makes Váli an Áss and son of Óðinn, not one of the Vanir.
- **[MSR] “Little warrior.”** A competing reconstruction relates it to a Germanic martial stem and interprets the name as “little warrior.” This suits the precocious child-avenger but remains reconstruction rather than an attested Old Norse gloss.
- **[MSR] Connection with *valr*, “the slain.”** The resemblance to the productive *val-* vocabulary surrounding Óðinn, the battle-slain, and Valhǫll has encouraged interpretations such as “of the slain.” The morphology is not universally accepted.
- **Unestablished:** No medieval text explains the name.

#### Áli

Snorri’s *Gylfaginning* gives the double form **“Áli or Váli”** (*Áli eða Váli*). **[LC/LS]** Áli is therefore a medieval textual alternative, not necessarily proof of an independent earlier god later fused with Váli. Some lists of Óðinn’s sons transmit both names in ways that may reflect duplication during compilation.

#### Bous, Boe, Boum

In Saxo Grammaticus’s Latin *Gesta Danorum*, the avenger is **Bous**, with inflected forms including *Boum*. **[RV]** Saxo’s figure is the son of Othinus and Rinda and kills Hotherus, but he dies of his wounds the following day. His name is not linguistically equivalent to *Váli*. It has generally been related to Old Norse *Búi*, “dweller” or “inhabitant,” or a cognate personal name. The equivalence is functional and narrative, not etymological.

### Documentary epithets and relational titles

Snorri’s *Skáldskaparmál* gives a compact medieval catalogue of *Válakenningar*, ways to refer to Váli:

- **son of Óðinn and Rindr**
- **stepson of Frigg**
- **brother of the Æsir**
- **Baldr’s avenging Áss**
- **enemy of Hǫðr**
- **slayer of Hǫðr**
- **inhabitant of the fathers’ homesteads**

These are **[LC/LS]** learned poetic classifications written down in the thirteenth century, although some may derive from older poetic diction. They should not automatically be treated as distinct cult titles.

Calling Váli simply “the Avenger,” “God of Vengeance,” “God of Eternal Light,” or “God of Rebirth” is **[MI]** unless explicitly identified as a modern devotional or interpretive title.

### The namesake “Váli, son of Loki”

A different Váli appears in Snorri’s account of Loki’s punishment. The gods seize Loki’s sons “Váli and Nari or Narfi”; Váli is transformed into a wolf and tears apart his brother, whose entrails are then used to bind Loki. This is **[LC/disputed textual tradition]** and is attested explicitly only in Snorri’s prose.

The prose conclusion to *Lokasenna* instead says that Loki was bound with the entrails of his son Nari, while Narfi became a wolf. That configuration contains no Váli. Scholars have therefore proposed that:

1. Snorri knew a genuinely different version with a son named Váli; or
2. Snorri or his source misunderstood a phrase such as *Vála vígbǫnd*, “bonds from slaughter,” and generated a second Váli; or
3. manuscript transmission redistributed the names Váli, Nari, and Narfi.

There is no source that explicitly identifies Óðinn’s son Váli with Loki’s son. They must be catalogued as two separate figures or as a disputed textual duplication, not harmonized into one biography.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Manuscript reality

The mythology comes chiefly from Christian-period Icelandic manuscripts. There is no autograph pagan manuscript of an Eddic poem.

The principal witness, **GKS 2365 4to**, the Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda, was written in Iceland in the late thirteenth century, usually c. 1270. *Baldrs draumar* is absent from it and survives uniquely among medieval manuscripts in **AM 748 I 4to**, generally dated c. 1300–1325. The Copenhagen Arnamagnæan electronic edition confirms that this is the only medieval manuscript containing that poem. [Editiones Arnamagnæanæ Electronicæ](https://eae.ku.dk/q?p=eae%2Fvols%2Ftext%2F1)

Dating the poems to the Viking Age rests on meter, language, intertextual relationships, worldview, and oral-formulaic features. Estimates remain disputed and do not supply exact composition years.

### *Vǫluspá*

**Language:** Old Norse.  
**Probable composition:** commonly late tenth or around the year 1000; disputed.  
**Principal manuscripts:** Codex Regius, c. 1270; Hauksbók, early fourteenth century; fragments and quotations in Snorri.

In the Baldr passage, Rindr bears Váli in the western halls. Óðinn’s son, one night old, fights or kills; he does not wash his hands or comb his head before carrying Baldr’s adversary to the pyre. The wording makes the avenger a son of Óðinn but does not name Hǫðr in the core vengeance lines. Modern stanza numbering varies by edition because editors combine the witnesses differently. The Codex Regius text is available in a manuscript-oriented edition from Open Book Publishers. [*Vǫluspá*, Codex Regius version](https://books.openbookpublishers.com/10.11647/obp.0308.01.pdf)

**What it establishes [EA]:**

- Rindr is the avenger’s mother.
- Váli is Óðinn’s son.
- His birth and vengeance follow Baldr’s death.
- He acts when only one night old.
- He observes an unwashed, uncombed state until the vengeance is carried out.

**What it does not establish:**

- It does not narrate his conception.
- It does not say that Rindr freely consented.
- It does not describe Váli’s appearance, weapon, temple, priesthood, or cult.
- It does not explicitly explain the unwashed state as a historical initiation rite.

### *Baldrs draumar*

**Language:** Old Norse.  
**Composition:** frequently assigned to the tenth century, but the dating is contested.  
**Surviving medieval manuscript:** AM 748 I 4to, c. 1300–1325.

In stanza 11, the dead seeress tells Óðinn that Rindr will bear Váli in the western halls. Óðinn’s son will fight when one night old and will neither wash his hands nor comb his hair before carrying Baldr’s enemy to the pyre. The lines closely resemble *Vǫluspá*, raising unresolved questions of common inheritance, direct borrowing, or interpolation. The physical manuscript is later than Codex Regius even if the poem is older. [*Baldrs draumar* text and manuscript notice](https://www.heimskringla.no/wiki/Balders_dr%C3%B8mme_ell._Vegtamskv%C3%A6de_%28Baldrsdraumar_e%C3%BEa_Vegtamskvi%C3%BEa%29)

### *Vafþrúðnismál*

**Language:** Old Norse.  
**Probable composition:** often assigned to the tenth century; uncertain.  
**Witness:** Codex Regius, c. 1270.

At stanza 51 the giant Vafþrúðnir answers Óðinn’s question about the world after Surtr’s fire:

> *Víðarr ok Váli byggja vé goða …*  
> “Víðarr and Váli will inhabit the gods’ sanctuaries …”

Móði and Magni will possess Mjǫllnir after Þórr falls. This is the earliest recoverable evidence for Váli as a survivor of Ragnarǫk. It does not say he rules the renewed world alone or that he has been resurrected; he survives. [Old Norse and English text of *Vafþrúðnismál* 51](https://wevikings.com/library/vafthrudnismal-eng/)

### *Hyndluljóð* and *Vǫluspá in skamma*

**Language:** Old Norse.  
**Composition:** disputed; commonly considered later than the oldest Eddic poetry.  
**Witness:** Flateyjarbók, GKS 1005 fol., written 1387–1394.

Stanza 29, conventionally included in the embedded *Vǫluspá in skamma*, states explicitly that Baldr’s brother was quickly born: Váli avenged the deed, slew his brother’s killer, and was one night old. This is important because, as John Lindow observes, it is the Eddic passage that most explicitly joins the proper name Váli to the actual vengeance. [John Lindow, *Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs*](https://chilonas.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/04/norse-mythology.pdf)

### Snorri Sturluson’s *Edda*

**Language:** Old Norse prose with poetic quotations.  
**Composition:** conventionally c. 1220.  
**Principal manuscripts:** Codex Upsaliensis, c. 1300; Codex Regius of Snorri’s *Edda*, first half of the fourteenth century; Codex Wormianus, mid-fourteenth century; Codex Trajectinus, a later copy.

#### *Gylfaginning*

Snorri lists “Áli or Váli,” son of Óðinn and Rindr, as bold in battle and an excellent shot. In his account of Baldr’s death, Váli kills Hǫðr. After Ragnarǫk, Váli and Víðarr survive the sea and Surtr’s fire and dwell at Iðavǫllr.

These details are **[LC/LS]**. “Excellent shot” is not stated in the surviving Eddic vengeance verses, which do not identify Váli’s weapon.

#### *Skáldskaparmál*

Snorri supplies the relational kennings listed above. He also quotes a verse attributed to the tenth-century poet Kormákr Ǫgmundarson commonly understood to say that Óðinn practiced magic or enchantment upon Rindr. The verse attests an older poetic association of Óðinn with Rindr, but it does not name Váli or narrate the full conception.

The standard English translation is Anthony Faulkes’s; the Viking Society for Northern Research has made its editions and translations available online. [Faulkes translation of the *Prose Edda*](https://www.heathengods.com/library/prose_edda/YoungProseEdda.pdf)

### Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum*, Book III

**Language:** Latin.  
**Composition:** c. 1185–1220, probably completed in the early thirteenth century.  
**Transmission:** medieval manuscript fragments and later complete copies; no authorial manuscript survives.

Saxo offers the fullest independent or independently reshaped version. Othinus seeks diviners after Balderus’s death. The Finnic prophet Rostiophus says that a son born to Rinda, daughter of the king of the Ruthenians, will exact vengeance.

Othinus approaches Rinda repeatedly under disguises. She rejects him. He eventually uses magic to make her ill and returns disguised as a female healer; he secures access to her on the pretext that binding is required for treatment and then has intercourse with her. In modern legal and ethical language, Saxo describes deception, incapacitation, restraint, and rape. The narrative should not be softened into a consensual courtship.

Their son Bous grows into a martial young man. Othinus orders him to avenge Balderus rather than attack innocents. Bous kills Hotherus but is mortally wounded and dies the next day; his army gives him a mound burial. [Latin *Gesta Danorum*, Book III](https://la.wikisource.org/wiki/Gesta_Danorum/Liber_III)

This is a **[RV]**, not merely a longer version of Snorri:

- Bous has a different name.
- Rinda is a human Ruthenian princess.
- Balderus and Hotherus are heroic warriors rather than Snorri’s blind victim and divine favorite.
- Bous does not survive Ragnarǫk; he dies.
- Saxo embeds the myth in pseudo-history and moralized Latin historiography.

### Texts and corpora that do not securely attest Váli

- **Skaldic corpus:** Kormákr’s verse connects Óðinn and Rindr, but no securely Viking-Age skaldic verse plainly names Váli as their son.
- **Runic inscriptions:** No accepted Scandinavian runic inscription names Váli the god.
- **Tacitus, *Germania* (c. 98 CE):** no Váli.
- **Merseburg Charms (manuscript c. 900–950):** no Váli.
- **Adam of Bremen (c. 1070s):** no Váli and no cult of Váli at Uppsala.
- **Heimskringla:** no independent developed Váli myth or cult record.
- ***Nibelungenlied* and *Vǫlsunga saga*:** no Váli the god.
- **Wagner’s *Ring*:** no direct character Váli; Wagner’s avenging and sibling structures should not be retroactively read as Váli’s reception without specific documentary evidence.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

The narrow primary-source domain is **kin-vengeance for Baldr**. Váli is not shown hearing prayers, arbitrating disputes, protecting a social estate, controlling a natural phenomenon, or distributing victory generally.

The phrase “god of vengeance” is a reasonable modern summary if kept narrow, but a misleading ancient title if it implies a documented cultic portfolio. “God of justice” is still less secure: the medieval sources portray the killing as required vengeance, not as an abstract legal jurisdiction.

His second securely attested function is eschatological: he survives Ragnarǫk with Víðarr and occupies the gods’ holy places afterward.

### Characteristics and powers

- **Supernatural precocity [EA]:** able to take vengeance at one night old.
- **Ritual-like abstention [EA, interpretation disputed]:** neither hand-washing nor hair-combing before vengeance.
- **Martial boldness [LC]:** Snorri calls him bold in battle.
- **Excellent marksmanship [LC]:** Snorri calls him a very good or fortunate shot.
- **Survival of cosmic fire and flood [EA/LC]:** *Vafþrúðnismál* names him among the post-catastrophe gods; Snorri elaborates survival of both sea and fire.
- **No securely specified weapon:** bows and arrows are common in later art because Snorri calls him a good shot, but the killing of Hǫðr is not textually described as an archery episode.

### Unwashed hands and uncombed hair

The Eddic formula resembles documented Germanic vengeance or warrior vows. Tacitus’s *Germania* 31 says that young Chatti allowed hair and beard to grow and did not put aside this appearance until they had killed an enemy. That is a useful **[MSR comparative parallel]**, not evidence that the Chatti worshipped Váli or that an institutional Váli initiation survived from the first century to medieval Iceland.

It may express:

- a vow not to perform ordinary grooming until vengeance;
- liminality, with the newborn avenger outside normal social life;
- the speed of his action—he avenges Baldr before normal post-birth care;
- poetic intensification rather than a remembered ritual.

The sources do not decide among these explanations.

### Iconography

There is no securely identified Viking-Age or medieval image of Váli.

No surviving carving, bracteate, picture stone, textile, manuscript miniature, or statue bears an inscription or unique attributes sufficient to establish the identification. Images of a youthful archer labeled Váli are modern illustrations. Carl Emil Doepler and other nineteenth-century revival artists helped create a visual vocabulary for Norse gods, but their images are **[MI]**, not copies of lost cult statues.

### Symbols and correspondences

No ancient source gives Váli:

- a sacred animal;
- sacred plant;
- color;
- number;
- weekday;
- celestial body;
- festival;
- rune;
- altar substance;
- magical seal.

Modern assignments of wolf, falcon, bow, red, mistletoe, the rune *Tiwaz*, February 14, or a particular moon phase are **[MI]** unless presented simply as the practice of a named modern group.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical versions

| Tradition | Father | Mother | Status and outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eddic–Snorrian | Óðinn | Rindr | Áss; avenges Baldr; survives Ragnarǫk |
| Saxo | Othinus | Rinda, Ruthenian princess | Bous, warrior avenger; kills Hotherus and dies the next day |
| Snorri’s Loki-punishment narrative | Loki | unnamed in that passage; Sigyn is elsewhere mother of Nari/Narfi | A different Váli is transformed into a wolf and kills Nari/Narfi |

Frigg is Váli’s stepmother only through Snorri’s relational kenning. Baldr and Hǫðr are his half-brothers within Snorri’s genealogy. Víðarr is his paternal half-brother and eschatological counterpart. No wife, consort, child, servant, or mount is attested.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

- **Óðinn — father and begetter.** Óðinn fathers Váli on Rindr so that Baldr will be avenged; *Vǫluspá* 32–33 and *Baldrs draumar* 11, probably tenth–eleventh-century poems in thirteenth-century manuscripts. **[EA]**

- **Óðinn — posthumous avenger commissioned.** Snorri makes the purpose explicit; *Gylfaginning*, c. 1220. **[LC/LS]**

- **Othinus — receives the command to avenge Balderus.** Saxo’s Othinus summons Bous and directs his violence toward Balderus’s killer rather than innocents; *Gesta Danorum* III, c. 1200. **[RV]**

- **Rindr — mother/son relationship.** Rindr bears Váli in the western halls; *Vǫluspá* and *Baldrs draumar*. No conversation between them survives. **[EA]**

- **Baldr — avenging half-brother.** Váli is born to avenge Baldr and kills his adversary; *Vǫluspá*, *Baldrs draumar*, and especially *Hyndluljóð* 29. The texts do not record a meeting between living Baldr and Váli. **[EA]**

- **Hǫðr — Váli kills him.** Explicit in *Hyndluljóð* 29 and Snorri; implicit through the expression “Baldr’s adversary” in the vengeance stanzas. **[EA/LC]**

- **Víðarr — joint survival and habitation after Ragnarǫk.** The two inhabit the gods’ sanctuaries after Surtr’s fire; *Vafþrúðnismál* 51. No dialogue between them survives. **[EA]**

- **Móði — joint post-Ragnarǫk appearance.** Váli and Víðarr are named in the same stanza with Móði and Magni, who inherit Mjǫllnir; *Vafþrúðnismál* 51. This is a shared eschatological scene, not a described personal encounter. **[EA]**

- **Magni — joint post-Ragnarǫk appearance.** Same evidence and limitation as Móði. **[EA]**

- **Frigg — genealogical stepmother only.** *Skáldskaparmál* permits the kenning “stepson of Frigg.” No narrative interaction is preserved. **[LC/LS]**

- **Loki — no documented interaction with Óðinn’s son Váli.** Loki causes Baldr’s death in Snorri, but no text depicts Loki and the avenger meeting. **Absence of evidence.**

For the separate **Váli son of Loki**:

- **Loki — son and instrument of punishment.** The gods transform Loki’s son Váli into a wolf during Loki’s punishment; *Gylfaginning*, c. 1220. **[LC/disputed]**
- **Nari/Narfi — fratricide while transformed.** Wolf-Váli tears apart his brother, and the gods use the entrails to bind Loki; *Gylfaginning*. **[LC/disputed]**
- **The unnamed Æsir collectively — transformation.** Snorri says the gods seize Loki’s sons and change Váli into a wolf; no individual transformer is named in the usual text. **[LC]**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The vengeance for Baldr

#### Early poetic core

Baldr has been killed. Rindr gives birth to Óðinn’s avenging son. Though only one night old, Váli acts before washing or combing his hair and sends Baldr’s enemy to the pyre.

This is not a full continuous tale in *Vǫluspá*. It is a compressed allusion whose audience was evidently expected to recognize the story. *Baldrs draumar* places the prophecy before the killing, in Óðinn’s interrogation of a dead seeress. *Hyndluljóð* explicitly names Váli as the one who slays his brother’s killer.

#### Snorri’s synthesis

Snorri integrates the allusive poetry into a sequential narrative:

1. Baldr dreams of danger.
2. Frigg obtains oaths from beings and substances.
3. Loki discovers that mistletoe was exempted.
4. Loki gives mistletoe to the blind Hǫðr and directs his throw.
5. Baldr dies.
6. Váli, son of Óðinn and Rindr, kills Hǫðr.
7. Loki is eventually captured and bound.

This synthesis is **[LC/LS]**. The Eddic vengeance stanzas themselves do not say Loki guided Hǫðr’s hand, nor do they narrate Váli’s weapon or emotional response.

### The conception of the avenger

The Eddic poems merely prophesy that Rindr will bear Váli. Saxo supplies a sustained and disturbing conception narrative.

After repeated rejection by Rinda, Othinus touches her with a magical bark inscription or charm that induces apparent madness or grave illness. He later returns disguised as a female physician, claims that the treatment requires restraint, and uses the situation to have intercourse with her. Bous is the result.

The difference must be retained:

- **Eddic version [EA]:** birth prophecy, no account of sexual consent.
- **Kormákr fragment [EA but cryptic]:** Óðinn employed magic on Rindr.
- **Saxo [RV]:** deception, magical incapacitation, restraint, and rape.
- **Modern softened retellings [MI]:** “Óðinn courted,” “won,” or “seduced” Rindr erase what Saxo actually narrates.

Scholars debate whether Saxo preserves an old and otherwise lost myth, has rationalized a more symbolic encounter, or has expanded a poetic reference using medieval narrative motifs about magic, illness, disguise, and violated women. The older Kormákr line makes some pre-Saxo connection between Óðinn, Rindr, and magic likely, but cannot validate every detail in Saxo.

### The avenger’s death in Saxo

Bous kills Hotherus in battle but receives a mortal wound and dies the next day. His soldiers bury him in a mound.

This directly conflicts with the Eddic Váli who survives Ragnarǫk. Possible explanations include independent regional traditions, Saxo’s reassignment of motifs among characters, and his insistence on fitting divine stories into mortal dynastic history. It is methodologically unsafe to write that Váli “sometimes dies at Ragnarǫk”: Bous dies soon after killing Hotherus, not at Ragnarǫk, and Saxo provides no Ragnarǫk framework here.

### Survivor of Ragnarǫk

In *Vafþrúðnismál* 51, Váli and Víðarr inhabit the gods’ sanctuaries after Surtr’s flame is extinguished; Móði and Magni inherit Þórr’s hammer. Snorri elaborates that Víðarr and Váli are unharmed by sea and fire and live at Iðavǫllr, the former site of Ásgarðr.

The sources do not say Váli becomes supreme god, resurrects Baldr, presides over a court of justice, or founds a new human race. Later retellings sometimes merge *Vǫluspá*’s return of Baldr and Hǫðr from Hel with *Vafþrúðnismál*’s survivors into a single council; that synthesis is reasonable as mythography but is not a single preserved poetic scene.

### The other Váli’s transformation

Snorri’s gods transform Loki’s son Váli into a wolf. He tears apart Nari/Narfi; the gods turn the victim’s intestines into fetters and bind Loki over stones while a serpent drips venom.

The *Lokasenna* prose differs: Narfi becomes the wolf and Nari supplies the entrails. Because the names shift, this episode is a prime example of unstable prose transmission. Claims that the transformation initiated historical *úlfheðnar* or that the two Vális are secretly identical are **[MI/MSR unsupported]**.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Direct evidence

There is no direct evidence of an organized cult of Váli:

- no temple securely dedicated to him;
- no altar or votive inscription;
- no priestly office;
- no festival;
- no sacrificial prescription;
- no prayer or hymn addressed to him;
- no oracle;
- no theophoric personal-name series securely demonstrating worship;
- no cult image;
- no archaeological deposit identifiable as his.

This is an important positive result. Váli is textually clear but cultically obscure.

### Valaskjálf and Valaskioll

Some scholars have associated **Valaskjálf**, a divine hall in *Grímnismál* and Snorri, or the Norwegian place-name **Valaskioll**, with Váli. If the first element were Váli’s name, these might suggest a divine residence and perhaps a cultic place-name.

The argument remains weak:

- *Grímnismál* does not name Váli as the hall’s owner.
- Snorri assigns Valaskjálf to Óðinn.
- The compound may instead contain *valr*, “the slain,” and mean something like “shelf/hall of the slain.”
- A detailed study of mythic topography concludes that there is nothing firm connecting Valaskjálf to Váli and warns against moving directly from literary cosmic geography to cultic place-names. [Study of Old Norse mythic topography and Valaskjálf](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/19428/1/19428.pdf)

Accordingly, “Váli had a hall named Valaskjálf” and “Valaskioll proves his worship” are **[MSR disputed]**, not facts.

### Vengeance-vow ritual

The unwashed and uncombed state may preserve a cultural image of the avenger under vow. Tacitus’s description of Chatti who maintained uncut hair until killing an enemy supplies a distant comparative analogue. It does not establish a Váli ritual, priesthood, or initiation ceremony.

### Human sacrifice and other contested cult material

General Scandinavian evidence for human sacrifice—literary, archaeological, and contested in its interpretation—cannot be assigned to Váli. Adam of Bremen’s account of Uppsala names other gods and describes human and animal victims, but never Váli. No source links Váli with temple prostitution, sacrificial hanging, blood offerings, execution, or cult violence.

The myth does glorify lethal kin-vengeance, and Saxo explicitly directs Bous to a “just” target for violence. That is narrative ideology, not evidence of a sacrificial institution.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Possible Viking-Age poetic formation, ninth–tenth centuries

A verse attributed to Kormákr indicates that the Óðinn–Rindr complex circulated in skaldic diction. The poems later written as *Vǫluspá* and *Vafþrúðnismál* may substantially date from this period. Váli’s defining traits were already compressed into formulas: miraculous avenger, son of Óðinn and Rindr, abstaining from grooming, and survivor of cosmic destruction.

Whether the figure is older Germanic inheritance cannot be demonstrated. There is no continental Germanic cognate deity and no first-millennium inscription.

### Conversion period, c. 950–1100

The Christianization of Scandinavia transformed the institutions that transmitted pre-Christian religion, but no document records the suppression of a specifically Váli-centered cult. Unlike Þórr, Óðinn, or Freyr, Váli never appears in missionary polemic, royal conversion narratives, or named temple destruction.

Christian eschatological concepts may have affected the written form of the renewed-world passages, but scholars disagree over direction and degree. Váli himself has no clear Christian equivalent.

### Danish Latin historiography, c. 1200

Saxo remade the divine cycle as heroic pseudo-history. Bous became a mortal avenger, Rinda a Ruthenian princess, and the conception an extended narrative of magical deception and sexual violence. The avenger dies and is buried rather than surviving the end of the world.

This is not simple degeneration of a canonical myth. It is evidence that the Baldr complex remained variable and could be adapted to different regional, political, and literary programs.

### Icelandic mythographic systematization, thirteenth century

Snorri coordinated scattered verses into a family tree and continuous story. He classified Váli among the Æsir, specified his combat qualities, named Hǫðr as his victim, defined his kennings, and placed him at Iðavǫllr after Ragnarǫk.

At approximately the same period, prose framing around Eddic poems produced or preserved the unstable “Váli son of Loki” tradition. Medieval mythography therefore both conserved older material and generated new harmonizations or ambiguities.

### Manuscript preservation, thirteenth–fourteenth centuries

- Codex Regius preserved *Vǫluspá* and *Vafþrúðnismál*.
- AM 748 I 4to uniquely preserved *Baldrs draumar*.
- Flateyjarbók preserved *Hyndluljóð* and the embedded *Vǫluspá in skamma*.
- Multiple *Edda* manuscripts preserved differing forms of Snorri’s synthesis.

The figure’s survival depended on Christian Icelandic scribes; there is no evidence of continuous public worship running alongside this manuscript transmission.

### Antiquarian recovery, seventeenth–eighteenth centuries

The Eddas entered European print culture, and editors attempted to reconstruct a coherent pagan system. Váli became a conventional dictionary entry as “Baldr’s avenger,” often with variants silently combined.

### Romantic and nationalist period, nineteenth–early twentieth centuries

Comparative mythologists proposed nature-myth readings: Váli as returning light, spring vegetation, or solar rebirth after winter. Others treated him as a ritual embodiment of the avenging son. These are **[MSR]**, not medieval explanations.

Visual artists supplied him with idealized Germanic clothing, bows, blond youthfulness, and heroic poses. Such iconography reflects nineteenth-century historicism.

### Modern Heathen revival, twentieth century to present

Ásatrú and Heathen communities sometimes honor Váli as a deity of justified vengeance, family duty, endurance, renewal, or recovery after catastrophe. These are genuine modern religious practices but cannot be represented as uninterrupted Viking-Age cult.

A particularly clear modern construction equates Váli with **St Valentine** or treats Valentine’s Day as Váli’s feast. Modern Heathen writers have promoted it through superficial name resemblance and a rebirth/vengeance symbolism. There is no medieval evidence for such a connection. The Troth explicitly identifies the proposed Váli–Valentine equation with modern authors such as Edred Thorsson rather than ancient sources. [The Troth, “Vidar and Vali”](https://thetroth.org/resource/vidar-vali/)

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship, handbooks, and popular mythology

Modern reference works by Jan de Vries, Georges Dumézil, Rudolf Simek, John Lindow, Andy Orchard, and E. O. G. Turville-Petre generally keep Váli subordinate to the Baldr cycle. The scholarly tendency has shifted away from confidently decoding each god as a natural phenomenon and toward analyzing textual transmission, vengeance ideology, ritual analogy, and Snorri’s mythographic methods.

Popular summaries often inflate the evidence by assigning Váli a portfolio of vengeance, justice, light, spring, youth, rebirth, and archery. Of these, only vengeance and Snorri’s marksmanship have direct textual footing.

### Literature, opera, and visual art

Wagner did not include a character corresponding directly to Váli in *Der Ring des Nibelungen*. Claims that a Ring character “is Váli” require a specific argument and are not part of the opera’s explicit nomenclature.

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century illustrated mythologies sometimes depict Váli as an armed adolescent or archer. These images materially shaped modern expectations but have no ancient iconographic pedigree.

### Comics and games

Marvel created **Vali Halfling**, a son of Loki and a mortal woman, primarily derived from the disputed Loki-son tradition rather than Óðinn’s avenger. This is a modern fictional character, not a translation of the medieval Váli. [Marvel Database, “Vali Halfling”](https://marvel.fandom.com/wiki/Vali_Halfling_%28Earth-616%29)

Role-playing supplements, online deity catalogues, and fantasy games commonly give Váli alignments, clerical domains, sacred weapons, runes, spell portfolios, and feast days. Those details belong to the relevant game or modern religious system.

### Occultism and esotericism

Váli is not a major named figure in the canonical Renaissance grimoires, Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Theosophical canon, or early ceremonial-magic manuals. His occasional modern rune correspondences derive from twentieth-century Germanic occultism or contemporary eclectic practice.

Claims that a “secret Váli cult” survived Christianization, that he is an ancient patron of chaos magic, or that a rune encoded his name are unsubstantiated.

### Political appropriation

Germanic mythology and runes have been appropriated by both racist and non-racist modern movements. Váli is far less prominent in extremist symbolism than Óðinn, Þórr, Týr, the Valknut, or certain runes. Individual racialist organizations nevertheless publish devotional pages about him and interpret blood vengeance as ethnic duty. Such usage is modern political theology; it does not establish an ancient racial doctrine or Váli cult.

Conversely, inclusive Heathen groups interpret him through personal accountability, protection of kin, or ending cycles of injury. This too is modern theology, openly distinct from proof of medieval practice.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Did Váli originate as an avenger or something else?

The minimal explanation is that the preserved figure is an avenger generated by the Baldr myth. Nature-myth approaches reconstructed him as new spring vegetation or returning sunlight born after Baldr’s solar death. Those explanations rely on identifying Baldr as a sun or vegetation god, itself disputed.

A more ambitious theory connects Váli’s name with the Vanir and proposes that he originally avenged Freyr rather than Baldr. It lacks a surviving text in which he does so and remains speculative.

### Is Bous the same figure?

Narratively, Bous occupies Váli’s structural position: son of Othinus and Rinda, born to avenge Balderus, killer of Hotherus. Linguistically and eschatologically, he differs. “Saxo’s counterpart to Váli” is more exact than “Váli under his Latin name.”

John Lindow notes that Saxo may have redistributed motifs: Bous kills Hotherus yet dies, producing a reciprocal destruction absent from the Icelandic account. The variation warns against reconstructing one seamless pre-Christian original.

### How old is the one-night-old motif?

It is in *Vǫluspá*, *Baldrs draumar*, and *Hyndluljóð*, so it is older than Snorri’s prose synthesis in the form accessible to him. Exact antiquity cannot be proven. Comparable precocious avengers and supernatural children occur elsewhere in Norse narrative, but parallel motif does not demonstrate a common cult.

### Does the grooming taboo preserve ritual?

The Tacitean Chatti comparison is suggestive. Dumézilian and ritualist scholarship has treated the detail as an avenger’s consecration or warrior vow. Literary explanations emphasize that the formula dramatizes impossible speed: vengeance occurs before a newborn’s first washing.

Both remain interpretations. There is no ritual manual or eyewitness account of a Váli devotee observing the taboo.

### Was “Váli son of Loki” a scribal or mythographic error?

This is one of the central textual disputes. Snorri names Váli, Nari/Narfi; *Lokasenna* prose assigns the wolf role to Narfi. The isolated nature of Snorri’s Váli makes error or reinterpretation plausible. Yet absence elsewhere cannot prove that Snorri invented him: he may have known an otherwise lost variant.

The responsible conclusion is therefore:

- the Loki-son Váli is medievally attested in Snorri;
- the name is unstable across related prose;
- he is not securely identical with Óðinn’s son;
- “Snorri invented him” is a scholarly hypothesis, not a demonstrated fact.

### Valaskjálf as Váli’s hall

The identification rests on segmenting the first element as Váli’s name. Objections include alternative derivation from *valr*, Snorri’s attribution to Óðinn, and the absence of Váli from the relevant *Grímnismál* stanza. No consensus permits the hall to be used as firm cult evidence.

### State of the evidence

The figure’s evidence is remarkably asymmetrical:

- **Strong:** several interconnected medieval literary attestations.
- **Moderate:** likelihood that some poetry and the Kormákr fragment preserve Viking-Age tradition.
- **Weak or absent:** archaeology, epigraphy, iconography, cult sites, priesthood, festivals, popular votives, and continuous folklore.
- **Highly unstable:** etymology, natural symbolism, Valaskjálf, and the Loki-son names.

The principal manuscripts are held in Icelandic and Danish collections, with major materials accessible through Handrit.is, the Árni Magnússon institutes, and critical editions. The loss of oral variants and of the missing Codex Regius leaves means that the surviving record is not a complete census of medieval tradition.

## 11. Sources

### Primary texts and editions

The indispensable primary corpus comprises *Vǫluspá* 32–34; *Vafþrúðnismál* 51; *Baldrs draumar* 11; *Hyndluljóð* 29; Snorri’s *Gylfaginning* and *Skáldskaparmál*; the prose conclusion to *Lokasenna*; and Saxo’s *Gesta Danorum* III. The translations of Carolyne Larrington, Henry Adams Bellows, and Anthony Faulkes differ in diction and sometimes in textual decisions; none should be mistaken for the manuscript itself.

The Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages project is the appropriate critical resource for Kormákr’s verse and the distinction between attributed composition date and manuscript transmission. Runic databases are relevant principally because they disclose no accepted Váli inscription.

### Principal modern scholarship

- Jan de Vries, *Altgermanische Religionsgeschichte*: broad comparative and cult-historical synthesis, influential but sometimes more reconstructive than present evidentiary standards permit.
- Georges Dumézil, studies of Germanic myth: structural and Indo-European comparisons; influential and contested.
- John Lindow, *Norse Mythology: A Guide to the Gods, Heroes, Rituals, and Beliefs*: especially useful on the limited explicit evidence and differences between Eddic and Saxonian versions.
- Rudolf Simek, *Dictionary of Northern Mythology*: concise source inventory, etymological hypotheses, and bibliography.
- E. O. G. Turville-Petre, *Myth and Religion of the North*: historical-religious synthesis with attention to poetic evidence.
- Andy Orchard, *Dictionary of Norse Myth and Legend*: compact textual orientation.
- Kulturhistorisk leksikon for nordisk middelalder: historical context for medieval Scandinavian institutions, literature, and religion.
- Critical manuscript and electronic editions from the Árni Magnússon institutes and Copenhagen’s Arnamagnæan project.
