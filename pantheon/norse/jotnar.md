# The Jotnar: Research Dossier

**Class:** Collective class of mythological beings, Old Norse *jǫtnar* (singular *jǫtunn*), overlapping but not perfectly synonymous with *þursar*, *risar*, *troll*, and several narrower designations. Medieval texts contrast them especially with the Æsir, yet also make them ancestors, spouses, sexual partners, foster-relatives, hosts, and occasional allies of gods. “Giants” is conventional but misleading: extraordinary size is not a necessary defining feature.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The vocabulary is Common Germanic: Old English *eoten* and Old Norse *jǫtunn* continue reconstructed Proto-Germanic **\*etunaz**. For a securely datable narrative attestation in the specifically Norse mythological tradition, the strongest early evidence is the late ninth-century skaldic poem *Haustlǫng*, attributed to Þjóðólfr of Hvinir, whose surviving verses recount Þjazi’s seizure of Iðunn and Þórr’s combat with Hrungnir. The poem survives only through Snorri Sturluson’s thirteenth-century *Skáldskaparmál*, so the date attaches to the attributed composition, not to an extant Viking-Age manuscript. Old English *eotenas* occurs in *Beowulf*, whose sole manuscript is palaeographically dated around 1000, although the poem’s composition remains disputed. Neither Tacitus’s *Germania* (98 CE), the Merseburg Charms, nor Adam of Bremen’s account of Scandinavian cult supplies an earlier secure attestation of “the Jotnar” as the later Norse class.

## Evidentiary key

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** Viking-Age or earlier linguistic, poetic, runic, or visual evidence, while acknowledging later manuscript preservation.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** chiefly the thirteenth-century Eddas and closely related medieval Icelandic texts.
- **[RV] Regional or textual variant:** a version confined to, or importantly altered in, a particular poem, saga, language area, or manuscript tradition.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** medieval antiquarian, euhemerizing, grammatical, or cosmographic organization of inherited material.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** an explicit or reconstructable fusion or equation between previously distinguishable beings or traditions.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion:** material arising after the principal mythographic corpus.
- **[F] Folklore:** post-medieval vernacular belief and narrative, without presuming unchanged pagan survival.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** a reasoned hypothesis rather than a statement made by a primary source.
- **[MI] Modern invention or reinvention:** a demonstrably modern system, image, taxonomy, or narrative.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The class

The Jotnar are not one divine “being” with a biography. They are a shifting category of beings who inhabit the temporal, spatial, and genealogical margins of the gods’ world. In the Eddic cosmogony, they precede the organized cosmos: Ymir/Aurgelmir is the primordial ancestor of the frost-giants, and Óðinn, Vili, and Vé make the inhabited world from his slain body. In narratives, Jotnar may embody or control dangerous exterior forces—sea, fire, winter, mountains, wilderness, appetite, death, and unbounded knowledge—but the sources never reduce them to a single naturalistic function.

The Æsir–Jotnar opposition is therefore relational rather than biological. Óðinn descends maternally from Jotnar; Þórr’s mother Jörð is usually classified among them; Freyr marries Gerðr; Njörðr marries Skaði; Loki fathers or mothers both divine helpers and cosmic enemies; and several Jotnar possess treasures, women, wisdom, or ritual objects required by the gods. John Lindow’s formulation of an “inside/outside” opposition captures much of the narrative pattern, but it is an analytical model, not an Old Norse creed. Ritual-hierarchical analysis likewise finds the Æsir placed above Jotnar in mythic ordering, without implying that the two categories were metaphysically unrelated ([John Lindow, “Ritual and Hierarchy in Old Norse Mythology”](https://tidsskrift.dk/rvt/article/view/132098)).

### Names

- **Old Norse:** *jǫtunn*, plural *jǫtnar*. Modern normalized Icelandic often prints *jötunn/jötnar*.
- **Modern Scandinavian:** Icelandic *jötunn*; Norwegian *jotun/jøtul*; Swedish *jätte*; Danish *jætte*. These descendants underwent semantic convergence with the ordinary European “giant.”
- **Old English:** *eoten*, plural *eotenas*, as in *Beowulf*.
- **Proto-Germanic reconstruction:** **\*etunaz**. The traditional etymology connects it with “eating” and interprets the being as an “eater” or “devourer,” comparing Old Norse *eta*, “to eat.” This is plausible but remains reconstruction, not an ancient gloss. Recent scholarship cautions that the geographical distribution and semantic history of *jǫtunn/eoten* are not simple evidence that it was always the dominant pan-Germanic monster-word ([RMN 17 discussion](https://www.helsinki.fi/assets/drupal/2023-12/RMN17_full_volume.pdf)).
- **Old Norse *þurs***, plural *þursar*: a dangerous or monstrous being, often translated “giant.” Cognates include Old English *þyrs* and Old High German *duris/turis*. It overlaps strongly with *jǫtunn* but can carry a more explicitly harmful, demonic, or disease-causing sense.
- **Old Norse *risi***, plural *risar*: usually a physically large giant, particularly prominent in legendary sagas and translated romances. It is not a fully interchangeable mythological synonym; saga usage can distinguish *risar* from *jǫtnar* ([“Distinguishing Risar and Jötnar”](https://research-portal.uu.nl/en/publications/a-problem-of-giant-proportions-distinguishing-risar-and-j%C3%B6tnar-in/)).
- **Old Norse *troll***: a very broad word for a dangerous supernatural being, monster, magician, revenant, or giant. In medieval and later Scandinavian language it progressively absorbs much of the semantic territory of *jǫtunn*.
- **Old Norse *gýgr***, plural *gýgjar*: specifically a giantess.
- **Old Norse *bergrisi*, *fjalljǫtunn*, *hraunbúi*:** mountain giant, rock giant, or lava-dweller; often descriptive rather than stable theological ranks.
- **Old Norse *hrímþurs/hrímþursar*:** “rime-” or “frost-thurs(es).” This designation belongs especially to cosmogonic and apocalyptic contexts.
- **Old Norse *eldjǫtunn*:** “fire-giant,” familiar in modern retellings but not the organizing ancient counterpart of *hrímþurs*. Surtr and the sons or people of Muspell are associated with fire; “fire giants” as a standardized race is largely a post-medieval taxonomy.
- **Old Norse *Jǫtunheimr/Jǫtunheimar*:** “giant-world” or “giant-homes.” Singular and plural forms occur; Snorri’s map-like Nine Worlds encourages a more fixed geography than all poetic passages warrant.
- **Útgarðr:** “outer enclosure,” the conceptual outside opposed to the gods’ and humans’ enclosed worlds. Útgarða-Loki is “Loki of the outer enclosures,” not necessarily Loki in another costume.

### What “giant” gets wrong

Many named Jotnar are never described as enormous. Gerðr passes as a desirable bride; Skaði enters Ásgarðr and negotiates compensation; Loki and Þjazi change shape; Útgarða-Loki’s household differs in scale, but its decisive characteristic is illusion. Size is episodic, not taxonomic. Translation as “giant” reflects the convergence of medieval Germanic, biblical, classical, and later folktale monster-categories.

### No fixed elemental taxonomy

Modern charts commonly divide the Jotnar into frost, mountain, and fire races. The sources do not present such a consistent classification.

- **[LC/LS]** Snorri derives Ymir and the frost-giants from the meeting of Niflheim’s cold and Muspell’s heat.
- **[EA/LC]** *Vafþrúðnismál* knows Ymir/Aurgelmir, Bergelmir, and ancient giant ancestry but does not offer a role-playing-game-style racial chart.
- **[LC]** Surtr guards or comes from Muspell and leads its forces at Ragnarök.
- **[MI]** A systematic species called “fire giants,” coequal with “frost giants” and possessing a standardized appearance and culture, belongs principally to modern fantasy.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Germanic prehistory and non-Norse comparisons

**[MSR]** The cognate set *jǫtunn/eoten* establishes that a related word existed before North and West Germanic separated. It does not establish that Proto-Germanic speakers possessed Snorri’s Ymir narrative, Jötunheim geography, or named Icelandic cast.

Tacitus’s *Germania* 2 says that Germanic peoples celebrated the earth-born god Tuisto and his son Mannus in ancient songs. It does not call Tuisto a giant or identify him with Ymir ([Tacitus text](https://sourcebooks.fordham.edu/basis/tacitus-germanygord.asp)). The Tuisto–Ymir comparison and an Indo-European Manu–Yemo reconstruction are **[MSR]**, not an attested Germanic equation.

The Old High German Merseburg Charms, copied in the tenth century, contain named gods and healing magic but no Jotnar. Adam of Bremen, writing about 1070, describes Scandinavian gods and sacrifice but supplies no usable Jotnar cult. Their silence is significant: the class is richly mythological but poorly documented as an object of public cult.

### Old English evidence

**[EA/RV]** *Beowulf* uses *eotenas* in a Christianized monster genealogy involving Cain and the Flood. Grendel’s kindred belong among *eotenas*, *ylfe*, *orcneas*, and *gigantas*. This is not a pristine pagan list: biblical and vernacular monster traditions have been deliberately combined. The manuscript dates to approximately 1000; proposed composition dates range considerably earlier, but the gap between conjectured composition and manuscript must remain visible ([dating discussion](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.3138/j.ctt1287v33); [Tolkien’s classic analysis](https://eclass.uoa.gr/modules/document/file.php/ENL599/Tolkien_Monsters%20and%20Critics.pdf)).

### Skaldic corpus

The oldest specifically Norse narrative evidence is poetry attributed to Viking-Age poets but transmitted in later manuscripts.

- **Þjóðólfr of Hvinir, *Haustlǫng*, late ninth century [EA]:** Þjazi seizes Iðunn; Loki is forced to secure her; the gods kill Þjazi. Other stanzas narrate Þórr’s duel with Hrungnir. The poem is preserved in *Skáldskaparmál*. Its elaborate kennings show that poet and audience already knew these myths. The Skaldic Project provides edited text, translation, manuscript readings, and commentary; for example, Þjazi appears as Miðjungr and as an eagle carrying Loki ([Skaldic Project, *Haustlǫng* stanza 8](https://skaldic.org/m.php?i=234500&p=wordtextlp)).
- **Eilífr Goðrúnarson, *Þórsdrápa*, late tenth century [EA]:** an exceptionally dense account of Þórr’s expedition to Geirröðr, with Loki, Þjálfi, Gjálp, Greip, and Gríðr. Snorri retells and regularizes it.
- **Úlfr Uggason, *Húsdrápa*, c. 983 [EA]:** describes mythological scenes displayed in Óláfr pái’s hall. Its subjects include Þórr’s fishing and Loki’s struggle with Heimdallr; it proves that myths circulated visually and poetically before the Eddas.
- **Þjóðólfr Arnórsson and other eleventh-century poets [EA]:** giant-killing is a productive source of Þórr-kennings, showing how central the relationship was to poetic diction.

### The Poetic Edda

The principal manuscript, GKS 2365 4to, was written in Iceland around 1270, probably by a single scribe from older written exemplars. It contains forty-five surviving leaves and has a lost quire. It returned from Copenhagen to Iceland in 1971 and is held by the Árni Magnússon Institute. A modern electronic edition supplies facsimile, diplomatic, normalized, and morphologically analyzed texts ([Codex Regius electronic edition](https://eae.ku.dk/q?p=eae%2Fvols%2Ftext%2F1)). Individual poems are older than the manuscript, but dates are debated and must not be confused with the date of the physical witness.

Principal giant-related poems include:

- ** *Vǫluspá* [EA/LC]:** primordial Jotnar; Ymir’s age; the giant builder episode alluded to indirectly; the sons of Muspell; Surtr at Ragnarök.
- ** *Vafþrúðnismál* [EA/LC]:** Óðinn’s fatal wisdom contest with Vafþrúðnir; Ymir/Aurgelmir, Bergelmir, the origin of winds, sun, moon, and post-Ragnarök survival.
- ** *Grímnismál* [EA/LC]:** cosmography built from Ymir and the giant Hræsvelgr as producer of wind.
- ** *Skírnismál* [EA/LC]:** Freyr desires Gerðr; Skírnir coerces her with threats, including the carving of a *þurs* rune.
- ** *Hárbarðsljóð* [EA/LC]:** Þórr boasts of killing Jotnar and giantesses; Óðinn/Harbarðr mocks him.
- ** *Hymiskviða* [EA/LC]:** Þórr and Týr obtain Hymir’s cauldron; Þórr fishes for Jörmungandr; the poem gives Hymir a formidable wife and a helpful, fair-browed mistress or mother.
- ** *Lokasenna* [EA/LC]:** Loki alleges sexual and genealogical scandals connecting gods and Jotnar; Þórr’s arrival ends the contest.
- ** *Þrymskviða* [LC, perhaps older]:** Þrymr steals Mjǫllnir; Þórr and Loki recover it through bridal disguise. Arguments for composition or revision in Norse England remain debated ([edition and translation](https://www.worldtreeproject.org/document/2295)).
- ** *Alvíssmál* [LC]:** Þórr detains the dwarf Alvíss until sunrise; its language lists distinguish Jotnar’s vocabulary from that of gods, humans, elves, and dwarfs.
- ** *Hyndluljóð/Vǫluspá in skamma* [LC]:** genealogical material including Loki and Angrboða’s monstrous offspring.
- ** *Grottasǫngr* [LC]:** the giantesses Fenja and Menja grind wealth, peace, and ultimately destruction.
- ** *Helgakviða Hjǫrvarðssonar* [LC]:** Atli and the giantess Hrímgerðr exchange insults until daylight turns her to stone, an important bridge toward troll folklore.

Old Norse texts of these poems are conveniently gathered in the Bugge electronic corpus ([Old Norse e-texts](https://etext.old.no/)); reliable modern consultation should use critical editions and translations such as Neckel–Kuhn, Ursula Dronke, Carolyne Larrington, and Edward Pettit rather than treating any one English rendering as the source.

### Snorri Sturluson’s Edda

Composed about 1220, the *Edda* survives in several fourteenth-century and later manuscripts. It is a handbook for poets written in Christian Iceland. Snorri preserves indispensable material, including older verse, but also reconciles contradictions, supplies connective prose, and constructs genealogical and cosmographic order.

- ** *Gylfaginning* [LC/LS]:** Ymir’s formation and death; the world made from his body; the giant builder; Útgarða-Loki; Þórr and Hymir; Baldr and Þökk; Fenrir, Jörmungandr, Hel, Surtr, and Ragnarök.
- ** *Skáldskaparmál* [LC/LS]:** myths explaining kennings: Þjazi and Iðunn; Skaði’s compensation and marriage; Suttungr and the mead; Hrungnir’s duel; Geirröðr; Ægir’s banquet.
- ** *Háttatal*:** primarily metrical and of little independent value for Jotnar mythology.

Anthony Faulkes’s editions remain foundational ([Faulkes, *Edda: Prologue and Gylfaginning*](https://www.vsnrweb-publications.org.uk/Edda-1.pdf)).

### Sagas and historiography

- ** *Heimskringla*, especially *Ynglinga saga*, c. 1230 [LS]:** euhemerizes gods as ancient rulers. It is evidence for Snorri’s historiography, not straightforward pagan theology.
- ** *Vǫlsunga saga*, later thirteenth century [LC/MA]:** preserves heroic materials related to Eddic lays. Its supernatural antagonists overlap with giant, troll, and shape-shifter categories, but Fáfnir is not originally a Jotunn; he is a transformed human or dwarf-like figure depending on source.
- ** *Nibelungenlied*, c. 1200 [RV]:** a Middle High German heroic epic, not a source for the Norse Jotnar as a divine class.
- ** *Hervarar saga*, *Ketils saga hœngs*, *Örvar-Odds saga*, *Bósa saga*, and other *fornaldarsögur*, thirteenth–fourteenth centuries [MA]:** multiply giants, giantesses, trolls, mixed marriages, and northern otherworld kingdoms. These are crucial to reception but cannot automatically be projected into Viking-Age religion.
- **Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum*, c. 1208–1219 [RV/LS]:** Latin Danish historiography that euhemerizes or demonizes supernatural beings. Saxo’s Utgarthilocus is chained in a noisome cavern and appears to fuse Útgarða-Loki with the bound Loki. The work’s first nine books provide independent and often sharply divergent Danish legendary material ([overview and editions](https://www.norsegods.org/texts/gesta-danorum); [dating discussion](https://doi.org/10.57086/sources.927)).

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and characteristic functions

The Jotnar have no single cultic portfolio. Their recurring narrative functions are:

- **Primordial material:** Ymir’s body becomes earth, sea, mountains, sky, clouds, and human geography.
- **Exteriority:** they inhabit mountains, forests, caves, the eastern wilderness, northern margins, sea halls, and *útgarðr*, places beyond the protected enclosure.
- **Dangerous abundance:** they possess vast food, drink, cattle, cauldrons, treasures, women, and magical objects.
- **Ancient knowledge:** Vafþrúðnir knows cosmogony; Mímir possesses wisdom; Vafþrúðnir, Hyndla, and other giant figures remember remote origins because their kind precedes the gods’ order.
- **Control or embodiment of natural processes:** Ægir and Rán the sea; Jörð the earth; Skaði mountain travel and skiing; Surtr fire; Hræsvelgr wind; Nótt night. Classification varies: some of these figures are simultaneously counted with gods.
- **Threat to boundaries:** giants seek divine women, enter Ásgarðr, steal divine objects, or threaten the cosmos. Conversely, gods repeatedly enter giant territory to take wisdom, women, vessels, food, or treasure.
- **Necessary ancestry:** gods cannot be genealogically purified of Jotnar. The relationship is generative as well as adversarial.

### Form

Jotnar may be beautiful, monstrous, animal-shaped, multiheaded, stone-bodied, or indistinguishable from gods and humans.

- Gerðr is radiant and desirable.
- Skaði appears as an armed traveler and divine bride.
- Þjazi becomes an eagle.
- Suttungr becomes an eagle; Gunnlǫð appears as a woman.
- Hrungnir has a stone head, stone shield, whetstone weapon, and—only in Snorri—a famous three-cornered stone heart.
- Þrívaldi is three-headed.
- Ymir is large enough to supply the cosmos, but this follows from the creation story rather than a general rule about Jotnar.
- Útgarða-Loki’s companions are defined by deceptive scale and illusion.
- Some saga giants have multiple heads, huge noses, animal skins, and cannibal appetites; these features intensify in late medieval romance and folklore.

### Iconography

There is no certain universal “Jotunn type” in Viking-Age art. Identification normally depends on a depicted myth.

- **Þórr’s fishing expedition:** probably represented on the tenth-century Gosforth fishing stone in Cumbria, the Hørdum stone in Denmark, and the eleventh-century Altuna stone in Sweden. The companion in the boat is interpreted as Hymir. The Gosforth image stood in a Christian monumental environment, demonstrating adaptation rather than a sealed pagan iconographic system ([Gosforth discussion](https://tile.loc.gov/storage-services/master/gdc/gdcebookspublic/20/19/45/29/64/2019452964/2019452964.pdf)).
- **Ragnarök monuments:** crosses, runestones, textiles, and picture stones have been read as depicting Surtr or other giant-associated events, but many identifications remain contested ([Oxford survey of Ragnarök imagery](https://academic.oup.com/book/44543/chapter-abstract/376520861)).
- **Hrungnir’s heart:** Snorri describes a hard, three-pointed stone heart resembling a carved sign called *Hrungnis hjarta*. Its equation with the modernly named *valknut* is not secure. A triquetra-like sign may fit the wording better, but no labeled medieval image establishes the identification ([recent iconographic argument](https://revistas.ufrj.br/index.php/medievalis/article/download/63753/42014)).
- **Manuscript illustration:** post-medieval Icelandic Edda manuscripts picture Þórr, Hymir, and other giants. These illustrate reception, not necessarily Viking-Age visual conventions.
- **Romantic art [MI]:** nineteenth-century painters made giants huge, racially distinct, fur-clad embodiments of raw nature. Mårten Eskil Winge’s *Thor’s Fight with the Giants* (1872) is influential but not a reconstruction of an ancient image.

### Symbols and correspondences

No ancient source assigns the Jotnar as a class a sacred color, weekday, planet, number, plant, or animal. Modern correspondence lists that do so are **[MI]**. Particular Jotnar have objects or animal forms: Hrungnir’s whetstone and stone shield, Þjazi’s eagle form, Þrymr’s stolen hammer, Hymir’s cauldron and ox, Surtr’s flaming weapon, Ægir’s cauldron, and Skaði’s skis and bow.

## 4. Genealogy and relations

### Competing genealogical structures

#### Ymir’s line

**[EA/LC]** *Vafþrúðnismál* says Aurgelmir came from the venom-drops or *élivágar* and produced descendants from beneath his arms and from his joined feet. Þrúðgelmir is his son and Bergelmir his grandson. Bergelmir survives the flood of Ymir’s blood in a *lúðr*, a disputed word variously rendered “coffin,” “box,” “mill-frame,” or “boat.”

**[LC/LS]** Snorri identifies Aurgelmir explicitly with Ymir, calls him the ancestor of the frost-giants, and explains that all but Bergelmir and his wife drown after Borr’s sons kill Ymir. The tidy equivalence Ymir = Aurgelmir is Snorri’s explicit systematization; the poem itself uses Aurgelmir.

#### Divine descent through giant women

**[LC]** Borr marries Bestla, daughter of the Jotunn Bölþorn; their sons are Óðinn, Vili, and Vé. Thus the chief Æsir are maternally of giant descent.

**[LC]** Óðinn fathers Þórr with Jörð, who is both Earth personified and classified as a giantess in genealogical lists. The primary giant-killer is therefore himself giant-descended.

**[EA/LC]** Týr calls Hymir his father in *Hymiskviða*’s immediate narrative, while Snorri elsewhere calls Týr a son of Óðinn. These must not be harmonized: they are competing genealogies or a poem-specific kinship construction.

#### Marriages and sexual unions

- **Freyr–Gerðr:** marriage or union after Skírnir’s coercive embassy.
- **Njörðr–Skaði:** compensation marriage after Þjazi’s death; dissolves or fails because neither tolerates the other’s home.
- **Óðinn–Jörð:** union producing Þórr.
- **Óðinn–Gunnlǫð:** three nights together in exchange for access to the mead; Snorri frames it as seduction and deception.
- **Loki–Angrboða:** parents of Fenrir, Jörmungandr, and Hel in Snorri.
- **Loki–Skaði:** *Lokasenna* alleges a sexual history, but the claim occurs in ritualized insult and is not independently narrated.
- **Ægir–Rán:** a giant-associated sea couple who host the gods.
- **Gymir–Aurboða:** parents of Gerðr. Whether this Gymir is identical with Ægir, who also bears the name Gymir in poetic usage, is uncertain.
- **Gríðr–Óðinn:** later genealogical tradition makes them parents of Víðarr; Gríðr also assists Þórr against Geirröðr.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The primordial giant and creation

**Earliest recoverable layer [EA]:** *Vafþrúðnismál* names Aurgelmir and describes his strange reproduction; *Grímnismál* gives the body-to-world correspondences; *Vǫluspá* asks when Ymir lived and speaks of the world’s earliest state.

**Snorri’s full synthesis [LC/LS]:** cold streams from Niflheim and heat from Muspell meet in Ginnungagap. Drops quicken into Ymir; Auðhumla nourishes him and licks Búri from salty ice. Ymir produces giant offspring. Búri’s descendant Borr fathers Óðinn, Vili, and Vé with Bestla. The brothers kill Ymir; his blood drowns most giants, while Bergelmir escapes. They fashion the world from the corpse.

The poetic sources do not themselves provide this entire sequence. Auðhumla, Búri, the detailed mechanics of ice-licking, and the coordinated family narrative are known from Snorri. A comparison with Indo-European “primal twin” dismemberment is **[MSR]**, not proof of a continuous Proto-Indo-European story.

### The giant builder and Ásgarðr’s wall

**[LC]** In *Gylfaginning*, an unnamed builder offers to complete a defensive wall in three seasons in exchange for Freyja, the sun, and the moon. Loki persuades the gods to accept under an impossible deadline, allowing the builder only his stallion Svaðilfari. When success becomes likely, the gods threaten Loki. Loki becomes a mare and lures Svaðilfari away; the builder reveals *jǫtunmóðr*, “giant rage/nature,” and Þórr kills him. Loki later bears Sleipnir.

The episode dramatizes dependence on dangerous outsiders, oath-breaking, sexual exchange, and the acquisition of a wonder-animal. The text calls the builder’s giant nature a revelation, but gives him no name. Modern names assigned to him are invention.

### Þjazi, Iðunn, and the apples

**[EA]** *Haustlǫng* is the oldest witness. Óðinn, Hœnir, and Loki cannot cook an ox because the eagle Þjazi interferes. Loki strikes him and becomes attached to the eagle’s pole; dragged through the air, he bargains for release by promising Iðunn. Þjazi carries her and her apples away. The gods age and force Loki to retrieve her. He borrows Freyja’s falcon form, transforms Iðunn into a nut according to Snorri, and flees; the Æsir burn Þjazi’s eagle plumage.

**[LC]** Skaði comes armed for vengeance. Compensation includes a husband selected by feet and the feat of making her laugh. Njörðr and Skaði cannot reconcile sea and mountains.

The tale is not merely “good gods versus evil giant”: the gods kill Þjazi after Loki’s deception, Skaði’s legal claim is honored, and she joins the gods’ social world.

### Hrungnir’s duel

Óðinn wagers that Sleipnir is faster than Hrungnir’s Gullfaxi. Hrungnir rides into Ásgarðr, drinks, boasts, and threatens. Þórr challenges him. The Jotnar construct Mǫkkurkálfi from clay; Þjálfi misdirects Hrungnir about Þórr’s approach. Þórr’s hammer and Hrungnir’s whetstone collide; the hammer breaks Hrungnir’s skull, while a fragment lodges in Þórr’s head. Hrungnir’s leg pins Þórr until the three-night-old Magni lifts it.

*Haustlǫng* supplies the early poetic core; Snorri adds connective prose, the clay helper, stone anatomy, and etiological details. Gróa’s attempted extraction of the whetstone links the myth to a separate healing narrative.

### Þórr and Geirröðr

**[EA]** *Þórsdrápa* tells the story in difficult skaldic diction. Loki’s betrayal brings Þórr, lacking his usual equipment, into Geirröðr’s domain. Gríðr equips him. He crosses the swollen Vimur, resists Gjálp, survives the crushing chair, and kills Geirröðr with heated iron.

**[LC]** Snorri clarifies the plot and identifies Gríðr’s staff, belt, and gloves. The prose must not be assumed to reproduce every nuance of Eilífr’s poem.

### Þrymr and the stolen hammer

Þrymr hides Mjǫllnir eight leagues beneath the earth and demands Freyja. She refuses. Heimdallr proposes dressing Þórr as the bride; Loki accompanies him as maid. At the feast Þórr consumes an ox, salmon, and mead; Loki explains that “Freyja” has fasted from longing. The hammer is placed in the bride’s lap to consecrate the marriage, whereupon Þórr seizes it and kills Þrymr’s household.

The poem is comic but depends on real ritual and social anxieties: bride exchange, emasculation, failed reciprocity, and the loss of the gods’ defensive weapon. Claims that it directly records a historical wedding rite are **[MSR]**.

### Hymir, the cauldron, and fishing

In *Hymiskviða*, the gods require an immense brewing vessel. Týr directs Þórr to Hymir, here called his father. Hymir’s household contains a nine-hundred-headed grandmother and a benevolent gold-browed woman. Þórr eats two oxen. While fishing, Hymir catches whales; Þórr tears off the head of Hymir’s ox Himinhrjóðr, catches Jörmungandr, and strikes at it. Later he smashes a hard cup against Hymir’s head and carries away the cauldron after fighting pursuers.

In *Gylfaginning*, Þórr goes independently, Hymir cuts the line in fear, and Þórr throws him overboard in some readings. *Húsdrápa* confirms that the fishing myth was known by about 983. The surviving visual stones show its broad circulation but do not determine which literary version is oldest.

### Útgarða-Loki

Snorri alone supplies the full surviving tale. Þórr, Loki, Þjálfi, and Röskva encounter Skrýmir, whose glove they mistake for a hall. At Útgarðr:

- Loki loses an eating contest to Logi, wildfire.
- Þjálfi loses a race to Hugi, thought.
- Þórr cannot drain a horn connected to the sea, lift the world-serpent disguised as a cat, or defeat Elli, old age.

Útgarða-Loki later admits that Þórr terrified the court and makes the fortress vanish. Saxo’s bound *Utgarthilocus* is a divergent Danish-Christian version, probably fusing this figure with Loki’s punishment. It is not evidence that Snorri’s Útgarða-Loki and Loki were originally identical.

### The mead of poetry

Kvasir’s blood becomes the mead; dwarfs kill him, and Suttungr takes the drink as compensation after the dwarfs kill his parents. Gunnlǫð guards it within Hnitbjörg. Óðinn, calling himself Bölverkr, works for Baugi, bores into the mountain, sleeps with Gunnlǫð, consumes the mead, and escapes as an eagle. Suttungr pursues him.

*Hávamál* presents a morally ambiguous first-person version in which Óðinn admits false swearing and Gunnlǫð’s betrayal. Snorri provides the full causal narrative. The Jotnar are custodians and losers of culture-producing substance; the gods’ acquisition depends on deception and sexual exploitation.

### Ragnarök

*Vǫluspá* and *Vafþrúðnismál* supply poetic components; Snorri coordinates them. Fenrir kills Óðinn; Jörmungandr and Þórr kill each other; Surtr fights Freyr and burns the world; the sons or people of Muspell cross Bifröst. Not every enemy is straightforwardly a Jotunn, and “the giants attack Asgard as one race” is an oversimplification. The forces are genealogically and terminologically diverse.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### General finding

There is no securely identified temple, priesthood, festival calendar, or organized public cult devoted to “the Jotnar” as a collective. No source describes a *goði* of the Jotnar, a Jotnar temple economy, or a recurring pan-Scandinavian sacrifice to them. Their abundance in myth should not be converted into evidence of worship.

Possible exceptions concern individual giant-associated beings who cross categorical boundaries—Skaði, Gerðr, Jörð, Ægir, and especially Surtr—or rites of averting dangerous powers rather than devotional cult.

### Surtshellir

The strongest material case is Surtshellir in Iceland. Deep inside the lava cave, archaeologists found a boat-shaped stone structure and deposits of fragmented and burned domestic-animal bone. Bayesian analysis of radiocarbon dates indicates activity beginning soon after the Hallmundarhraun eruption and continuing for roughly 80–120 years, ending shortly after Iceland’s conversion.

The cave was associated in medieval texts with Surtr, and *Landnámabók* reports that Þorvaldr holbarki went to Surtr’s cave and recited a poem about “the giant in the cave.” The excavators interpret the deposits as ritual responses to catastrophic volcanism, possibly directed toward or against Surtr ([Smith, Ólafsson, and Pálsdóttir 2021](https://digitalcommons.usf.edu/kip_articles/8669/)).

What the evidence establishes:

- animals were slaughtered, their bones deposited and partly burned deep in the cave;
- the activity belongs to the settlement and conversion era;
- medieval Icelanders connected the cave with Surtr.

What it does **not** establish:

- a standardized “cult of Surtr” throughout Scandinavia;
- the precise spoken theology or purpose of the deposits;
- that offerings were affectionate worship rather than propitiation, containment, or apocalyptic response.

### Magic and the *þurs* rune

The rune ᚦ is called *þurs* in the Old Norse rune poems. In *Skírnismál*, Skírnir threatens to carve a *þurs* rune and associated signs to impose sexual and social misery on Gerðr. Runic and manuscript charms can call disease-causing beings *þursar* and invoke Þórr or Christian powers against them. Here “thurs” functions closer to a harmful demon, illness agent, or curse-being than to a neutral mythological ethnicity. The Scandinavian Runic-text Database provides searchable inscriptions and normalized readings ([Runic.is](https://runic.is/en/ristur)).

### Sacrifice, human sacrifice, and sexual ritual

No primary source documents human sacrifice **to the Jotnar as a class**. Adam of Bremen’s contested description of sacrifices at Uppsala concerns the gods worshipped there, not Jotnar. Claims that Norse people sacrificed captives or children to frost giants are modern extrapolations unless tied to a specific, critically evaluated source.

No evidence connects the Jotnar with “temple prostitution.” Sexual coercion and exchange are prominent in myths—Gerðr, Gunnlǫð, Freyja as the builder’s demanded wage—but these narratives are not documentation of temple institutions.

### Politics and economics

Jotnar myths repeatedly stage transactions: walls built for women and celestial bodies, marriage as legal compensation, mead taken through labor and fraud, cauldrons acquired by ordeal, and stolen property recovered through violence. Scholars plausibly analyze these as reflections on reciprocity, exogamy, feud, resource control, and boundary maintenance. That is social interpretation, not direct proof of corresponding rites.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Proto-Germanic and early medieval background

The shared *jǫtunn/eoten* and *þurs/þyrs* vocabulary demonstrates an early Germanic monster-complex, but named Norse genealogies cannot be projected wholesale into it. Tacitus does not attest the Jotnar. Old English literature Christianizes *eotenas* by inserting them into Cainite and Flood genealogies.

### Viking Age, c. 800–1050

Skaldic poetry shows fully developed named giant myths by the late ninth century: Þjazi, Iðunn, Hrungnir, and Þórr’s giant-fighting identity were already conventional enough for allusive kennings. *Húsdrápa* and archaeological images confirm circulation through performance and art. Surtshellir provides rare material evidence for ritual engagement with a being later classified as a giant.

The surviving evidence does not show a uniform Scandinavian doctrine. Oral traditions, local visual programs, court poetry, ritual practice, and place-lore likely differed.

### Conversion, c. 950–1100

Christianization suppressed public pagan sacrifice and reclassified many older beings as devils, trolls, monsters, or deceptive spirits. Yet conversion did not erase the narrative material. Giant myths could be retold as heroic entertainment, poetic lore, ancestral history, or moralized monstrosity.

The ending of ritual activity at Surtshellir shortly after conversion is a rare material correlation. Elsewhere, the lack of temples or inscriptions prevents comparable cult histories.

### Twelfth and thirteenth centuries

Christian Icelandic and Danish authors preserved and transformed the corpus:

- Eddic poems were copied, arranged, and sometimes revised.
- Snorri organized fragmentary traditions into a cosmology useful for interpreting skaldic poetry.
- Saxo euhemerized gods and monsterized supernatural rivals.
- Legendary sagas expanded northern giant realms, mixed marriages, cannibalism, and troll combat.

This is simultaneously preservation and innovation. Snorri is not a neutral stenographer, but dismissing him because he was Christian would discard the fullest medieval witness.

### Late medieval to early-modern period

*jǫtunn*, *risi*, and *troll* increasingly overlap. Giants migrate from cosmology into landscape legend: unusual rocks are thrown or carried by trolls; mountains contain giant halls; trolls flee church bells or turn to stone in sunlight. Christian motifs coexist with older names.

Saxo’s *Gesta Danorum* was printed in 1514. The Eddic manuscript GKS 2365 4to came into Bishop Brynjólfur Sveinsson’s hands in 1643 and was sent to King Frederik III in 1662. Antiquarian publication made the material available to European scholarship and Romanticism.

### Eighteenth and nineteenth centuries

Collectors and scholars—especially the Brothers Grimm and Scandinavian antiquarians—grouped medieval Jotnar with living troll traditions and comparative Indo-European mythology. Romantic literature and painting transformed the Jotnar into monumental embodiments of savage nature and opponents of national gods.

Wagner’s *Der Ring des Nibelungen* (text developed 1848–1852; first complete performance 1876) created the giants Fasolt and Fafner as builders of Valhalla who demand Freia and receive the Rhine treasure. Wagner drew most heavily on *Vǫlsunga saga*, alongside the *Nibelungenlied*, Eddic texts, Grimm, and other scholarship ([University of Texas guide to the *Ring*’s sources](https://www.laits.utexas.edu/wagner/ringsources/RingLiterarySources.htm)). Wagner’s giants are therefore **[MI]**, not otherwise-lost ancient Jotnar.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Fantasy taxonomies fixed Jotnar as biologically distinct giant races. Tolkien drew deeply on Germanic philology but created his own giants, trolls, and *ettins*. Marvel Comics, beginning with the modern superhero Thor in 1962, popularized “Frost Giants” as a coherent blue-skinned or monstrous people and made Loki their adopted child; that genealogy is not Eddic. Games subsequently standardized frost, fire, hill, stone, and storm giants.

Modern Heathen communities vary. Some honor only Æsir and Vanir; some include giant-associated deities such as Skaði, Gerðr, Ægir, and Jörð; others construct Jotunn-centered practices under labels such as *Jötnatrú*, *Rökkatrú*, or *Þursatrú*. These are small, decentralized modern movements whose scale cannot be established reliably. Their liturgies and ethical readings are **[MI]**, even when grounded in medieval texts.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship, occultism, and esotericism

Unlike Egyptian or Greco-Roman gods, Jotnar were not central to Renaissance Hermetism, ceremonial magic, or the Golden Dawn. Claims that Golden Dawn adepts transmitted ancient Jotunn invocations are unsupported. Their significant occult reception is largely late twentieth- and twenty-first-century Heathen, runic, and “northern tradition” practice.

Modern rune manuals often transform *þurs* into an abstract cosmic “force,” assign it colors, tarot correspondences, chakras, or deterministic divinatory meanings, and present these as ancient. The medieval rune poems give brief gnomic verses, not a complete divinatory system. Such correspondence systems are **[MI]**.

### Romantic nationalism and racialism

Nineteenth-century nationalism treated conflicts between gods and giants as allegories of civilization conquering nature or one “race” defeating another. Older theories identifying Jotnar with Finns, Sámi, Celts, or conquered aboriginal peoples lack adequate evidence and are discredited as universal explanations.

Germanic myth was later appropriated by völkisch and National Socialist movements. Modern far-right scenes continue to use Norse-Germanic names and images, though Jotnar themselves are less stable identity symbols than Óðinn, Thor’s hammer, or runes. No political meaning inheres in the medieval beings; appropriation is historically subsequent. Scholarship stresses that modern Heathenism also includes anti-racist, feminist, ecological, queer, and universalist currents ([review of *Old Norse Myths as Political Ideologies*](https://scholarworks.iu.edu/journals/index.php/tmr/article/view/33485); [study of far-right appropriation](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/oa-mono/10.4324/9781003206309-4/aims-causes-consequences-far-right-appropriation-germanic-history-culture-georg-schuppener)).

### Literature, opera, comics, film, and games

- **Jacob Grimm, *Deutsche Mythologie* (1835):** comparative synthesis that powerfully shaped later ideas of a unified Germanic mythology.
- **Wagner, *Ring* (1848–1876):** Fasolt and Fafner build Valhalla; Fafner kills Fasolt and becomes a dragon. This is synthetic opera, not a medieval Jotunn story.
- **Tolkien:** revived *ettin* and reworked Germanic giant/troll material within an invented mythology.
- **Marvel Comics and film:** Loki becomes a Frost Giant adopted by Odin; Laufey becomes his father; Jotunheim becomes a visually coherent alien world. Medieval sources instead make Loki the son of Fárbauti and Laufey/Nál; they do not narrate adoption by Óðinn.
- **Dungeons & Dragons:** from the 1970s systematized elemental giant “races,” social hierarchies, alignments, and statistics.
- **Video games:** *God of War*, *Assassin’s Creed Valhalla*, *Jotun*, and others turn Jotnar into a civilization, moral faction, or extinct people. These works have materially shaped popular understanding but remain modern retellings.
- **Commercial use:** Jotun is the name of a Norwegian paints and coatings company; “Jotun” also appears in software, sports, music, and product branding. Such use draws on the modern association with size, northernness, and elemental strength.

### Troll continuity

Medieval *troll* already overlaps with Jotnar, but continuity must not be imagined as an unchanged pagan belief surviving intact. Later Scandinavian trolls inherit giant-associated features—mountain homes, hostility to churches, rock-throwing, great age, transformed stone bodies—but also absorb ghosts, witches, devils, huldrefolk, and fairy-tale ogres.

Folklore continuity is dynamic transmission and recontextualization, not a fossil. Stephen Mitchell emphasizes that demonstrable continuity requires attention to communality, variation, transmission, and changing function ([“Continuity: Folklore’s Problem Child?”](https://smitchell.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/continuity-folklores-problem-child)). The proposition “modern trolls are simply the old Jotnar under a new name” is therefore too strong; “later troll traditions inherited and transformed parts of the Jotunn complex” is defensible.

## 9. Historiography and contested material

### Etymology

The “devourer/eater” derivation of *jǫtunn* is traditional and semantically attractive, but its exact prehistoric formation is reconstructed. It must not be expanded into a claim that all Jotnar were originally cannibal demons. *Þurs* has a different history and semantic field; modern attempts to distinguish “Jotnar = neutral nature spirits” from “Thursar = wholly evil beings” create a theological binary the medieval corpus does not consistently maintain.

### Origin theories

- **Nature-myth school:** giants as glaciers, storms, mountains, winter, or volcanic fire. Individual figures support such associations; a total one-to-one code does not.
- **Historical-race theory:** giants as memory of Finns, Sámi, indigenous Scandinavians, or conquered peoples. Some saga geography engages ethnic frontiers, but the global theory is discredited.
- **Indo-European comparativism:** Ymir compared with Indic Yama and reconstructed *Yemo*, and his killing with primal-sacrifice myths. The structural parallels are important but do not license rewriting the Norse sources as fragments of one recoverable ur-text.
- **Dumézilian trifunctionalism:** productive for some divine structures, less successful as an exhaustive classification of Jotnar.
- **Structuralism:** gods and giants as center/periphery, culture/nature, order/chaos, male exchange/female exogamy. This explains many plots but risks flattening hospitable, beautiful, divine, and legally integrated Jotnar.
- **Postcolonial readings:** interpret the Æsir’s acquisition of land, women, labor, knowledge, and resources from outsiders as ideological narratives of extraction. These are modern critical readings, not claims of direct descent from colonial doctrine.

### Are Jotnar gods?

The sources do not give a universal ontological answer.

- They are often contrasted with *goð* and *æsir*.
- Some receive divine spouses or become members of the gods’ social order.
- Some—Jörð, Skaði, Gerðr, Ægir—are included in divine lists or kennings.
- Loki crosses boundaries more radically than any simple taxonomy permits.

Thus “Jotnar are gods,” “Jotnar are demons,” and “Jotnar are merely another species” are all overstatements. Medieval classification is contextual.

### Frost and fire giants

“Frost-giants” are textually secure. Surtr and Muspell’s forces are textually associated with fire. A balanced two-race scheme is **[LS/MI]**, produced partly by Snorri’s ordered cosmography and decisively by modern fantasy.

### Jotunn worship

The majority historical conclusion remains that there is no evidence for a general Jotunn cult. Surtshellir complicates an absolute denial and provides evidence of ritual engagement with Surtr. Giant-associated divine women may also have been worshipped, though proof varies. It is better to distinguish:

1. no documented collective cult;
2. possible cult or propitiation of individual beings;
3. literary claims about worship, often late and Christian;
4. modern devotional practice.

### Human sacrifice and violence

No evidence ties human sacrifice specifically to the Jotnar. Animal deposition at Surtshellir is documented. Mythic violence is pervasive: gods kill Jotnar; Jotnar threaten, abduct, and kill; women are treated as payment or coerced; oaths are broken. Sanitizing this produces a false corpus, but converting literary violence into reconstructed ritual is equally false.

### Suppression

Christianization ended or transformed pre-Christian rites, but no known conversion law singles out Jotnar worship. “The church persecuted Jotunn priests” is unsupported. Jotnar survived readily as literary monsters and folklore beings precisely because belief in non-god supernatural beings could be demonized, historicized, or treated as entertainment.

### Primary evidence and repositories

- **GKS 2365 4to:** Árni Magnússon Institute, Reykjavík; principal Poetic Edda manuscript.
- **AM 748 I a 4to:** early fourteenth-century fragment preserving several mythological poems.
- **Prose Edda manuscripts:** Codex Upsaliensis, Codex Regius of Snorri’s Edda, Codex Wormianus, and Codex Trajectinus.
- **Skaldic verse:** preserved across Edda and saga manuscripts; now critically edited by the Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages project.
- **Runic inscriptions:** distributed among Scandinavian museums and archaeological collections; searchable through the Scandinavian Runic-text Database.
- **Visual monuments:** Altuna, Hørdum, Gosforth, and other stones remain context-dependent archaeological objects rather than illustrations keyed to modern editions.

### Major modern scholarship

Foundational works include Jan de Vries’s *Altgermanische Religionsgeschichte*, Georges Dumézil’s comparative studies, Gabriel Turville-Petre’s *Myth and Religion of the North*, Rudolf Simek’s *Dictionary of Northern Mythology*, John Lindow’s *Norse Mythology*, Andy Orchard’s *Dictionary of Norse Myth and Legend*, Margaret Clunies Ross’s work on myth and poetics, and the relevant entries in *Kulturhistorisk leksikon for nordisk middelalder*. These differ in method and age; none should replace consultation of the primary text and manuscript.
