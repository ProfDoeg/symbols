# Ratatoskr: Research Dossier

**Class:** Ratatoskr is classified by the medieval sources as an **íkorni**, “squirrel,” inhabiting Yggdrasil—not as a god, jötunn, demon, land-spirit, or member of either the Æsir or Vanir. Modern labels such as “messenger god,” “spirit,” “trickster deity,” or “guardian of the Nine Realms” are interpretations or inventions, not medieval classifications.

**Earliest secure attestation:** *Grímnismál* 32, preserved in the Icelandic Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda, GKS 2365 4to, written in the later thirteenth century, probably around 1270. The poem itself is older than this manuscript; proposed dates range widely, while Vittorio Mattioli’s source-critical study argues for composition in Norway between the mid-tenth and mid-eleventh centuries. That reconstruction remains a scholarly dating, not physical evidence from the Viking Age. Ratatoskr’s earliest surviving material witness is therefore the thirteenth-century manuscript; his existence in an earlier oral or poetic tradition is probable but cannot be dated securely to a particular year.

**Documentary baseline:** Ratatoskr’s complete medieval narrative record consists essentially of **one Eddic stanza and one prose passage based on the same tradition**. No securely identified Viking-Age inscription, image, cult site, prayer, sacrifice, theophoric name, or independent skaldic narrative names him.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### The figure

In *Grímnismál* 32 Ratatoskr is the squirrel that runs on or along the ash Yggdrasil, carrying the eagle’s words downward to Níðhöggr. In *Gylfaginning* 16 Snorri Sturluson changes the neutral “eagle’s words” into **öfundarorð**, hostile, envious, or malicious words, carried “between” the eagle and Níðhöggr. The familiar modern personality—a gleeful gossip who deliberately keeps a cosmic feud alive—depends principally upon Snorri’s elaboration.

Ratatoskr has no stated parentage, sex narrative, residence apart from Yggdrasil, independent mythic adventure, cult office, or role at Ragnarök. Old Norse grammatical references to the squirrel are masculine, but the sources supply no biological or genealogical account.

### Name forms

- **Ratatoskr** — normalized Old Norse, masculine nominative singular.
- **Ratatoscr** — spelling in GKS 2365 4to, *Grímnismál* 32.
- **Raratoskr** — variant reported from AM 748 I 4to; generally treated as scribal rather than as a separate traditional name.
- **Ratatǫskr / Ratatöskr** — common normalized or modern Scandinavian/German forms employing an *ǫ/ö* spelling. The older lexicographical discussion cautions that the second element is properly *-toskr*, not independently attested *töskr*.
- **Ratatosk** — conventional English, Danish, and Norwegian simplification, dropping the Old Norse nominative *-r*.
- **Ratatösk**, **Ratatwisker**, and similar forms occur in modern popular retellings but have no claim to separate medieval attestation.

There are **no documented medieval epithets or cult titles**. “Sly Messenger,” “Drill-Tooth,” “Bore-Tooth,” “Gnaw-Tooth,” and “Gossiping Squirrel” are translations, interpretive descriptions, or commercial titles.

### Etymology: the contested evidence

The name is a compound, but both its exact morphology and translation have been disputed.

1. **“Bore-tooth,” “drill-tooth,” or “gnaw-tooth” — current majority interpretation.**  
   The first member is associated with Old Norse *rati*, “borer, drill,” familiar as **Rati**, the auger used by Óðinn in the mead-of-poetry episode in *Hávamál* 106. The second is reconstructed as *toskr*, “tusk/tooth,” cognate with Old English *tusc* and Old Frisian *tusk*. Jens Peter Schjødt summarizes the current analysis as *rati* “drill” plus otherwise unattested *toskr* “tusk,” a North Germanic reflex of the Germanic word represented by English *tusk*. Modern reference works consequently offer “Drill-Tooth,” “Bore-Tooth,” or “Gnaw-Tooth.” This is a **modern philological reconstruction**, not a gloss supplied by either Edda. [Schjødt, *Lex.dk*](https://lex.dk/Ratatosk)

2. **Guðbrandur Vigfússon’s “traveller/climber tooth.”**  
   The Cleasby–Vigfússon dictionary connected *rati* with travelling or finding a way and understood the compound approximately as “Toskr the traveller” or “climber.” This reading fits the squirrel’s ceaseless movement but is no longer the prevailing explanation. Mattioli’s commentary records the proposal alongside the alternatives. [Mattioli, pp. 211–12](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/12219/VittorioMattioliPhDThesis.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=3)

3. **Sophus Bugge’s Old English loan theory: “rat-tooth.”**  
   Bugge argued that the second element was borrowed from Old English *tusc* and that the first represented Old English *ræt*, “rat.” His principal difficulty was that *-toskr* occurs nowhere else as an independent Old Norse word. Albert Morey Sturtevant rejected the treatment of the first member as an English loan because native Old Norse already had *Rati*, the named boring instrument in *Hávamál*. Sturtevant further argued that a hapax legomenon in a proper name does not establish foreign origin. His article, “Three Old Norse Words: Gamban, Ratatoskr, and Gymir,” appeared in *Scandinavian Studies* 28 (1956), pp. 109–14. The publication is independently catalogued in the Árni Magnússon Institute’s onomastic bibliography. [Árni Magnússon Institute bibliography](https://arnastofnun.is/sites/default/files/2024-05/Ritaskra%CC%81%20um%20i%CC%81slenska%20nafnr%C3%A6%C3%B0i.%20Ma%C3%AD%202024.pdf)

The majority “boring/gnawing tooth” interpretation is plausible and zoologically apt, but it does **not** prove that a lost myth described Ratatoskr boring into the tree. Etymology cannot substitute for narrative evidence.

### Classification and alleged equivalents

No ancient or medieval source equates Ratatoskr with a Greek, Roman, Celtic, Slavic, Sámi, Christian, or other Germanic being. Comparisons between the eagle-and-serpent world-tree complex and other Indo-European cosmologies are scholarly comparison, not historical syncretism. Ratatoskr has no documented *interpretatio romana* and does not appear in Tacitus’s *Germania*, the Merseburg Charms, Saxo Grammaticus, Adam of Bremen, or surviving continental Germanic mythological texts.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### *Grímnismál* 32

**Tier:** securely attested medieval material; possibly preserving an earlier poetic tradition.

**Language:** Old Norse, in an Icelandic manuscript tradition.

**Physical witnesses:**

- **GKS 2365 4to**, the Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda, written in Iceland in the later thirteenth century.
- **AM 748 I 4to**, a fragmentary independent witness. Textual analysis indicates that neither surviving manuscript was copied directly from the other; both probably descend through lost copies from an earlier archetype. [Mattioli on the two witnesses](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/12219/VittorioMattioliPhDThesis.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=3)

Codex Regius reads:

> *Ratatoscr heitir íkorni,  
> er renna skal  
> at aski Yggdrasils;  
> arnar orð  
> hann skal ofan bera  
> ok segja Níðhǫggvi niðr.*

A deliberately close translation is:

> “Ratatoskr is the squirrel called, who shall run along the ash of Yggdrasil; the eagle’s word or words he shall carry from above and speak to Níðhöggr below.”

The stanza establishes only the following:

- Ratatoskr is a squirrel.
- Ratatoskr moves along Yggdrasil.
- An eagle is above.
- Níðhöggr is below.
- Ratatoskr carries the eagle’s *orð*, “word/words,” downward.

It does **not** explicitly say that Ratatoskr:

- carries messages upward;
- invents or alters the messages;
- carries “insults” or gossip;
- gnaws Yggdrasil;
- speaks with Veðrfölnir;
- serves the gods;
- travels among nine worlds;
- causes the eagle’s and Níðhöggr’s hostility;
- survives or participates in Ragnarök.

The stanza is surrounded by a catalogue of Yggdrasil’s inhabitants and injuries. Stanza 33 names four stags that gnaw its high branches; stanza 34 lists serpents below it; stanza 35 says a stag bites above while Níðhöggr cuts or damages below. Ratatoskr is not named among the tree’s gnawers. The critical text and manuscript transcription make this division explicit. [Critical edition, stanzas 32–35](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/12219/VittorioMattioliPhDThesis.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=3)

### Dating *Grímnismál*

The surviving manuscript is thirteenth-century, but the poem’s composition is debated. Linguistic, metrical, religious, and political arguments have produced dates from the pagan Viking Age into Christian Iceland. Mattioli argues for a Norwegian setting between the mid-tenth and mid-eleventh centuries, while also stressing the problems of recovering a fixed “original” behind later written copies. His dating makes Ratatoskr potentially a late-pagan or conversion-era poetic figure, but this remains **modern scholarly reconstruction** rather than a securely dated tenth-century attestation. [Mattioli’s dating conclusion](https://research-repository.st-andrews.ac.uk/bitstream/handle/10023/12219/VittorioMattioliPhDThesis.pdf?isAllowed=y&sequence=3)

### *Gylfaginning* 16

**Tier:** later canonical elaboration / learned systematization.

**Composition:** conventionally associated with Snorri Sturluson and dated around 1220.

**Surviving principal witnesses:** Codex Upsaliensis, first quarter of the fourteenth century; Codex Regius of the Prose Edda, first half of the fourteenth century; Codex Wormianus, mid-fourteenth century; and the much later Codex Trajectinus, copied around 1595 from a lost medieval exemplar. Anthony Faulkes emphasizes that the manuscripts differ substantially and that no single manuscript is a transparent authorial original. [Faulkes, manuscript introduction](https://vsnr.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/VSNR_Edda-1_prologue_gylfa.pdf)

The relevant sentence reads:

> *Íkorni sá, er heitir Ratatoskr, renn upp ok niðr eftir askinum ok berr öfundarorð milli arnarins ok Níðhöggs.*

“That squirrel which is called Ratatoskr runs up and down along the ash and carries malicious/envious words between the eagle and Níðhöggr.” [Old Norse *Gylfaginning*](https://www.heimskringla.no/wiki/Gylfaginning)

Snorri makes two consequential changes:

- movement becomes explicitly **up and down**;
- the cargo becomes **öfundarorð**, words of envy, hostility, or malice, passed **between** the two creatures.

These details underlie the standard “insult-bearing gossip” account. Because *Grímnismál* has merely *arnar orð*, “the eagle’s words,” the malicious quality may be Snorri’s interpretation or an otherwise unattested tradition. It should not automatically be projected backward into the poem’s earliest recoverable form.

### The rest of the proposed corpus

No additional securely identified occurrence has been established in:

- other Poetic Edda poems;
- *Skáldskaparmál* or *Háttatal* as an independent narrative;
- *Heimskringla*;
- the indexed skaldic corpus;
- Saxo Grammaticus;
- Adam of Bremen;
- Tacitus’s *Germania*;
- the Merseburg Charms;
- known runic inscriptions;
- *Völsunga saga*;
- *Nibelungenlied*;
- Wagner’s *Ring* texts.

Those works are indispensable for other Norse or Germanic beings, but they do not enlarge Ratatoskr’s medieval dossier. Claims that Ratatoskr is widely attested “throughout the sagas” are therefore false or misleading.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain and function

Ratatoskr’s securely attested function is vertical communication along Yggdrasil. Snorri’s version makes him a carrier of antagonistic speech and thereby an agent maintaining conflict between the tree’s summit and root.

Symbolic descriptions of him as the embodiment of gossip, rumor, social media, communication networks, cosmic nervous systems, diplomacy, mischief, or psychological “shadow work” are modern readings. They may be coherent applications, but they are not recoverable medieval doctrines.

John Lindow interprets Ratatoskr against saga society: low-status intermediaries who carried hostile words could perpetuate feuds, which may explain why the part is assigned to a small animal. Richard Thorington and Katie Ferrell instead suggest a naturalistic stimulus—the European red squirrel’s loud scolding alarm calls. Both are **modern scholarly interpretations**, not preserved explanations.

### Powers and limitations

Attested powers are minimal:

- rapid movement on Yggdrasil;
- speech or at least transmission of speech;
- passage between the eagle’s upper station and Níðhöggr’s lower one.

The sources give no supernatural weapon, shape-changing power, immortality, magic, divine omniscience, control over the tree, or ability to cross independently into every world.

### The “gnawing Yggdrasil” claim

This is not directly attested. *Grímnismál* separates Ratatoskr from the damaging animals:

- Ratatoskr carries the eagle’s words in stanza 32.
- The four stags gnaw the high shoots in stanza 33.
- serpents lie beneath the ash in stanza 34.
- Níðhöggr damages it from below in stanza 35.

“Gnaw-Tooth” may allude to rodent dentition, but the name’s probable meaning cannot establish an otherwise unrecorded action. Accounts making Ratatoskr one of the creatures physically consuming the world-tree conflate adjacent stanzas or infer behavior from the proposed etymology.

### Medieval and early-modern iconography

There is no securely identified Viking-Age depiction. Squirrels or quadrupeds in Germanic art cannot be called Ratatoskr without identifying context.

The earliest unambiguous surviving depiction appears to be in **AM 738 4to**, the *Edda oblongata* or *Langa Edda*, an Icelandic paper manuscript dated approximately 1680. Its illustrated Yggdrasil labels the creature with text explaining that Ratatoskr carries hostile words between the eagle and Níðhöggr. The animal is oddly doglike and bears what looks like a horn or projecting tusk. No accompanying text explains the horn, so interpretations connecting it to *-toskr* “tusk” remain speculation. [Digitized-image description](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AAM_738_4to_Ratatoskr.png)

AM 738 4to contains 135 leaves, Prose Edda material, Eddic and skaldic poems, a rune poem, Christian poetry, and numerous illustrations. It is an early-modern antiquarian witness, not Viking-Age cult art. It entered Árni Magnússon’s possession around 1700, remained in Copenhagen, and returned to Iceland in 1991. [Manuscript description](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/AM_738_4to)

Modern depictions usually supply attributes absent from the sources: acorns, bags, letters, horns, magical armor, multiple spectral personalities, or weapons. No sacred colors, numbers, days, plants, celestial bodies, regalia, or ritual substances are historically attested.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Beings

### Genealogy

No source names Ratatoskr’s parents, offspring, siblings, mate, species-community, creator, owner, or master. No source places him in a divine hierarchy. Family trees supplied by games, role-playing systems, fan works, or modern devotional writers are inventions.

### Documented interactions

| Other being | Interaction | Source and date | Evidentiary tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Níðhöggr** | Ratatoskr carries the eagle’s word downward to Níðhöggr. Snorri later says he carries malicious words in both directions between Níðhöggr and the eagle. | *Grímnismál* 32, preserved c. 1270; *Gylfaginning* 16, composed c. 1220 and preserved in fourteenth-century manuscripts | Secure medieval attestation; Snorri’s bidirectionality and malicious wording are later canonical elaboration |
| **The unnamed eagle on Yggdrasil** | Its word or words are carried downward by Ratatoskr; Snorri makes it one party to a reciprocal exchange of hostile speech. | Same passages | Secure medieval attestation / later elaboration |
| **Óðinn as Grímnir** | Óðinn, within the poem’s frame, tells the audience that Ratatoskr exists and describes his function. He does not meet or command the squirrel. | *Grímnismál* narrative frame and stanza 32 | Indirect joint textual appearance, not an enacted encounter |

### Commonly alleged but unattested interactions

- **Veðrfölnir:** *Gylfaginning* places the hawk Veðrfölnir between the eyes of the unnamed eagle. Ratatoskr’s messages, however, are said to pass between **the eagle** and Níðhöggr. Replacing the eagle with Veðrfölnir is a modern conflation.
- **Yggdrasil:** Ratatoskr moves on the tree, but the sources do not personify a conversation or pact between them.
- **The four stags:** they appear in the same catalogue but do not interact with Ratatoskr.
- **The Norns, Heimdallr, Loki, Þórr, Freyja, and other gods:** no interaction is recorded.
- **Hræsvelgr:** identifying Yggdrasil’s unnamed eagle with Hræsvelgr is unsupported; Hræsvelgr is a jötunn in eagle-form associated with winds elsewhere.
- **Ragnarök participants:** Ratatoskr receives no role in the surviving Ragnarök narratives.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Ratatoskr has one surviving mythic situation rather than a developed cycle.

### The traffic of words on Yggdrasil

**Early recoverable form:** In *Grímnismál*, Óðinn—speaking as Grímnir while tortured between fires by King Geirröðr—delivers cosmological knowledge. Ratatoskr appears in a sequence describing Yggdrasil’s roots and animals. The squirrel runs along the ash, bearing the eagle’s words down to Níðhöggr. No beginning of the hostility, content of the communication, motive, or conclusion is supplied.

**Snorrian form:** *Gylfaginning* places the information within Gangleri’s interrogation of the three enthroned speakers. Snorri says the squirrel runs both upward and downward and transports *öfundarorð* between the eagle and Níðhöggr. This turns a one-way courier into a reciprocal carrier of hostility.

**Possible textual loss:** The eagle appears abruptly in *Grímnismál* 32 without previous identification. Some editors have suspected that a stanza once stood between stanzas 31 and 32 and attempted to reconstruct it using Snorri’s description of the eagle and Veðrfölnir. No manuscript preserves that hypothetical stanza. It is therefore editorial reconstruction, not lost text recovered from evidence.

**Interpretive functions:**

- A vertical link between the opposed extremities of the world-tree.
- A feud-bearer analogous to human intermediaries who repeat injurious speech.
- A small-scale agent of the continuing stress and deterioration affecting Yggdrasil.
- Possibly a mythologization of squirrels’ rapid climbing and scolding calls.

The last three are scholarly readings. The texts themselves offer no explicit allegorical explanation.

### No documented resolution

Neither the eagle nor Níðhöggr kills Ratatoskr. Ratatoskr does not reconcile them. No source says what happens to him at Ragnarök or after the renewed world emerges. Any such ending is modern fiction.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

No cult of Ratatoskr is documented in the pre-Christian or medieval record.

There are:

- no temples or shrines assigned to him;
- no priesthood;
- no festival or calendar day;
- no sacrifices or offerings;
- no prayers, hymns, invocations, charms, or magical formulae;
- no votive images;
- no theophoric personal names securely derived from Ratatoskr;
- no runic dedications;
- no place-name securely demonstrating worship;
- no legal or ecclesiastical prohibition naming his cult.

Consequently, human sacrifice, animal sacrifice, temple prostitution, cult violence, and cult economics cannot be attributed to Ratatoskr. General evidence concerning sacrifice in Norse religion does not authorize assignment of those practices to every named mythic creature.

The absence of cult evidence does not prove that no one ever told stories about, invoked, or made offerings to the squirrel. It means that no such practice is presently documentable. Treating Ratatoskr as a widely worshipped Viking “messenger god” is a **modern invention**.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Figure

### Possible oral and Viking-Age background

If the proposed tenth- or eleventh-century dating of *Grímnismál* is accepted, Ratatoskr belonged to oral-poetic cosmology around the conversion period. There is no contemporary inscription or object confirming that date, and the figure’s geographic distribution cannot be reconstructed beyond the later Icelandic manuscript tradition.

### Thirteenth century: poetic preservation and Snorrian systematization

Around 1220, the Prose Edda’s compiler incorporated Ratatoskr into a systematic description of Yggdrasil. The addition of *öfundarorð* and reciprocal movement furnished the character with the malicious-gossip identity that later became standard.

Later in the thirteenth century, GKS 2365 4to preserved *Grímnismál* 32. This is the earliest surviving physical artifact that names Ratatoskr.

### Fourteenth century: manuscript transmission

The principal Prose Edda manuscripts transmitted the *Gylfaginning* account in differing textual recensions. Their broader differences warn against treating “Snorri’s text” as a single perfectly preserved authorial wording. Faulkes dates the Uppsala manuscript to the first quarter, the Prose Edda Codex Regius to the first half, and Codex Wormianus to around the middle of the fourteenth century. [Faulkes](https://vsnr.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/VSNR_Edda-1_prologue_gylfa.pdf)

### Conversion and suppression

Ratatoskr’s sources were written and copied by Christians after Iceland’s conversion. The Prose Edda is a learned Christian-era project preserving and reorganizing pre-Christian poetic material. Nevertheless, no conversion narrative records missionaries suppressing a Ratatoskr cult, destroying his shrine, or demonizing him. Claims of such suppression are unsupported.

### Seventeenth century: antiquarian visualization

AM 738 4to, c. 1680, produced the first securely identifiable surviving image. Its labeled, horned squirrel demonstrates early-modern Icelandic reception of Eddic learning, not continuity of pre-Christian sacred iconography.

### Nineteenth and twentieth centuries: philology and reference mythology

Editions, dictionaries, and translations transformed the two passages into an internationally accessible capsule myth. Guðbrandur Vigfússon, Sophus Bugge, Sturtevant, de Vries, Turville-Petre, Dumézil, Simek, Lindow, Orchard, and later translators placed Ratatoskr within reconstructed “Norse mythology.” Ratatoskr remained peripheral in scholarship because the evidence is so slight.

Wagner did not give Ratatoskr a role in *Der Ring des Nibelungen*. The omission is significant: the nineteenth-century operatic construction that profoundly shaped modern Norse-Germanic imagery did not materially develop this figure.

### Present-day religion

Individual Heathens and eclectic practitioners now sometimes pray to, offer to, or “work with” Ratatoskr as a messenger, communication spirit, trickster, mediator, or patron of neurodivergence. Online testimonies document that such practices occur, but they do not establish institutional scale or historical continuity. A 2020 discussion, for example, describes using a squirrel tail as a vessel and Ratatoskr as a messenger in divination—clear evidence of contemporary personal practice, not ancient tradition. [Modern practitioner testimony](https://www.reddit.com/r/heathenry/comments/f2lx06/ratatoskr_help_a_witch_out_i_dont_know_anything/)

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Occult and esoteric literature

Ratatoskr has no established role in Renaissance Hermetism, ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn, Thelema, classical grimoires, or the principal early-modern Icelandic magical books. Modern occult websites sometimes classify him as an independent spirit who communicates with every class of Norse being, but this exceeds the medieval evidence. “Ratatoskr energy,” shadow-work exercises, communication spells, acorn offerings, and claims that he moves between upper, middle, and lower spiritual worlds are contemporary constructions.

No evidence located supports a continuous Ratatoskr occult tradition before the modern revival of Norse mythology.

### Literature

Neil Gaiman’s *Norse Mythology* (2017) helped popularize a coherent, novelistic Norse cosmos for contemporary readers. Gaiman presents his work as a modern literary retelling moving from creation through Ragnarök, not as a critical edition. Modern retellings commonly amplify Ratatoskr’s personality and treat Snorri’s malicious words as the ancient figure’s defining nature. [Gaiman’s official description](https://www.neilgaiman.com/works/Books/Norse%2BMythology/ed/468/)

Katherine Rundell’s *Impossible Creatures* uses plural “ratatoskas,” turning a unique named squirrel into a creature-type. That is a modern fantasy transformation.

### Comics

Marvel’s *The Unbeatable Squirrel Girl* reimagined Ratatoskr as a powerful, shapeshifting female antagonist capable of spreading discord on a mass scale. Medieval Ratatoskr has no recorded sex-changing or shape-changing power and is not called a god. The adaptation materially strengthened the association between Ratatoskr and actively manufactured social conflict.

### Games

- **SMITE** introduced Ratatoskr as a playable “Norse god” on 2 June 2015, titled “The Sly Messenger” and classed mechanically as an assassin. The divine classification, combat abilities, magical acorns, and battlefield role are game inventions. [Official SMITE community record](https://smite.fandom.com/wiki/Ratatoskr)
- **God of War** (2018) presents a summonable “Bitter Squirrel”; *God of War Ragnarök* (2022) expands Ratatoskr into Yggdrasil’s immortal caretaker with several spectral personality aspects. The stewardship, immortality, and divided selves are modern narrative inventions, although the insulting speech adapts Snorri. [PlayStation game page](https://www.playstation.com/en-us/games/god-of-war-ragnarok/)
- **La-Mulana 2** makes Ratatoskr a recurring combatant and an illusion associated with Hræsvelgr; that relationship is unattested in Norse sources.
- **Fire Emblem Heroes** transforms Ratatoskr into a female humanoid “Healing Hands” character. The gender, family, healing office, and humanoid form belong to the game.
- Tabletop fantasy has pluralized “ratatosks” into a species inhabiting Yggdrasil. This is modern taxonomic world-building.

### Modern art

Modern illustrators normally depict a red squirrel, reflecting the native Eurasian red squirrel rather than a physical description in the Eddas. Dagfin Werenskiold included Ratatoskr in the monumental *Yggdrasilfrisen* at Oslo City Hall, part of the twentieth-century national-romantic monumental visualization of Norse myth. Such art has helped make Ratatoskr visually recognizable despite the absence of Viking-Age iconography.

### Political and extremist appropriation

Norse symbols broadly have been appropriated by nationalist and extremist movements, but this research found no well-documented, sustained political or extremist Ratatoskr cult, emblem, or doctrine. It would be unsound to transfer the political history of runes, Óðinn, or Þórr’s hammer automatically to this minor squirrel. Absence of specific evidence is the finding.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### How much independent evidence exists?

The principal debate is less about competing myths than about whether the two passages represent two witnesses or one tradition reused by Snorri. Since *Gylfaginning* closely follows Eddic material throughout its Yggdrasil account, the conservative conclusion is that Ratatoskr has one recoverable poetic notice plus Snorri’s interpretive expansion.

Rudolf Simek accordingly treated the squirrel as an “embellishing detail.” That judgment describes Ratatoskr’s limited structural place in the surviving material; it does not demonstrate that the figure was invented by the poet or lacked significance in oral tradition.

### Did Snorri preserve or invent the malicious words?

Two readings remain possible:

- Snorri possessed oral or written information, now lost, explaining the eagle’s “words” as hostile.
- Snorri inferred hostility and reciprocal transmission from the eagle–Níðhöggr opposition and expanded the laconic stanza.

Because no independent source preserves *öfundarorð*, it should be labeled a **Snorrian elaboration**, though not necessarily Snorri’s personal invention.

### Is the eagle Veðrfölnir?

No. In *Gylfaginning*, an unnamed eagle occupies the crown and the hawk Veðrfölnir sits between its eyes. Ratatoskr’s exchange is with the eagle. Popular retellings often replace the eagle with the only named bird in the scene, but the grammar does not support that identification.

### Does Ratatoskr gnaw the tree?

No extant passage says so. The confusion arises from:

- the likely “boring/gnawing tooth” etymology;
- the natural behavior of squirrels;
- Ratatoskr’s placement beside stanzas naming actual gnawers;
- modern summaries that treat every Yggdrasil animal as one of its destroyers.

The claim is plausible imaginative inference, not attested action.

### Etymology

The main historical positions are:

- traveller/climber interpretation: Cleasby–Vigfússon;
- Old English “rat-tooth” loan: Bugge;
- native “borer/gnawer” plus Germanic “tusk”: Sturtevant and the prevailing modern line;
- cautious modern recognition that the second element is otherwise unattested in Old Norse.

Certainty is unwarranted because the compound is unique.

### State of the evidence

The principal poetic witness is preserved at the Árni Magnússon Institute for Icelandic Studies in Reykjavík. The independent AM fragment and multiple Prose Edda recensions provide textual controls. AM 738 4to supplies the major early-modern image. Modern critical editions by Neckel–Kuhn, Jónas Kristjánsson and Vésteinn Ólason, Faulkes, and Mattioli permit comparison among manuscript readings.

Translations by Bellows, Larrington, Faulkes, Byock, and others vary in interpretive force. Translations that put “slander,” “gossip,” or “insults” directly into *Grímnismál* 32 silently import Snorri’s *öfundarorð* into the older poetic wording.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Ratatoskr was a Norse god of messages.
- Vikings worshipped him in dedicated temples.
- He carried messages among all nine worlds.
- He served Óðinn or Heimdallr.
- He spoke specifically to Veðrfölnir rather than the unnamed eagle.
- He gnawed Yggdrasil in the Eddas.
- He was punished, killed, or transformed at Ragnarök.
- The horn in AM 738 4to proves a Viking unicorn-squirrel tradition.
- Modern offerings, spirit-work systems, or personality correspondences descend continuously from pre-Christian practice.

None is supported by the surviving medieval record.

## 10. Chronology

| Date or period | Documentary event |
|---|---|
| **Possibly mid-10th to mid-11th century** | Proposed composition range for *Grímnismál* in Norway according to Mattioli. This is reconstructed, not a surviving contemporary attestation. |
| **c. 1000** | Iceland’s formal conversion provides the historical background for later Christian manuscript preservation; no Ratatoskr-specific suppression is recorded. |
| **c. 1220** | Conventional composition date of Snorri’s Prose Edda. *Gylfaginning* expands the squirrel’s activity into reciprocal carriage of *öfundarorð*. |
| **Later 13th century, commonly c. 1270** | GKS 2365 4to, Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda, records *Grímnismál* 32—the earliest securely surviving physical attestation of Ratatoskr. |
| **Late 13th/early 14th century** | AM 748 I 4to independently preserves *Grímnismál*, including the Ratatoskr stanza with minor spelling variation. |
| **First quarter of 14th century** | Codex Upsaliensis preserves an early recension of the Prose Edda. |
| **First half of 14th century** | Prose Edda Codex Regius is written. |
| **Mid-14th century** | Codex Wormianus is written. |
| **c. 1595** | Codex Trajectinus is copied from a now-lost medieval Prose Edda manuscript. |
| **c. 1680** | AM 738 4to depicts and labels Ratatoskr on Yggdrasil; this is the earliest unambiguous surviving image presently established. |
| **1874** | Cleasby–Vigfússon’s dictionary publishes the traveller/climber analysis of the name. |
| **Late 19th century** | Sophus Bugge advances the Old English “rat-tooth” loan hypothesis. |
| **1936** | Henry Adams Bellows’s English *Poetic Edda* helps standardize “Ratatosk” in Anglophone popular reception. |
| **1956** | Albert Morey Sturtevant publishes “Three Old Norse Words: Gamban, Ratatoskr, and Gymir,” defending a native “borer/gnawer” analysis. |
| **Late 20th–early 21st century** | Simek, Orchard, Lindow, Faulkes, Byock, and Larrington consolidate the modern reference-book image of “Bore-Tooth,” the squirrel carrying hostile words. |
| **2015** | Ratatoskr becomes a playable assassin and nominal “god” in *SMITE*. |
| **2015–16** | Marvel’s *The Unbeatable Squirrel Girl* develops Ratatoskr as a shapeshifting female fomenter of mass discord. |
| **2017** | Neil Gaiman’s *Norse Mythology* contributes to a new wave of popular Eddic retelling. |
| **2018** | *God of War* introduces the insulting “Bitter Squirrel” summon. |
| **2022** | *God of War Ragnarök* makes Ratatoskr Yggdrasil’s immortal caretaker with multiple spectral personality-aspects. |
| **2020s** | Online Heathen and eclectic practitioners document individual prayers, offerings, divination, and spirit-work involving Ratatoskr. These are modern religious innovations without demonstrable medieval continuity. |
| **2026** | The evidentiary position remains unchanged: two central medieval textual passages, no identified pre-Christian cult record, and one unambiguous early-modern manuscript image. |

### Evidentiary conclusion

Ratatoskr’s documented history is strikingly narrower than his present cultural visibility. The recoverable medieval being is a named squirrel on Yggdrasil who conveys the eagle’s words to Níðhöggr. Snorri makes the traffic reciprocal and malicious. Everything beyond that—gnawing the tree, travelling through nine worlds, divine rank, trickster theology, cult, magical patronage, immortality, guardianship, combat powers, gendered humanoid embodiments, or elaborate Ragnarök adventures—belongs to inference or modern reinvention unless new evidence appears.
