# Odin: Research Dossier

**Class:** God (*áss*, plural *Æsir*) in Old Norse tradition; a deified ancestral king in Snorri Sturluson’s euhemeristic histories; Wōdan/Wōden/Wuotan in related continental and English Germanic traditions; demonized ancestral spirit or spectral hunter in some post-conversion folklore; central deity in several modern Heathen religions.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The fifth-century CE gold bracteate X13 from the Vindelev hoard, Jutland, Denmark, bearing the Proto-Norse Elder Futhark sequence read *iz Wōd[a]nas weraz*, “he is Wōdan’s/Odin’s man.” The reading, announced in 2023, displaced the sixth-century Nordendorf fibula as the earliest direct named attestation. The object proves that the divine name and an elite relationship to its bearer existed in Migration-period Scandinavia; it does not by itself attest the later Eddic mythology. Tacitus’s *Germania* 9, written c. 98 CE, is an earlier but indirect and interpretive witness: Germanic peoples, he says, chiefly worship “Mercury” and sacrifice humans to him. Identifying this *Mercurius* as Wōdan is highly probable from later weekday equations and medieval glosses but remains Roman *interpretatio*, not a recorded native name.

## Evidentiary labels used

- **[EARLY]** Secure pre-Christian or Migration-/Viking-period evidence.
- **[MEDIEVAL]** Medieval textual attestation, often preserving older material but written under Christianity.
- **[SYSTEMATIZATION]** Learned organization or harmonization, especially by Snorri.
- **[REGIONAL]** A geographically restricted form or variant.
- **[SYNCRETISM]** Historical identification with another deity or figure.
- **[FOLKLORE]** Post-conversion vernacular tradition.
- **[RECONSTRUCTION]** Modern scholarly inference rather than an explicit primary-source statement.
- **[MODERN]** Modern religious, artistic, political, or commercial creation.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Identity

Odin is the most polymorphic of the named Norse gods: ruler and “father” of gods and humans in some medieval Icelandic texts; god of kingship, victory, battle-slain warriors, hanging, the spear, ecstatic inspiration, poetry, runes, magic, disguise, prophecy, necromancy, and dangerous knowledge. He bestows victory but may revoke it; gathers dead warriors for Ragnarök; crosses social, sexual, and moral boundaries to acquire power; and habitually appears as an old, one-eyed, cloaked wanderer. These characteristics come from different periods and genres and should not be treated as a timeless creed. The Vindelev inscription connects Wōdan to elite identity; skaldic verse connects Odin to rulers, battle, poetry, and the slain; Eddic poems dramatize his knowledge-seeking and deceptions; Snorri consolidates these strands into the sovereign “Allfather.”

“Chief god” is a literary and regional description, not proof of a uniform Germanic hierarchy. Adam of Bremen, describing eleventh-century Uppsala, places Thor centrally and calls him most powerful, with Wodan and Fricco beside him. Odin’s place-name distribution is markedly stronger in Denmark and eastern Sweden than in Iceland or western Norway. Scholarship therefore often distinguishes his prominence among warrior aristocracies and poets from Thor’s wider protective or agrarian appeal.

### The divine name

- Old Norse: **Óðinn**, conventionally Odin.
- Proto-Norse inscription: genitive **Wōd[a]nas**, Vindelev X13.
- Reconstructed Proto-Germanic: ***Wōðanaz** or ***Wōðinaz**.
- Old English: **Wōden**; reflected in *Wōdnesdæg*, Wednesday.
- Old Saxon: **Wōdan/Uuoden**.
- Old High German: **Wuotan**, **Wodan**, manuscript **Uuodan**.
- Lombardic: **Godan/Guodan**, reported by Paul the Deacon.
- Middle Dutch: **Wōden/Wuodan**.
- Modern German reception: **Wotan**, especially through Wagner.
- Younger Futhark spellings include forms equivalent to **Óðinn**, but many runic occurrences are uncertain because cognate personal names and adjectives can resemble the theonym.

The standard etymology relates the name to Proto-Germanic ***wōðaz*: ecstatic agitation, fury, possession, inspiration, mind, or poetry. Cognates include Old Norse *óðr*, “mind, inspiration, poetry; fury,” Old English *wōd*, “mad,” and Old High German *wuot*, “violent excitement.” Adam of Bremen’s *Wodan, id est furor*—“Wodan, that is fury”—shows an eleventh-century interpretation along the same lines. The ultimate Indo-European relationship to Celtic words for seer or poet, such as Old Irish *fáith* and Gaulish *uātis*, is plausible but reconstructed, not an ancient equation. The competing ***Wōðinaz* analysis argues that some West Germanic forms may have been reshaped through association with “fury”; there is no scholarly consensus sufficient to treat the suffix or exact original meaning as settled.

### Major epithets and aliases

The densest ancient-looking lists occur in *Grímnismál* 46–54; Snorri reproduces and expands them in *Gylfaginning* 20 and the *þulur*. Their multiplicity reflects disguise, poetic variation, cult titles, and learned cataloguing.

- **Alfǫðr**, “Allfather”: *Grímnismál* 48; later central in *Gylfaginning*. [MEDIEVAL/SYSTEMATIZATION] It need not mean biological father of every god.
- **Aldafǫðr**, “father of humankind/the age”: *Vafþrúðnismál* 4, 53.
- **Valfǫðr**, “father of the slain”: *Völuspá* and Snorri; expresses his claim over battle-dead.
- **Herjafǫðr / Herföðr**, “father/lord of hosts”: Eddic and skaldic usage.
- **Hár**, “High”; **Jafnhár**, “Equally High”; **Þriði**, “Third”: Odin-names in *Grímnismál* and the three enthroned speakers of *Gylfaginning*. Snorri’s dramatic triad is literary, not evidence of a historical triune cult.
- **Hávi**, “High One”: speaker-title behind *Hávamál*, “Sayings of the High One.”
- **Yggr**, “Terrible One”: Eddic and skaldic; gives the conventional interpretation of *Yggdrasill* as “Yggr’s horse,” hence Odin’s gallows.
- **Grímnir**, “Masked/Hooded One”: Odin’s disguise in *Grímnismál*.
- **Gangleri**, “Wanderer/Way-weary”: *Grímnismál*; also Gylfi’s assumed name in Snorri.
- **Bǫlverkr**, “Evil-worker/Worker of misfortune”: Odin’s name while stealing the mead of poetry, *Hávamál* 109 and *Skáldskaparmál*.
- **Hangi**, **Hangaguð**, **Hangatýr**, “Hanged One/God of the hanged”: skaldic and prose evidence connecting him with hanging.
- **Geirtýr**, **Geirvaldr**, “Spear-god/Spear-ruler”; **Darraðr**, “Spearman”: battle titles.
- **Sigföðr**, **Sigtýr**, “Father/God of victory.”
- **Óski**, “Wish-giver/Wished-for.”
- **Sviðurr/Sviðrir**, “Wise One” or “Spear-god,” etymology disputed.
- **Fjölnir**, “Much-wise/Many-shaped,” and **Fimbultýr**, “Mighty God.”
- **Báleygr**, “Flame-eye,” and **Bileygr**, possibly “Shifty-eyed/Weak-eyed”: potentially allusive to his altered sight, but the translations are disputed.
- **Viðrir**, associated with weather or opponent; common in kennings.
- **Jólnir**, a name linked to *jól*, Yule, in skaldic and Eddic material. It supports an Odin–Yule association but does not establish the modern claim that Santa Claus derives from Odin.

Snorri says Odin’s many names arose because different peoples changed his name in their languages and because he assumed names on journeys. That explanation is a thirteenth-century learned theory of onomastic variation.

### Historical identifications

- **Mercury:** Tacitus, *Germania* 9 (c. 98), calls the principal Germanic god *Mercurius*. Translation of *dies Mercurii* as Woden’s day in Germanic languages confirms a later functional equation. [SYNCRETISM] It is not evidence that Wodan was originally a Roman-style commerce god.
- **Hermes:** A Greek equivalent implicit in the Mercury equation; learned, not independently cultic.
- **Godan and Frea:** Paul the Deacon’s *Historia Langobardorum* I.8 (late eighth century) gives the Lombard origin legend in which Godan is tricked by his wife Frea into granting victory and the name “Longobards.” [REGIONAL/MEDIEVAL]
- **A Trojan/Asian king:** Snorri’s *Prose Edda* Prologue and *Ynglinga saga* make Odin an extraordinarily skilled migrant ruler from “Asia” or Troy whose descendants were worshipped as gods. [SYSTEMATIZATION] This is Christian euhemerism, not pagan memory of a migration from Troy.
- **The Devil or demonic hunter:** Conversion-period and later clerical/folk interpretation. [FOLKLORE]
- **Wagner’s Wotan:** a nineteenth-century dramatic recomposition rather than the Old Norse god unchanged. [MODERN]

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

**Vindelev X13, early fifth century CE.** Excavated in 2020 near Jelling in southeastern Jutland and now in the National Museum of Denmark, this gold C-bracteate bears a long Elder Futhark inscription. Lisbeth Imer and Krister Vasshus read its crucial clause as *iz Wōd[a]nas weraz*, “he is Wodan’s man,” probably referring to the pictured ruler. A die-linked bracteate from Bolbro preserves a less legible parallel. The find proves the name’s Scandinavian presence approximately 150 years before Nordendorf and may show sacral legitimation of an elite man. Whether the central image is Odin, the human “Wodan’s man,” or an ideologically merged ruler remains disputed.

**Tacitus, c. 98 CE.** *Germania* 9 says Germanic peoples chiefly worship Mercury and consider human sacrifice to him lawful on appointed days. [EARLY/INDIRECT] Tacitus supplies no native name, myth, image, or location; “Wodan” is an inference based on later equations.

**Nordendorf I fibula, c. 560–585.** A gilt-silver bow fibula from an Alamannic grave near Augsburg reads, in part, *logaþore wodan wigiþonar*. Wodan and Donar are recognizable; *logaþore* and the sentence’s force remain disputed. Interpretations range from an invocation to a Christian renunciation—“deceivers/sorcerers are Wodan and Wigi-Þonar.” It cannot safely be quoted as a straightforward pagan prayer.

**Ribe skull fragment, c. 725–750.** A runic healing or protective text may invoke Odin among other names, but readings are contested.

**Second Merseburg Charm.** Copied in Old High German in the first half of the tenth century on a blank leaf of Merseburg Cathedral Library Codex 136, probably from an older oral formula. Phol and Wodan ride into the wood; Balder’s horse sprains its foot; Sinthgunt and Sunna, Friia and Volla, then Wodan charm it, “as he well knew how,” bone to bone, blood to blood, limb to limb. This securely presents continental Wodan as a healer and master of incantation, but the identities of Phol and “Balder” are disputed.

### Core textual corpus

**Old English evidence, seventh–eleventh centuries.** Royal genealogies in the *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle*, Bedean genealogical traditions, and other lists make Woden an ancestor of royal dynasties. The Old English *Nine Herbs Charm*, copied c. 1000, says Woden took nine “glory-twigs” and struck a serpent into nine pieces. These are Christian-manuscript witnesses to remembered Woden, magical tradition, and political ancestry; they do not preserve the Icelandic mythology wholesale.

**Early skaldic poetry, ninth–eleventh centuries.** Poetry attributed to Þjóðólfr of Hvinir, Bragi Boddason, Eyvindr skáldaspillir, Egill Skallagrímsson, Hallfreðr and others supplies pre-Snorrian Odin kennings and allusions. *Sonatorrek* calls him a former friend who gave poetic skill but took the poet’s son; *Hákonarmál* depicts Odin sending valkyries to bring King Hákon to Valhalla; *Eiríksmál* has Odin prepare Valhalla for Eiríkr Bloodaxe. Individual poems survive only in later manuscripts, so dating depends on linguistic, metrical, and attributional criticism. The [Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages](https://skaldic.org/db.php?if=default&table=files&view=) database supplies critical editions and manuscript apparatus.

**Poetic Edda.** Most mythological poems survive in Codex Regius GKS 2365 4to, copied c. 1270; an electronic critical presentation is maintained by the Árni Magnússon Institute. Composition dates are debated and poems may be composite.

- *Völuspá*: Odin questions a seeress; creation, Baldr’s death, Ragnarök, Odin’s death, and renewal.
- *Hávamál*: wisdom sayings plus Odin’s erotic deceptions, mead theft, runic self-hanging, and charms.
- *Vafþrúðnismál*: Odin defeats the giant Vafþrúðnir in a knowledge contest.
- *Grímnismál*: Odin, tortured between fires, reveals cosmological lore and his names.
- *Baldrs draumar*: Odin rides to Hel and compels a dead seeress to explain Baldr’s dreams.
- *Hárbarðsljóð*: a ferryman generally identified as Odin trades boasts and insults with Thor.
- *Lokasenna*: Loki accuses Odin of unjustly dispensing victory and practicing *seiðr*.
- *Reginsmál* and *Fáfnismál*: Odin intervenes in the Völsung cycle and gives counsel.
- *Hyndluljóð*: genealogical and cosmological allusions.
- Heroic lays repeatedly connect Odin to the Völsungs, battle, runes, and doomed favorites.

**Snorri Sturluson’s *Edda*, c. 1220.** *Gylfaginning* constructs a systematic mythology from poems and other learning; *Skáldskaparmál* explains kennings through narratives, including the mead of poetry; the Prologue euhemerizes the gods as Trojans. The work is indispensable but late, Christian, literary, and pedagogical. Anthony Faulkes’s edition and translation remain standard; an accessible copy is available [here](https://mimameidir.wordpress.com/wp-content/uploads/2023/08/the-prose-edda-snorri-sturluson-anthony-faulkes.pdf).

**Snorri’s *Heimskringla*, c. 1230.** *Ynglinga saga* treats Odin as an Asian conqueror, magician, lawgiver, temple founder, and ancestor who settles in Sweden and is marked with a spear before death. This is euhemeristic historiography using mythic and genealogical materials, not an independent pagan biography.

**Saxo Grammaticus, *Gesta Danorum*, c. 1185–1220.** Latin Danish history recasts Odin as a fraudulent or exiled god and gives sharply variant Balder, Rind, and royal legends. Saxo’s hostility and classicizing method preserve variants precisely because they do not agree with Snorri.

**Völsunga saga, c. 1250–1270.** Odin plants the sword later called Gram in the tree Barnstokkr, aids and ultimately destroys favored Völsung heroes, and appears as a one-eyed stranger. The saga is late but draws on Eddic heroic poetry.

**Nibelungenlied, c. 1200.** It contains no acting Odin. Its German heroic world is related to the Völsung tradition, but inserting Odin into its plot is a modern harmonization.

**Adam of Bremen, *Gesta Hammaburgensis* IV.26–27, c. 1075.** Wodan “governs wars and gives courage against enemies” at Uppsala; his image stands beside Thor and Fricco. Adam reports a nine-year sacrificial festival and bodies hanging in a sacred grove. His account mixes informant material, missionary politics, and classical/Christian literary conventions; its reliability is debated.

**Later sagas and þættir.** *Gautreks saga* gives King Víkarr’s hanging-and-spearing sacrifice to Odin; *Hervarar saga*, *Örvar-Odds saga*, *Bósa saga*, and others contain Odin-like wanderers, genealogies, and interventions. Their manuscripts and extant forms are late medieval, so they are evidence for medieval reception and possible inherited motifs, not automatically Viking-age cult.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and powers

**Sovereignty and kingship.** Royal genealogies descend from Woden; skaldic poems portray Odin receiving rulers; the Vindelev formula may sacralize an elite “Odin’s man.” Dumézil classified him as the ecstatic or magical pole of Indo-European sovereignty beside Týr’s juridical pole. That scheme is influential but reconstructive and cannot substitute for source-by-source history.

**War and victory.** Odin decides battle outcomes, lends or breaks weapons, incites conflict, and chooses slain warriors. *Lokasenna* 22 accuses him of giving victory to the less deserving. His favor is therefore powerful and treacherous, not consistently protective.

**The dead.** Half the battle-slain go to Odin in Valhalla and half to Freyja in *Grímnismál* 14. Snorri emphasizes Odin’s einherjar as a force training for Ragnarök. Odin also wakes dead seeresses and preserves Mímir’s head in *Ynglinga saga*.

**Knowledge, poetry, and magic.** He sacrifices bodily integrity and safety for wisdom, steals the poetic mead, learns runes and spells, questions giants and the dead, and practices *seiðr*. *Ynglinga saga* says Freyja taught *seiðr* to the Æsir and that its practice carried *ergi*, a charge of unmanliness. This gender transgression is explicit in *Lokasenna* and Snorri, not a modern invention.

### Form and equipment

The literary Odin is generally an older man, bearded, cloaked or hooded, often one-eyed, carrying a spear. No pre-Christian image bears an unambiguous caption “this is Odin,” except that the Vindelev text and image now provide unusually close association without settling whether the figure is divine or human.

- **Eye:** *Völuspá* 28 knows where Odin’s eye is hidden in Mímir’s well; *Gylfaginning* 15 explains that he pledged it for a drink of wisdom. The familiar full transaction is therefore chiefly Snorrian elaboration of an Eddic allusion.
- **Gungnir:** Odin’s spear, made by dwarfs in *Skáldskaparmál*. He casts a spear over the Vanir host at the first war in *Völuspá* 24 and confronts Fenrir with it at Ragnarök.
- **Draupnir:** dwarf-made ring placed on Baldr’s pyre; it produces eight equal rings every ninth night.
- **Sleipnir:** grey, eight-legged “best of horses,” born when Loki, as a mare, mates with the stallion Svaðilfari; Odin rides him between worlds. The birth narrative is in Snorri; poetic kennings attest the horse independently.
- **Hlíðskjálf:** high seat from which Odin and Frigg view the worlds; especially Snorrian.
- **Geri and Freki:** “Ravenous/Greedy,” wolves fed Odin’s meat while he lives on wine, *Grímnismál* 19 and *Gylfaginning* 38.
- **Huginn and Muninn:** “Thought” and “Memory/Mind,” ravens who fly over the world daily, *Grímnismál* 20 and *Gylfaginning* 38. No source makes them supernatural detachable pieces of Odin’s psyche; that is interpretation.
- **Valhalla:** Odin’s many-doored hall at Glaðsheimr. *Grímnismál* describes its shields, spears, wolf, eagle, goat, stag, and 540 doors; Snorri turns these details into a coherent institution for the einherjar.
- **Valkyries:** Odin sends or directs female choosers of the slain in Eddic, skaldic, and prose sources, though individual valkyries can resist him.

### Images

Migration-period C-bracteates often show a large head above a quadruped with birds and sometimes a spear. Karl Hauck interpreted many as Wodan healing Balder’s horse, relating them to the Merseburg Charm. This iconological program is influential but contested; alternative studies warn against decoding nearly every rider-and-horse design through much later texts.

Vendel- and Viking-period helmet plates and dies from Torslunda, Vendel, Valsgärde, and Sutton Hoo show weapon dancers, bird-horned figures, riders, and warriors accompanied by birds. Some may depict Odin or Odinic ritual specialists, but none is securely labeled.

Gotland picture stones—Tjängvide I, Ardre VIII, and Stora Hammars stones—show riders on eight-legged horses, cup-bearing women, ships, battle, or sacrifice. Tjängvide’s rider is commonly identified as Odin entering Valhalla, but other readings remain possible.

The three interlocking triangles called the **valknut** occur on objects including Stora Hammars I, the Tängelgårda stone, and an Oseberg bedpost. No ancient text names or explains the sign; “valknut” is a modern scholarly name, and its specific status as “Odin’s symbol” is inferential. Claims that it had one fixed ancient meaning—nine worlds, Odin’s heart, or a magical binding rune—are modern inventions.

No ancient source assigns Odin an exclusive color, modern “Odin rune,” crystal, planetary correspondence, or standardized altar direction. Wednesday derives from the Mercury/Woden equation; treating it as a universally observed ancient holy day goes beyond the evidence.

## 4. Genealogy and divine relations

### Genealogical versions

In *Völuspá* and Snorri, Odin, Vili, and Vé are sons of Borr and Bestla; Borr is son of Búri, licked from the ice by Auðhumla. The brothers kill Ymir and make the world from his body. *Völuspá* 17–18 instead names Odin, Hœnir, and Lóðurr as the gods who animate Ask and Embla; Snorri substitutes Odin, Vili, and Vé and assigns them breath, intelligence, and senses. These are conflicting triads, not securely interchangeable identities.

Frigg is Odin’s principal wife. Jörð is Thor’s mother; Rindr bears Váli; Gríðr bears Víðarr in Snorri; Baldr is Odin’s son and normally Frigg’s son in Snorri. Thor, Baldr, Víðarr, and Váli are the most securely and repeatedly attested divine sons. Meili is called Odin’s son in *Þórsdrápa*. Bragi, Heimdallr, Týr, and Höðr appear as sons in isolated Snorrian kennings or lists, but other evidence conflicts: *Hymiskviða* makes the giant Hymir Týr’s father, while Heimdall’s nine mothers are prominent and his father is not consistently named. Hermóðr is “Odin’s boy/son” depending on manuscript wording. Skjöldr, Sigi, Sæmingr, and Yngvi belong chiefly to royal genealogical euhemerism.

### Discrete documented interactions with named divine beings

The following covers the specific interactions preserved in the principal Germanic, Eddic, skaldic, Snorrian, Saxonian, and major saga corpora. Mere co-membership in a list is excluded.

- **Borr and Bestla:** Odin is their son with Vili and Vé; *Gylfaginning* 6, c. 1220. [SYSTEMATIZATION]
- **Vili and Vé:** With Odin, kill Ymir and form the world; *Vafþrúðnismál* 21 attributes creation to “Borr’s sons,” expanded in *Gylfaginning* 7–9. [MEDIEVAL/SYSTEMATIZATION]
- **Hœnir and Lóðurr:** With Odin, find Ask and Embla and give them life; *Völuspá* 17–18, manuscript c. 1270. [MEDIEVAL, probably older poetic material]
- **Ymir:** Odin and Borr’s sons kill him and create the cosmos from his body; *Vafþrúðnismál* and *Gylfaginning*. [MEDIEVAL]
- **Frigg:** Wife and counselor; she warns him against visiting Vafþrúðnir, wagers with him over their foster sons, and shares Hlíðskjálf; *Vafþrúðnismál*, *Grímnismál* prose frame, *Grímnismál* 14, *Gylfaginning*. [MEDIEVAL]
- **Frea/Frigg:** Tricks Godan into naming and granting victory to the Lombards; Paul the Deacon, *Historia Langobardorum* I.8, late eighth century. [REGIONAL]
- **Thor:** Odin’s son in *Hárbarðsljóð* 9 and Snorri; as Hárbarðr, Odin refuses Thor passage and mocks his giant-killing; *Hárbarðsljóð*. [MEDIEVAL]
- **Jörð:** Named as Thor’s mother and Odin’s partner in Snorrian/skaldic genealogical formulas; *Skáldskaparmál*. No surviving courtship narrative. [SYSTEMATIZATION]
- **Baldr:** Odin rides to Hel to investigate his dreams, places Draupnir on his pyre, and fathers Váli to avenge him; *Baldrs draumar*, *Völuspá*, *Gylfaginning*. [MEDIEVAL]
- **Höðr:** In Snorri, Odin’s blind son is manipulated by Loki into killing Baldr; Odin fathers Váli for vengeance. In Saxo, Høtherus is a human hero opposed by Othinus and Balderus; *Gesta Danorum* III. [REGIONAL VARIANT]
- **Váli/Ali:** Odin fathers him on Rindr; he kills Höðr while one night old; *Baldrs draumar* 11, *Völuspá* 32–33, *Gylfaginning*. [MEDIEVAL]
- **Rindr/Rinda:** Odin fathers Baldr’s avenger. Saxo gives a long coercive pursuit in which Othinus uses disguise, magic, and incapacitation before impregnating Rinda; *Gesta Danorum* III. Snorri suppresses the narrative. [REGIONAL VARIANT]
- **Víðarr:** Odin’s silent son avenges him by killing Fenrir; *Vafþrúðnismál* 53, *Völuspá* 55, *Gylfaginning* 51. [MEDIEVAL]
- **Gríðr:** Mother of Víðarr by Odin in *Skáldskaparmál*. No surviving interaction narrative. [SYSTEMATIZATION]
- **Loki:** Odin says they mixed blood and vowed to drink together, *Lokasenna* 9; Loki engineers Baldr’s death, indirectly destroying Odin’s son; Snorri. Their modern portrayal as adoptive father and son is invented by Marvel.
- **Freyja:** Teaches *seiðr* to the Æsir/Odin’s circle in *Ynglinga saga* 4; shares the battle-dead with Odin in *Grímnismál* 14. [MEDIEVAL/EUHEMERISTIC]
- **Njörðr, Freyr, and Vanir:** Odin leads the Æsir in the Æsir–Vanir war; hostages are exchanged; *Völuspá* 21–24 and *Ynglinga saga* 4. Snorri’s prose rationalizes a fragmentary poetic conflict. [MEDIEVAL]
- **Mímir:** Odin pledges an eye in Mímir’s well; *Völuspá* 28, expanded in *Gylfaginning* 15. In *Ynglinga saga* 4, after Vanir decapitate Mímir, Odin preserves and consults the head. [MEDIEVAL/SYSTEMATIZATION]
- **Kvasir:** Odin does not meet him alive in the preserved story but steals the mead made from his blood; *Skáldskaparmál*. [MEDIEVAL]
- **Bragi:** Snorri’s lists sometimes call Bragi Odin’s son; *Eiríksmál* has Odin ask Bragi to greet the arriving king. [MEDIEVAL]
- **Týr:** Listed as Odin’s son in one Snorrian formula, but *Hymiskviða* supplies Hymir as father; no major direct mythic encounter survives. [CONFLICTING GENEALOGY]
- **Heimdallr:** A Snorrian kenning calls him Odin’s son; otherwise the texts give no father-son episode. [ISOLATED SYSTEMATIZATION]
- **Hermóðr:** Odin sends him on Sleipnir to Hel to ransom Baldr; *Gylfaginning* 49. [SYSTEMATIZATION]
- **Fulla:** Serves Frigg and carries her messages; in the Second Merseburg Charm, Volla and Friia participate before Wodan heals the horse. [REGIONAL]
- **Sunna and Sinthgunt:** Attempt to charm Balder’s horse before Wodan succeeds; Second Merseburg Charm, tenth-century manuscript. [REGIONAL]
- **Donar/Þonar:** Named beside Wodan on the Nordendorf fibula; the grammatical relationship is disputed and no episode is described. [EARLY]
- **Gunnlöð:** Gives Odin three drinks of mead after three nights together; he abandons her, *Hávamál* 105–110; Snorri makes the exchange part of an elaborate theft. She is a jötunn, not normally classed as a goddess. [MEDIEVAL]
- **Suttungr and Baugi:** Odin deceives Baugi, penetrates Suttungr’s mountain, steals the mead, and flees as an eagle while Suttungr pursues; *Hávamál* and *Skáldskaparmál*. [MEDIEVAL]
- **Vafþrúðnir:** Odin defeats and kills or dooms the giant in a wisdom contest by asking what Odin whispered to Baldr; *Vafþrúðnismál*. [MEDIEVAL]
- **Fenrir:** Odin has knowledge of the wolf’s danger; Fenrir swallows him at Ragnarök; Víðarr avenges him; *Völuspá*, *Vafþrúðnismál*, *Gylfaginning*. [MEDIEVAL]
- **Hel:** Hermóðr negotiates with her at Odin’s behest; she sets the condition that all beings weep for Baldr; *Gylfaginning*. [SYSTEMATIZATION]
- **The unnamed völva in *Völuspá*:** Odin gives her rings and necklaces for prophecy, or rewards her narration; the exact dramatic situation is debated. [MEDIEVAL]
- **The dead seeress in *Baldrs draumar*:** Odin compels her from the grave with spells and questions her until she recognizes him. [MEDIEVAL]
- **Gefjon:** In *Ynglinga saga*, Odin sends her north to seek land; she ploughs Zealand from Sweden. [EUHEMERISTIC]
- **Sága:** Drinks daily with Odin at Sökkvabekkr; *Grímnismál* 7. No further interaction survives. [MEDIEVAL]
- **Vili:** *Lokasenna* 26 alleges that Frigg slept with Odin’s brothers Vili and Vé while Odin was absent. This is an accusation within a flyting, not independently narrated fact. [MEDIEVAL]
- **Ullr:** Saxo says Ollerus temporarily replaces the exiled Othinus using a bone marked with spells; Othinus returns and drives him out, *Gesta Danorum* III. [REGIONAL VARIANT]
- **Mithothyn:** Saxo’s false god seizes power while Othinus is away and changes sacrifice; Othinus returns and Mithothyn flees, *Gesta Danorum* I. [REGIONAL VARIANT]
- **The valkyries Hrist, Mist, Göndul, Skögul and others:** Odin sends them to choose or serve the slain; *Grímnismál*, *Hákonarmál*, *Eiríksmál*. [SKALDIC/MEDIEVAL]
- **Sleipnir:** Odin rides Loki’s eight-legged offspring to Jötunheimr, Hel, and Ragnarök; Eddic and Snorrian sources. No text says Odin raised him.
- **Huginn, Muninn, Geri, Freki:** Odin dispatches the ravens and feeds the wolves; *Grímnismál* 19–20 and *Gylfaginning* 38.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Creation and humanity

[Völuspá, medieval witness] Odin, Hœnir, and Lóðurr find Ask and Embla lifeless and give them breath, mind/spirit, blood or warmth, and good appearance. [Snorrian systematization] *Gylfaginning* replaces the triad with Odin, Vili, and Vé and assigns soul, understanding, movement, appearance, speech, hearing, and sight. Snorri also explicitly makes Borr’s sons kill Ymir and fashion earth, sea, sky, rocks, clouds, and Midgard from his body. Neither source warrants identifying Lóðurr as Loki, though that remains a scholarly proposal.

### The eye and Mímir’s well

*Völuspá* says Odin’s eye lies in Mímir’s famous well and Mímir drinks mead each morning from Valföðr’s pledge. It does not narrate how the eye was surrendered. *Gylfaginning* 15 supplies the familiar causal story: Odin asked for a drink of the wisdom-giving well and pledged his eye. Thus “Odin tore out his eye” is stronger than the source, which says he left or pledged it; the physical method is unstated.

### Hanging and the runes

In *Hávamál* 138–141, Odin says:

> I know that I hung on a windy tree  
> nine long nights,  
> wounded with a spear, given to Odin,  
> myself to myself…

He receives neither bread nor drink, peers downward, takes up the runes screaming, and falls back. The poem never names the tree Yggdrasil in this passage, although the identification is ancient-looking and reinforced by *Yggdrasill*, conventionally “Yggr’s steed,” as a gallows kenning. Nor does the poem say Odin invented the runic characters: he “takes up” or apprehends them. The episode is securely medievally attested but its date and relationship to Christian crucifixion imagery are debated. Similarity to Christ does not prove Christian invention; hanging and spear sacrifice are independently Odinic, but Christian scribal and poetic contexts make influence possible.

Stanzas 142–145 describe carving, interpretation, coloring, testing, invocation, sacrifice, and exchange; 146–163 enumerate eighteen charms. Modern rune manuals that attach fixed divinatory meanings to each rune are largely nineteenth- and twentieth-century systems, not instructions from *Hávamál*.

### Mead of poetry

*Hávamál* 104–110 gives an elliptical first-person version: Odin reaches Suttungr’s halls, obtains drink from Gunnlöð after intimacy, uses the auger Rati, breaks a ring-oath, and escapes, leaving Gunnlöð weeping. *Skáldskaparmál* supplies a continuous prehistory. The Æsir and Vanir spit into a vessel after making peace; from it they make Kvasir. Dwarfs Fjalar and Galar kill Kvasir, mix his blood with honey, and create the mead. Suttungr acquires it in compensation and has Gunnlöð guard it inside Hnitbjörg. Calling himself Bǫlverkr, Odin causes Baugi’s nine workers to kill one another, works in their place, has Baugi drill into the mountain, enters as a snake, sleeps three nights with Gunnlöð, drains three vessels in three drinks, and escapes as an eagle. He regurgitates the good mead for gods and poets; the portion discharged behind becomes the bad poet’s share.

Snorri’s smooth tale is later synthesis. *Hávamál* is morally harsher and less explanatory: Odin acknowledges bad repayment, sexual exploitation, and oath-breaking.

### Grímnir and Geirröðr

The prose frame to *Grímnismál* says Odin and Frigg foster rival princes. Frigg tricks Odin into believing King Geirröðr inhospitable; Odin visits disguised as Grímnir. Geirröðr tortures him between fires for eight nights. The king’s son Agnarr gives him drink; Odin recites cosmological knowledge, reveals himself, and Geirröðr falls on his sword while trying to release him. The poem joins divine competition, royal hospitality, initiation-like ordeal, and encyclopedic lore. Whether the prose frame is original to the verse is uncertain.

### Vafþrúðnir’s wisdom contest

Against Frigg’s warning, Odin visits the giant disguised as Gagnráðr. They exchange questions about horses, rivers, cosmology, creation, Ragnarök, and renewal. Odin wins through a question only he can answer: what he whispered in Baldr’s ear on the pyre. The content of the whisper is never revealed. Claims to know it are modern invention.

### Baldr’s death and revenge

*Völuspá* tersely foretells Baldr’s death through mistletoe and Höðr, followed by Váli’s one-night-old vengeance. *Baldrs draumar* has Odin ride to Hel to question a dead seeress. Snorri creates the canonical full narrative: Baldr dreams danger; Frigg takes oaths from all things except seemingly harmless mistletoe; Loki discovers the omission, makes a missile, and guides blind Höðr’s hand. Hermóðr rides Sleipnir to Hel; Hel will release Baldr if everything weeps. All do except Þökk, probably Loki. Baldr remains dead; Odin places Draupnir on his pyre.

Saxo’s variant is radically different. Balderus and the human Høtherus compete for Nanna; Balderus is physically vulnerable only to a special sword and eventually dies from Høtherus’s wound. Othinus seeks Rinda to beget the avenger Bous. There is no blind innocent Höðr manipulated with mistletoe. Neither version should be collapsed into the other.

### Völsung interventions

In *Völsunga saga*, a one-eyed stranger thrusts a sword into Barnstokkr at King Völsung’s hall; only Sigmund can draw it. Odin later shatters that sword with his spear in battle, withdrawing favor and causing Sigmund’s death. In *Reginsmál*, Odin, Hœnir, and Loki become entangled in the killing of Ótr and the cursed gold. Odin’s relationship with the Völsungs exemplifies a recurring pattern: election, supernatural gift, victory, then abandonment or destruction.

### Valhalla and Ragnarök

*Grímnismál* describes Valhalla, where Odin selects half the slain; *Hákonarmál* and *Eiríksmál* dramatize royal arrivals. Snorri systematizes the einherjar’s daily combat and feasting as preparation for Ragnarök. In *Völuspá* and *Vafþrúðnismál*, Odin consults Mímir before the last battle and is swallowed by Fenrir; Víðarr kills the wolf. Odin is neither immortal nor omnipotent. He knows much of the catastrophe yet cannot avert it.

### The Wild Hunt

Medieval Europe contains many tales of nocturnal processions, armies of the dead, condemned hunters, and spectral riders led by figures such as Herod, Arthur, Hellequin, Diana, Holda, or local nobles. Explicit association with Wodan/Odin is much later and regionally variable. A Rostock text by Nicolaus Gryse in 1593 records peasant invocation of Wodan at harvest and connects him to a spectral hunter. Jacob Grimm’s *Deutsche Mythologie* (1835) assembled disparate traditions into *das wilde Heer/die wilde Jagd* and argued that Wodan had once led a pan-Germanic pagan host.

Ronald Hutton’s historical review concludes that “the Wild Hunt” as a unified scholarly category was substantially Grimm’s construction. Therefore:

- spectral processions are genuinely medieval and early-modern;
- Wodan leads some later German/Scandinavian variants;
- an unbroken pan-Germanic Odin-led Wild Hunt from antiquity is unproved;
- Odin riding Sleipnir through the winter sky and becoming Santa Claus is a modern comparative synthesis, not an ancient narrative.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Cult centers and place-names

No securely excavated building can be labeled an “Odin temple” solely by its contents. The Old Norse word *hof* does not consistently denote one architectural type. Place-names provide broader but indirect evidence:

- **Odense**, from Old Danish *Óðins-vé*, “Odin’s sanctuary,” is strong Danish evidence.
- **Onsala**, “Odin’s hall,” and names in *Óðinsakr*, *Óðinsvé*, or *Óðinslundr* preserve cultic or proprietary compounds.
- Danish *-vé* compounds are especially important because *vé* means a sacred place.
- Western Norway and Iceland have comparatively few secure Odin place-names; this regional imbalance argues against a uniform cult.

Gamla Uppsala was a major elite and ritual center, but Adam’s gold-covered temple with three idols has not been archaeologically verified in that form. Large halls, mounds, feasting, and long-term ritual activity are secure; assigning every feature to Odin is not.

### Sacrifice

Tacitus reports human sacrifice to “Mercury.” Adam of Bremen reports that at Uppsala nine males of every living species, including humans, were sacrificed at nine-year intervals and hung in a sacred grove. His account is not eyewitness testimony: a scholion attributes information to a Christian observer, and the description employs polemical conventions. Anders Hultgård concludes that genuine Scandinavian sacrificial elements probably underlie the account while its exact scale and presentation remain debatable.

The literary pattern of an “Odin sacrifice” combines hanging and spearing:

- *Hávamál*: Odin hangs wounded by a spear, “himself to himself.”
- *Gautreks saga*: Starkaðr arranges King Víkarr’s apparent mock sacrifice, but the reed becomes a spear and the calf-gut a rope, killing him for Odin.
- *Ynglinga saga*: Odin marks himself with a spear at death; Njörðr is likewise marked “for Odin.”
- Saga battle formulas dedicate an enemy host by casting a spear over it, reflecting *Völuspá*’s first war.

Bog bodies and hanged or wounded human remains cannot normally be assigned to Odin without inscriptions. Archaeology supports human sacrifice in some Iron Age northern European contexts, but “all hanged bog bodies were Odin sacrifices” is unsustainable.

### Prayer, magic, and divination

The Second Merseburg Charm and *Nine Herbs Charm* show Wodan in healing or anti-venom magic. *Hávamál* gives Odin eighteen unnamed or partly described charms—for help, healing, fetter-breaking, calming storms, turning weapons, speaking with the dead, and winning love—but it does not provide a modern ritual manual. *Ynglinga saga* credits him with shape-changing, prophecy, necromancy, rune magic, and binding opponents’ minds.

Warriors and poets invoked Odin through kennings and dedication, but no surviving pre-Christian liturgy lays out an Odin priesthood. The saga term *goði* denotes a chieftain-priest in Iceland; evidence does not establish a centralized Odinic clergy. The poetic and royal record suggests patronage by rulers, war-bands, and skalds rather than exclusive popular worship.

### Political economy

Odin ideology authenticated dynasties: Anglo-Saxon kings traced descent from Woden, Scandinavian houses from Odin’s sons, and skaldic praise represented dead kings as his chosen guests. Feasting halls, gold bracteates, weapon deposits, and monumental centers tied divine favor to elite gift exchange and military power. The interpretation of Odin as primarily an aristocratic warrior god is well supported but should not erase healing, poetry, magic, and household-level traditions.

## 7. Historical evolution

### First–fourth centuries: indirect Roman evidence

Tacitus’s Mercury and weekday translation show that by the Roman period a Germanic deity could be equated with Mercury. No Roman inscription securely gives Wodan’s name. Claims that rock art or Bronze Age spear figures depict Odin are retrospective and unprovable.

### Fifth–sixth centuries: Migration-period elite deity

Vindelev directly attests Wōdan in fifth-century Jutland and connects him to an elite male. Bracteates disseminated rider, bird, horse, and healing-like images across Scandinavia and continental Germanic regions. Nordendorf directly attests Wodan in sixth-century Alamannia. The period supports a broad Germanic god connected with elite authority and perhaps healing or ecstatic power, but not the complete Eddic biography.

### Seventh–eighth centuries: dynasties, charms, and conversion

Anglo-Saxon royal genealogies made Woden an ancestor while Christian authors recast him as a mortal forebear. Paul the Deacon preserved the Lombard Godan-Frea legend. Ribe and other runic objects may attest magical invocation. Conversion did not instantly erase the name; it reclassified it.

### Ninth–eleventh centuries: Viking-age poetic prominence

Skaldic poets developed dense Odin kennings and portrayed him as giver of poetry, chooser of kings, and lord of the slain. Eddic poems took shape over uncertain spans. Denmark and Norway officially Christianized in the tenth–eleventh centuries; Sweden followed more gradually. Christian kings, bishops, church building, legal prohibitions, and political consolidation displaced public sacrifice. The record is uneven: accommodation, dual practice, polemic, and force all occurred. There is no evidence of a single coordinated “suppression of Odinism,” because no centralized Odinist church existed.

### Eleventh–thirteenth centuries: antiquarian preservation and Christian reframing

Adam portrayed Uppsala as a missionary frontier. Icelandic scholars preserved poetry and myth after conversion. Snorri’s *Edda* protected skaldic literacy while rationalizing the gods as Trojan ancestors and ordering scattered lore into a system. Saxo made the gods fraudulent magicians or former rulers. These writers preserved indispensable material while transforming it.

### Fourteenth–eighteenth centuries: folklore and antiquarianism

Odin survived unevenly as ancestral king, demonized magician, healing name, leader of spectral riders, and subject of Scandinavian antiquarian chronicles. Early-modern scholars attempted to synchronize him with Mercury, historical conquerors, biblical genealogies, and Trojan migrations. Such histories are evidence for reception, not ancient origins.

### Nineteenth century: Romantic national god

Jacob Grimm reconstructed a Germanic Wodan from language and folklore. Scandinavian artists gave Odin a standardized one-eyed, spear-bearing visual form. Wagner’s Wotan became the tragic lawgiver at the center of *Der Ring des Nibelungen*, composed 1848–1874 and first performed as a complete cycle at Bayreuth in 1876. Wagner combined Eddic Odin, German Wodan, *Völsunga saga*, the *Nibelungenlied*, Grimm, his own politics, and Feuerbachian drama. Wotan’s spear contracts, doomed world-order, and relationship to Brünnhilde are Wagnerian architecture, not direct reproduction of one medieval source.

### Twentieth–twenty-first centuries: religion, entertainment, and politics

Religious revivals emerged through Germanic Romanticism, Ariosophy, occult nationalism, Icelandic Ásatrú, American Heathenry, Odinism, Theodism, and reconstructionism. Their rites necessarily reconstruct or create practice because no continuous institutional priesthood or liturgy survived.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Esotericism and occultism

Guido von List’s *Armanism* and “Armanen runes” at the beginning of the twentieth century blended racial mysticism, runes, and Wotanism. His eighteen-rune system was inspired by *Hávamál*’s eighteen charms but is not an ancient rune row. Karl Maria Wiligut supplied SS esoteric mythology; Alexander Rud Mills developed racialized Odinism in Australia in the 1930s. These are modern ideological religions.

Odin was less central to the Golden Dawn’s original ceremonial system than Egyptian, Jewish, and Hermetic figures; later occultists incorporated him through planetary Mercury, runes, seiðr, and archetypal psychology. Fixed correspondences—Odin equals Mercury equals Wednesday equals purple or a particular tarot card—are modern syncretic systems.

### Modern Heathen religion

Iceland’s Ásatrúarfélagið was founded in 1972 and recognized in 1973. Other organizations developed independently in Scandinavia, Britain, North America, and elsewhere. Odin may be honored as a god of wisdom, poetry, death, magic, leadership, or ordeal, but modern Heathenry is decentralized and many practitioners emphasize Thor, Freyja, land spirits, or ancestors instead.

Reconstructionists use medieval texts, archaeology, and comparative religion; others accept personal revelation. Blót, sumbel, offerings, oath-making, and seasonal festivals are modern practices with ancient vocabulary and selected historical analogues, not demonstrably unchanged rites.

### Nationalist and extremist appropriation

“Odinism” has never had one universally racial meaning, but part of its twentieth-century history is inseparable from racial nationalism. Mills’s Odinism was fascist and racial; Else Christensen’s Odinist Fellowship and prison outreach influenced North American racialist currents; Stephen McNallen founded the Viking Brotherhood/Asatru Free Assembly and later the Asatru Folk Assembly. The Anti-Defamation League characterizes the present AFA as white-supremacist while stressing that most Ásatrú adherents are not racist. Blood Tribe and some prison, skinhead, neo-Nazi, and accelerationist formations use Odin/Wotan as symbols of an imagined white warrior religion.

This appropriation is modern. Pre-Christian Germanic societies did not possess modern biological race theory, and the surviving Odin myths do not prescribe a white nation-state. Conversely, it would be inaccurate to claim that extremist Odinism is merely outsiders’ slander: named organizations and writers have explicitly articulated racial exclusion. Inclusive Heathen organizations have issued anti-racist declarations and reject “folkish” restriction. Symbols such as runes, Mjölnir, ravens, and the valknut are not intrinsically extremist; context determines use.

### Literature, stage, film, comics, and games

- **Wagner, 1848–1876:** Wotan, central tragic ruler of the *Ring*.
- **J. R. R. Tolkien:** Gandalf’s wandering, hat, staff, wisdom, runic knowledge, and relationship to kingship were partly shaped by the Odinic wanderer, although Gandalf is not simply Odin.
- **Marvel Comics, from 1962:** Odin becomes Thor’s royal father, often a Zeus-like moral patriarch. The MCU continued this transformation, then *Thor: Ragnarok* (2017) exposed a concealed imperial past. Loki as Odin’s adopted son is Marvel’s invention.
- **Neil Gaiman, *American Gods* (2001):** Mr. Wednesday is an Americanized, deceptive Odin whose scheme depends on sacrifice and war.
- **Games:** *God of War* (2018/2022), *Assassin’s Creed Valhalla* (2020), *Valheim*, and many others amplify Odin as manipulator, quest-giver, tyrant, or hidden wanderer.
- **Commercial imagery:** eye patches, horned helmets, “Viking” tattoos, ravens, and valknuts dominate. No source gives Odin a horned Viking helmet; horned weapon-dancer images belong to specialized Migration-period iconography, not normal Viking helmets.

## 9. Historiography and contested material

### Origin and promotion

Older evolutionary theories proposed that Týr was originally the Indo-European sky-father and Odin later displaced him. The linguistic antiquity of *Tīwaz* and the prominence of Odin in later aristocratic sources make this possible, but there is no dated record of an actual “promotion.” Vindelev now proves Odin’s Scandinavian importance by the fifth century, weakening claims of a very late Viking-age import.

Dumézil’s tripartite model placed Odin and Týr in complementary sovereign functions, Thor in force, and Njörðr/Freyr in fertility. It illuminates structural parallels but can force fluid evidence into an Indo-European template.

Odin has often been called “shamanic” because of trance, shape-changing, animal helpers, self-ordeal, soul travel, *seiðr*, and consultation of the dead. The comparison can describe similarities but is not a native Germanic category, and direct derivation from Sámi or Central Asian shamanism has not been demonstrated.

### Frigg and Freyja

Their names, roles, falcon plumage, sexuality, magic, and connection to Odin/Óðr have prompted theories that Frigg and Freyja derive from one earlier goddess and that Freyja’s absent husband Óðr is a double of Odin. The names Óðr/Óðinn are related and the distributions are suggestive, but the medieval texts consistently distinguish Odin from Óðr and Frigg from Freyja. Common origin remains reconstruction.

### Human sacrifice

The convergence of Tacitus, hanging-and-spearing literature, saga dedications, and archaeology makes categorical denial unreasonable. Equally, Adam’s exact ninefold Uppsala spectacle and the assignment of individual bodies to Odin remain contestable. “Documented practice existed” and “every lurid report is literal” are not equivalent positions.

### Sexual violence and gender

Saxo’s Rinda narrative depicts deception, magical incapacitation, and coercive impregnation. The mead narratives depict Odin obtaining Gunnlöð’s trust or love and abandoning her after acquiring the drink. These elements are in the sources and should not be sanitized into benign romance. *Seiðr* exposes him to *ergi*, a serious gendered insult; later admirers sometimes omit this because it conflicts with a hypermasculine warrior image.

### Wild Hunt

The contested-material pass finds no secure ancient or Viking-age text saying “Odin leads the Wild Hunt.” Wodan-led variants are real early-modern folklore; Grimm’s 1835 synthesis made the identification central. Presenting the Wild Hunt as a fully documented pagan Odin myth is therefore anachronistic.

### Primary evidence and preservation

Most Eddic poetry survives in Christian Icelandic manuscripts copied two or three centuries after conversion. Oral antiquity must be argued from meter, language, intertext, and early skaldic parallels, not assumed. Codex Regius, now GKS 2365 4to in Reykjavík, is the central witness; [the Árni Magnússon Institute’s electronic edition](https://www.arnastofnun.is/en/news/new-electronic-edition-codex-regius-poetic-edda) permits comparison of facsimile, diplomatic, and normalized texts.

The Merseburg Charms survive uniquely in Merseburg Cathedral Library Codex 136. Vindelev objects are held by the National Museum of Denmark. Runic evidence is dispersed across Scandinavian and continental collections and catalogued through national databases and the Scandinavian Runic Text Database. Physical loss, Christian manuscript selection, uncertain oral transmission, and ambiguous images leave major gaps.

## 10. Chronology

- **c. 98 CE:** Tacitus writes that Germanic peoples chiefly worship “Mercury” and sacrifice humans to him. [EARLY, indirect]
- **Early fifth century:** Vindelev X13 is made; *Wōd[a]nas* is the earliest secure named Odin/Wodan attestation. [EARLY]
- **Fifth–sixth centuries:** C-bracteates circulate with horse, bird, rider, and healing-like imagery; Odinic identification varies in certainty.
- **c. 560–585:** Nordendorf I fibula names Wodan and Wigi-Þonar; syntax and religious stance disputed.
- **Sixth–seventh centuries:** Weapon-dancer and bird-associated imagery appears on Torslunda, Vendel, Valsgärde, and related objects.
- **Seventh–eighth centuries:** Woden enters Anglo-Saxon royal genealogies as dynastic ancestor.
- **Late eighth century:** Paul the Deacon records Godan and Frea’s Lombard naming legend.
- **c. 725–750:** Ribe skull-fragment charm, possibly naming Odin, is inscribed.
- **Ninth–tenth centuries:** Major skaldic Odin kennings and royal reception poems emerge; exact dating of individual poems remains debated.
- **Early tenth century:** Second Merseburg Charm is copied, showing Wodan healing Balder’s horse.
- **Tenth century:** Christianization of Denmark and Norway transforms and suppresses public pagan cult through royal and ecclesiastical institutions.
- **c. 960–990:** *Hákonarmál* and *Eiríksmál* depict kings welcomed to Odin’s Valhalla.
- **c. 1000:** Iceland adopts Christianity by legal decision; old poetry continues to circulate.
- **c. 1075:** Adam of Bremen describes Wodan, Uppsala, war, and sacrificial hanging.
- **c. 1185–1220:** Saxo composes *Gesta Danorum*, preserving euhemerized and variant Odin narratives.
- **c. 1200:** *Nibelungenlied* is composed without an acting Odin.
- **c. 1220:** Snorri composes the *Edda*, systematizing Odin’s mythology.
- **c. 1230:** Snorri composes *Heimskringla*; *Ynglinga saga* turns Odin into an Asian conqueror and ancestral king.
- **c. 1250–1270:** *Völsunga saga* takes extant form.
- **c. 1270:** Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda is copied.
- **1593:** Nicolaus Gryse records continuing Wodan traditions associated with harvest and the spectral hunt near Rostock.
- **1643:** Brynjólfur Sveinsson acquires Codex Regius; it enters early-modern scholarship.
- **1835:** Jacob Grimm publishes *Deutsche Mythologie*, consolidating Wodan and the Wild Hunt.
- **1841:** Georg Waitz discovers the Merseburg Charms in Codex 136.
- **1842:** Jacob Grimm publishes the charms.
- **1848–1874:** Wagner writes the text and music of the *Ring*; Wotan becomes a defining modern Odin.
- **1876:** First complete Bayreuth performance of *Der Ring des Nibelungen*.
- **Early twentieth century:** Guido von List creates Armanism and the modern Armanen rune system.
- **1930s:** Alexander Rud Mills formulates racialized Australian Odinism.
- **1962:** Marvel’s modern Odin enters the *Journey into Mystery/Thor* comic universe.
- **1972–1973:** Ásatrúarfélagið is founded and legally recognized in Iceland.
- **1995:** Asatru Folk Assembly is founded in the United States.
- **2001:** Neil Gaiman publishes *American Gods*, with Odin as Mr. Wednesday.
- **2020:** Vindelev hoard is discovered.
- **2023:** The Vindelev Odin reading is announced, moving the earliest secure direct attestation into the fifth century.
- **2026:** Odin remains a deity of active, diverse Heathen worship; a major popular-culture figure; and a contested symbol appropriated both by inclusive religious communities and a documented racial-extremist fringe.

### Major scholarship

Jan de Vries’s *Altgermanische Religionsgeschichte*, Georges Dumézil’s comparative studies, Gabriel Turville-Petre’s *Myth and Religion of the North*, Rudolf Simek’s *Dictionary of Northern Mythology*, John Lindow’s *Norse Mythology*, Andy Orchard’s *Dictionary of Norse Myth and Legend*, Margaret Clunies Ross’s work on Old Norse myth and poetics, Terry Gunnell on performance and ritual, Neil Price’s archaeology of Viking religion and magic, Jens Peter Schjødt on initiation and Odinic knowledge, Anders Hultgård on Uppsala sacrifice, and the multi-author *Pre-Christian Religions of the North* collectively define the modern field. The *Kulturhistorisk leksikon for nordisk middelalder* remains important for specialist historical entries but reflects the scholarship of its publication era.

No single work resolves the central methodological problem: the richest narratives are late, while the earliest objects and inscriptions are terse. Odin’s reconstructable history therefore consists not of peeling away “corruptions” to reveal one original god, but of mapping the changing conjunction of name, image, ritual, poetry, rulership, folklore, scholarship, religion, and political appropriation.
