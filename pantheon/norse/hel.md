# Hel: Research Dossier

**Class and earliest secure attestation.** Hel is an Old Norse female supernatural ruler/personification of death and of the underworld called *Hel*. Snorri Sturluson never explicitly classes her among the *ásynjur* (“goddesses”): he presents her as Loki’s daughter by the giantess Angrboða, sister of Fenrir and Jörmungandr, and sovereign over those assigned to her. “Goddess” is therefore a convenient modern classification, not an uncontested native title. The earliest probable attestation of Hel as an acting female being is *Ynglingatal*, conventionally dated to around 900 CE but preserved only in thirteenth-century manuscripts and subject to a long dating dispute. The securely datable physical witnesses are medieval: notably manuscripts of Snorri’s *Edda* from about 1300 onward and the approximately 1270 Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda. The oldest Germanic evidence for the word is much earlier—Gothic *halja* in the fourth-century Bible—but denotes the underworld, not demonstrably a goddess.

**Evidence labels used below**

- **Early attested:** plausibly pre-Christian poetic material, although usually transmitted in later manuscripts.
- **Later canonical:** medieval Icelandic synthesis that became standard, especially Snorri’s *Edda*.
- **Regional variant:** a materially different Scandinavian or Germanic version.
- **Learned systematization:** an ordering probably shaped by medieval antiquarian, poetic, or Christian intellectual practice.
- **Syncretistic identification:** an explicit or proposed equation with another being.
- **Medieval/early-modern accretion:** post-conversion development not securely recoverable for pre-Christian religion.
- **Folklore:** vernacular material recorded substantially later.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference rather than a directly attested tradition.
- **Modern invention:** a claim, symbol, ritual, or characterization not found in the historical sources.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Hel in orientation

The evidence preserves three overlapping meanings:

1. **hel as death or the grave**, especially in formulae such as *fara til Heljar* (“go to Hel,” die);
2. **Hel as a subterranean destination or collective abode of the dead**;
3. **Hel as a female agent who takes, possesses, receives, or rules the dead**.

Poetry can deliberately exploit all three. A phrase translated “Hel took him” may personify death without presupposing Snorri’s completely furnished underworld court. The fully individualized figure—Loki’s daughter, assigned rule by Odin, half *blár* and half flesh-colored, residing in Éljúðnir with named utensils and servants—is first securely documented in *Gylfaginning* 34, composed in Christian Iceland around 1220. Modern scholarship consequently ranges from treating her as an inherited death-being to treating her as a relatively late personification of an older place-name. Old Norse usage itself remains fluid between place, death, and agent.[Frog’s survey of the linguistic problem](https://www.helsinki.fi/assets/drupal/2023-12/RMN17_full_volume.pdf)

### Name and cognates

- **Old Norse:** *Hel*, genitive *Heljar*, dative *Helju*. The same form designates the being and the realm.
- **Proto-Germanic reconstruction:** conventionally **\*haljō-**, “concealed place; underworld.”
- **Gothic:** 𐌷𐌰𐌻𐌾𐌰, *halja*, used in Wulfila’s fourth-century biblical translation for the underworld/Hades.
- **Old English:** *hel(l)*.
- **Old Frisian:** *helle*.
- **Old Saxon:** *hellia*, *hella*.
- **Old High German:** *hella*.
- **Modern English:** *hell*, inherited through Old English—not borrowed from the Norse goddess’s proper name.
- **Modern Scandinavian:** Icelandic *hel*; Danish/Norwegian/Swedish forms survive chiefly in compounds and expressions.

The standard etymology derives the noun from the Germanic verb represented by Old English *helan* and Old Norse *hylja*, “hide, cover,” ultimately conventionally connected with Proto-Indo-European *ḱel-* “cover, conceal.” On this reading, Hel was originally “the concealed [place]” or the covered grave. Guus Kroonen reconstructs \**haljō-* as an underworld term; John Lindow and others infer a development from grave or hidden realm to female personification. A recent competing proposal derives the word from a Proto-Finnic term reconstructed as *kolja*, “guardian of the realm of the dead.” That loan theory remains non-consensus and should not replace the inherited-Germanic derivation without qualification.[Danish lexical summary of both proposals](https://lex.dk/Hel)

### Titles and circumlocutions

Hel has no securely attested cult epithets comparable to Odin’s or Freyja’s. Her poetic designations are genealogical or circumlocutory:

- **Loka mær**, “Loki’s maiden/daughter,” in *Ynglingatal*—**early attested**, if the traditional dating holds.
- **Loka dóttir**, “Loki’s daughter,” likewise associated with the Yngling tradition and made literal in Snorri.
- **ulfs ok Narfa systir**, “sister of the wolf and Narfi,” in *Ynglingatal*. The “wolf” is normally Fenrir. The name *Narfi* is textually problematic because Snorri elsewhere uses Narfi/Nari for a son of Loki, whereas Hel’s serpent-brother is Jörmungandr—**early poetic wording; genealogy uncertain**.
- **Býleists bróður mær/dóttir**, “daughter of Býleistr’s brother,” Býleistr’s brother being Loki—*Ynglingatal*, **early attested/poetic**.
- **hauga vörðr**, “guardian of burial mounds,” a kenning or interpretive rendering in the Yngling material—**early poetic**, not an independently attested priestly title.
- **drottning**, “queen,” applied to the personified Hell in medieval Christian Icelandic texts—**Christian medieval accretion/comparison**, not proof of a pagan title.
- “Queen of Hel,” “Lady of the Dead,” “Hidden One,” “Hospitable One,” “Caretaker of Ancestors,” and “Lady of Comfort” are common modern devotional titles. Apart from transparent paraphrases such as “ruler of Hel,” they are **modern inventions or modern religious formulations**.

### Hel, Helheim, Niflhel, and Christian hell

The medieval sources ordinarily call the realm simply **Hel**. *Helheimr* (“Hel-home”) is grammatically possible and occurs in later Icelandic usage, but its ubiquitous use as the formal ancient name of a discrete “world” is largely modern systematization.

*Niflhel*, “mist-Hel,” occurs in *Vafþrúðnismál* 43 and *Baldrs draumar* 2. Its relation to Hel is unclear: it can mean a deeper, misty underworld rather than a distinct planet-like realm. Snorri’s *Gylfaginning* distinguishes Niflheim, an icy primordial region, from Hel while also saying Odin cast Hel into Niflheim. Modern diagrams that make “Helheim” one of a fixed set of nine neatly bounded worlds exceed the evidence.

The English Christian word **hell** and cognates were already Germanic common nouns. Missionaries used them to translate Latin *infernus/infernum* and Greek *Hades* or *Gehenna*. Christianity therefore transformed an inherited Germanic underworld word into the name for the punitive Christian afterlife. It is false to say that Christianity named Hell after a historically worshipped Norse goddess. Direction of proof runs the other way: the place-word is pan-Germanic; the distinctly individualized female Hel is securely documented only in North Germanic medieval material. Medieval Christian texts could also personify Hell as female: the Old English *Gospel of Nicodemus* has *seo hell* debate Satan, and an Icelandic apostolic saga calls Hel “our queen.” These parallels show the productive ambiguity of a feminine place-noun, not necessarily a single continuous pan-Germanic goddess.[Bell, “Hel Our Queen”](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/harvard-theological-review/article/abs/hel-our-queen-an-old-norse-analogue-to-an-old-english-female-hell/DCB456D89888F9195D4AD2AA0F61E640)

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

#### The word

The earliest securely datable Germanic occurrence belongs to the Gothic biblical tradition of the fourth century CE. *Halja* translates a Mediterranean scriptural underworld. It establishes a Germanic underworld noun, not Loki’s daughter, an image, or a cult.

Old English, Old Saxon, and Old High German Christian texts likewise attest cognate underworld words. The *Merseburg Charms*, Tacitus’s *Germania*, Adam of Bremen’s *Gesta Hammaburgensis*, the *Nibelungenlied*, and the Scandinavian runic corpus contain no secure attestation of Hel as the Norse female ruler. That negative result matters: none supplies an independent temple, sacrifice, image, priesthood, or continental genealogy for her.

#### Hel as female agent: *Ynglingatal*

*Ynglingatal*, attributed to Þjóðólfr of Hvinir, describes royal deaths and repeatedly gives Hel agency:

- Hel chooses or takes King Dyggvi.
- She is Loki’s daughter and sister of the wolf and Narfi.
- She takes Halfdan Whiteleg, described through a grave-guardian circumlocution.
- Eystein is said to fare to Hel, with another genealogical kenning identifying her through Loki and Býleistr.

The poem is traditionally placed around 900, at the courtly transition between the ninth and tenth centuries. Most scholars still accept an early date, but proposed dates extend into the eleventh or twelfth century. The poem survives chiefly as quotations in Snorri’s *Ynglinga saga*, composed around 1225–1235; no Viking-Age manuscript survives. Thus it is the **earliest probable female Hel**, not an archaeologically fixed contemporary document. The dating controversy prevents calling every detail securely ninth-century.[Skaldic Project record](https://skaldic.org/db.php?id=4391&if=runic&table=verses&val=reykholt) [survey of the dating dispute](https://snl.no/Ynglingatal)

#### *Sonatorrek*

Egill Skallagrímsson’s lament *Sonatorrek* is traditionally dated about 960. In stanza 25 the speaker waits for Hel calmly or gladly. The line can evoke a female death-being, but it does not give Snorri’s genealogy or appearance. The poem is transmitted in medieval *Egils saga* manuscripts, and attribution, textual reconstruction, and individual stanzas have been debated. It is therefore **early attributed poetry, later manuscript evidence**.

### Principal primary corpus

#### Skaldic poetry

- ***Ynglingatal*** — Old Norse; traditionally c. 900, disputed; preserved in thirteenth-century *Heimskringla* witnesses. Earliest developed poetic personification and Loki genealogy.
- ***Sonatorrek*** — Old Norse; attributed c. 960; medieval transmission. Hel as anticipated death.
- ***Eiríksmál*** — Old Norse; composed after Erik Bloodaxe’s death, c. 954. Odin asks why Valhöll sounds as if Baldr were returning “from Hel.” Here Hel is principally a place/state.
- Other skaldic kennings use Hel to mean death, grave, or a devouring female. Each must be assessed grammatically; capitalization in modern editions can prejudge whether a person is meant.
- The standard modern corpus is *Skaldic Poetry of the Scandinavian Middle Ages*, which records manuscript witnesses, variant readings, prose order, translation, and commentary.

#### Poetic Edda

The main witness, GKS 2365 4to or Codex Regius, was written in Iceland around 1270 on 45 surviving vellum leaves; one eight-leaf quire is missing. Its poems are older than the codex to varying and frequently disputed degrees. The electronic diplomatic edition makes the physical manuscript and transcription available.[Codex Regius electronic edition](https://eae.ku.dk/q?p=eae%2Fvols%2Ftext%2F1)

Relevant poems include:

- ***Völuspá*** — Hel is predominantly a region or metonym of death. In the Ragnarök sequence Loki comes with forces from the east and “Hel’s people” or those of Hel participate, depending on textual reading and translation. After renewal Baldr and Höðr return.
- ***Vafþrúðnismál* 43** — Vafþrúðnir claims to have traveled nine worlds below Niflhel, “where men die out of Hel.” This is cosmographic but does not name Hel as ruler.
- ***Grímnismál* 31** — one root of Yggdrasill extends toward Hel; place, not necessarily person.
- ***Lokasenna*** — Loki predicts or describes death with Hel-language; it does not narrate him fathering the ruler.
- ***Fáfnismál*** and heroic lays — conventional “Hel take you,” “send to Hel,” or journey-to-death language.
- ***Guðrúnarkviða I*** and related heroic elegies — preparing or sending the dead to Hel.
- ***Helreið Brynhildar*** (“Brynhildr’s Hel-ride”) — Brynhildr travels toward the underworld and disputes with a giantess. Hel herself does not appear as a speaking hostess.
- ***Baldrs draumar*** — outside Codex Regius, chiefly preserved in AM 748 I 4to and later copies. Odin rides to Niflhel and awakens a dead seeress; a bench is prepared and mead brewed for Baldr. Hel herself never speaks or appears. Older datings have placed its present form in the tenth century, but dating remains uncertain; claims of precise tenth-century composition are not secure.[Bellows text and manuscript note](https://sacred-texts.com/book/the-poetic-edda/shell/baldrs-draumar)
- ***Hyndluljóð/Völuspá in skamma*** — gives Loki’s monstrous offspring in language not identical to Snorri’s tidy triad; textual relevance is indirect.

Carolyne Larrington’s *The Poetic Edda*, Andy Orchard’s translation, Ursula Dronke’s editions, and Henry Adams Bellows’s older public-domain version differ in punctuation, emendation, and whether *hel* is capitalized. Bellows is useful for access, not the last word in dating or philology.

#### Snorri’s *Edda*

Composed in Iceland around 1220, chiefly to explain traditional poetry. The earliest principal manuscripts are approximately a century later; their texts differ. Anthony Faulkes’s critical editions remain standard.[Faulkes edition information](https://vsnr.org/editions/snorri-sturluson-edda-prologue-and-gylfaginning/)

- ***Gylfaginning* 3:** sinners go to Hel and thence to Niflhel. This moral sorting is unusually Christian in form and conflicts with chapter 34’s assignment of sickness- and age-dead to Hel.
- ***Gylfaginning* 15:** a root of Yggdrasill is over Niflheim; Hel/Niflhel cosmography is systematized.
- ***Gylfaginning* 34:** Loki and Angrboða have three children—Fenrir, Jörmungandr, Hel. Odin has them brought from Jötunheimr, casts Hel into Niflheim, grants her authority over nine worlds, and assigns her those who die of sickness or old age. Her hall and household are described.
- ***Gylfaginning* 49:** Hermóðr rides Sleipnir to Hel after Baldr’s death; Hel sets the universal-weeping condition. Þökk’s refusal prevents release.
- ***Gylfaginning* 51:** at Ragnarök Loki arrives with Hel’s people; Snorri integrates underworld inhabitants into the final battle.
- ***Skáldskaparmál*:** preserves poetic diction and quotations relevant to Hel’s genealogy and to “Hel” as death.

This is the **later canonical** portrait. It may preserve older traditions, especially where supported by *Ynglingatal*, but its architecture, moral geography, and exhaustive household nomenclature show strong **learned systematization**.

#### *Heimskringla*: *Ynglinga saga*

Snorri’s prose narrates the deaths of Dyggvi, Halfdan Whiteleg, Eystein, and another Halfdan, then quotes *Ynglingatal*. The prose is a thirteenth-century interpretation of possibly much earlier verse. Hel’s female agency is clearer in some stanzas than in Snorri’s connecting prose.[Critical English edition](https://vsnr.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/11/Heimskringla-I.pdf)

#### Saxo Grammaticus

Saxo’s Latin *Gesta Danorum*, written around 1200, preserves a sharply different Balderus tradition. Balderus and Høtherus are martial rivals over Nanna; Høtherus wounds Balderus with a special sword. A spectral female identified in interpretation with Proserpina foretells or claims Balderus’s death. Saxo does not reproduce Snorri’s Hel bargaining with Hermóðr or the universal weeping. This is a **regional learned variant**, filtered through Latin historiography and classical vocabulary—not independent confirmation of every Snorrian detail.

#### Christian analogues

- The Old English *Gospel of Nicodemus* personifies feminine Hell in Christ’s Harrowing.
- Old Norse *Bartholomeus saga postola* uses *Hel drottning vár*, “Hel, our queen,” in Christian demonological speech.
- Homilies and vision literature use *helvíti*, “Hel-punishment,” for Christian Hell.

These show medieval interaction between vernacular underworld language and Christian eschatology. They cannot simply be mined as pagan mythology.

#### Texts named in the brief but yielding no Hel dossier

- **Tacitus, *Germania* (c. 98 CE):** no Hel.
- **Merseburg Charms (manuscript c. 900s):** no Hel.
- **Adam of Bremen (c. 1070s):** describes Uppsala and alleged sacrifices but no cult of Hel.
- **Runic inscriptions:** no securely identified invocation or dedication to Hel.
- ***Nibelungenlied* (c. 1200):** no Norse ruler Hel.
- ***Völsunga saga* (late thirteenth century):** abundant death/otherworld material, but no independent developed Hel myth comparable to Snorri.
- **Wagner’s *Ring*:** no dramatic character corresponding to Snorri’s Hel; “Hella” may appear in translations or allusive death-language, while Helmwige is a Valkyrie whose name merely begins *Helm-* (“helmet”), not Hel.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain and powers

**Early attested:** Hel receives, takes, or signifies the dead. The early poetry does not establish that every non-battle death goes to her or that she tortures them.

**Later canonical:** Snorri grants her authority over “nine worlds” and the duty to distribute lodging and provisions among the sickness-dead and age-dead. She possesses great mansions and servants. In the Baldr episode she can retain or release a divine dead person and impose a cosmic condition. This is sovereignty, not merely imprisonment.

The widespread modern binary—battle-dead to Valhalla, everyone else to Hel—is too neat. Freyja receives half the battle-slain in *Grímnismál*; drowned persons may belong to Rán; burial-mound residence, family mountains, reincarnation-like naming traditions, and continued grave existence coexist in the literature. The sources do not preserve one universal Scandinavian doctrine.[Abram’s study summary](https://discovery.ucl.ac.uk/id/eprint/3488/)

### Appearance

*Gylfaginning* 34 says:

- *hon er blá hálf en hálf með hörundarlit*—“she is half *blár* and half skin/flesh-colored”;
- therefore easily recognized;
- *gnúpleit ok grimmlig*—downcast/gloomy of aspect and grim/fierce.

*Blár* can denote dark blue, blue-black, livid, or corpse-dark. “Half alive and half skeletal” is not what Snorri says. The now-standard split living-face/skull-face is a **modern pictorial interpretation**, though decay imagery is compatible with the description. Nor does the text specify that the division runs vertically, that one side is beautiful, or that she has white and black hair.

### Éljúðnir and household

Snorri alone supplies the famous catalogue:

- **Éljúðnir** — Hel’s hall; etymology uncertain, commonly interpreted as “rain-damp” or “storm-damp.”
- **Hungr**, “Hunger” — her dish.
- **Sultr**, “Famine” — her knife.
- **Ganglati**, “Slow-goer/Idler” — male servant.
- **Ganglöt**, “Slow-goer/Idler” — female servant.
- **Fallandaforað**, “Falling Peril/Stumbling Block” — threshold.
- **Kör**, “Sickbed” — bed.
- **Blíkjandaböl**, “Gleaming/bleak Misfortune” — bed curtains or hangings.

The grotesque nomenclature is **later canonical/learned literary elaboration**. None is independently confirmed by pre-Christian ritual, runes, or archaeology.

### Geography

The Hel-road goes downward and northward in *Gylfaginning* 49. Hermóðr rides nine nights through dark, deep valleys, crosses the Gjöll bridge roofed with shining gold, speaks with its guardian Móðguðr, passes Hel’s gate, and finds Baldr in the hall. *Baldrs draumar* places the seeress’s grave toward Niflhel and describes underworld preparations. The sources disagree or remain imprecise about whether Hel, Niflhel, and Niflheim are nested, overlapping, or distinct.

### Animals, objects, colors, and symbols

No surviving source gives Hel:

- a sacred animal;
- a sacred plant;
- a ritual color;
- a number;
- a weekday;
- a celestial body;
- a weapon;
- an ancient rune or bind-rune;
- an authenticated cult emblem.

Garmr is stationed at or associated with Gnipahellir and the underworld in *Völuspá* and Snorri, but no text calls him “Hel’s pet dog.” That familiar relationship is inference and modern retelling.

Keys, black candles, skulls, black roses, yew, elder, ravens, owls, wolves, the rune Hagalaz, and black-and-white spiral symbols are **modern devotional or commercial associations** unless a modern community explicitly claims them as newly adopted symbols.

### Iconography

No pre-Christian depiction is securely identifiable as Hel. Migration-period bracteates IK 14 and IK 124, showing a rider approaching a female figure, have been interpreted as a journey to Hel; the identification is speculative because there is no inscription or unique attribute. Proposed Hel figures on Anglo-Scandinavian sculpture are likewise contested.

The first unambiguous images are post-medieval illustrations keyed to Snorri. A 1760 Icelandic manuscript, NKS 1867 4to, illustrates Hermóðr’s ride and Hel’s reception; this is antiquarian Christian-era art, not Viking-Age iconography.[Manuscript illustration record](https://myndir.uvic.ca/Nks1867-096r.html) Nineteenth- and twentieth-century artists developed the half-corpse queen, often under Romantic, Symbolist, and later fantasy influences.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical versions

| Relationship | Source and date | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|
| Daughter of Loki | *Ynglingatal*, conventionally c. 900; medieval transmission | **Early probable**, though poem’s date disputed |
| Daughter of Loki and Angrboða | *Gylfaginning* 34, c. 1220 | **Later canonical**; Angrboða maternity is not stated in the surviving early skaldic lines |
| Sister of “the wolf” and Narfi | *Ynglingatal* | **Early poetic**, textually/genealogically difficult |
| Sister of Fenrir and Jörmungandr | *Gylfaginning* 34 | **Later canonical systematization** |
| Member of the jötunn family | Inferred from Angrboða’s and Loki’s associations | **Modern classification/inference**; Snorri does not simply introduce Hel with the noun “goddess” or “giantess” |
| Consort or offspring | None in medieval sources | **Not attested** |

No source names a husband, lover, child, mount, priest, or cultic companion for Hel.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

“Interaction” here means a specific shared relationship or episode, not mere membership in the cosmology.

- **Loki — parentage.** Hel is identified as Loki’s daughter in *Ynglingatal* and *Gylfaginning* 34. **Early probable; later canonical expansion.**
- **Angrboða — maternity.** Angrboða bears Hel, Fenrir, and Jörmungandr with Loki. *Gylfaginning* 34, c. 1220. **Later canonical.** No conversation or joint action is preserved.
- **Fenrir — sibling relationship.** One early poetic circumlocution probably calls Hel sister of “the wolf”; Snorri explicitly makes Fenrir her brother. *Ynglingatal*; *Gylfaginning* 34. **Early probable genealogy/later canonical identification.** No direct encounter survives.
- **Jörmungandr — sibling relationship.** Explicit in Snorri’s three-child grouping, *Gylfaginning* 34. **Later canonical.** No direct encounter survives. *Ynglingatal* says Narfi rather than Jörmungandr in the relevant wording.
- **Odin — seizure, banishment, and investiture.** Odin sends gods to collect Loki’s children, casts Hel into Niflheim, and gives her power over nine worlds and specified dead. *Gylfaginning* 34. **Later canonical/learned systematization.**
- **Odin — indirect underworld visit.** In *Baldrs draumar*, Odin rides to Niflhel and raises a dead seeress. Hel does not appear; this is **not a documented Odin–Hel meeting**. Absence should not be converted into one.
- **Baldr — reception and detention.** After Baldr’s death Hel receives him with honor; preparations are foreseen in *Baldrs draumar*, while *Gylfaginning* 49 places him in her hall. She will release him only if all beings weep. **Eddic underworld tradition; explicit personal interaction only in later canonical Snorri.**
- **Nanna — reception.** Nanna dies at Baldr’s funeral and is found beside him in Hel’s hall; she sends gifts back through Hermóðr. *Gylfaginning* 49. **Later canonical.** No direct speech between Nanna and Hel is recorded.
- **Hermóðr — negotiation.** Hermóðr asks Hel to release Baldr; she proposes the universal-weeping test. *Gylfaginning* 49. **Later canonical.**
- **Þökk — convergent action, not a meeting.** Þökk refuses to weep and says, “Let Hel hold what she has,” thereby satisfying Hel’s condition for retaining Baldr. *Gylfaginning* 49. **Later canonical.** Snorri reports that people suppose Þökk was Loki; neither Hel nor Þökk meets the other in the narrative.
- **Frigg — indirect exchange.** Nanna sends Frigg linen from Hel through Hermóðr. *Gylfaginning* 49. **Later canonical.** Hel and Frigg never meet.
- **Fulla — indirect exchange.** Nanna sends Fulla a finger-ring from Hel. *Gylfaginning* 49. **Later canonical.**
- **The Æsir collectively — negotiation by embassy.** Hermóðr acts after the Æsir decide to seek Baldr’s return; Hel sets terms for the group. *Gylfaginning* 49. **Later canonical.**
- **Loki at Ragnarök — association with Hel’s people.** Loki arrives with people or forces of Hel in *Völuspá*’s apocalyptic tradition and Snorri’s *Gylfaginning* 51. **Eddic/later canonical**, but the texts do not show Loki receiving an order from Hel or Hel personally fighting.
- **Garmr — no securely documented personal interaction.** Garmr is an underworld hound or wolf-like monster, but no preserved source calls him Hel’s servant or shows them together.
- **Höðr — no direct interaction.** Höðr goes to the renewed world with Baldr after Ragnarök; no text narrates Hel receiving, judging, or speaking to him.
- **Hyrrokkin, Móðguðr, Váli, Thor, Freyja, and the Norns — no direct interaction with Hel is recorded.** They participate in related death, funeral, bridge, or afterlife narratives, but not in a scene with her.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. Loki’s children and Hel’s installation

**Fullest version:** In *Gylfaginning* 34, Loki has three children with Angrboða in Jötunheimr. Prophecy tells the gods that great mischief will arise from them. Odin has the children brought before him. He throws Jörmungandr into the encircling sea, confronts and binds Fenrir in the subsequent narrative, and casts Hel into Niflheim. He gives her power over nine worlds and orders her to allocate lodgings to those sent to her, namely people dead from sickness or old age. Snorri then supplies her body and household.

**Development:**

- The Loki-daughter relationship appears in *Ynglingatal* if its early date is accepted.
- The triad, Angrboða, Odin’s disposal of each child, nine-world jurisdiction, natural-death criterion, half-dark body, and named furnishings are securely documented only in Snorri.
- The narrative turns three destructive beings into a structured cosmological set: sea-serpent, bound wolf, underworld ruler. This symmetry may organize inherited fragments but cannot be assumed prehistoric in every detail.
- Calling Odin’s act “banishment for evil” adds motivation not explicitly stated: the gods act because prophecies and lineage make them expect danger.

### B. Baldr’s death and Hel’s condition

This is Hel’s only sustained speaking role.

In Snorri’s account, Loki learns that mistletoe has not sworn to spare Baldr, makes a missile, and guides the blind Höðr in throwing it. Baldr dies. Hermóðr, using Odin’s horse Sleipnir, rides nine nights down the Hel-road. Móðguðr tells him that Baldr has crossed the Gjöll bridge. Hermóðr leaps Hel’s gate and finds Baldr seated in the hall with Nanna.

Hermóðr asks Hel to let Baldr ride home, stressing the gods’ grief. Hel replies that Baldr’s universal popularity shall be tested: if everything living and dead weeps, he may return; if anyone refuses, he remains. All beings and substances weep except a giantess named Þökk, who declares she loved Baldr neither living nor dead and says Hel should keep what she has. Snorri says people believe Þökk was Loki. Baldr consequently remains until the world’s renewal.

**Earlier and variant evidence:**

- *Völuspá* knows Baldr’s killing by Höðr, Váli’s vengeance, and Baldr’s post-Ragnarök return, but does not narrate Hel’s bargain.
- *Baldrs draumar* knows an underworld preparing mead and a bench for Baldr. Odin, not Hermóðr, makes the journey, and he consults a seeress rather than Hel.
- Saxo’s *Gesta Danorum* turns Balderus and Høtherus into rival heroic figures competing for Nanna. A death-being resembling classical Proserpina appears in a dream or prophecy, but no universal weeping occurs.
- The Þökk stanza may be older verse embedded by Snorri, but its independent source is unknown.

Thus “Hel refused to return Baldr” is incomplete. In Snorri she accepts a conditional release; Þökk’s refusal defeats it. Nothing says Hel secretly engineered an impossible test, although modern retellings often characterize it that way.[Old Norse text of *Gylfaginning* 49](https://northerntales.se/en/eddas/prose-edda/gylfaginning/49/)

### C. Hel takes the Yngling kings

*Ynglingatal* repeatedly casts royal death as Hel’s erotic, familial, or possessive action. Dyggvi becomes her chosen companion or playmate; other kings pass to her or are seized by the guardian of graves. These compact stanzas are not fragments of a recoverable continuous myth. Their function is elegiac and genealogical: each ruler’s death advances the dynasty. They nevertheless show that female personification did not originate wholly with the surviving prose of *Gylfaginning*, assuming the accepted early dating.

### D. Ragnarök and “Hel’s people”

*Völuspá* describes infernal or chthonic forces joining the final catastrophe. Manuscript readings and syntactic divisions differ, but Loki and people associated with Hel belong to the hostile assembly. Snorri makes this more explicit: Naglfar, the ship made from dead people’s nails, is launched; Loki comes with Hel’s people. Hel herself is not said to leave her realm, command the army in person, fight, die, or survive.

Modern images of Hel leading an undead legion are therefore **modern retellings** based on the collective “Hel’s people.”

### E. The journey to Hel

The broader narrative complex includes:

- Odin’s ride in *Baldrs draumar*;
- Hermóðr’s ride in *Gylfaginning*;
- Brynhildr’s journey in *Helreið Brynhildar*;
- formulaic journeys to Hel in heroic poetry;
- kings “taken” by Hel in skaldic verse.

These share descent, darkness, difficult roads, bridges or gates, mounted travel, and communication with the dead, but no source presents them as one canonical initiation cycle.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### The evidentiary result

There is **no securely documented historical cult of Hel**.

No known inscription dedicates an object to her. No temple, sanctuary, altar, priesthood, festival, pilgrimage, oracle, sacrifice, votive deposit, or theophoric personal name can be securely assigned to her. There is no reliable Hel place-name series comparable to names involving Thor, Odin, Freyr, Freyja, Ullr, or Njörðr. No archaeological object bears an inscription identifying her. Rudolf Simek therefore judged that the evidence does not establish belief in a pre-Christian goddess Hel as a recipient of worship, though the poetry may personify death.[Simek index and bibliographic locus, pp. 137–38](https://www.mimisbrunnr.info/dnm-index)

This negative evidence does not prove no person anywhere imagined or addressed Hel. It means organized or popular cult cannot presently be demonstrated.

### Funerary practice is not automatically Hel worship

Scandinavian archaeology documents cremation, inhumation, ship burial, chamber graves, mound burial, grave goods, sacrificed animals, and some probable human killings associated with funerals. Written sources describe offerings and communication with the dead. These practices attest complex afterlife ideas, not necessarily a cult of Hel.

Likewise:

- Adam of Bremen’s description of sacrifices at Uppsala does not name Hel.
- Ibn Faḍlān’s account of a Rus ship funeral does not name Hel.
- Bog bodies, decapitations, bound corpses, or female ritual specialists cannot be assigned to Hel merely because they concern death.
- Claims that horses, black animals, human beings, or “the dishonored dead” were sacrificed specifically to Hel are **unsupported**.

Human sacrifice existed or probably existed in some northern European contexts, but no source connects it directly to Hel. The distinction is crucial: evidence for Norse sacrifice is not evidence for every proposed recipient.

### Alleged traditional offerings

Modern lists recommending black bread, apples, coffee, dark chocolate, alcohol, bones, dead flowers, grave soil, black candles, yew, blood, or buried food do not descend from a documented Hel ritual calendar. They are **modern devotional constructions**, often based on sympathetic symbolism, contemporary ancestor veneration, or personal revelation.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Proto-Germanic and early Germanic period

A feminine Germanic noun \**haljō-* denoted the hidden underworld. Cognates across Gothic and West and North Germanic show that the word predates Norse literature. There is no comparably widespread early evidence for a female divine personality named \**Haljō*. The most conservative reconstruction is therefore lexical and spatial, not biographical.

### Viking Age, approximately 800–1050

If *Ynglingatal* and *Sonatorrek* substantially preserve their attributed dates, Norwegian-Icelandic poets could portray Hel as a female agent: Loki’s daughter, chooser or possessor of the dead, and an inevitable figure awaiting the mortal. This does not establish temples or a uniform popular theology.

Burial practice and poetry show multiple destinations and modes of postmortem existence. Hel was one expression within that plurality. There is no evidence that she was the universally feared punitive queen of all non-warrior dead.

### Conversion period, tenth–twelfth centuries

The Scandinavian conversions gave inherited *hel* vocabulary Christian work. It translated the abode of punishment, was compounded in *helvíti*, and absorbed imagery from Latin infernal literature, sermons, apocrypha, and the Harrowing of Hell. At the same time, traditional poetic diction continued to circulate.

There is no documented campaign specifically suppressing temples of Hel, because none is documented. General prohibitions on sacrifice, exposure, divination, and pagan ritual affected the religious environment in which an older death-personification might have operated, but a Hel-specific persecution narrative would be invented.

### Thirteenth-century Icelandic synthesis

Snorri’s *Edda* gives Hel her enduring canonical form. It combines:

- old poetic genealogy;
- a structured family of monsters;
- an Odin-centered cosmic government;
- a morally and spatially ordered underworld;
- antiquarian explanations of skaldic diction;
- potentially Christian infernal motifs;
- memorable allegorical household names.

The Poetic Edda manuscripts preserved varied older-seeming usages beside this system. The coexistence of those texts, rather than one harmonized theology, is the record.

### Late medieval and early modern transmission

Hel remained present in copies, commentaries, Christian uses of *hel*, antiquarian collections, and learned comparison with classical Hades or Proserpina. The 1760 NKS 1867 4to illustration shows that Icelandic readers could visualize the Baldr episode long after conversion, but it is manuscript reception, not cult continuity.

Continental Frau Holle/Hulda traditions were increasingly compared with Hel. Similar names, femininity, winter, death, children, wells, and subterranean motifs encouraged equation. The names are not straightforwardly identical, and the folkloric profiles differ substantially. “Frau Holle is Hel under another name” is a **contested modern scholarly or esoteric synthesis**, not a documented medieval identification.

### Nineteenth and early twentieth centuries

Romantic nationalism, comparative mythology, editions of the Eddas, and illustrated myth handbooks transformed Hel into a standard “Norse goddess of death.” Jacob Grimm treated her within a broader Germanic mythological reconstruction. Artists increasingly depicted a vertically divided living/dead woman.

Wagner drew extensively on Eddic and Völsung material, but Hel is not a principal character in *Der Ring des Nibelungen* (librettos developed 1848–1874; first complete cycle 1876). Helmwige is a Valkyrie, not Hel. Identifying her as Hel is a name-based error.

### Later twentieth century to present

Academic study has increasingly separated:

- the pan-Germanic underworld word;
- pre-Christian poetic personification;
- Snorri’s systematic ruler;
- Christian Hell;
- modern devotional Hel.

Contemporary Heathens, Norse pagans, occultists, and eclectic practitioners do worship or “work with” Hel, often as an ancestral guardian, psychopomp, grief deity, impartial keeper of the dead, or power of decay and renewal. These are living religious developments, but their rituals are not demonstrably continuous from Viking-Age cult. No reliable global count isolates Hel worshippers from broader Heathen populations.

Modern organizations such as the Troth explicitly present reconstructed and innovative devotion. Their characterization of Hel as a gracious hostess or caretaker is a modern theological rereading grounded partly in Baldr’s honorable reception but not stated as an ancient epithet.[The Troth’s contemporary devotional profile](https://thetroth.org/resource/hel/)

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Occultism and esotericism

Renaissance ceremonial magic did not possess an established Hel cult. She is marginal or absent from the major Solomonic grimoires, Christian Cabala, early Rosicrucian literature, and the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn’s standard planetary system. Claims that Hel had an ancient “dark goddess” initiation rite, fixed sigil, tarot correspondence, planetary hour, or traditional black-candle liturgy are **modern inventions**.

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century occult currents incorporated her through:

- Germanic neopagan reconstruction;
- Wiccan and post-Wiccan goddess spirituality;
- “dark goddess” and underworld-descent psychology;
- Jungian shadow work;
- devotional polytheism;
- chaos magic and personally designed sigils.

Equations with Hecate, Persephone, Ereshkigal, Inanna, Kali, the Morrígan, Santa Muerte, Frau Holle, or Perchta are **modern comparative or syncretistic practices** unless explicitly presented as contemporary theology. No medieval Norse source makes them.

### Neopagan theology

Common contemporary themes include:

- Hel as neutral rather than evil;
- protector of ancestors;
- compassionate receiver of those excluded from warrior ideals;
- patron of the chronically ill or disabled;
- guide in bereavement;
- embodiment of necessary decay;
- guardian of boundaries and consent.

Some are reasonable theological extrapolations from her receipt of sickness- and age-dead and her firm treatment of Baldr. None should be retrojected as a securely attested Viking-Age doctrine.

“Samhain is Hel’s feast,” “Saturday is her day,” “Hagalaz is her rune,” “elder and yew are her sacred trees,” and prescribed offerings of coffee or chocolate are **modern inventions**, often blending Celtic, runic, occult, and personal traditions.

### Literature, comics, film, and games

- **Marvel Comics:** Hela, created by Stan Lee and Jack Kirby, debuted in *Journey into Mystery* no. 102 in 1964. Marvel retained the death-ruler and visual division but greatly expanded her as a recurring supervillain and combatant.[Marvel’s publication history](https://www.marvel.com/articles/comics/hela-goddess-of-death-origin-explained)
- **Marvel Cinematic Universe:** *Thor: Ragnarok* (2017) made Hela Odin’s firstborn daughter and Thor’s sister. That genealogy is **Marvel invention**, not Norse tradition.
- **Hellblade: Senua’s Sacrifice** (2017): uses Hela and Helheim within a psychological horror narrative shaped by grief and psychosis; its monumental corpse-woman visual is modern game design.
- **God of War** (2018) and related fantasy games use Hel/Helheim as navigable icy realms, generally privileging Snorri’s cosmographic system and modern nine-realms maps.
- Modern fantasy novels regularly make Hel a beautiful/skeletal dual woman, necromancer, demon queen, or antihero. These types owe as much to modern fantasy art and Christian infernal imagery as to *Gylfaginning*.

### Nationalist and extremist use

Hel is less central to historic völkisch and contemporary white-supremacist symbolism than Odin, Thor’s hammer, runes, or Valhalla. Nevertheless, racist and ethnonationalist Heathen groups sometimes absorb her into an allegedly pure ancestral pantheon. No evidence supports racial exclusivity as an ancient attribute of Hel. Modern inclusive Heathen groups also worship her. Political appropriation must therefore be assigned to particular modern organizations, not to the medieval figure herself.

### Commercial image

Jewelry marketed as “Hel’s rune,” split-face statues, altar cloths, candles, perfumes, tarot cards, and “authentic Viking” sigils are commercially successful but overwhelmingly modern designs. There is no archaeologically authenticated Hel pendant or ancient personal sigil.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Personification versus inherited goddess

The central debate asks whether Hel was:

1. an old Germanic female ruler of the dead;
2. a North Germanic personification developed from the feminine place-word;
3. a poetic figure given a quasi-genealogy before conversion;
4. largely Snorri’s literary construction under Christian influence.

The strongest evidence against a fully developed early cult goddess is the absence of dedications, cult sites, place-names, images, ritual testimony, and pan-Germanic personal mythology. The strongest evidence against attributing the person entirely to Snorri is *Ynglingatal*’s Loki-daughter language, if its early dating and transmitted wording are accepted.

A cautious synthesis is: the underworld term is ancient; female poetic personification probably predates Snorri; the fully systematized queen is first securely recoverable from thirteenth-century Iceland.

### Dating *Ynglingatal*

Traditional scholarship connected Þjóðólfr with Harald Fairhair’s period and dated the poem around 900. Gustav Neckel, Claus Krag, and others argued for later composition, citing genealogy, ideology, and literary construction. Metrical and linguistic counterarguments have defended an early date. No autograph exists. Consequently, “ninth-century goddess Hel” is probable under the conventional dating, not physically contemporary evidence.

### The Snorri problem

Snorri is indispensable but wrote more than two centuries after Iceland’s conversion, for readers learning traditional poetics. His work can:

- preserve lost verse and traditions;
- rationalize variants;
- manufacture orderly genealogies;
- translate poetic metaphor into prose biography;
- incorporate Christian cosmography;
- present speculation as narrative.

The conflict between *Gylfaginning* 3’s wicked dead and chapter 34’s sickness- and age-dead illustrates why his Edda cannot be treated as a unitary pagan catechism.

### Hel and Christian Hell

Some earlier scholars treated Christian influence as corruption of an originally neutral Hel; others treated the Norse queen largely as Christian allegory. Both extremes exceed the evidence. The shared inherited vocabulary allowed continual two-way reinterpretation. Cold, darkness, gates, bridges, punishment, and female personification may come from multiple sources and cannot always be assigned securely.

### Comparative theories

- **Frau Holle/Hulda:** suggestive similarity but no demonstrated identity; folklore was recorded late and has domestic, spinning, weather, fertility, and child-associated traits absent from Hel.
- **Persephone/Proserpina:** Saxo’s Latin interpretive framework supplies a comparison, not proof of borrowing or identity.
- **Kali/Bhavani:** nineteenth- and twentieth-century comparativism based largely on death imagery; no historical contact chain establishes identity.
- **Hecate:** modern occult syncretism.
- **Finnic/Sámi parallels:** female otherworld rulers may reflect northern contact zones. Recent scholarship has explored Germanic–Uralic interaction, but the direction, date, and lexical history remain debated.
- **Proto-Indo-European death ruler:** comparisons with Yama, Yima, Hades, and Donn reconstruct a usually male ruler; Hel’s gender may represent a North Germanic innovation rather than direct retention.

### Primary evidence and repositories

- **GKS 2365 4to**, Codex Regius of the Poetic Edda: Árni Magnússon Institute, Reykjavík; c. 1270; digitized.
- **AM 748 I 4to:** preserves *Baldrs draumar* and other Eddic material; Árni Magnússon collections.
- Principal *Edda* manuscripts include **Codex Regius GKS 2367 4to**, **Codex Wormianus AM 242 fol.**, **Codex Trajectinus**, and **Codex Upsaliensis DG 11**.
- *Heimskringla*’s medieval transmission suffered major losses, including the destruction of the Kringla manuscript in the 1728 Copenhagen fire, although copies and fragments preserve readings.
- Skaldic verses survive embedded in prose manuscripts rather than in Viking-Age authorial copies.
- No securely identified Hel cult object is held by any museum.

Major modern scholarship includes Jan de Vries’s *Altgermanische Religionsgeschichte*; Hilda Ellis Davidson’s *The Road to Hel* (1943); Rudolf Simek’s *Lexikon der germanischen Mythologie/Dictionary of Northern Mythology*; John Lindow’s *Norse Mythology*; Gabriel Turville-Petre’s *Myth and Religion of the North*; Andy Orchard’s dictionary and Edda translation; Margaret Clunies Ross’s work on Old Norse myth and poetics; and Christopher Abram’s research on medieval representations of the pagan afterlife.[Davidson bibliographic record](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL185605M/The_road_to_hel) [De Vries bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books?id=iIIIAQAAIAAJ)

### Persistent popular errors

- “Helheim was one of a universally fixed ancient nine-world map.” — **Not established.**
- “Hel ruled those who died dishonorably.” — Snorri says sickness or age, not dishonor.
- “She punished evil souls.” — Confuses *Gylfaginning* 3 and Christian Hell with the personal ruler’s securely stated conduct.
- “Half her body was a skeleton.” — Snorri says half *blár*, half flesh-colored.
- “Garm was her pet.” — **Not stated.**
- “Her symbol was Hagalaz/a three-pronged rune/a black-and-white spiral.” — **Modern invention.**
- “Vikings sacrificed black horses or humans to her.” — **No Hel-specific evidence.**
- “Frau Holle proves a continuous pan-Germanic cult.” — **Unproved.**
- “English Hell was named after the goddess.” — Misleading; both inherit the Germanic underworld noun.
- “Hela was Odin’s daughter and Thor’s sister.” — Marvel invention.
- “Hel was evil.” — A theological or fictional judgment, not a medieval source classification.
- “Hel was a gentle maternal caretaker in ancient worship.” — Modern devotional interpretation.
