# Hiisi: Research Dossier

**Class:** In the earliest secure written source, Hiisi is classified by a Christian author among the former gods or supernatural powers of Karelia and is associated with victory over forest-dwellers or forest game. In later recorded Finnish and Karelian tradition *hiisi* can mean a sacred or dangerous locality, the indwelling power or population of such a place, a hostile forest or mountain spirit, a giant, a demon, “the Devil,” or a collective of goblin-like beings. Modern reconstructionists often restore the older place-centered sense and call a *hiisi* a sacred natural site or its spirit. It is therefore misleading to assign Hiisi one timeless class.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Mikael Agricola’s Finnish verse preface to *Dauidin Psaltari*, printed in 1551: **“Hijsi Metzeleist soi Woiton”**—in normalized Finnish, *Hiisi metsäläisistä soi voiton*, approximately “Hiisi granted victory over the forest-dwellers/game.” This is the earliest unambiguous printed identification of Hiisi as a named religious power. Older *Hiisi-/Hiite-* place-names may preserve the word’s sacral use, but a place-name by itself does not establish belief in an anthropomorphic god. No archaeological image, Sámi drum figure, or prehistoric cairn can securely be identified as a depiction of Hiisi.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early attested]** The earliest written or securely contextualized evidence.
- **[Later canonical]** Above all Lönnrot’s synthetic *Kalevala* of 1835 or 1849.
- **[Regional variant]** A localized oral-poetry, place-lore, or belief variant.
- **[Early-modern systematization]** An author’s post-Reformation ordering of oral or antiquarian material.
- **[Folklore]** Recorded oral tradition, normally documented in the eighteenth–twentieth centuries, not automatically prehistoric.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A historical model inferred from language, place-names, archaeology, or comparative evidence.
- **[Modern revival]** Contemporary religious reuse.
- **[Modern invention/reception]** Fantasy, art, games, astronomy, or unsupported material presented as ancient.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### The central problem

Hiisi is not recoverable as a single pre-Christian “god of evil.” The documentary sequence instead shows several overlapping phenomena:

1. a Finnic word for a delimited, numinous, and probably cultically important place;
2. a power, owner, or population associated with that place;
3. an increasingly hostile being or category of beings in Christian-period charms and legends;
4. an individualized literary antagonist in Lönnrot’s *Kalevala*;
5. a modern sacred-place concept restored by scholarship and Finnish native-faith movements.

The common formula “a sacred grove that Christianity turned into a demon” captures the broad semantic direction but oversimplifies it. “Grove” is only one proposed or regionally preserved meaning; cemetery, cult place, bounded communal sanctuary, remarkable hill, and anomalous natural site also occur in scholarship. Nor does the evidence identify a single decree or missionary who deliberately transformed the word.

### Forms

- Finnish: **hiisi**, genitive **hiiden**, partitive **hiittä**, illative **hiiteen**; plural **hiidet**.
- Older spelling: Agricola’s **Hijsi**.
- Derived or related Finnish forms: **hiito/hiitto**, **hitto**, **hittolainen**; the latter forms became mild devil-words and expletives.
- Karelian and Ingrian: cognate *hiisi/hiito*-type forms occur in oral poetry and charms.
- Estonian: **hiis**, genitive **hiie**, chiefly a sacred natural site or grove; **hiid**, “giant,” is morphologically related but semantically differentiated.
- Toponymic forms include **Hiitola**, “Hiisi’s place/domain”; **Hiidenmaa**, the Finnish name of Estonia’s Hiiumaa; and compounds such as *Hiidenmäki* (“Hiisi Hill”), *Hiidenvuori* (“Hiisi Mountain”), *Hiidenkivi* (“Hiisi’s stone”), and *Hiidenkirnu* (“Hiisi’s churn,” a glacial pothole).

### Etymology

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction] Mauno Koski’s cult-place model.** Koski reconstructed the comparative primary sense as “cult place,” possibly preceded by “burial place.” Western Finnish and Estonian meanings, place-name distributions, proximity to some Iron Age cemeteries, and later traditions of offerings support a sacral-locality interpretation. Koski regarded the personified being as secondary.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction] Cemetery emphasis.** A. M. Tallgren and later Koski connected numerous *hiisi* place-names with Iron Age cemeteries and central places. Anna Wessman’s archaeological review found meaningful associations at some sites—cemeteries, springs, sacred trees, and cup-marked stones—but not enough consistency to equate every *hiisi* with a cemetery.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction] Veikko Anttonen’s boundary model.** Anttonen interpreted *hiisi* and *pyhä* through territorial classification. In this model, a *hiisi* was a socially delimited place within or near settled territory where access, ownership, resources, death, and ritual were specially regulated. Christianization displaced its positive communal value and helped move *hiisi* toward “the habitation of hostile beings.”

**[Disputed reconstruction] Germanic derivation.** Some etymological arguments connect Proto-Finnic *hiisi* with a Germanic territorial or “side/section” word. This can support the boundary or apportioned-territory model, but it is not a universally settled etymology.

**[Disputed reconstruction] Sámi *siida/sïjte/sijdâ*.** Finnish *hiisi* has been compared with North Sámi *siida*, a village or reindeer-herding community and its territory, reconstructed from Proto-Sámi *sijte*. The semantic comparison is attractive—community, territory, restricted place—but published surveys acknowledge phonological and semantic problems.

**[Disputed reconstruction] Sámi *sieidi*.** *Sieidi* denotes a worshipped natural formation, especially a sacrificial stone. A relationship among Finnic *hiisi*, Sámi *siida*, and *sieidi* has repeatedly been proposed, but recent historical-linguistic discussion requires irregular sound developments or mediation through an unknown language. Similar sacred-place meanings do not prove cognacy.

### Titles and poetic expressions

There is no secure pre-Christian litany of Hiisi’s epithets. Recorded expressions are mostly compounds or parallel formulas:

- **Hiiden isäntä**, “master of Hiisi,” and place-owner formulations: folklore rather than evidence for a canonical pantheon.
- **Hiitolainen**, an inhabitant or being of Hiitola; later also a demon-word.
- **Hiiden väki**, “the folk/power of Hiisi.” Finnish *väki* can signify both a population and a dangerous immanent force.
- **Hiiden neiti/immet**, “maiden(s) of Hiisi,” in charm-poetry.
- **Hiiden koira**, “hound of Hiisi,” either a supernatural dog or an insult.
- **Hiiden hevonen**, “Hiisi’s horse.”
- **Hiiden hirvi**, “Hiisi’s elk/moose.”
- **Hiiden härkä**, “Hiisi’s ox.”
- **Hiiden kissa** and **Kipinätär**, in later compilations and charms; these are unstable poetic personifications, not a securely attested zoological entourage.
- **Jutas/Juutas**, **Lempo**, **Piru**, and *paha* occur as parallel hostile terms in Christian-period poetry. Parallelism does not establish that these were originally one god.

### Sámi classification

**Finding: no securely documented Sámi deity named Hiisi.** Schefferus’s *Lapponia* (1673), later missionary accounts, surviving noaidi drums, and Manker’s drum corpus document Sámi gods, sacred places, *sieidi*, *saiva*, and other powers, but do not securely identify a drum figure or indigenous deity called Hiisi. Hiisi should not therefore be inserted into a “Finnic and Sámi pantheon” as a shared named god.

The Sámi material belongs in this dossier because of disputed etymologies and analogous sacred-place institutions. Rydving and later scholars also show why caution is essential: most surviving drums lack an owner’s explanation, and twentieth-century interpreters sometimes converted mundane or ambiguous figures into a systematic divine iconography.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Material before 1551

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction] Toponymy.** *Hiisi*-names may be medieval or older and often cluster near old settlement, cemetery, hill, water, or boundary landscapes. They demonstrate the antiquity and geographic range of the word more securely than they demonstrate the personality of a god.

**[Unestablished] Prehistoric iconography.** Finnish rock paintings, Bronze Age cairns, cup-marked stones, and Iron Age objects cannot presently be labeled “Hiisi” from their images alone. There is no identifying inscription.

### Agricola, 1551

Mikael Agricola placed a verse catalogue of allegedly former Tavastian and Karelian gods in the preface to his Finnish Psalter. The relevant line is:

> **Hijsi Metzeleist soi Woiton**  
> “Hiisi granted victory over the forest-dwellers/game.”

**[Early attested]** The line establishes that Agricola—or his informants—treated Hiisi as a named power associated with the forest and success or victory there. It does not say that Hiisi was evil, a giant, a goblin, a supreme god, or lord of an underworld.

Agricola wrote as a Lutheran bishop condemning prior religion. His catalogue is indispensable but not a neutral ethnographic transcript. Its compact meter, Christian polemic, possible literary models, and mixture of gods, saints, culture heroes, festival names, and spirits make the modern word “pantheon” hazardous.

### Christfrid Ganander, 1789

Ganander’s Swedish-language *Mythologia Fennica* is an alphabetical antiquarian dictionary compiled from charms, poems, riddles, oral explanations, and earlier scholarship.

**[Early-modern systematization]** Ganander brought together Hiisi as place, mountain or forest power, giant, troll, and demonic being. His work preserves material older than its publication but dates the evidence imperfectly and frequently systematizes poetic parallel words as mythological persons.

### Nineteenth-century collection and interpretation

- **Elias Lönnrot**, *Vanha Kalevala* (1835) and expanded *Kalevala* (1849): literary epics compiled from oral poems, with connective composition and reassignment of lines by Lönnrot.
- **M. A. Castrén**, lectures on Finnish mythology, published in the 1850s: comparative mythological classification that treated forest, water, mountain, and underworld powers as an organized religious system.
- **Elias Lönnrot**, *Suomen kansan muinaisia loitsurunoja* (1880): a major collection of charms, including serpent-origin traditions involving Hiisi.
- **John Abercromby**, *The Pre- and Proto-historic Finns* (1898): English translations and comparative commentary. Valuable as a source history, but its evolutionary and missionary-era assumptions require scrutiny.

### SKVR

*Suomen Kansan Vanhat Runot* was printed primarily in 1908–1948, with later supplementation, and is now searchable digitally. It preserves roughly 100,000 traditional poems and variants, with collectors, singers, locations, and collection dates.

**[Primary folklore corpus]** SKVR is more useful than the *Kalevala* for determining actual variant distribution. Its texts are nevertheless nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century records of oral performances, not verbatim Iron Age scriptures.

Relevant clusters include:

- skiing the Elk of Hiisi;
- bridling Hiisi’s horse;
- origin charms for snakes, stone injuries, illness, iron, fire, and animals;
- hunting invocations and expulsive formulas;
- riddles involving Hiisi’s elk, horse, maiden, scourge, or eye;
- legends and formulas locating harmful powers in Hiitola or Hiisi’s mountain.

### The two Kalevalas

**Old Kalevala, 1835, canto 7.** Lönnrot already joins the Elk of Hiisi and Hiisi’s fire-maned horse to a bridal-task framework.

**New Kalevala, 1849, cantos 13–14.** Lemminkäinen must ski down the Elk of Hiisi, bridle Hiisi’s horse, and then shoot the swan on Tuoni’s river to win Louhi’s daughter. Hiisi and Hiisi’s folk construct and animate the elk from pieces of the landscape; the creature devastates settlements before Lemminkäinen captures it.

The 1849 sequence is **[Later canonical]**, not a single orally transmitted canonical myth. The Open Kalevala source commentary traces Lönnrot’s framing partly to Juhana Kainulainen’s Kesälahti performance, SKVR VII1 823, while other components came from different singers and regions.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains over time

**[Reconstructed older layer] Sacred locality.** A grove, hill, cemetery, sanctuary, or bounded ritual place. Estonian *hiis* preserves this sense most clearly.

**[Agricola, 1551] Forest success.** Hiisi grants “victory” over *metsäläiset*. The object may mean forest beings, game, or those inhabiting the forest; translating it simply as “animals” is interpretive.

**[Recorded folklore] Wild and anomalous terrain.** Hills, mountains, woods, clefts, boulders, potholes, rapids, and remote fields are called Hiisi’s. These are often outside routine cultivated order.

**[Christian-period folklore] Harm and pollution.** Charms send disease, pain, iron, fire, snakes, and other dangers “to Hiisi,” or describe their origin in Hiitola. In such formulas Hiisi functions both as hostile agent and expulsive destination.

**[Later folklore] Giants and builders.** Hiisi or the *hiidet* throw enormous boulders, make potholes, carry stones, build cairns, and leave striking landforms.

**[Kalevala] Owner and manufacturer of monstrous animals.** Hiisi possesses an elk and horse and commands a collective workforce.

### Form

No standard pre-Christian body is attested. Later descriptions vary:

- an unseen place-power;
- a singular male owner;
- a population of small demonic beings;
- gigantic prehistoric inhabitants;
- devil-like beings;
- in fantasy, goblins or armed humanoids.

These forms should not be combined into one portrait.

### Iconography

**No securely identified historical cult image survives.** Agricola provides no visual description. Hiisi is not identifiable on Sámi drums. Nineteenth-century and later artists illustrate scenes from the *Kalevala*, especially the hunt of the elk and bridling of the horse, but those are receptions of literary episodes.

### Animals and objects

- **Elk/moose:** the defining Kalevalaic animal, but different oral variants assign its construction differently or merely call it *Hiiden hirvi*.
- **Horse:** a dangerous but excellent, sometimes fire-maned or foam-mouthed courser. The association is not purely negative.
- **Hound:** supernatural helper, hostile creature, or abusive metaphor.
- **Snake:** “Hiisi’s scourge” or a creature animated in serpent-origin charms.
- **Hare:** “Hiisi’s bandy legs” in riddling or figurative language.
- **Pine:** a many-horned “Elk of Hiisi” in riddles.
- **Thunder:** “the neighing of Hiisi’s horse” in a riddle.

No ancient source establishes sacred colors, weekdays, planets, or canonical numbers for Hiisi. Modern lists supplying such correspondences are **[Modern invention]** unless individually sourced.

### Hiidenkiuas and giants’ stones

A **hiidenkiuas**, literally “Hiisi’s stove,” is the folk and archaeological name for a stone cairn, commonly a Bronze Age coastal burial cairn. The word records later interpretation, not the cairn builders’ terminology. Panelia’s Kuninkaanhauta, for example, is approximately 36 metres across and about 4–4.5 metres high.

Likewise:

- *hiidenkivi*, an erratic boulder;
- *hiidenkirnu*, a glacial pothole;
- *hiidenpesä*, “Hiisi’s nest”;
- *hiidenlinna*, “Hiisi’s fortress”;
- *jätinkirkko*, “giant’s church,” for large prehistoric stone enclosures.

**[Folklore]** These features were attributed to Hiisi, *hiidet*, or giants because their geological or archaeological origins were unknown.  
**[Modern scholarly finding]** Glaciers produced erratics and potholes; prehistoric communities built the cairns. Their *hiisi* names are evidence for later reception, not proof that they were all Hiisi cult monuments.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other beings

### Genealogy

No early source gives Hiisi a secure parentage, spouse, or birth narrative. Later mythographic lists sometimes assign a daughter, maidens, servants, animals, or a household, but these arise from poetic grammar and folklore rather than a stable genealogy.

- **Hiiden neiti/immet:** female figures belonging to or dwelling in Hiisi; not securely daughters.
- **Hippa:** sometimes described in secondary compilations as Hiisi’s daughter; the primary basis is weak and late.
- **Kipinätär:** a fiery or spark-associated female figure sometimes called Hiisi’s cat in charms or compilations; relationship varies.
- **Hiiden väki:** collective people or force, not necessarily offspring.
- **Hiitola:** domain or household rather than a spouse or relative.

### Documented divine or supernatural interactions

The list below distinguishes actual episodes from mere co-occurrence.

- **Hiisi — Hiiden väki (“Hiisi’s folk”).** Hiisi directs or participates with his people in constructing the supernatural elk from natural materials. *Kalevala* 13 (1849), based on variant oral elk-skiing poems. **[Later canonical; orally derived]**
- **Hiisi — Syöjätär.** In serpent-origin charm variants, foam or saliva produced in a Hiisi episode is swallowed, licked, or spat out by Syöjätär; the resulting object becomes a snake, sometimes receiving life from Hiisi. Lönnrot’s *Suomen kansan muinaisia loitsurunoja* (1880) and SKVR variants. **[Folklore; regional variants]**
- **Hiisi — Lempo.** The names occur as parallel hostile powers or substitutes in charms and in the *Kalevala*. No surviving early narrative securely records the two meeting or acting upon one another. **[Folkloric synonymy/parallelism; no interaction established]**
- **Hiisi — Piru.** Christian-period language equates or parallels Hiisi with *piru*, “devil/demon.” This is semantic syncretism, not a preserved story of two beings interacting. **[Christian-period syncretistic identification]**
- **Hiisi — Juutas/Jutas.** Later poetry and dictionaries use these as hostile parallel names, ultimately influenced by “Judas.” No discrete encounter is attested. **[Christian-period accretion]**
- **Hiisi — Tapio.** Agricola lists both as forest-associated powers, Tapio granting catches and Hiisi victory over *metsäläiset*. They do not interact in the text. **[Early attested co-listing; absence of interaction]**
- **Hiisi — Ukko.** In Lemminkäinen’s capture of Hiisi’s horse, the hero petitions the heavenly power—often Ukko in the broader tradition—for snow or ice to subdue the animal. Hiisi himself neither meets nor contests Ukko in the episode. **[Later canonical adjacency; no direct interaction]**
- **Hiisi — Louhi.** Louhi requires Lemminkäinen to capture Hiisi’s elk and horse. The text does not show Louhi negotiating with Hiisi, and it does not establish that they are kin or allies. **[Later canonical indirect relation]**
- **Hiisi — Tuoni.** The bridal-task sequence moves from Hiisi’s animals to Tuoni’s river and swan, but the beings do not interact. **[Lönnrotian narrative adjacency only]**
- **Hiisi — Väinämöinen.** In *Kalevala* 17, Antero Vipunen calls the tormenting Väinämöinen a *hiisi* or “hound of Hiisi,” while Väinämöinen calls Vipunen’s interior Hiisi’s stable. These are insults/metaphors, not a meeting with Hiisi. **[Later canonical figurative usage]**
- **Hiisi — Antero Vipunen.** “Hiisi’s stable” is Väinämöinen’s abusive description of Vipunen’s belly. No genealogy or alliance is established. **[Later canonical metaphor]**
- **Hiisi — Sámi gods represented on drums.** No named interaction is documented. **[Absence of evidence]**

Lemminkäinen is the principal character who confronts Hiisi’s property, but he is a heroic human or culture-hero rather than securely a god in the source tradition. His encounters are treated in the myths below.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The Elk of Hiisi

#### Kalevala version

Louhi refuses Lemminkäinen her daughter unless he completes dangerous tasks. First he must ski down the Elk of Hiisi. Hiisi’s folk construct the creature from forest matter: its head, horns, limbs, ears, hide, eyes, and other parts are assembled from stumps, branches, reeds, bark, flowers, and similar materials. Hiisi directs the elk to run so violently that it breaks vessels, overturns domestic order, alarms women and children, and escapes across an enormous landscape.

Lemminkäinen boasts that no elk can escape him. His equipment fails and the chase exhausts him, dramatizing the danger of boastfulness and deficient ritual preparation. He then invokes forest powers more carefully, approaches the elk, and captures it.

**Earliest source status:** The fully integrated episode is Lönnrot’s literary composition, first in *Vanha Kalevala* 7 (1835) and expanded as *Kalevala* 13 (1849).  
**Oral basis:** Numerous SKVR elk-skiing poems, chiefly from Karelia and eastern Finland, collected in varying forms.

#### Variants

- **White and Olonets Karelia:** the skier may be Lystikki, Lysmetti, Lyylikki, or Lemminkäinen.
- **North Karelia:** the hunter may be *Laurinpoika lappalainen*, “the Sámi son of Lauri”; in one form the sequence ends with a squirrel rather than the canonical elk capture.
- **Kainuu:** the poem may emphasize the elk’s construction and devastation without naming the hunter.
- **Other variants:** Väinämöinen may perform the hunt.
- **North Savo and North Ostrobothnia:** recorded poems explicitly say Hiisi created elk/moose.
- **Material of the body:** details vary considerably. The elaborate catalogue is formulaic and not evidence for a fixed zoological doctrine.

#### Interpretation

The chase can be read as hunting poetry, a contest with the wild forest’s dangerous owner, a bridal-task tale, or a performance about controlling game and human overconfidence. Lönnrot made it an episode in Lemminkäinen’s biography. No evidence establishes a historical annual ritual reenactment of the full Kalevala story.

### Hiisi’s horse

After the elk, Lemminkäinen must bridle a great, fire-maned or foam-mouthed horse on Hiisi’s meadow. He asks the sky for snow, sleet, or ice to cool or restrain it, fits the golden bridle and silver bit, mounts it, and returns to Pohjola.

**[Later canonical]** *Vanha Kalevala* 7 and *Kalevala* 14.  
**[Regional folklore]** In the oral source cited by Open Kalevala, after Lemminkäinen skis down the elk he is told to “bridle the great gelding from the end of Hiisi’s meadows.”  
**Variation:** Hiisi’s horse also appears independently in charms, travel formulas, and cosmogonic or heroic poetry. Sometimes it is an excellent supernatural mount, demonstrating that “of Hiisi” need not always mean morally evil.

### Origin of the snake

Serpent-origin charms were recited in the treatment of snakebite. Versions construct the snake from spit, foam, thread, hair, grass, or substances produced by hostile beings. In a well-known recorded sequence, Hiisi runs or sleeps and produces foam or saliva; Syöjätär consumes or spits it out; teeth or other components are gathered; and the incipient snake is animated.

**[Folklore]** The evidence consists of multiple charm variants rather than one canonical narrative.  
**[Lönnrotian publication]** *Suomen kansan muinaisia loitsurunoja* (1880) supplies an influential edited text.  
**Function:** The *synty*, or origin formula, gives the healer verbal mastery by identifying the injury’s beginning. It should not be treated as a universal Finnish creation myth.

### Hiisi as builder and landscape-maker

Legends attribute erratic boulders, cairns, potholes, stone ridges, fortifications, and churches or attempted churches to Hiisi, *hiidet*, or giants. Common patterns include:

- a giant throws a stone at a church but misses;
- stones spill from an apron, sack, or burden;
- a supernatural builder abandons a structure when church bells ring;
- a giant creates a pothole, fireplace, stove, bed, or footprint;
- Hiisi’s ancient constructions mark a former non-Christian population.

**[Folklore; Christian-period]** These tales explain unfamiliar remains and dramatize the replacement of an imagined giant/pagan order by Christianity. They cannot date the physical monuments.

### Processions and the cultivated-field boundary

Later belief describes *hiidet* as a noisy migrating company. A person threatened by them can escape into a cultivated field or other ordered human space.

**[Folklore]** This opposition—wild/uncultivated versus cultivated/consecrated—fits the late hostile meaning. It may preserve spatial classification, but deriving it directly from Iron Age ritual is a scholarly inference.

## 6. Cult, sites, and ritual practice

### Was Hiisi worshipped as a god?

Agricola’s catalogue says Hiisi granted victory, which supports the existence of religious expectations directed toward Hiisi or a Hiisi power. Place-name and folklore evidence supports cultic uses of some *hiisi* places. Neither source demonstrates a centralized “Cult of the God Hiisi,” a dedicated priesthood, or a standard temple.

### Hiisi sites

Proposed indicators include:

- a *Hiisi-/Hiite-* place-name;
- location near an old settlement or route;
- elevated, rocky, or otherwise conspicuous terrain;
- nearby Iron Age cemetery;
- sacred tree, spring, cup-marked stone, or offering tradition;
- later memory of healing, sacrifice, taboo, or assembly.

A. M. Tallgren in the early twentieth century and Mauno Koski in the 1960s attempted systematic identification. Wessman warns that correlations vary and that a place-name cannot by itself determine site function.

At Hiidenmäki sites, archaeology sometimes finds Iron Age activity and later healing traditions, but conclusions remain site-specific. The modern return of neopagan visitors is a new phase, not proof of uninterrupted worship.

### Groves and Christian seizure

A papal bull of Gregory IX dated 1229 permitted the Church in Finland to take possession of pagan *luci et delubra*, “groves and shrines,” and convert or replace them with Christian institutions.

**[Medieval attested]** This is evidence for suppression and appropriation of non-Christian sacred places.  
**[Not established]** The Latin bull does not call these places *hiisi*. Connecting every confiscated grove with a named Hiisi cult goes beyond the document.

### Offerings and sacrifice

Animal products, food, drink, coins, metal objects, and ritual attention to springs, trees, stones, cemeteries, and hunting sites are documented broadly in Finnish and Karelian folk religion. Some *hiisi*-named sites are associated with offerings.

**Human sacrifice:** No secure archaeological or textual evidence establishes that humans were sacrificed to Hiisi. Later giant legends, graves, anomalous bones, or the word “sacrifice” cannot supply that evidence. Claims of a regular Hiisi human-sacrifice cult are **[Unestablished or modern invention]**.

**Temple prostitution:** No evidence associates it with Hiisi or the relevant Finnic sacred sites.

### Priesthood

No distinct priesthood of Hiisi is documented. Ritual specialists such as the *tietäjä* used charms containing Hiisi’s name, usually to command, threaten, diagnose, redirect, or expel dangerous powers. This is not equivalent to serving as Hiisi’s priest.

### Hiidenkiuas

A *hiidenkiuas* is an archaeological cairn type only by later nomenclature. Some cairns are graves, and some Hiisi place-names lie near cemeteries, but the compound does not prove that Bronze Age cairns were altars to Hiisi. “Hiisi’s stove” belongs principally to the later giant-builder explanatory layer.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Proto-Finnic and pre-literary period

**[Linguistic reconstruction]** Cognate Finnic forms and the Estonian sacral meaning indicate that *hiisi* predates the Finnish written record. The safest reconstructed semantic field is a specially bounded, powerful place; “grove” and “burial/cult place” are plausible narrower realizations.

There is no reconstructable biography, icon, genealogy, or centralized doctrine from this period.

### Iron Age to medieval conversion

**[Archaeological inference]** Some *hiisi* names coincide with cemeteries, old settlement centers, springs, sacred trees, and cup-marked stones. These correlations support cult-place interpretations at individual sites.

**[Medieval attested]** From the twelfth and thirteenth centuries onward, Swedish political expansion, ecclesiastical organization, parish formation, burial in churchyards, and seizure or destruction of non-Christian sites transformed Finland’s ritual landscape. The 1229 bull is explicit evidence of institutional appropriation.

**[Scholarly model]** As formerly valued ritual localities lost official legitimacy, *hiisi* moved toward the uncontrolled exterior—forest, hill, waste, dangerous place—and its inhabitants toward demons or giants. This was a gradual semantic and social process, not a securely documented one-time renaming campaign.

### Sixteenth century

Agricola’s 1551 catalogue simultaneously preserves Hiisi and condemns the religious system in which it belonged. Hiisi remains forest-associated and efficacious; Agricola does not yet describe a goblin.

### Seventeenth and eighteenth centuries

Clerical and antiquarian authors recorded remnants of local religion through Christian categories. Ganander’s 1789 synthesis gives Hiisi an increasingly demonological and mythographic personality.

Sámi missionary materials from the same broad era document violent and administrative suppression of drums, *sieidi*, and noaidi practices. That history is relevant comparatively but cannot be reassigned to Hiisi without evidence.

### Nineteenth century: personification and canonization

Romantic nationalism, folklore collecting, comparative mythology, and Lönnrot’s editorial labor made Hiisi a literary proper name. The *Kalevala* gave the figure:

- a domain, Hiitola;
- a folk or workforce;
- an elk and horse;
- a coherent place in Lemminkäinen’s adventures;
- a public role in Finnish national culture.

This is not wholesale invention—its elements occur in oral poetry—but their canonical integration is Lönnrot’s.

### Twentieth-century scholarship

Tallgren emphasized archaeological sites; Koski undertook the principal semantic and toponymic study; Haavio incorporated Hiisi into comparative Finnish mythology; Pentikäinen examined death culture and northern religion; Anttonen reframed sacredness through territorial boundaries; Siikala situated Hiisi within the performance world of Finnic oral mythology. Wessman tested cemetery claims against archaeological distributions.

The cumulative result has been to weaken the popular picture of an originally anthropomorphic evil god and strengthen the historical sequence place → place-power/inhabitant → demon/giant/literary character.

### Twenty-first century

Hiisi now operates simultaneously as:

- an archaic word and mild profanity;
- a reconstructed sacred place;
- a heritage-protection category;
- a neopagan religious concept;
- a goblin term in translated fantasy;
- an enemy category in games;
- a name in music, businesses, products, and astronomy.

These are different receptions, not evidence for a continuous unaltered cult.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### National Romanticism and art

The *Kalevala* made the Elk of Hiisi and Hiisi’s horse available to painters, illustrators, composers, and schools as national mythology. Visual depictions reflect the 1849 epic and contemporary artistic conventions; they are not copies of prehistoric cult art.

### Tolkien and fantasy translation

Finnish translations have used **hiisi** for English **goblin**, notably in Tolkien-related translation. Finnish **örkki** became the usual rendering of *orc*. This domestication strongly shaped the modern small-humanoid image of a *hiisi*.

**[Modern invention/reception]** A Tolkien-style goblin is not an ancient standardized Hiisi form. Finnish library guidance explicitly notes that goblin has no exact Finnish counterpart and has also been translated as *peikko*, *menninkäinen*, or *peikkolainen*.

### Games

Nolla Games’ *Noita* uses **Hiisi** for a technologically equipped hostile faction. The game borrows Finnish mythological vocabulary but invents its weapons, social organization, and underground behavior.

### Astronomy

In the IAU’s 2019 NameExoWorlds campaign, Finland named exoplanet **HAT-P-38 b “Hiisi”** and its host star **Horna**. The IAU glosses Hiisi as representing sacred localities and later evil spirits in Finnic mythology. This is an official modern astronomical name, not a traditional celestial association.

### Modern native faith

Finnish native-faith organizations founded in the early twenty-first century regard unspoiled woods, springs, rocks, ancestors, and place spirits as sacred. Taivaannaula has promoted inventory and protection of *hiisi* sites; *Hiisien päivä*, a day honoring sacred sites, is a modern observance. Karhun kansa received official religious-community status in Finland in 2014.

**[Modern revival]** Calling Hiisi “the spirit of holy places” deliberately foregrounds the reconstructed sacral meaning. It should not be presented as an unchanged creed continuously documented since the Iron Age.

### Occult and esoteric claims

The dedicated search found no evidence that Hiisi had a substantial role in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, classical ceremonial magic, Theosophy, or early modern European grimoires. Contemporary individual practitioners may place Hiisi in eclectic paganism, chaos magic, or online occult systems, but planetary seals, tarot correspondences, fixed colors, and demonological ranks are **[Modern invention]** unless a specific modern author is cited.

### Politics and nationalism

The *Kalevala* as a whole became central to nineteenth- and twentieth-century Finnish national identity. Hiisi participated indirectly through the epic and landscape vocabulary. Evidence does not support describing Hiisi itself as a major independent emblem of a historical extremist movement. Modern ethnonationalist appropriation of “native faith” should be evaluated organization by organization; it must not be assumed from religious revival alone.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Is Hiisi originally a god, a place, or a dead-person power?

Agricola’s grammar personifies Hiisi as an agent that “gave” victory. Koski’s linguistic evidence points toward a place-word. The conflict may be partly false: a sacral place, its indwelling force, its owner, and its collective inhabitants need not have been rigidly separated in oral religion.

The safest formulation is:

- “Cult/sacred place” is the strongest comparative lexical reconstruction.
- Agricola securely attests a named agent or power in 1551.
- Later sources increasingly anthropomorphize and demonize it.
- An original, singular “god Hiisi” with a fixed personality cannot be demonstrated.

### Grove versus cemetery

**Grove model:** supported by Estonian *hiis*, later descriptions, sacred-tree traditions, and translations of non-Christian sanctuaries.

**Cemetery model:** supported by some western Finnish place-name distributions and proximity to Iron Age burial grounds.

**Objection:** not all *hiisi* sites are cemeteries. Some appear connected to fertility, assembly, hunting, healing, springs, or territorial centrality. Agricola reportedly used *hiisi* in translating a biblical pagan grove, which need not be funerary.

The current defensible position is functional plurality: a *hiisi* was a specially classified place whose local function could include burial, sacrifice, assembly, resource regulation, or other ritual activity.

### Was Christian demonization deliberate?

The semantic development is clear; the mechanism is less so. Church seizure of pagan sites, polemical classification, relocation of burial, and equation with devil-terms all contributed. The popular statement “missionaries chose Hiisi in particular because he was the chief pagan god” comes from older comparative speculation and is not directly documented.

### The Sámi hypothesis

Similarity among *hiisi*, *siida*, and *sieidi* generated a long scholarly debate. Modern treatments emphasize phonological difficulties. Even if borrowing or distant relationship were eventually demonstrated, lexical relationship would not prove a shared deity.

The Sámi drum material is a warning against retrospective system-building. Rydving shows that Manker sometimes replaced indigenous or contemporary explanations with supposedly more “authentic” religious interpretations. Hiisi is absent from secure owner-explained drum iconography.

### Kalevala versus oral tradition

Earlier scholarship often cited the *Kalevala* as though it directly transmitted an ancient national scripture. Current source criticism separates:

- singers’ recorded poems;
- collectors’ transcription and normalization;
- Lönnrot’s selection;
- his recombination of geographically separate variants;
- newly composed transitions;
- later national reception.

Hiisi’s elk and horse are genuinely rooted in oral poetry, while their position in a continuous Lemminkäinen courtship cycle is largely editorial.

### Human sacrifice and sensational claims

Searches produced no primary or archaeological evidence for human sacrifice to Hiisi. Claims that a particular cairn, cemetery, or sacred grove proves it confuse burial, offering, legend, and sacrifice. Likewise, there is no evidence for temple prostitution, an organized demonic hierarchy, or a Hiisi “black mass.”

### State of the evidence

- Agricola’s *Dauidin Psaltari* survives in early printed copies and digital facsimiles.
- Ganander’s 1789 work is digitized.
- The Finnish Literature Society preserves and publishes the SKVR materials.
- Archaeological records are distributed among Finland’s heritage authorities, museums, and research publications.
- Oral poems were recorded centuries after conversion, and much earlier performance history is unrecoverable.
- No cult statue, temple inscription, or indigenous doctrinal text names Hiisi.
