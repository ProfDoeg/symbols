# Pomona: Research Dossier

**Class:** Roman/Latin goddess (*dea*); in Ovid also a Latin nymph or hamadryad.  
**Earliest secure attestation:** Pomona is securely named in Augustan literature: Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* 14.623–771, probably completed about 8 CE. The cult institutions attributed to her—the *flamen Pomonalis* and the suburban sanctuary called the *Pomonal*—may be substantially older, but they are known from later antiquarian testimony and cannot themselves be dated securely to Rome’s regal period.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Pomona is the specifically Roman or Latin goddess of *poma*: cultivated tree-fruit and, by extension, fruit trees, orchards, grafting, pruning, and the human management of arboreal fertility. She is not a general grain or field goddess equivalent to Ceres, nor simply an “apple goddess.” Latin *pomum* denotes orchard fruit broadly—apples, pears, figs, nuts, dates, and similar produce—and sometimes the fruit tree itself. Her best-attested institutional features are a specialized priest, the *flamen Pomonalis*, and a sacred place outside Rome called the *Pomonal*. Her only extensive surviving myth is Ovid’s Augustan courtship narrative involving Vertumnus. The disparity is important: the cult appears old and narrowly functional, whereas Pomona’s familiar personality, secluded garden, pruning knife, numerous suitors, and eventual union with Vertumnus come overwhelmingly from one sophisticated literary text.

### Name

**Pōmōna** is Latin, conventionally written **POMONA** in Roman capitals and pronounced approximately /poːˈmoː.na/. Augustine preserves the transparent Roman explanation: the goddess’s name was formed from *pomum*, as Bellona from *bellum* and Segetia from *seges* (*De civitate Dei* 4.24). His purpose is polemical, but the linguistic observation is sound.

The deeper etymology of *pomum* remains disputed:

- **Secure:** *Pomona* belongs to the Latin *pomum/pomus* word-family, “orchard fruit/fruit tree.”
- **Modern linguistic reconstruction:** Michiel de Vaan cautiously proposes Proto-Italic formations such as *po-emo-* or *po-omo-*, perhaps “what is taken off/picked,” while allowing that the word’s ultimate origin is uncertain.
- **Italic comparison:** Umbrian **Puemune Puprike**, “Public Pomonus/Pomo,” occurs in the Iguvine Tables. The similarity supports a shared Italic divine-name complex, but it does not prove that Roman Pomona was borrowed directly from Umbria or that the two cults were identical.
- **Older scholarly hypothesis:** Some nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century writers posited a lost masculine Roman *Pomonus* or a male-female divine pair. The Umbrian evidence makes such comparison possible, not certain.
- **Etruscan origin:** No securely identified Etruscan inscription or mirror names Pomona. Claims that she was straightforwardly an “Etruscan fertility deity” exceed the evidence. Etruscan mirrors illuminate broader Italic mythic exchange but do not establish an Etruscan Pomona.

### Cult title and related nouns

- **Flamen Pomonalis** — “flamen of Pomona.” Festus says that among fifteen flamines the Dialis possessed the greatest dignity and the Pomonalis the least because Pomona presided over the lightest or least weighty produce of the fields, namely *poma*. “Least” describes ceremonial rank, not proof of popular insignificance.
- **Pomonal** (*Pōmōnal*) — the sacred place or sanctuary of Pomona in the *ager Solonius* off the Via Ostiensis.
- **Pomonis** — a genitive form appearing in the phrase *ad exornandam aedem Pomonis*, “for adorning the temple of Pomona,” in CIL X 531 from Salerno.
- **Patrona pomorum**, “mistress/patroness of fruits,” is a descriptive learned formulation, not a securely documented ancient cult title.
- No ancient evidence securely gives her a standardized sequence of formal epithets.

### Classification changes

- **Roman cult:** specialized goddess presiding over orchard fruit.
- **Ovid, c. 8 CE:** simultaneously a goddess and the most accomplished of the Latin hamadryads, imagined as a secluded horticultural nymph.
- **Late antiquarian and Christian discourse:** one of Rome’s many narrowly departmental deities.
- **Renaissance onward:** allegorical goddess of orchards, autumn, abundance, horticulture, and sometimes female fertility or chastity.
- **Modern Paganism:** a revived personal deity of fruit trees, gardening, harvest, and ecological cultivation. These devotional systems are modern reconstructions, not demonstrable continuations of an ancient Pomona liturgy.

### Equations and false equations

Pomona has **no securely attested Greek counterpart** and no ancient *interpretatio graeca* identifying her with Demeter, Persephone, Aphrodite, or another major Greek goddess. Opora, the Greek personification of late summer or ripe fruit, is a functional comparison, not a documented ancient equation. Modern identifications with Demeter/Ceres, Flora, Ops, “the Mother Goddess,” or an Etruscan universal fertility goddess flatten distinct divine portfolios.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### What counts as the earliest evidence?

The evidence must be separated into three layers.

1. **Possible archaic institution, preserved late.** The *flamen Pomonalis* belongs to the group of minor flamines traditionally regarded as archaic. Its existence strongly suggests that Pomona’s public cult antedated the Empire, possibly by centuries. No contemporary regal- or early-Republican inscription names the office, however. Attribution of all fifteen flamines to Numa is Roman foundation tradition, not securely dated archival evidence.

2. **Augustan antiquarian evidence.** Verrius Flaccus, writing under Augustus, probably supplied material later excerpted by Festus and Paul the Deacon concerning the priest and Pomonal. Because Verrius’s original text is lost, the surviving physical witnesses are medieval manuscripts of later epitomes.

3. **Earliest surviving explicit literary portrayal.** Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* 14.623–771, completed around 8 CE, is the earliest securely dated, directly surviving extended text that names and characterizes Pomona. It proves that an Augustan audience could recognize her as a Latin orchard divinity; it does not prove that her love story was ancient cult myth.

### Principal ancient texts

#### Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.623–771

**Language and date:** Latin; composed approximately 1–8 CE. The work survives through medieval manuscript transmission, not an authorial papyrus.

**Content:** Under the legendary Alban king Proca, Pomona is the most skilled of the Latin hamadryads. Her name derives from her devotion to fruit trees. She prefers cultivated countryside and fruit-bearing branches to wild woods and rivers. With a curved pruning knife she trims growth, grafts shoots, and waters roots. She encloses her orchard against male intrusion. Satyrs, Pans, Silvanus, Priapus, Picus, and Vertumnus desire her. Vertumnus enters in multiple disguises and finally as an old woman; he praises a vine embracing an elm, recommends himself as husband, and tells the cautionary story of Iphis and Anaxarete. He then reveals his youthful form. Pomona is moved by his beauty, and their desire becomes mutual.

**Historical assessment:** [Later canonical elaboration.] It is the canonical source for Pomona’s modern narrative identity but probably not a transcript of cult legend. The Oxford Classical Dictionary judges the tale disconnected from known cult and possibly invented by Ovid or a comparatively recent predecessor. Its language of grafting and pruning belongs to Roman horticultural expertise, while its embedded story and gendered rhetoric serve Ovid’s literary architecture.

Ovid’s conclusion does **not** explicitly narrate a wedding ceremony, children, or an annually celebrated joint festival. “Pomona married Vertumnus” is a reasonable later summary, not the exact extent of Ovid’s text.

#### Ovid, *Fasti*

**Language and date:** Latin; begun under Augustus and revised in exile after 8 CE.

Pomona is not assigned a secure festival in the surviving six books. The absence matters. Claims that Ovid documents a Pomona festival on 13 August or 1 November are not borne out by the surviving *Fasti*.

#### Festus, *De verborum significatu*

**Language and transmission:** Latin; second century CE epitome of Verrius Flaccus’s late-first-century BCE/early-first-century CE lexicon, itself later abbreviated by Paul the Deacon in the eighth century. The principal Festus manuscript is the damaged eleventh-century Codex Farnesianus.

Festus supplies two indispensable notices:

- *Maximae dignationis flamen Dialis est inter quindecim flamines … minimi habetur Pomonalis, quod Pomona levissimo fructui agrorum praesidet, id est pomis*: among the fifteen flamines the Dialis ranks highest and the Pomonalis lowest because Pomona presides over the least weighty produce, orchard fruits.
- *Pomonal est in agro Solonio, via Ostiensi …*: the Pomonal lay in the *ager Solonius*, reached from the Via Ostiensis. The damaged mileage has produced slightly different modern topographical interpretations; the traditional placement is near the twelfth milestone, with a turning from about the eighth.

These notices securely attest what Roman antiquarians believed about the cult’s institutions. They do not date the creation of either institution.

#### Pliny the Elder, *Natural History* 23.1

**Language and date:** Latin; completed by 77 CE.

Opening his survey of medicines derived from cultivated trees, Pliny personifies orchard nature: *non cessit iis Pomona partesque medicas et pendentibus dedit*—“Pomona did not yield to them and gave medicinal properties also to hanging fruits.” This is literary personification grounded in her recognized domain, not a ritual prescription.

#### Servius, commentary on *Aeneid* 7.190

**Language and date:** Latin; late fourth or early fifth century CE, with later additions in the expanded Servian tradition.

Servius reports: *Picum amavit Pomona, pomorum dea, et eius volentis est sortita coniugium*—“Pomona, goddess of fruits, loved Picus and, with his consent, obtained marriage with him.” Circe, rejected by Picus, transforms him into the Martian woodpecker. This conflicts with Ovid, who makes Canens the wife of Picus and Pomona a later figure courted by Vertumnus.

**Assessment:** [Late antiquarian variant.] It may preserve a lost mythographic or pontifical tradition, but its date and relationship to Ovid cannot be established.

#### Augustine, *De civitate Dei* 4.24 and 4.34

**Language and date:** Latin; written 413–426 CE.

Augustine says Romans derived divine names from the things believed to be gifts: *a pomis Pomonam, non pomum*—“from fruits, Pomona, not *pomum*.” Elsewhere he contrasts Jewish receipt of fruits without Pomona with Roman polytheism. Augustine is a hostile Christian witness, but his polemic independently shows that Pomona still functioned in late-antique learned accounts of Roman religion.

His discussion draws broadly on Roman antiquarian theology, especially Varro, whose *Antiquitates rerum divinarum* is lost. It is unsafe to attribute every Pomona sentence verbatim to Varro.

#### Arnobius, *Adversus nationes* 3

**Language and date:** Latin; early fourth century CE.

Arnobius lists **Pomana** among Roman divine names while attacking the multiplication of deities and the divine personification of natural functions. The spelling may reflect textual transmission or a variant formation. Like Augustine, Arnobius is hostile but valuable because polemicists preserved names and classifications from pagan sources now lost.

#### Ausonius, *Eclogues* on the months

**Language and date:** Latin; later fourth century CE.

Ausonius associates Pomona with September because fruits ripen then. This is [late learned systematization], not evidence that September was her ancient festival month.

#### Livy, Virgil, Cicero, and Macrobius

These authors are essential for Roman religion and neighboring figures, but no secure Pomona episode should be manufactured from their silence:

- Virgil’s *Aeneid* supplies Picus, Circe, and Latin dynastic context, but not the Pomona marriage; that comes from Servius’s commentary.
- Livy identifies the *ager Solonius* in Roman territorial history but does not provide a Pomona narrative.
- Cicero discusses the Solonian district and Roman theological naming, but there is no major surviving Pomona passage comparable to Ovid or Festus.
- Macrobius is useful for Roman calendars and late-antique antiquarianism but does not securely establish a Pomona festival.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**Secure core:** orchard fruit, fruit trees, and cultivated arboreal productivity.

Ovid specifies the practical arts of arboriculture:

- pruning excessive growth;
- cutting misdirected branches;
- splitting bark and inserting grafts;
- watering dry roots;
- protecting an enclosed orchard.

Pomona’s domain is therefore not undifferentiated “nature.” Ovid contrasts her love of cultivated land and fruit-bearing boughs with wild forests and streams. This makes her an unusually precise divinity of controlled cultivation.

**Extensions in later reception:** abundance, plenty, autumn, harvest, garden design, female fertility, domestic prosperity, and horticultural science. These are reasonable symbolic developments, but not all are ancient cult functions.

### Attributes

- **Curved pruning knife or billhook:** directly attested in Ovid; her most diagnostic narrative attribute.
- **Fruit, fruit basket, cornucopia, apple, pear, grapes:** common in postclassical art. The isolated apple became dominant partly because European descendants of *pomum* narrowed toward “apple.”
- **Orchard, grafted tree, vine and elm:** Ovidian setting and symbols. The vine embracing the elm is Vertumnus’s exemplum for partnership.
- **Walled or enclosed garden:** Ovidian; later artists treated it as a symbol of virginity or guarded chastity.
- **Autumnal colors, September, harvest garlands:** late antique or modern extrapolations.
- **Celestial body, sacred number, weekday, or canonical color:** no secure ancient evidence.

### Ancient images

The ancient iconographic record is weak. No surviving archaic or Republican image can be securely identified by inscription as Pomona. Generic female figures bearing fruit, cornucopiae, or apples may instead represent Abundantia, Fortuna, Ceres, Flora, a nymph, a season, a province, or a mortal assimilated to divine imagery. The older *Dictionnaire des Antiquités Grecques et Romaines* accordingly observed that no representation then known could be identified with certainty.

A frequently reproduced late-second-century female statue labeled “Pomona” illustrates the problem: identification often rests on restored or associated fruit attributes, not an ancient name inscription. LIMC-style iconographic cataloguing is indispensable, but labels of convenience must not be turned into secure cult evidence.

### Postclassical iconography

Renaissance and Baroque art created the recognizable visual Pomona:

- young, often nude or loosely draped;
- pruning knife in hand;
- surrounded by apples, grapes, and garden produce;
- accompanied by Vertumnus disguised as an old woman;
- placed beneath a fruit tree or beside the emblematic vine and elm.

Hendrick Goltzius’s 1613 *Vertumnus and Pomona* preserves the pruning knife and emphasizes the erotic ambiguity of the disguised suitor. Peter Paul Rubens’s 1636–37 treatment shows Vertumnus after disclosure, while Pomona’s sickle identifies her horticultural domain. Such paintings are evidence for early-modern reception, not ancient iconography.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No surviving ancient source securely names Pomona’s parents, siblings, or offspring. Describing her as a “daughter of Ceres,” “daughter of Flora,” or member of a universal Mother-Goddess lineage is modern invention unless a specific modern creative work is being cited.

Ovid calls her one of the Latin hamadryads but does not give a family tree. Her place in Roman hierarchy is institutional rather than genealogical: her specialized flamen places her among individually served public deities, although Festus ranks that priest last among the fifteen.

### Consorts and competing traditions

- **Vertumnus:** Ovid makes him her successful suitor; later reception commonly calls them spouses.
- **Picus:** Servius calls Pomona the willing wife of Picus.
- **Canens:** Ovid instead makes Canens Picus’s wife. This produces incompatible literary genealogies; they should not be harmonized.
- **No offspring:** none securely attested from either union.

### Every documented divine interaction

- **Vertumnus — courtship through disguises; entry as an old woman; self-recommendation; revelation and mutual desire.** Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.641–771, c. 8 CE. **Tier:** later canonical elaboration, probably Augustan literary myth rather than cult narrative.
- **Picus — one of Pomona’s rejected rustic suitors.** Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.320–396 and 14.623–642 by cross-reference, c. 8 CE. **Tier:** Augustan literary tradition.
- **Picus — Pomona loves and marries him willingly.** Servius on *Aeneid* 7.190, late fourth/early fifth century CE. **Tier:** late-antique regional or mythographic variant.
- **Circe — indirect rival within Servius’s Picus story: Circe desires Pomona’s husband and transforms him after rejection.** Servius on *Aeneid* 7.190. **Tier:** late-antique variant. No direct meeting or conversation between Circe and Pomona is recorded there.
- **Silvanus — unsuccessful pursuit.** Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.639, c. 8 CE. **Tier:** Augustan literary elaboration.
- **Priapus — unsuccessful pursuit.** Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.640, c. 8 CE. **Tier:** Augustan literary elaboration.
- **Pans — collective unsuccessful pursuit.** Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.638–639. **Tier:** Augustan literary elaboration.
- **Satyrs — collective unsuccessful pursuit.** Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.637–638. **Tier:** Augustan literary elaboration.
- **Iphis and Anaxarete — no direct interaction.** Vertumnus, disguised as an old woman, narrates their Cypriot tragedy to Pomona as persuasive warning. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.698–764. **Tier:** embedded Augustan literary exemplum.
- **Venus — no direct encounter.** In Vertumnus’s embedded story, Anaxarete is punished through the power of a deity invoked in connection with rejected love; Pomona only hears the narrative. **Tier:** indirect literary relation.
- **Ceres, Flora, Ops, Demeter, Opora, Fortuna, Abundantia — no securely documented ancient personal interaction with Pomona.** Similar domains or later allegorical pairing do not create mythic encounters.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The courtship of Vertumnus

[Later canonical elaboration: Ovid, c. 8 CE.]

Pomona lives during the legendary reign of Proca, king of Alba Longa. Ovid thereby inserts her story into pre-Roman Latin chronology while telling it in the technical language of contemporary horticulture. She is unrivaled among Latin tree nymphs and loves only orchard work. Unlike a woodland nymph who inhabits untamed nature, she prunes, grafts, irrigates, and encloses.

Her enclosure is both horticultural protection and erotic boundary. Satyrs, Pans, Silvanus, Priapus, and Picus fail to enter. Vertumnus, whose own identity is associated with change and garden produce, repeatedly changes shape: reaper, haymaker, ploughman, orchard worker, soldier, fisherman, and finally an old woman. The disguises let him observe Pomona and make his divine mutability narratively useful.

As the old woman, he admires the fruit and kisses Pomona with more intensity than an elderly woman should. Pointing to a vine trained around an elm, he argues that neither is complete alone: the vine needs support and the elm adornment. He recommends Vertumnus as uniquely faithful, youthful, and sympathetic to agriculture. He also invokes the god’s capacity to punish rejection.

The disguised Vertumnus then tells of Iphis, a poor Cypriot youth who loves the aristocratic Anaxarete. She rejects him cruelly; he hangs himself at her doorway. When she coldly watches his funeral procession, she turns to stone. The inset tale threatens that contempt for a suitor can provoke divine or moral retribution.

Vertumnus abandons the disguise and appears in radiant youth, ready—Ovid says—to use force, though he does not need to: Pomona is struck by his beauty, and equal desire affects them both.

### Consent and coercion

The episode has generated sharply different readings:

- Ovid’s final grammar presents reciprocal desire, and no rape occurs.
- Vertumnus gains access through deception, kisses Pomona while disguised, threatens consequences through the Iphis story, and is described as prepared to resort to force.
- Early-modern art often eroticized the gap between the apparent old woman and concealed male lover.
- Modern retellings that make it an uncomplicated romantic marriage omit the story’s coercive apparatus.
- Conversely, saying Ovid explicitly narrates Pomona’s rape is inaccurate. The text makes the danger present but says force proved unnecessary.

### Pomona and Picus

[Late-antique variant: Servius.]

Servius makes Pomona, *pomorum dea*, the lover and consenting wife of Picus. Circe desires Picus; when rejected, she transforms him into the *picus Martius*, the woodpecker associated with Mars. In Ovid’s separate Picus narrative, however, Picus is husband to Canens. The Servian version may reflect a lost local, antiquarian, or mythographic source, but it cannot be combined with Ovid into a single orderly biography.

### Absence of other cycles

There is no surviving Roman creation myth, descent narrative, combat myth, divine trial, seasonal death-and-return cycle, or story of Pomona bearing children. Modern attempts to assimilate her to Persephone or construct a lost annual apple mystery are unsupported.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### The *flamen Pomonalis*

The clearest institutional evidence is Festus’s notice that Pomona possessed her own flamen. Roman flamines were priests attached to particular deities. The Pomonalis was one of the *flamines minores*, not one of the three great priests of Jupiter, Mars, and Quirinus.

What can be established:

- the title existed in Roman antiquarian tradition;
- it implies sacrifices or rites specific to Pomona;
- Festus placed it last in dignity among fifteen flamines;
- the office’s presence among obscure specialized priesthoods is often taken as evidence of antiquity.

What cannot be established:

- the name of any individual holder;
- the priest’s precise ritual calendar, costume beyond general flaminical practice, recruitment procedure, or privileges;
- that Numa personally created the office;
- continuous occupation of the office through the Empire.

Dumézil, Wissowa, Latte, and later historians regard such priesthoods as evidence for an early Roman system of sharply differentiated divine functions. That reconstruction is plausible but depends on late sources and should not be confused with a surviving archaic priestly archive.

### The Pomonal

Festus locates the **Pomonal** in the *ager Solonius* off the Via Ostiensis. The conventional reading places it around twelve Roman miles from Rome, reached by a branch road beginning near the eighth milestone. Filippo Coarelli has argued for reading the mileage as four miles along a diversion from the eighth milestone rather than simply at mile twelve of the main road.

The broader district lay between the Roman, Ostian, Ardeatine, and Lanuvian territorial worlds. Calling the Pomonal a “temple near Ostia” is too definite: *Pomonal* may mean sacred grove, precinct, or sanctuary, and no excavated building has been conclusively identified with it.

Its location fits the goddess’s character. Orchards require land, irrigation, access to markets, and long-term cultivation; a suburban or extra-urban sanctuary could serve growers in Rome’s agricultural hinterland.

### Salerno inscription: CIL X 531

An inscription reused at the archiepiscopal complex in Salerno records money or a bequest *ad exornandam aedem Pomonis*, “for adorning the temple of Pomona.” It is generally dated to the third or fourth century CE.

**Significance:** this is direct epigraphic evidence for an *aedes Pomonis* and for expenditure on its adornment outside Rome, showing that Pomona was not merely an Augustan poetic memory.

**Limits:** the inscription’s reuse and context complicate reconstruction of the original sanctuary. Later claims that Salerno Cathedral stands directly on a fully documented Temple of Pomona go beyond what the inscription alone proves.

### Festivals and calendars

No surviving Roman fasti securely assign Pomona an annual public festival. In particular:

- **13 August:** the Vertumnalia is associated with Vertumnus, not securely with a joint Pomona-Vertumnus festival.
- **September:** Ausonius’s late assignment of the month to Pomona is literary calendar symbolism; Wissowa’s suggestion of a movable festival tied to fruit ripening is a scholarly inference.
- **1 November:** no ancient Roman calendar attests a Pomona festival on this date.
- **Halloween:** the popular claim that Roman Pomona rites merged with Samhain to create Halloween is a modern diffusionist story lacking ancient or medieval documentary support.

### Sacrifice, offerings, and prayer

No surviving ancient ritual manual gives a prayer formula, sacrificial victim, procession, or votive recipe specifically for Pomona. Fruit offerings are intuitively appropriate but should be marked as reconstruction unless tied to a particular modern rite.

There is no evidence for human sacrifice, temple prostitution, ritual sexual violence, or cultic warfare in Pomona’s worship. Ovid’s sexual threat belongs to narrative, not cult practice.

### Politics and economics

Pomona’s cult protected a meaningful economic sector: orchards, grafted fruit trees, and suburban horticulture. Roman agronomists show the capital, time, labor, and expertise invested in orchards, although they do not make Pomona central to their manuals. The cult’s possession of a flamen indicates some public recognition. There is no evidence that her sanctuary became a major political treasury, oracle, pilgrimage center, or imperial cult institution.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Possible Italic background

The Umbrian Iguvine Tables, inscribed between approximately the third and first centuries BCE but containing older ritual material, invoke *Puemune Puprike*. Linguistic comparison with Pomona suggests a wider Italic complex of deities concerned with produce or fertility. Differences matter: the Umbrian figure is masculine and embedded in Iguvium’s civic ritual system. It is a cognate comparison, not proof of one pan-Italic cult.

### Archaic and Republican Rome

The minor flaminate and Pomonal are conventionally assigned to archaic Roman religion. This is a historical reconstruction from later evidence. There is no contemporary archaic inscription, temple foundation record, or named priest.

During the Republic, orchard cultivation expanded around Rome, but the surviving evidence does not show Pomona becoming a major state goddess. Her cult may have remained specialized and geographically local.

### Augustan period

The Augustan age transformed Pomona into a literary personality. Ovid joined an obscure but authentically Roman goddess to Vertumnus, a god of seasonal change and produce, and placed their story in Alban legendary time. This suited Augustan literary interest in Italian religious antiquity while allowing sophisticated exploration of desire, disguise, cultivation, and transformation.

### High Empire

Pliny could invoke “Pomona” as a transparent personification of the medicinal powers of fruit. The Salerno inscription, if correctly dated to the third/fourth century, shows material cult investment far from the Pomonal. Yet no broad imperial distribution comparable to Isis, Mithras, Jupiter Dolichenus, or the imperial cult is visible.

### Late antiquity

Servius preserves a competing Picus marriage. Ausonius associates Pomona with September. Arnobius and Augustine use her as an example of Roman functional polytheism. Their hostile testimony is historically useful but does not prove active mass worship in their own day.

Christian imperial legislation against sacrifice and temple cult in the late fourth and early fifth centuries supplied the general mechanism by which traditional public cult was suppressed. No edict or martyr narrative specifically records destruction of the Pomonal, execution of a Pomona priest, or forced conversion centered on her sanctuary.

### Middle Ages

No evidence demonstrates continuous worship. Pomona survived primarily through manuscripts of Ovid, mythography, grammar, agricultural vocabulary, and occasional learned geographical naming. The old designation “Pomona” for Mainland Orkney is an early-modern antiquarian error or learned geographical invention, not evidence of a northern cult.

### Renaissance and early modern Europe

Printing, translation, and illustration of Ovid created Pomona’s strongest afterlife. Artists favored the moment when Vertumnus, disguised as an old woman, gains access to the orchard. The subject allowed fruit still life, luxuriant gardens, the contrast of youth and age, erotic concealment, and moral allegory about courtship or marriage.

Pomona also became a suitable statue for orchards and formal gardens. This is the period when the fruit basket, cornucopia, and generalized “abundance” imagery became more decisive than ancient cult specificity.

### Modern revival

Contemporary Roman-reconstructionist and eclectic Pagan practitioners honor Pomona with orchard fruit, cider, pruning work, tree planting, and seasonal observances. These practices may be historically informed, but surviving ancient evidence is too sparse to reconstruct a complete Pomona liturgy. There are no reliable demographic figures for her present worship and no evidence of an unbroken priestly succession from antiquity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Literature and visual art

Pomona’s modern image is substantially an Ovidian and artistic creation.

Major examples include:

- Francesco Melzi, *Vertumnus and Pomona*, early sixteenth century;
- Jacopo Pontormo’s Medici-villa fresco cycle, traditionally identified with Vertumnus and Pomona;
- Hendrick Goltzius, *Vertumnus and Pomona* (1613);
- Juan van der Hamen, *Pomona and Vertumnus* (1626);
- Peter Paul Rubens, *Vertumnus and Pomona* (1636–37);
- Caesar van Everdingen, c. 1637–40;
- repeated treatments by Boucher in drawings, overdoors, and tapestry designs during the eighteenth century;
- a Boston workshop figure, *Plenty or Pomona*, c. 1790, showing her adaptation to prosperity and garden decoration.

The paintings frequently make Pomona nude and passive, even though Ovid initially defines her through skilled agricultural labor. They also convert a troubling deceptive courtship into an elegant pastoral romance.

### Marino Marini

Between the mid-1930s and about 1950, Marino Marini produced numerous sculptures and images entitled *Pomona*. His 1941 bronze in the Uffizi treats her as a monumental female embodiment of fertile, stable, agrarian nature. This is a modern classicizing reinvention, not an attempted archaeological reconstruction.

### Place names and institutions

Pomona became a durable name for fruit-growing settlements and institutions. Pomona, California, was named through the classical association with fruit cultivation; Pomona College retained the regional name. Such usages helped make “Pomona” shorthand for orchards even where no religious content was intended.

### Occultism and esotericism

A dedicated contested-material search finds no significant role for Pomona in the canonical ritual systems of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Thelema, or major Renaissance grimoires. Modern correspondence lists may assign her to earth, autumn, apples, Venus, abundance, or particular colors; these are modern occult taxonomies.

There is likewise no ancient basis for treating Pomona as:

- a triple goddess;
- a lunar goddess;
- a ruler of a “western watchtower”;
- a guardian of witches;
- a goddess of Halloween;
- a form of Persephone;
- an Etruscan Great Mother.

These are modern inventions or syncretistic devotional choices when they occur.

### Psychological readings

Modern readers have interpreted the walled orchard as psychic self-protection, Pomona as autonomous feminine creativity, Vertumnus as transformative desire, or grafting as integration of identities. Such Jungian or psychoanalytic approaches are interpretive traditions of the nineteenth through twenty-first centuries, not recoveries of ancient doctrine.

### Political appropriation

Pomona has not been a major emblem of modern extremist or nationalist religion. Italian classicism and agrarian imagery have occasionally used Roman fertility figures, but no well-documented mass political cult of Pomona compares with appropriations of Mars, Roma, or Nordic gods. Assertions of such an organized tradition require evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Antiquarian reconstruction

Georg Wissowa placed Pomona among old Roman functional divinities and treated the flaminate and Pomonal as evidence for archaic cult. His suggestion that her festival might have been movable according to fruit ripening explains the calendar’s silence but remains conjectural.

Hermann Usener’s category of *Sondergötter* and related scholarship encouraged interpretation of figures like Pomona as narrowly specialized “departmental gods.” The model is illuminating but can make Roman divine persons appear to be nothing more than abstract labels.

Georges Dumézil integrated the minor flamines into a reconstructed system of archaic Roman religion. His structural comparisons remain influential, but the exact antiquity and organization of the minor priesthoods cannot be independently verified from early documents.

Kurt Latte, John Scheid, and Beard–North–Price emphasize ritual institutions, civic location, and historical practice over smooth mythological biography. This approach is especially important for Pomona, whose cult evidence and literary personality belong to different evidentiary layers.

### Was Pomona originally male?

The Umbrian *Puemune* prompted theories of masculine *Pomo/Pomonus* preceding or accompanying feminine Pomona. The cognate is real enough to merit comparison. What remains unproved is a Roman sex change, a divine pair, or direct transmission from Iguvium. Grammatical and cultic gender differed among Italic communities.

### Was Ovid’s story traditional?

Evidence for a pre-Ovidian Vertumnus–Pomona romance is absent. Their complementary agricultural functions could have encouraged earlier association, but the detailed courtship, old-woman disguise, elm-and-vine speech, and Iphis-Anaxarete exemplum are first attested in Ovid. Modern scholarship therefore commonly regards the narrative as Ovid’s composition or recent literary tradition.

### Cult significance

The Pomonalis’s low rank has been read as proof of Pomona’s marginality. That goes too far. Rank within the flaminical order does not measure private devotion or economic relevance. Conversely, the existence of a dedicated flamen does not prove a populous or empire-wide cult.

### Iconographic uncertainty

The principal methodological problem is circular identification: an ancient woman holding fruit is labeled Pomona, then cited as proof of Pomona’s standard ancient fruit-bearing iconography. Without inscription, secure find context, or a uniquely Ovidian scene, such identifications remain tentative.

### Etruscan claims

Older Italic religion scholarship sometimes moved too readily among Latin, Umbrian, Sabellian, and Etruscan material. No inscribed Etruscan mirror securely names Pomona. The Etruscan hypothesis should therefore be labeled comparative reconstruction, not fact.

### Discredited popular claims

- **“Pomona had a Roman festival on 1 November.”** Unattested.
- **“Her feast merged with Samhain and produced Halloween apple-bobbing.”** Modern conjecture without a documented chain of transmission.
- **“Vertumnalia on 13 August was certainly their joint wedding festival.”** Not supported by surviving calendars.
- **“Every ancient fruit-bearing goddess statue is Pomona.”** Iconographically unsound.
- **“Pomona was simply the Roman Demeter.”** False equivalence.
- **“She was certainly an Etruscan goddess before becoming Roman.”** Unestablished.
- **“Ovid records an ancient sacred marriage ritual.”** No ritual connection is demonstrated.
- **“Pomona’s myth proves an ancient seasonal death-and-rebirth mystery.”** Modern comparativist construction.

## 10. Chronology

- **Possibly archaic period:** A specialized Pomona cult and minor flamen may emerge in Latium. **Evidence:** retrospective Festan antiquarian record; exact date unknown.
- **Third–first centuries BCE:** Iguvine Tables attest Umbrian *Puemune Puprike*, a masculine Italic cognate or comparison. They do not attest Roman Pomona.
- **Late first century BCE–early first century CE:** Verrius Flaccus compiles antiquarian material later preserved by Festus, probably including notices on the Pomonalis and Pomonal.
- **c. 1–8 CE:** Ovid composes *Metamorphoses* 14, the earliest securely dated surviving extended Pomona text and canonical source for Vertumnus’s courtship.
- **77 CE:** Pliny, *Natural History* 23.1, personifies Pomona as giver of medicinal virtues to hanging fruit.
- **Second century CE:** Festus epitomizes Verrius; surviving tradition explicitly records the Pomonalis and Pomonal.
- **Third–fourth century CE:** CIL X 531 at Salerno records provision *ad exornandam aedem Pomonis*.
- **Early fourth century CE:** Arnobius lists Pomana/Pomona while attacking Roman functional deities.
- **Later fourth century CE:** Ausonius associates Pomona with September’s ripening fruit.
- **Late fourth–early fifth century CE:** Servius records the competing tradition that Pomona married Picus.
- **413–426 CE:** Augustine discusses the derivation of Pomona’s name and uses her polemically as an example of Roman polytheism.
- **Late fourth–fifth centuries CE:** Imperial prohibition of sacrifice and temple cult provides the general context for the disappearance of public Pomona worship; no Pomona-specific suppression event survives.
- **Eighth century:** Paul the Deacon further epitomizes Festus, helping transmit Roman antiquarian notices into the Middle Ages.
- **Medieval period:** Pomona persists principally in manuscripts and mythography; continuous cult is unattested.
- **Early sixteenth century:** Melzi and Pontormo help establish Vertumnus and Pomona as a major Renaissance visual subject.
- **1613:** Goltzius paints *Vertumnus and Pomona*.
- **1626:** Juan van der Hamen paints his Ovidian garden scene.
- **1636–37:** Rubens depicts Vertumnus revealing himself to Pomona.
- **Seventeenth–eighteenth centuries:** the episode becomes widespread in European painting, sculpture, decorative arts, and tapestries.
- **c. 1790:** Boston woodcarvers produce *Plenty or Pomona*, adapting her to American prosperity imagery.
- **Nineteenth century:** Pomona becomes a common civic, agricultural, and commercial name in fruit-growing districts; comparative mythographers elaborate Italic origin theories.
- **1935–50:** Marino Marini develops his modern sculptural *Pomona* series.
- **Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries:** Roman reconstructionists and eclectic Pagans revive devotional practice; the unsupported Pomona–Samhain–Halloween theory circulates widely in popular media.
- **Present:** Pomona remains active principally in art history, classical reception, place names, horticultural symbolism, and small-scale revived religion rather than an institutionally continuous ancient cult.

## 11. Sources and evidentiary conclusions

Pomona’s record is small but unusually instructive. Three kinds of evidence must remain distinct:

- **Cultic-antiquarian:** the Pomonalis and Pomonal, preserved through Verrius–Festus, suggest an old and specialized Roman cult.
- **Epigraphic:** CIL X 531 demonstrates a temple and benefaction at Salerno in later antiquity.
- **Literary:** Ovid supplies almost everything recognizable as Pomona’s “mythology,” while Servius preserves an incompatible Picus variant.
- **Late polemical:** Arnobius and Augustine show how Christian intellectuals recast her as an example of excessively subdivided Roman divinity.
- **Visual:** secure ancient iconography is lacking; Renaissance and Baroque artists effectively created her standard image.
- **Modern devotional:** contemporary worship exists as revival and reconstruction, not documented survival.

No evidence supports a Pomona-centered mystery cult, human sacrifice, temple prostitution, an ancient Halloween festival, a canonical Greek equation, or a continuous post-Roman priesthood. The strongest reconstruction is therefore of an old Latin orchard goddess whose precise cult is largely lost, but whose sparse institutional traces were transformed by Ovid into one of Europe’s most durable myths of horticulture, guarded autonomy, disguise, persuasion, and desire.
