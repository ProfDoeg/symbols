# Silvanus: Research Dossier

**Class:** Roman male god (*deus*), principally of woodland, uncultivated and cultivated land, estates, boundaries, livestock, and rural labor; in provincial settings also a guardian of hunting, mining, military communities, health, and local landscapes.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Plautus’ comedy *Aulularia*, conventionally dated about 195–184 BCE, mentions a suburban shrine or grove of Silvanus (*lucus Silvani*). The earliest detailed ritual evidence is Cato, *De agricultura* 83, composed about 160 BCE, prescribing a daytime offering to **Mars Silvanus** for cattle. No securely identifiable archaic Roman temple, calendar entry, or inscription proves that Silvanus belonged to Rome’s earliest state cult.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early attested]** Republican evidence or evidence reasonably preserving a Republican practice.
- **[Imperial attested]** contemporary literary, epigraphic, archaeological, or visual evidence of the Roman imperial period.
- **[Late testimony]** late-antique testimony, sometimes preserving much earlier material.
- **[Regional variant]** a locally distinctive provincial form.
- **[Learned systematization]** an antiquarian, philosophical, poetic, or priestly ordering that need not describe ordinary cult.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** an ancient equation or fusion between Silvanus and another deity.
- **[Medieval/early-modern accretion]**, **[Folklore]**, or **[Modern invention]** as applicable.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** an inference rather than an explicit ancient statement.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

**[Early and imperial attested]** Silvanus is a Roman god of the *silva*—woodland or tree-covered ground—but his documented sphere is broader than “forest god.” Cato connects him with bovine health; Virgil calls him *agrestum pecorisque deus*, “god of fields and flocks” (*Aeneid* 8.600–601); agricultural, boundary, garden, estate, woodland, hunting, and protective functions recur in inscriptions and images. In the surviving imperial corpus he is normally a benign, bearded, human-shaped rural guardian, not the capricious or prophetic woodland being characteristic of literary Faunus.

**[Imperial attested]** More than 1,100 Latin inscriptions invoke Silvanus. This makes him one of the best-attested Roman gods epigraphically, despite his absence from the public Roman calendar and the absence of a demonstrable state priesthood or state temple. James Rives summarizes the central paradox: no public cult, but exceptional popularity in the western and Danubian provinces, with fewer than ten percent of identifiable dedicants belonging to the elite ([Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/550499810)).

### Name and spelling

- **Latin:** *Silvānus*; later and variant spelling *Sylvanus*. The spelling with *y* reflects learned Greek-influenced orthography, not a different ancient god.
- **Greek transcription:** normally Σιλουανός, *Silouanos*, in Greek imperial contexts.
- **Etruscan:** *Selvans*, with inflected forms such as *Selvansl*. The divine name occurs on the Piacenza Liver and in votive and boundary-related inscriptions.
- **Plural literary form:** *Silvani*, “Silvanuses” or woodland divinities, occurs in Latin poetry. This does not establish an organized class of gods equivalent to Greek satyrs.

### Etymology and the Etruscan problem

**[Ancient/modern standard explanation]** Romans naturally understood *Silvanus* from *silva*, “wood, woodland,” with the adjectival/divine suffix *-ānus*: approximately “the one belonging to the woods.” This interpretation governs Roman literary presentation and most modern accounts.

**[Disputed modern reconstruction]** Older scholarship sometimes derived Roman Silvanus from Etruscan Selvans. The evidence does not permit a simple one-directional derivation. Etruscan forms are early: *selvansel* has been dated approximately 475–450 BCE, earlier than surviving Latin attestations. Yet linguistic studies have also proposed that Etruscan borrowed the divine name from an Italic, possibly Umbrian, form. The formal resemblance is undeniable; whether Selvans is the source, recipient, cognate development, or product of sustained central-Italian exchange remains disputed. The responsible conclusion is historical contact, not automatic identity.

**[Regional Etruscan attested]** Etruscan Selvans was associated with open space and boundaries. *Selvans Tularias* is generally understood as “Selvans of the boundaries,” from Etruscan *tular*, boundary. The Piacenza Liver places Selvans in one sector of its divinatory cosmos. These data support comparison with Roman boundary Silvanus, but do not prove that every Etruscan conception passed unchanged into Rome ([Treccani, “Selvans”](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/selvans_%28Enciclopedia-dell%27-Arte-Antica%29/); [Italian study of Selvans/Silvanus](https://www.otium.unipg.it/otium/article/view/58)).

### Cult titles and epithets

More than eighty epithets or expanded formulas are recorded. The principal ones include:

- **Mars Silvanus.** **[Early attested]** Cato’s compound at *De agricultura* 83. It may express functional overlap between Mars as protector of fields and Silvanus, not a permanent claim that the two were universally identical.
- **Silvanus Domesticus.** **[Learned systematization with cultic parallels]** Silvanus of the household, farmstead, or enclosed estate.
- **Silvanus Agrestis.** Silvanus of the outlying land or pasture.
- **Silvanus Orientalis.** Silvanus of the boundary from which an estate’s survey began—not “eastern” in a modern geographic sense.
- **Larum / Sanctus Sacer Larum.** Protector associated with the household Lares.
- **Sanctus, Augustus, Invictus, Conservator, Salutaris.** “Holy,” “Augustan,” “Unconquered,” “Preserver,” and “Health-giving.” These common imperial honorifics must be interpreted inscription by inscription.
- **Dendrophorus.** “Tree-bearer.” It suits Silvanus’ visual and functional identity, though the title also overlaps with *dendrophori* associated with Magna Mater and Attis.
- **Silvestris.** “Of the woods,” especially prominent in parts of the Danubian provinces.
- **Cocidius Silvanus.** **[Regional syncretism]** A Romano-British equation with the local god Cocidius.
- **Sucellus Silvanus / Deus Sucellus Silvanus.** **[Regional syncretism]** A Gallo-Roman equation considered below.
- **Silvanus Augustus.** Especially common in imperial provincial and associational settings; normally signals participation in imperial religious language, not deification of an emperor as Silvanus.

### Silvanus, Faunus, Pan, and Silenus

**[Literary syncretism]** Roman poets placed Silvanus beside Pan, Faunus, satyrs, and nymphs because all inhabited the pastoral-poetic landscape. They were nevertheless distinguishable. Virgil’s *Eclogue* 10 has Silvanus and Pan appear separately. Silvanus lacks Faunus’ securely attested prophetic and oracular character.

**[Late learned identification]** Servius, Macrobius, and other late writers increasingly entangle Pan, Faunus, Inuus, Silvanus, and even Silenus. Such equations document late-antique interpretation, not an original undifferentiated deity. Confusion between *Silvanus* and Greek *Silenus* was encouraged by similar names and rustic iconography, but the names are not established as etymologically identical.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest record

**Plautus, *Aulularia* 674–676, approximately 195–184 BCE — [Early attested].** A character directs another toward a grove or shrine of Silvanus outside the city. The passage proves that an audience could recognize a localized Silvanus sanctuary by the early second century BCE. It does not give theology, genealogy, or a public festival.

**Cato, *De agricultura* 83, approximately 160 BCE — [Early attested].** This is the earliest surviving ritual prescription:

> *Marti Silvano in silva interdius in capita singula boum votum facito.*

“Make the vow to Mars Silvanus in the wood, by daylight, for each head of cattle.”

The offering comprises three Roman pounds of emmer meal, four and a half pounds each of bacon and meat, and three sextarii of wine. Slave or free person may perform it; the offering is consumed immediately on site. A woman must neither attend nor see how it is performed. This restriction belongs explicitly to this rite; it cannot legitimately be generalized into a total ban on women in every Silvanus cult.

### Republican and Augustan texts

**Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.89, 45 BCE — [Learned philosophical testimony].** In the Stoic Balbus’ catalogue, cultivated fields and woods are assigned to appropriate divine powers including Silvanus. The passage shows his accepted divine classification but offers no independent myth.

**Virgil, *Georgics* 1.14–23, 29 BCE — [Augustan poetic elaboration].** Silvanus, carrying a young cypress by its roots (*teneram ab radice ferens, Silvane, cupressum*), is invoked among gods who care for rural work. The cypress becomes a major literary and visual attribute.

**Virgil, *Eclogue* 10.21–27, about 39–38 BCE — [Augustan poetic elaboration].** Silvanus appears to the love-stricken Gallus wearing a rustic crown, with large lilies and flowering fennel. Apollo and Pan appear separately in the same visitation ([Virgil text and notes](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/b/b7/Virgil%27s_Eclogues_and_Georgics%2C_with_notes_%28IA_virgilseclogues00virg%29.pdf)).

**Virgil, *Aeneid* 8.597–607, 20s BCE — [Poetic antiquarian construction].** Near Caere stands a sacred grove. Virgil says that ancient Pelasgians, supposedly the first occupants of Latin territory, dedicated the grove and a festival to Silvanus, “god of fields and livestock” ([text and commentary](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/vergil-aeneid/vergil-aeneid-viii-585-607)). This is evidence for Augustan representations of Italian antiquity, not archaeological proof of a prehistoric Pelasgian cult.

**Horace, *Epodes* 2.21–24, about 30 BCE; *Odes* 3.29.21–24, 23 BCE — [Poetic evidence].** Silvanus appears as guardian of boundaries and rural property and as recipient of rustic observance.

### Ovid and later poetry

**Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.622–697, about 8 CE — [Literary myth].** Silvanus is one of the rustic gods who court Pomona. He does not win her; Vertumnus does. The phrase *Silvanusque, suis semper iuvenilior annis* portrays him as “always younger than his years” ([Latin and translation](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/Metamorph14.php)).

**Important correction:** The famous youth Cyparissus is loved by Apollo in Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 10, not by Silvanus. Later dictionaries and retellings sometimes transfer the story to Silvanus because he carries a cypress. That transfer is a **late interpretive error or modern conflation**, not Ovid’s narrative.

**Ovid, *Fasti* 1.391–440 and 6.319–348 — [Literary context, not a Silvanus myth].** Rustic divine banquets involving Pan, satyrs, Silenus, Priapus, nymphs, and other powers helped shape later woodland iconography. Silenus in these passages must not be silently emended into Silvanus.

### Agrimensorial tradition

**Hyginus Gromaticus and the *Corpus Agrimensorum Romanorum*, texts principally transmitted from the first to later imperial centuries — [Learned professional systematization].** Surveyors credit Silvanus with instituting boundary stones and distinguish three Silvani:

1. *domesticus*, governing the farmstead;
2. *agrestis*, governing the fields or pasture;
3. *orientalis*, governing the point from which a boundary begins.

This tripartition is highly important for Roman land ideology, but it is a surveyors’ taxonomy, not proof that every worshipper honored three separate gods.

### Antiquarian and Christian witnesses

**Varro, *Antiquitates rerum divinarum*, written 40s BCE, lost; fragment preserved by Augustine, *City of God* 6.9, 413–427 CE — [Republican antiquarian report through hostile late transmission].** Three men guard a woman after childbirth so Silvanus will not enter at night and assault or trouble her. They strike the threshold with an axe and pestle and sweep it with a broom. These gestures represent Intercidona, Pilumnus, and Deverra, the signs of cultivation that repel the wild god ([Augustine, *City of God* 6.9](https://www.augustinus.it/italiano/cdd/cdd_06_libro.htm)).

The report is not independent evidence that Roman women universally believed Silvanus raped new mothers. It is Augustine’s polemical quotation or paraphrase of Varro, deployed to ridicule Roman divine specialization. Nevertheless, because Varro’s underlying work was a systematic Republican antiquarian account, the rite cannot simply be dismissed as Augustine’s invention.

**Festus, *De verborum significatu*, second century CE epitome of Verrius Flaccus — [Antiquarian testimony].** Festus connects Silvanus with woodland and rustic religious language, though the fragmentary transmission limits reconstruction.

**Servius, commentaries on Virgil, late fourth/early fifth century CE — [Late learned systematization].** Servius expands Silvanus’ agricultural, boundary, and mythographic associations and participates in the late conflation of rustic gods. His statements often preserve earlier scholarship but cannot automatically be projected into archaic Rome.

**Arnobius, *Adversus nationes*, about 303–310 CE; Augustine and other apologists — [Hostile late testimony].** Christian writers mention or classify Silvanus while criticizing pagan plurality, sexual danger, rustic superstition, or divine specialization. They are evidence for what educated late-antique Christians had read or encountered, but their rhetorical purpose requires caution.

### Epigraphic corpus

**[Imperial attested]** The principal archive is the *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum*, supplemented by *Inscriptiones Latinae Selectae*, *L’Année épigraphique*, regional corpora, and museum databases. Peter F. Dorcey’s corpus assembled more than 1,100 inscriptions—the bulk dating from the first through third centuries CE. They are predominantly Latin stone dedications: altars, bases, plaques, shrine-building records, collegial lists, and funerary-association documents.

The inscriptional corpus says much more about who invoked Silvanus, where, and for what benefit than literary myth does. Standard formulas include *Silvano sacrum*, *Silvano sancto*, *deo Silvano*, *pro salute*, and *votum solvit libens merito*.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**[Early and imperial attested]**

- woods, groves, trees, and access to timber;
- livestock, particularly cattle;
- cultivated fields and adjoining rough land;
- estate buildings, gardens, orchards, and property edges;
- boundary stones and survey lines;
- agricultural fertility and produce;
- protection of estate workers and overseers;
- hunting and wild animals, especially in provincial iconography;
- personal health, safe return, fulfillment of vows, and household welfare.

**[Regional imperial expansion]** Miners at Alburnus Maior, soldiers along the Danube and Rhine, quarry workers, estate managers, and collegia adapted Silvanus to occupational and communal needs. These are not necessarily forgotten pieces of a single archaic theology; they show the god’s continuing portability.

### Physical form

**[Imperial iconography]** The standard western image is a mature or elderly bearded man, sometimes muscular, usually standing. He may be nude except for a cloak or wear a short rustic tunic, boots, or animal skin. Common attributes are:

- pruning knife, sickle, or billhook;
- branch, sapling, or uprooted tree;
- fruit, grapes, pinecones, or a basket;
- wreath of pine, leaves, reeds, flowers, or grain;
- dog at his feet;
- pine or cypress beside him;
- pig, ram, goat, or other rural animal;
- occasionally a syrinx, club, hammer, or local attribute inherited through syncretism.

A British Museum altar shows Silvanus holding fruit and a pruning knife, with a dog, trees, pig, and sacrificial vessel; its inscription dedicates the monument to *Silvanus Sanctus* ([British Museum object](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/G_1805-0703-209)).

### Cypress

**[Augustan literary attribute]** Virgil’s Silvanus carries a tender cypress by the roots. Later interpreters supplied stories to explain it, sometimes wrongly transferring Apollo’s Cyparissus myth to Silvanus. The secure evidence is the attribute; a canonical Silvanus–Cyparissus love story is not securely ancient.

### Antinous as Silvanus

**[Imperial syncretistic portraiture]** A relief commissioned in the Hadrianic period, approximately 130–138 CE, represents the deified Antinous with Silvanus’ rustic and vegetative attributes. Signed by Antonianos of Aphrodisias and associated with the Lanuvium area, it is evidence for visual assimilation of the imperial favorite to a locally resonant rural god—not evidence that Antinous was universally identified with Silvanus ([Google Arts & Culture catalogue](https://artsandculture.google.com/asset/antinous-silvanus-antonianos-di-afrodisia/SQEn4OrJqnlioA?hl=en)).

### Colors, numbers, days, and celestial bodies

**[Absence of evidence]** No securely ancient universal sacred color, number, weekday, planet, zodiacal sign, or annual “Silvanalia” is attested. Modern lists assigning green, particular crystals, planetary hours, or a fixed festival date are modern ritual constructions unless individually grounded in a cited inscription or text.

## 4. Genealogy and divine relationships

### Genealogy

**[Absence of early evidence]** Silvanus has no securely attested Roman birth narrative, parents, wife, or children. Unlike the genealogically elaborate Greek Olympians, he enters the record primarily as a functional god addressed through ritual and dedication.

**[Modern or late conflation]** Genealogies sometimes assigned to Faunus, Pan, Silenus, or woodland spirits cannot automatically be transferred to Silvanus. Pomona is an unsuccessful love interest in Ovid, not his wife. The Silvanae are associated female powers, not securely his daughters.

### Every documented interaction or joint episode with another named divine being

- **Mars — functional compound:** Cato invokes *Mars Silvanus* for cattle; whether identity, coordination, or an epithet is disputed. *Cato, De agricultura* 83, ca. 160 BCE. **[Early attested; disputed syncretism]**
- **Apollo — joint appearance:** Both visit the despairing Gallus; Apollo speaks, while Silvanus arrives crowned with rustic plants. Virgil, *Eclogue* 10.21–27, ca. 39–38 BCE. **[Augustan poetic episode]**
- **Pan — joint appearance:** Pan follows Silvanus in the same Gallus scene and gives counsel. Their separate arrival demonstrates literary distinction. Virgil, *Eclogue* 10.24–30. **[Augustan poetic episode]**
- **Pomona — unsuccessful courtship:** Silvanus is one of the rustic male gods seeking access to Pomona’s orchard; she excludes him. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.622–642, ca. 8 CE. **[Canonical literary elaboration]**
- **Vertumnus — rival suitor by implication:** Both pursue Pomona, but no direct encounter between them is narrated. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.622–771. **[Canonical literary relation; no direct interaction]**
- **Priapus — fellow rejected suitor by implication:** Both are listed among Pomona’s pursuers; no direct interaction occurs. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.634–642. **[Canonical literary relation; no direct interaction]**
- **Pans and Satyrs — co-suitors:** Listed collectively with Silvanus as Pomona’s pursuers. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.634–642. **[Canonical literary relation]**
- **Intercidona — protective opposition:** Her axe-sign helps bar Silvanus from the postpartum house. Varro fr. 196, through Augustine, *City of God* 6.9. **[Republican antiquarian report in late hostile transmission]**
- **Pilumnus — protective opposition:** His pestle-sign helps repel Silvanus. Same source. **[Same tier]**
- **Deverra — protective opposition:** Her broom-sign completes the threshold protection. Same source. **[Same tier]**
- **Lares — cultic association:** Epithets such as *Larum* and *sanctus sacer Larum* associate Silvanus with household guardians; no surviving narrative meeting is known. **[Imperial epigraphic association]**
- **Silvanae — joint cult:** Silvanus appears with female Silvanae, particularly in Pannonia and neighboring regions; reliefs may show multiple women beside him. Their exact kinship is unstated. **[Regional imperial variant]**
- **Nymphae — joint dedication:** Provincial inscriptions couple Silvanus with nymphs, reflecting landscape and water cult. **[Regional imperial attested]**
- **Sucellus — explicit local equation:** *Sucellus Silvanus* and visual interchange occur in Gaul and the Rhine region. **[Regional syncretistic identification]**
- **Nantosuelta — associated through Sucellus:** Monuments pair Sucellus with Nantosuelta; where Sucellus bears Silvanus’ name or attributes, Silvanus enters that regional divine complex indirectly. No pan-Roman consort relation follows. **[Regional syncretism]**
- **Cocidius — explicit or functional equation:** Romano-British dedications produce *Silvanus Cocidius*. **[Regional syncretistic identification]**
- **Diana — joint provincial dedications and hunting affinity:** Documented locally, but there is no canonical narrative interaction. **[Regional cult association]**
- **Campestres — joint dedication:** In Dacia Silvanus and the Silvanae can appear with the Campestres, protectors of open ground and cavalry exercise. **[Regional cult association]**
- **Magna Mater/Attis — dendrophoric overlap:** The epithet or role *Dendrophorus* and tree-bearing associations brought Silvanus near organizations connected with Magna Mater and Attis. Direct identity is not established. **[Imperial associational overlap]**
- **Antinous — representational assimilation:** Antinous is portrayed as Silvanus. This is an interaction of imperial cult and iconography, not a narrated encounter. **[Hadrianic syncretistic portraiture]**
- **Faunus, Inuus, and Pan — late learned equation:** Late commentators sometimes merge or coordinate them. No early source establishes complete identity. **[Late learned systematization]**
- **Silenus — name/iconographic confusion:** Ancient rustic poetic settings and later reception blurred the figures, but a direct mythic encounter is not securely attested. **[Late/modern conflation]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

Silvanus has no surviving epic cycle. His dossier is therefore a warning against measuring divine importance by narrative bulk: cultically he was enormous; mythographically he was sparse.

### The Pelasgian foundation at Caere

**[Augustan poetic-antiquarian account]** In *Aeneid* 8.597–607, Aeneas’ allies approach a grove near Caere surrounded by dark firs. Tradition says the Pelasgians, earliest inhabitants of Latium, consecrated the grove and a festival day to Silvanus.

The episode makes Silvanus older than the Trojan-Roman settlement and embeds Italian landscape religion in Virgil’s national past. There is no independent evidence for the named Pelasgian founders or a datable prehistoric festival. The story’s function is poetic and political: it gives the Italian alliance ancient sacred depth.

### Silvanus and Pomona

**[Ovidian literary myth]** Pomona devotes herself to orchards and bars men and rustic gods from entering. Satyrs, Pans, Silvanus, Priapus, and especially Vertumnus court her. Silvanus’ role is limited to membership among the frustrated suitors. Vertumnus gains entry in disguise, tells an exemplum about rejected love, reveals himself, and wins Pomona.

Later art sometimes converts the passage into a generalized woodland “feast of the gods” or attributes a more developed romance to Silvanus. Ovid does not make him Pomona’s consort.

### The dangerous nocturnal Silvanus

**[Republican ritual tradition through late hostile testimony]** Varro’s account, preserved only by Augustine, casts Silvanus as the uncultivated outside threatening the postpartum household. Axe, pestle, and broom embody the technologies by which woodland, grain, and harvest become culture. Three named divine forces guard the threshold.

Interpretations divide:

- the rite may preserve a genuine apotropaic domestic ceremony;
- the three “gods” may be Varro’s learned personifications of ritual actions;
- Augustine may sharpen Silvanus into a morally repulsive attacker for polemical effect.

The underlying report is too specific to discard, but it is not corroborated by Silvanus’ abundant votive inscriptions.

### The three Silvani and the first boundary stones

**[Imperial professional myth/systematization]** Agrimensorial writers say Silvanus first established stones marking fields and divide his power among domestic, agrestic, and boundary forms. This is an etiological tradition for Roman surveying and property. It makes divine order responsible for converting undifferentiated land into legally legible estates.

### False or transferred myths

- **Silvanus and Cyparissus:** **[Late/modern conflation]** In Ovid, Apollo loves Cyparissus. Silvanus’ cypress is secure; the transferred romance is not.
- **Silvanus as Pomona’s husband:** **[Modern simplification]** Ovid makes him an unsuccessful suitor.
- **Silvanus as a horned Celtic god identical with Cernunnos:** **[Modern comparativist speculation]** No ancient inscription equates them.
- **A canonical Silvanus–Pan genealogy:** **[Modern invention or confusion]** None is attested.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### No state cult

**[Strong negative evidence]** Silvanus has no festival in the surviving Roman *fasti*, no known flaminate, no secure membership in a state priestly college, and no demonstrable publicly funded Roman temple. This is not proof that no magistrate ever sacrificed to him, but it distinguishes his cult sharply from those organized by the Roman state.

His sanctuaries were *sacella*, groves, estate shrines, rooms, collegial premises, and privately funded temples. Known Roman locations were identified through inscriptions near the Via Merulana, Lateran, Servian agger, and later Baths of Constantine area ([Platner and Ashby’s catalogue](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Places/Europe/Italy/Lazio/Roma/Rome/_Texts/PLATOP%2A/Sacella_Silvani.html)).

### Offerings

**[Early attested]** Cato’s rite supplies grain, bacon, meat, and wine for cattle and requires immediate consumption at the site. The rite may be performed by an enslaved or free man, making it unusually explicit evidence for delegated estate religion.

**[Imperial attested]** Inscriptions and reliefs document:

- pigs, wine, fruit, and agricultural produce;
- altars, statues, bronze images, lamps, and painted panels;
- construction or restoration of shrines, porticoes, kitchens, baths, and meeting rooms;
- vows for health, safe journeys, military welfare, family members, livestock, and successful work.

No credible evidence connects Silvanus specifically with institutional human sacrifice or temple prostitution.

### Women

**[Early restricted rite]** Cato excludes women from the *Mars Silvanus* cattle sacrifice. An inscription also barred women from a particular Silvanus-associated bath or cult space.

**[Imperial counter-evidence]** Women nevertheless dedicated to Silvanus. Dorcey counted approximately forty-six female dedicants, around four percent of his corpus. The correct conclusion is partial, rite-specific, or locally variable exclusion—not a universal female ban. The coexistence of prohibition and dedication is evidence for diversity within Roman cult.

### Social constituency

**[Imperial epigraphic evidence]** Dedicants include slaves, freedmen, freeborn non-elites, estate overseers (*vilici*), gardeners, herdsmen, woodworkers, quarrymen, miners, soldiers, veterans, imperial personnel, and collegial members. Elite dedicants occur but constitute a small minority.

Silvanus’ strong association with *vilici* is especially significant. The urban inscriptions of estate managers show that the cult was not confined to people physically living in forests; it also expressed occupational identity and remembered rural order within cities.

### Collegia

**[Imperial attested]** Associations of *cultores Silvani* maintained cult rooms, communal funds, dining, burial, and memorial practices. A Vatican inscription records a land donation to a Silvanus collegium for a *schola* and protected burial place ([Vatican Museums](https://www.museivaticani.va/content/museivaticani/en/collezioni/musei/galleria-lapidaria/sezione-i--religione-e-forme-di-devozione/atto-di-donazione-di-un-terreno-a-un-collegio-del-dio-silvano.html)).

At Philippi, second-century CE Latin inscriptions carved on the acropolis record a sanctuary, building contributors, and membership lists. One list contains sixty-nine members; a niche may have held the association’s regulations ([Inventory of Ancient Associations](https://ancientassociations.ku.dk/assoc/1639)).

### Ostia

**[Archaeological imperial evidence]** A room in Ostia, conventionally called the Shrine of Silvanus, was identified through a painted figure at its entrance. Its successive building phases show cult embedded in a working urban-commercial environment rather than a monumental state temple ([Ostia Antica archaeological catalogue](https://www.ostia-antica.org/regio1/3/3-2.htm)).

### Geography

Silvanus’ strongest epigraphic distribution lies in Italy and the western and Danubian provinces: Cisalpine Gaul, Narbonensis, the Germanies, Dalmatia, Pannonia, Moesia, Dacia, Britain, and parts of the Balkans. He is less prominent in the Greek East, though Latin-speaking soldiers, settlers, and associations carried him there.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Etruscan and central-Italian background, fifth–third centuries BCE

**[Secure Etruscan evidence; disputed relation]** Selvans is attested before Latin Silvanus in surviving evidence and possesses boundary associations. Central Italy supplied a long zone of Etruscan, Umbrian, Sabine, and Latin contact. A direct Etruscan origin for Roman Silvanus remains possible but unproved.

### Roman Republic, early second–first centuries BCE

**[Early attested]** Plautus establishes a recognizable sanctuary; Cato supplies an estate rite; Cicero recognizes Silvanus as a divine power of countryside and woods. The evidence is sparse but diverse. There is still no state calendar cult.

### Augustan period

**[Literary expansion]** Virgil and Horace integrate Silvanus into idealized Italian landscape, pastoral poetry, estate boundaries, and national antiquity. Ovid gives him a small mythographic role as Pomona’s suitor. Augustan poetry creates much of the image later handbooks mistake for a fixed archaic biography.

### First–third centuries CE: epigraphic peak

**[Imperial attested]** The inscriptional record expands dramatically. Private worship, collegia, soldiers, workers, estate personnel, and local provincial populations make this the securely documented high point.

No single imperial policy explains the spread. Latin language, military mobility, colonies, mining districts, estate administration, and the usefulness of a broad rural guardian all contributed.

### Gaul: Silvanus–Sucellus

**[Regional syncretistic identification]** Sucellus is a bearded Gallo-Roman god commonly shown with a long-handled mallet and vessel; Nantosuelta frequently accompanies him. At Augusta Rauricorum and in southern or eastern Gaul, inscriptions or images identify or assimilate him with Silvanus. Some monuments explicitly use *Sucellus Silvanus* ([Persée iconographic discussion](https://www.persee.fr/doc/ecelt_0373-1928_2012_num_38_1_2353)).

The equation was never empire-wide. Sucellus’ hammer, local distribution, and pairing with Nantosuelta retain a distinct Gallo-Roman profile. “Silvanus-Sucellus” is therefore a regional historical product, not proof that the pre-Roman gods were originally one.

### Danubian provinces

**[Regional imperial forms]** Pannonia, Dalmatia, Moesia, and Dacia produced dense concentrations of altars and reliefs. Local forms include *Silvanus Silvestris*, *Domesticus*, hunting imagery, female Silvanae, and dedications by miners and soldiers. Apparent “native” costume or attributes should be interpreted locally; not every Danubian Silvanus is simply an imported Italian god or a renamed indigenous deity.

### Britain

**[Regional imperial syncretism]** Silvanus appears in military and civilian inscriptions and in combinations such as Silvanus Cocidius. The equation reflects local translation and shared protection of woodland or hunting environments, not a universal Celtic theology.

### Fourth–sixth centuries CE: contraction and suppression

**[Late historical context]** Christian emperors progressively restricted sacrifice, temple revenues, and public pagan ritual during the fourth century, culminating in Theodosian legislation against sacrifice and temple cult. Because Silvanus lacked a centralized state institution, there is no documented empire-wide “closure of the cult of Silvanus.” Instead, dated dedications diminish with the wider disappearance of Latin votive epigraphy and sacrificial institutions.

Christian apologists’ continued use of Silvanus as an example shows literary memory, not necessarily flourishing worship. Claims that Silvanus survived unchanged beneath a particular saint, demon, or “Green Man” require local evidence and are generally unproved.

### Medieval period

**[Discontinuity]** The classical god survived in manuscripts, glossaries, place-language derived from *silva*, and learned mythology. Latin *silvanus* could also become a generic word for a woodland being. This semantic afterlife does not demonstrate continuous organized worship of the Roman god.

### Renaissance to the present

**[Revival through texts and art]** Humanist editions of Virgil, Ovid, and antiquarian works restored Silvanus to European literature and art. Modern Pagan and Roman reconstructionist practitioners now sometimes worship him using Cato’s rite, inscriptions, and reconstructed household liturgy. This is a documented modern revival, not an unbroken cult lineage.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Renaissance and early-modern literature and art

**[Early-modern reception]** Renaissance artists adopted rustic gods from Ovidian and Virgilian poetry for pastoral scenes and “feasts of the gods.” Because Silvanus, Faunus, Pan, and Silenus share old, bearded, vegetal imagery, identifications in particular paintings remain contested.

Edmund Spenser’s *The Faerie Queene* uses “Sylvanus” and woodland personification within a deliberately composite classical-fairy landscape. This is Christian-humanist mythopoeia, not preservation of a Roman cult narrative.

### Scientific and common vocabulary

**[Modern taxonomic reception]** *Silvanus/sylvan* became a productive learned label for woodland organisms and concepts. The Barbary macaque’s scientific name, *Macaca sylvanus*, belongs to taxonomic reception; it supplies no evidence for ancient sacred-animal status.

### Occultism

**[Absence of a major inherited tradition]** Silvanus has no substantial role comparable to planetary gods, angels, or demons in the principal Renaissance magical and Hermetic systems. Claims of an ancient Silvanus sigil, planetary correspondence, crystal system, initiatory order, or uninterrupted witch-cult are unsupported unless tied to a datable modern author.

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century occult and Pagan writers often merge Silvanus with Pan, Faunus, Cernunnos, the Green Man, an archetypal “Wild Man,” or a generalized Horned God. Those syntheses are **modern comparativist or ritual constructions**.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist worship

**[Modern revival]** Contemporary Roman-religion practitioners invoke Silvanus as a protector of gardens, woodland, rural property, boundaries, animals, conservation work, and household land. Historically careful reconstruction can draw on Cato, inscriptions, and iconography, but practitioners necessarily make choices where ancient evidence is silent—especially festival dates, gender inclusion, incense, prayers, and ecological ethics.

No reliable demographic study establishes the number of present-day Silvanus devotees. Online communities prove individual worship, not a large or continuous institution.

### Popular culture

**[Modern invention]** The “Silvanus” of *Dungeons & Dragons* and the *Forgotten Realms* is a modern fantasy deity with a developed church, alignment, clergy, symbols, and relationships created for the game setting. The name and nature-domain derive loosely from Rome, but the theology is not ancient evidence.

Modern novels, games, and fantasy encyclopedias often make Silvanus ruler of all wilderness, father of dryads, husband of Pomona, or a horned Celtic deity. These formulations should be credited to their individual fictional settings.

### Political and extremist appropriation

**[Negative finding]** No major, well-documented modern nationalist or extremist movement appears to have made Silvanus a central emblem. Generic Roman, “European pagan,” or eco-nationalist imagery may occasionally include him, but isolated internet usage should not be inflated into an organized historical tradition.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Older scholarship

Georg Wissowa’s *Religion und Kultus der Römer* placed Silvanus among old Italian functional gods and emphasized the lack of public cult. Karl Latte, Georges Dumézil, and later historians reconsidered how far Roman religion could be reconstructed from divine names, ritual functions, and Indo-European comparison.

Dumézil’s structural interpretations remain influential for understanding contrasts such as wild/cultivated and boundary/estate, but structural elegance cannot substitute for dated evidence.

### Etruscan scholarship

Carl Thulin and later Etruscologists used mirrors, inscriptions, and the Piacenza Liver to reconstruct Selvans. Debate persists over:

- whether Roman Silvanus derives from Etruscan Selvans;
- whether Etruscan borrowed an Italic name;
- how far shared boundary functions imply common origin;
- whether particular mirror figures are securely identifiable.

### Peter F. Dorcey

Peter F. Dorcey’s *The Cult of Silvanus: A Study in Roman Folk Religion* (Brill, 1992), developed from his 1987 Columbia dissertation, remains the foundational synthetic monograph. Its principal contributions were:

- assembling a corpus exceeding 1,100 inscriptions;
- treating epigraphy, not literary mythology, as the center of the cult;
- demonstrating extensive participation by slaves, freedmen, *vilici*, soldiers, and other non-elites;
- mapping provincial variation;
- cataloguing more than eighty epithets;
- challenging the claim of total exclusion of women;
- rejecting the automatic assumption that provincial Silvanus always concealed an indigenous god.

Dorcey’s expression “Roman folk religion” is useful for emphasizing worship beyond state institutions but can mislead if “folk” is taken to mean uniform, oral, rural, or socially isolated. Collegia, literate dedications, imperial estates, and soldiers connected the cult directly to Roman institutions.

### Current synthesis

Modern scholarship, represented by John Scheid, Mary Beard, John North, Simon Price, Jörg Rüpke, and provincial epigraphists, tends to reject a rigid opposition between “official” and “popular” religion. Silvanus had no state cult, but his worshippers participated in Roman law, household authority, imperial titulature, association, military organization, and epigraphic habit.

### State of the evidence

- Literary evidence is sparse, elite, and frequently poetic.
- Varro’s important testimony survives through Augustine.
- The epigraphic corpus is large but heavily imperial and geographically uneven.
- Most inscriptions are short and reveal little doctrine.
- Statues without inscriptions may be difficult to distinguish from Pan, Faunus, shepherds, or local gods.
- Archaeological contexts are often poorly recorded because many monuments were discovered before modern excavation.
- The disappearance of inscriptions does not precisely date the last private rite.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Silvanus was straightforwardly the Roman name for Cernunnos.
- He was universally identical with Pan or Faunus.
- Women were wholly forbidden to worship him.
- Pomona was canonically his wife.
- Cyparissus was his lover in Ovid.
- A fixed ancient Silvanalia occurred on a modernly assigned date.
- Silvanus possessed universal sacred crystals, colors, planets, or tarot correspondences.
- His cult practiced human sacrifice.
- The Green Man is a direct, continuous medieval survival of Silvanus.
- Modern fantasy theology preserves lost Roman lore.
