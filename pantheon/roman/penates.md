# The Penates: Research Dossier

**Class:** collective Roman divine powers (*di Penates*), comprising private household protectors and the public Penates of the Roman people. They are gods, not ancestral ghosts; ancient and modern conflation with the Lares must be resisted.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Plautus, *Mercator* 834–835, performed in the late third or early second century BCE, where Charinus addresses *di Penates meum parentum, familiai Lar pater*—“Penates of my parents, father Lar of the household.” This securely establishes the Penates as inherited household gods while grammatically distinguishing them from the singular household Lar. The earliest securely identified visual representation is the Roman Republican denarius of C. Sulpicius C. f. Galba, Crawford 312/1, minted in 106 BCE, whose legend *D·P·P* identifies the paired heads as the *Dei Penates Publici*.

## Evidentiary labels used

- **[EARLY]** Secure Republican evidence or material reasonably reflecting an early Roman/Latin institution.
- **[LATE REPUBLICAN]** First-century BCE literary, numismatic, or antiquarian evidence.
- **[AUGUSTAN CANON]** The politically and poetically systematized Trojan-Roman account of the Augustan period.
- **[IMPERIAL]** Evidence from the first through third centuries CE.
- **[LATE ANTIQUE SYSTEMATIZATION]** Antiquarian, philosophical, or Christian testimony of approximately the third through fifth centuries.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** A local Italic, Lavinian, Etruscan, or provincial form not safely generalizable to all Roman practice.
- **[SYNCRETISTIC IDENTIFICATION]** An equation with other named gods, not an original or timeless identity.
- **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** A scholarly inference rather than an explicit ancient statement.
- **[MODERN INVENTION/REVIVAL]** A post-antique usage or reconstructed religious practice.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

The Penates were the divine powers “of the inside,” especially the household’s protected reserves and the continuity sustained by them. Their name denotes a category or office, not two universally fixed personalities. Every household could have its own Penates; the Roman state, conceived as an enlarged household, possessed the *Penates publici populi Romani*. Their identity consequently ranges from largely anonymous powers of household provisioning to paired armed youths representing the ancestral gods of the Roman people.

Cicero’s Stoic spokesman Balbus gives the standard ancient etymology in *De natura deorum* 2.68 (45 BCE): *di Penates, sive a penu ducto nomine … sive ab eo quod penitus insident*—either from *penus*, everything on which humans feed, or because they sit *penitus*, “deep within.” Poets therefore called them *penetrales*, “gods of the interior.” This is an ancient etymological explanation, not proof that the cult historically began as literal pantry worship. [Cicero’s Latin and translation](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cicero/de_Natura_Deorum/2A%2A.html).

### Names and forms

- **Latin:** *Dī Penātēs*, usually shortened to *Penātēs*. Classical inscriptions and manuscripts also show accusative *Penātīs*, an i-stem form.
- **Singular:** A divine singular *Penās* or *Penātis* is not a normal ancient cult-name. Singular “a Penate” is principally a modern linguistic back-formation.
- **Dī Penātēs Privātī:** modern descriptive terminology for each household’s Penates; the contrast with public Penates is ancient, although this exact title is not a universally fixed formula.
- **Dī Penātēs Publicī** or **Dī Penātēs Publicī Populī Rōmānī:** “Public Penates [of the Roman People].” The coin abbreviation **D·P·P** is conventionally expanded this way.
- **Penātēs patriī / diī patriī:** ancestral or fatherland gods. This could denote the Penates but was not restricted to them.
- **Penātēs Troiānī / Phrygiī:** Trojan or Phrygian Penates, particularly in Virgilian and later accounts.
- **Penetrālēs:** “those of the innermost place,” a poetic epithet reported by Cicero.
- **Dī Magnī / Magnī Dī:** “Great Gods.” This was applied to the Penates in antiquarian syncretism but also to the gods of Samothrace and other groups; it cannot automatically identify every occurrence.
- **Greek translations reported by Dionysius of Halicarnassus:** *theoi patrōoi* (θεοὶ πατρῷοι, ancestral gods), *genethlioi* (γενέθλιοι, gods of birth/descent), *ktēsioi* (κτήσιοι, gods of possessions), *mychioi* (μύχιοι, gods of the inner recess), and *herkeioi* (ἕρκιοι, gods of the enclosure). Dionysius correctly presents these as interpretive translations of functions, not Greek proper names. [*Roman Antiquities* 1.67–68](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/thayer/e/roman/texts/dionysius_of_halicarnassus/1c%2A.html).

### Etymological dispute

1. **From *penus*, provisions/store:** Cicero’s first alternative and the conventional interpretation. It explains the association with food, the household’s reserve, and meal offerings. **[ANCIENT EXPLANATION; MODERN MAJORITY VIEW]**

2. **From *penitus*, inward/deep within:** Cicero’s second alternative. Modern linguistics generally treats this as a related semantic explanation rather than necessarily the immediate derivation. **[ANCIENT ALTERNATIVE]**

3. **“Pantry gods” as an exhaustive definition:** Too narrow. *Penus* could signify both provisions and an inner repository; public Penates protected the continuity and resources of the state rather than a literal state cupboard. Georges Dumézil particularly resisted reducing them to specialized storeroom spirits. **[MODERN SCHOLARLY DISPUTE]**

4. **Etruscan derivation:** No securely demonstrated Etruscan word is the source of *Penates*. Etruscan religious learning influenced Roman classifications of them, but influence on theology does not establish an Etruscan etymology. Annie Dubourdieu’s large-scale study instead argues for a specifically Latin institution of considerable antiquity. [Review and summary of Dubourdieu’s conclusions](https://www.persee.fr/doc/antiq_0770-2817_1992_num_61_1_1149).

### Penates, Lares, Genius, Manes, and Vesta

These categories overlap in domestic worship but are not synonyms:

- **Penates:** divine protectors associated with the household interior, sustenance, and inherited domestic cult.
- **Lar Familiaris:** guardian of the household place and community; Plautus expressly addresses it alongside, not as identical with, the Penates.
- **Genius of the paterfamilias:** the generative and personal divine power of the household’s male head.
- **Manes:** collective divine dead, principally funerary.
- **Vesta:** the hearth and its living fire, at household and state level.

The common modern picture of one lararium containing “the Lares and Penates” is possible as a description of a household shrine but misleading as a claim that all figurines found there are identifiable Penates. John Bodel stresses that domestic shrines could incorporate an open selection of gods; a household’s Penates could accordingly include major deities specially adopted as protectors. [Bodel, “Cicero’s Minerva, *Penates*, and the Mother of the *Lares*”](https://ndl.ethernet.edu.et/bitstream/123456789/25268/1/1.pdf.pdf).

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest literary evidence

**Plautus, *Mercator* 834–835, late third/early second century BCE. [EARLY]**  
Charinus, preparing to abandon home, says:

> *di Penates meum parentum, familiai Lar pater,  
> vobis mando meum parentum rem bene ut tutemini*

“Penates of my parents, father Lar of the household, I entrust to you the property of my parents, that you guard it well.”

The wording proves that the Penates were plural, inherited, protective, and conceptually distinguishable from the Lar. Because the comedy adapts a Greek model, details of the dramatic situation need not reproduce an actual Roman ritual; the Roman religious vocabulary itself is nevertheless secure.

### Republican evidence

**C. Sulpicius Galba denarius, 106 BCE, Crawford 312/1. [LATE REPUBLICAN; MATERIAL]**  
The obverse bears two jugate, laureate male heads with **D·P·P**. The reverse shows two armed men pointing toward a sow, a scene connecting the Sulpicii, Lavinium, the Penates, and the portent of the white sow in the Aeneas tradition. This is the earliest object that explicitly labels an image of the public Penates. [Catalogue description and type](https://www.forumancientcoins.com/numiswiki/view.asp?key=dei+penates).

**Varro, *De lingua Latina* 5.144, 40s BCE. [LATE REPUBLICAN ANTIQUARIAN]**  
Varro says Lavinium was the first Roman-line settlement in Latium, *nam ibi di Penates nostri*—“for there are our Penates.” The statement documents a Roman claim to Lavinium’s cult, not the historical arrival of objects from Bronze Age Troy.

**Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.68, 45 BCE. [LATE REPUBLICAN]**  
Provides the two etymologies and sets the Penates beside Vesta’s preservative power.

**Cicero, *De domo sua* 108–109, 57 BCE. [LATE REPUBLICAN]**  
Treats domestic altars, hearths, Lares, and Penates as constitutive of a legally and religiously inviolable home.

### Augustan narrative corpus

**Virgil, *Aeneid*, composed approximately 29–19 BCE. [AUGUSTAN CANON]**

- 1.6: Aeneas brings his gods into Latium.
- 1.68, 1.378, 1.527, 1.704: Penates designate Trojan/home gods and, metonymically, homes.
- 2.293–295: Hector’s ghost orders Aeneas to take Troy’s sacred things and Penates and seek new walls.
- 2.717–720: Aeneas, polluted by battle, instructs his father Anchises to carry the sacred objects and ancestral Penates until Aeneas can wash in living water.
- 3.11–12: Aeneas sails with companions, son, Penates, and Great Gods.
- 3.147–171: the Penates appear in a dream at Crete, declare that Apollo has sent them, and reveal that Italy—Dardanus’s ancestral land—is the Trojans’ destination.
- 5.744–746: Anchises’ ghost instructs Aeneas regarding the onward journey; the Penates remain part of the destined community.
- 7.120–134: the eating of bread “tables” marks arrival at the promised land; Aeneas invokes the Penates and other gods.
- 8.543 and elsewhere: the gods accompany the establishment of Trojan-Latin order.

Virgil makes the Penates mobile carriers of political and religious identity: Troy is transferable because its gods can be carried to a new home. [Latin and English text of *Aeneid* 2](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0054%3Abook%3D2).

**Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 1, composed from the late 20s BCE onward. [AUGUSTAN/early IMPERIAL]**  
Livy gives the Aeneas–Lavinium–Alba–Rome succession, although his treatment of the Penates is less dramatically developed than Virgil’s. At 5.52, in Camillus’s speech against abandoning Rome after the Gallic sack, the sacred fire of Vesta, heavenly shields, and the community’s *sacra* and Penates make the city religiously irreplaceable. [Livy 5 Latin text](https://www.ancienttexts.org/library/latinlibrary/livy/liv.5.html).

**Ovid, *Fasti*, composed around 2–8 CE and revised later. [AUGUSTAN CANON]**  
The calendar poem situates Vesta, the hearth, the Vestalia, and Trojan sacred objects within Rome’s ritual year. It does not furnish a separate annual “Festival of the Penates.” The lack of an individually named Penates festival in surviving fasti is significant: their domestic cult was continuous, while their public cult operated through Vesta, magistrates’ Lavinian obligations, and temple rites.

**Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 13–15, completed by 8 CE. [AUGUSTAN CANON]**  
Condenses the Trojan migration: Aeneas carries his father and sacred gods from Troy, and the Penates become elements in Rome’s providential prehistory.

### Greek historical and antiquarian corpus

**Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities* 1.67–69, published 7 BCE. [AUGUSTAN ERA; GREEK ANTIQUARIAN]**  
This is the fullest ancient dossier:

- Lavinium possessed secret *hiera* reportedly brought from Troy.
- Timaeus of Tauromenium, writing in the early third century BCE, had been told by inhabitants that they were iron and bronze caducei and a Trojan ceramic vessel.
- Dionysius refuses to disclose prohibited sacred things.
- At Rome he personally saw the small, dark shrine on the Velia and its publicly visible images: two seated youths holding spears, labelled as Penates.
- Other ancient images likewise showed two armed youths.
- Greek writers connected the gods with Samothracian traditions.

Dionysius carefully distinguishes secret objects at Lavinium from visible anthropomorphic statues at Rome; later writers and modern summaries often collapse them. [Full passage](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/thayer/e/roman/texts/dionysius_of_halicarnassus/1c%2A.html).

### Imperial and late-antique corpus

**Augustus, *Res gestae* 19, finalized 14 CE. [IMPERIAL INSCRIPTION]**  
Augustus states that he rebuilt or built *aedem deum Penatium in Velia*, the temple of the Penates on the Velia. The text survives most fully in the bilingual monumental copy at Ancyra. [*Res gestae* 19](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Augustus/Res_Gestae/4%2A.html).

**Tacitus, *Annals* 15.41, early second century CE. [IMPERIAL HISTORIOGRAPHY]**  
The great fire of 64 CE destroyed the sanctuary of Vesta *cum Penatibus populi Romani*. This explicitly locates public Penates, or sacred objects identified with them, within Vesta’s sanctuary. Tacitus says some ancient treasures could not be replaced; whether the Penates themselves were believed irrecoverably destroyed is not specified. [Tacitus 15.41](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/e/roman/texts/tacitus/annals/15b%2A.html).

**Festus, epitome of Verrius Flaccus, second century CE, preserved through Paul the Deacon. [IMPERIAL ANTIQUARIAN]**  
Defines *penus* and the innermost place of Vesta’s temple. The damaged and epitomized transmission makes exact reconstruction hazardous.

**Servius, commentary on the *Aeneid*, late fourth/early fifth century CE. [LATE ANTIQUE SYSTEMATIZATION]**  
Preserves otherwise lost Republican opinions. He reports identifications of Aeneas’s Penates as Neptune and Apollo; elsewhere he discusses the Great Gods, Samothracian gods, Dioscuri, and Etruscan Penates. [Servius’s Latin commentary](https://monumenta.ch/latein/text.php?domain=&hide_apparatus=&inframe=1&lang=0&level=&links=&rumpfid=Servius%2C+in+Vergilii+Aeneidos+Commentarius%2C+01&tabelle=Servius).

**Arnobius, *Adversus nationes* 3.40, approximately 300 CE. [HOSTILE CHRISTIAN WITNESS]**  
Arnobius catalogs pagan disagreement:

- Nigidius Figulus identified the Penates as Neptune and Apollo, builders of Troy’s walls.
- Following “Etruscan disciplines,” Nigidius divided Penates into four kinds: of Jupiter, of Neptune, of the underworld, and of mortal humans.
- Caesius identified them as Fortuna, Ceres, the Jovial Genius, and a male Pales.

Arnobius cites these disagreements polemically to demonstrate pagan incoherence. His report preserves lost antiquarian material, but his framing is adversarial. [Latin text](https://www.ancienttexts.org/library/latinlibrary/arnobius3.html).

**Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 3.4.8–9, early fifth century CE. [LATE ANTIQUE SYSTEMATIZATION]**  
Quoting or paraphrasing Varro, Macrobius gives a philosophical definition: the Penates are those through whom humans breathe inwardly, possess bodies, and possess rational minds. This is learned theology, not evidence that ordinary Romans conceived pantry gods as metaphysical principles.

**Augustine, *City of God* 4 and 7, 413–426 CE. [HOSTILE CHRISTIAN WITNESS]**  
Augustine uses Varro’s lost *Antiquitates rerum divinarum* to ridicule multiplication and rationalization of Roman gods. He preserves the penus/penitus interpretation and Varro’s attempts to identify divine powers with cosmic principles. Augustine is indispensable because Varro’s theological books are lost, but quotation and polemical selection must be separated from Varro’s exact original position. [*City of God* Book 7](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120107.htm).

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

1. **Stored sustenance. [EARLY/ANCIENT ETYMOLOGY]**  
Food, drink, oil, and the reserves that let a household survive periods without production. “Gods of the store-cupboard” is therefore justified, provided it is not made their only function.

2. **Interior and household continuity. [EARLY]**  
They inhabit or define the religiously protected interior. “Penates” consequently becomes a metonym for home, household, or homeland.

3. **Inherited family identity. [EARLY]**  
Plautus calls them the Penates “of my parents.” They belong to a continuing household but are not demonstrably the souls of those parents.

4. **State subsistence and continuity. [REPUBLICAN/IMPERIAL]**  
The Roman people’s Penates convert the domestic institution into a public one. Their preservation signifies preservation of the Roman community.

5. **Portable homeland. [AUGUSTAN CANON]**  
In Virgil, Aeneas can lose Troy yet transmit its sacral identity by carrying the Penates. This is political theology, not evidence for an actual Bronze Age migration of cult statues.

### Hearth and fire

The Penates were connected with the hearth because food offerings were placed or burned there and because Vesta embodied the fire that transformed and preserved food. Cicero places them immediately after Vesta in his theological exposition. The public analogy is equally strong: the Penates of the Roman people were kept in or associated with Vesta’s sanctuary, beside the perpetual state fire.

They are nevertheless not fire gods. Vesta is the hearth/fire; the Penates are beneficiaries and protectors of the sustenance and interior organized around it.

### Forms and images

- **Anonymous or variable household figures. [IMPERIAL MATERIAL CULTURE]** Household shrines at Pompeii and elsewhere contain Lares, Genius figures, snakes, and major gods. Without inscriptions or an identifying context, a small bronze god cannot safely be labelled “a Penate.” The title could describe a god’s domestic function rather than a fixed iconographic species.
- **Two laureate male heads. [LATE REPUBLICAN]** The 106 BCE Sulpician denarius explicitly labels them DPP.
- **Two armed youths. [AUGUSTAN-ERA EYEWITNESS]** Dionysius saw two seated youths with spears in the Velian temple and says other old images showed the pair in military dress.
- **Secret, non-anthropomorphic objects. [TIMAeus FRAGMENT/REGIONAL LAVINIAN]** Iron and bronze caducei and a Trojan ceramic vessel, according to Timaeus’s Lavinian informants.
- **Old men in the *Vergilius Vaticanus*. [LATE ANTIQUE ART]** The illustrated manuscript, made around 400 CE, represents the Penates as veiled older men in the dream scene. This is a late-antique visualization of Virgil, not evidence for archaic Roman iconography.

### Symbols and attributes

Secure attributes are the inner store, food portions, the table, hearth, fire-mediated offering, paired divine images, spear, military dress, and—within the Lavinian foundation complex—the white sow.

No securely attested ancient system assigns the Penates a unique planet, weekday, color, number beyond the frequent but non-universal pair, plant, gemstone, or zodiacal correspondence. Such lists on modern occult websites are **[MODERN INVENTION]** unless independently sourced.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

The Penates have no consistent parentage, birth, consorts, children, or siblings. This absence is structurally important: they are a collective cult category, not a genealogically individuated family like the Olympians. When named gods are declared to “be” the Penates, this is theological identification, not genealogy.

### Competing identity systems

| Source and date | Identification | Evidentiary status |

### The Dioscuri question

The paired, spear-bearing young men and the designation *Dii Magni* encouraged identification with Castor and Pollux. Servius supplies explicit late evidence for the equation. Republican coins may visually reinforce it, but paired youthful imagery alone does not prove that the earliest Latin Penates were originally the Dioscuri. The safest reconstruction is that public Penates acquired Dioscuric iconography or interpretation through Greek and South-Italian religious language. **[SYNCRETISTIC IDENTIFICATION, date uncertain but established by the Late Republic/early Empire]**

Because “Penates” is a collective office, the list distinguishes narrative action from later identity claims.

- **Hector — commands Aeneas to rescue the Penates from burning Troy and seek new walls for them; Virgil, *Aeneid* 2.268–295, 29–19 BCE. [AUGUSTAN CANON]**
- **Aeneas — carries, protects, invokes, and is guided by the Penates; Virgil, *Aeneid* 1–3 and 7, 29–19 BCE. [AUGUSTAN CANON]**
- **Anchises — physically bears the sacred objects and Penates because battle-polluted Aeneas will not touch them; Virgil, *Aeneid* 2.717–720. [AUGUSTAN CANON]**

- **Vesta — shares the household complex of hearth, food, and fire; the public Penates are kept in or with her sanctuary; Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.67–68; Tacitus, *Annals* 15.41. [LATE REPUBLICAN/IMPERIAL CULT ASSOCIATION]**
- **Jupiter — invoked with the Penates in public and domestic religious formulas; also heads one class in Nigidius’s Etruscanizing scheme; Nigidius via Arnobius 3.40. [LEARNED SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Neptune — identified with one of Aeneas’s Penates as a builder of Troy’s walls; Nigidius via Arnobius 3.40. [LEARNED IDENTIFICATION]**
- **Apollo — identified with the other Trojan Penate as Neptune’s fellow wall-builder; same source. [LEARNED IDENTIFICATION]**
- **Fortuna — named by Caesius as one of the Penates; Arnobius 3.40. [LEARNED IDENTIFICATION]**
- **Ceres — named by Caesius as one of the Penates; Arnobius 3.40. [LEARNED IDENTIFICATION]**
- **Genius Iovialis — named by Caesius as one of the Penates; Arnobius 3.40. [LEARNED IDENTIFICATION]**
- **Pales — a male Pales, described as Jupiter’s minister or steward, is named by Caesius; Arnobius 3.40. [LEARNED/REGIONAL IDENTIFICATION]**
- **Castor and Pollux — equated with or interpreted as the paired Lavinian Penates; Servius on *Aeneid* 3.12, late fourth/early fifth century. [SYNCRETISTIC IDENTIFICATION]**
- **Samothracian Great Gods — linked through traditions in which Dardanus carried mysteries or gods from Samothrace to Troy; Dionysius 1.68 and Servian antiquarian material. [HELLENISTIC/ROMAN LEARNED SYNCRETISM]**

- **Venus — protects Aeneas, the bearer of the Penates, but no direct Penates–Venus encounter is recorded. [ABSENCE OF DIRECT INTERACTION]**
- **Minerva/Pallas — the Palladium may accompany the Trojan *sacra* in later descriptions of Vesta’s secret repository, but the Palladium and Penates are not securely the same object. [LATE ANTIQUE/ANTIQUARIAN ASSOCIATION; IDENTITY UNESTABLISHED]**
- **The Lares — repeatedly share domestic space and formulas with the Penates; Plautus, *Mercator* 834–835, and later domestic evidence. No mythic encounter occurs. [EARLY CULT ASSOCIATION]**
- **The Genius — associated with domestic worship and paired in the 392 CE prohibition, but no narrative interaction occurs. [CULT ASSOCIATION]**

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. The escape from Troy

**Full Augustan version.** Hector appears to Aeneas during Troy’s destruction, entrusts him with the city’s sacred things and Penates, and orders him to seek a city that can receive them. Aeneas escapes carrying Anchises; Anchises carries the sacred objects because Aeneas, bloodied from combat, considers himself ritually unclean. The gods therefore travel as the sacral identity of Troy, not merely as private luggage.

**Earliest form:** The precise scene is first fully extant in Virgil. Greek traditions already made Aeneas a Trojan survivor, and third-century BCE Timaeus knew a story that Lavinium held Trojan sacred objects, but the complete Hector–Aeneas transfer cannot securely be projected before Virgil. **[AUGUSTAN CANON WITH HELLENISTIC PREHISTORY]**

**Political function:** The story makes Roman dominion a continuation and transformation of Troy. It also defines *pietas*: Aeneas saves father, son, and gods together.

### B. The Penates speak at Crete

Aeneas initially interprets Apollo’s oracle as directing him to Crete. During plague and crop failure, the Penates appear in a dream, illuminated in supernatural light. They say Apollo has sent them and explain that the Trojans’ proper destination is Hesperia/Italy, the ancient homeland of Dardanus.

This is their only major extant speaking scene. They possess knowledge, authorize migration, and correct human and prophetic misinterpretation. **[AUGUSTAN CANON]**

### C. Lavinium and the immovable gods

Later tradition states that after Aeneas founded Lavinium, Ascanius founded Alba Longa and tried to transfer the Penates there. The gods miraculously returned to Lavinium—sometimes more than once—demonstrating their chosen seat. Rome’s magistrates therefore continued to sacrifice at Lavinium.

The episode explains why Rome could claim the Penates while their primary ancestral cult remained outside Rome. It is an etiological story for the otherwise awkward ritual dependence of Rome on Lavinium. Its extant forms are later than the institutions they purport to explain. **[LATER CANONICAL ETIOLOGY]**

### D. The white sow and Lavinium

The oracle or portent of a white sow with thirty piglets marks the destined Italian settlement or foretells Alba Longa’s foundation after thirty years. The Sulpician denarius associates two armed figures and the sow with the public Penates. Virgil develops the sign in *Aeneid* 3 and 8.

The sow is therefore part of the Penates’ public foundation complex, not their universal sacred animal. **[REPUBLICAN ICONOGRAPHY; AUGUSTAN ELABORATION]**

### E. Samothrace–Troy–Italy

A learned genealogy of cult made Dardanus carry divine mysteries or Great Gods from Samothrace to Troy; Aeneas then carried them from Troy to Italy. This created a prestigious Greek mystery pedigree for the Roman Penates.

The chain is not homogeneous early tradition. Dionysius cites multiple writers and preserves uncertainty; Servius later harmonizes competing identifications. **[HELLENISTIC AND ROMAN LEARNED SYNCRETISM]**

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Private cult

The domestic Penates belonged to the household rather than a centralized priesthood. Evidence associates them with:

- portions of food placed before divine images or cast into the hearth;
- libations at meals;
- prayer and acknowledgment before or after eating;
- maintenance of sacred images in an inner room, cupboard, kitchen, atrium, or multipurpose household shrine;
- inheritance and removal with the household.

The paterfamilias could lead ritual, but domestic religion included women, children, enslaved people, and dependants. Claims that worship was exclusively male are unsupported. Archaeological “lararia” reveal household hierarchy but also participation beyond the freeborn nuclear family.

No special Penates priesthood is securely attested. Domestic ritual was integrated with household authority; public rites were performed by magistrates, Vestals, pontiffs, or local officials as context required.

### Lavinium

Lavinium, at Pratica di Mare south of Rome, was treated as the seat of Rome’s ancestral Penates. Roman magistrates, especially on entering office or departing for provinces, were expected to perform rites there to Vesta and the Penates. This expressed Rome’s kinship with and ritual dependence on its Latin *origo*.

The famous sanctuary of thirteen altars dates in stages from approximately the sixth through fourth centuries BCE. Excavation began in 1957. It proves a major archaic and Republican cult center but does **not** contain an inscription securely dedicating the entire complex to the Penates. Proposed identifications include the Penates, Venus, Latin federal cult, or several cults successively or concurrently. Assigning all thirteen altars to the Penates is **[MODERN ARCHAEOLOGICAL HYPOTHESIS]**, not fact. [Recent archaeological discussion](https://www.cambridge.org/core/services/aop-cambridge-core/content/view/A93B4D2280D08A08E60074D4A6AA8DFF/S1047759424000321a.pdf/ritual-time-the-seasonal-calendar-and-religious-festivals-in-archaic-and-republican-central-italy.pdf).

Timaeus’s report of caducei and a ceramic vessel establishes that inhabitants in the early third century BCE already narrated their secret objects as Trojan. It does not prove the objects were Bronze Age Trojan artifacts.

### Velian temple at Rome

Dionysius places a small, dark temple on the Velia, close to the Forum and on the short route toward the Carinae. It displayed two seated spear-bearing youths. Augustus records rebuilding the temple in *Res gestae* 19. Its exact archaeological footprint remains disputed, not least because ancient and modern construction radically altered the Velia. The literary dossier is summarized by [Digital Augustan Rome](https://www.digitalaugustanrome.org/records/penates-aedes/) and [Platner and Ashby](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Places/Europe/Italy/Lazio/Roma/Rome/_Texts/PLATOP%2A/Aedes_Deorum_Penatium.html).

No reliable evidence supports claims that Romulus founded this temple. Traditions instead put it on or near the former house of Tullus Hostilius; this is royal topography preserved by later antiquarians, not excavated fact.

### Temple of Vesta and *penus Vestae*

A separate manifestation of the public Penates was associated with Vesta’s sanctuary in the Forum. The *penus Vestae* was its innermost repository, accessible only under restricted conditions and ceremonially opened during the Vestalia. Tacitus explicitly says that Vesta’s sanctuary and the Penates of the Roman people burned in 64 CE.

The relation between the Velian images and Vesta’s secret objects is unresolved. A useful reconstruction distinguishes:

- visible anthropomorphic cult images on the Velia;
- concealed sacral pledges in Vesta’s inner repository;
- ancestral sacred objects at Lavinium.

Ancient theology connected all three, but they were not necessarily duplicate statues.

### Calendar and festivals

There is no securely attested festival named simply *Penatalia*. The main calendrical nexus is the **Vestalia**, June 7–15, because Vesta’s cult, the state hearth, the *penus Vestae*, and the Penates formed a single preservative complex. Surviving *fasti* mark Vesta’s rites, opening and cleansing, not a separate Penates feast.

Claims that March 1 was “the festival of the Penates” confuse magistrates’ calendrical and Lavinian duties with a named public festival.

### Offerings and sacrifice

Securely or plausibly attested offerings include portions of ordinary food, wine/libations, incense, lamps, garlands, and animal sacrifice in the public cult. The Theodosian prohibition of 392 names incense specifically for the Penates.

There is **no evidence that human sacrifice was offered to the Penates**. Searches linking them to Roman human-sacrifice controversies yield no Penates-specific rite, victim, altar, or accusation. Likewise, there is no evidence of temple prostitution, sexual initiation, divination, or an oracle proper to them. Their dream speech in Virgil is literary narrative, not proof of an incubatory oracle.

### Inscriptions

Epigraphic dedications such as *Dis Penatibus sacrum* occur but are much less numerous than dedications to Lares, Genius, Jupiter, or provincial gods. CIL X 5164 and a Puteolan stele attest public or semi-public dedication to the Penates. The evidence confirms diffusion of their cult terminology but does not support a large empire-wide institutional priesthood. The [CIL database](https://cil.bbaw.de/ace) remains the appropriate corpus for individual stones and archival records.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the literary record

The association of household interior, reserves, and divine protection is likely older than Plautus, but no object from archaic Rome can securely be labelled a private Penate. The term’s Latin formation and Lavinium’s archaic cultic importance support deep Latin roots; precise origins remain unrecoverable. **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]**

### Third–second centuries BCE

Plautus documents inherited household Penates. Timaeus, by the early third century BCE, documents Lavinians claiming Trojan sacred objects. These are independent but complementary developments: an ordinary domestic category and a public foundation cult.

### Late Republic

The public Penates became politically and genealogically useful. The 106 BCE Sulpician coin links a Roman family, the DPP, armed twin imagery, and Lavinian foundation legend. Varro systematized terminology, Lavinium, cosmic explanations, and competing identifications. Cicero used Penates as a serious category of domestic and civic religion.

### Augustan age

This is the decisive narrative crystallization. Virgil turned the Penates into active guarantors of a Troy–Italy–Rome destiny. Augustus restored the Velian temple and advertised the act in his monumental autobiography. Ovid and Dionysius further embedded them in calendar, topography, and Greek-readable antiquarian history.

This transformation should not be called invention from nothing: Lavinian Trojan claims existed by Timaeus. Augustus’s age selected, coordinated, monumentalized, and popularized them. **[AUGUSTAN CANONICAL ELABORATION]**

### First–third centuries CE

The cult continued domestically and publicly. Tacitus records the 64 fire’s destruction of Vesta’s sanctuary with the Penates. Rebuilding must have followed, although the precise fate of individual sacred objects is unknown. Imperial-household language extended the basic idea to the emperor’s *domus*, while inscriptions carried dedications through Italy and the provinces.

### Fourth century: suppression

Domestic cult remained sufficiently alive to be legislated against. On 8 November 392, Theodosius I, Arcadius, and Honorius issued *Codex Theodosianus* 16.10.12, forbidding sacrifice and even private rites: no one was to honor a Lar with fire, a Genius with wine, or Penates with fragrance, light lamps, offer incense, or hang garlands. [Latin decree](https://telma.irht.cnrs.fr/outils/relmin/extrait30322/).

This is direct evidence of legal suppression, not merely gradual cultural fading. Enforcement was uneven, and a prohibition does not establish immediate extinction in every household. No surviving evidence documents a distinct continuous Penates cult after Christianization.

### Fifth century and medieval transmission

Arnobius, Augustine, Servius, Macrobius, and Martianus Capella preserved the Penates chiefly as objects of polemic, commentary, allegory, and antiquarian taxonomy. Their work transformed living or recently living cult into textual heritage.

Medieval readers encountered the Penates mainly through Virgil, Virgilian commentary, Augustine, and mythography. They were understood as Trojan idols, household gods, or allegorical natural powers. This is literary survival, not cult continuity.

### Renaissance to nineteenth century

Humanists recovered Cicero, Livy, Dionysius, and inscriptions; illustrated mythographies regularized the Penates as portable statuettes of household gods. Antiquarians frequently harmonized contradictory identifications—Dioscuri, Great Gods, Neptune and Apollo, or generic domestic idols.

The Latin plural entered European vernaculars metaphorically. French *regagner ses pénates* means “return home”; English “lares and penates” means household possessions, cherished domestic objects, or home itself.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Modern Roman religious reconstructionists sometimes honor household gods under the name Penates, offering food, wine, incense, or light. Such practice consciously reconstructs ancient cult from literary and archaeological evidence. It is **[MODERN REVIVAL]**, not demonstrated institutional continuity.

No reliable census isolates worshippers of the Penates from Roman-polytheist or broader neopagan communities. Claims of an unbroken hereditary Penates tradition are unsubstantiated.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship and museums

Museum catalogues long used “Lar” and “Penate” loosely for almost any small Roman domestic divine bronze. Current contextual archaeology is more cautious: without an inscription, attributes, or find context, identification is often impossible.

### Literature and visual culture

Virgil’s image of Aeneas carrying the gods out of Troy remained the dominant reception. Manuscript illustration, Renaissance painting, opera, school editions, and nationalist art repeatedly treated the Penates as statuettes carried with Anchises.

The phrase “household gods” often erases the specifically Roman distinction among Penates, Lares, Vesta, and Genius. This translation is serviceable but imprecise.

### “Penates” as home and property

European languages developed secular metaphor:

- French *ses pénates*: one’s home.
- English “lares and penates”: household goods or intimate domestic possessions.
- Russian *Penaty* likewise became a literary word for home.

The estate of the artist Ilya Repin near St Petersburg was named **Penaty**, making “the Penates” a modern cultural toponym. This is classical reception, not religious continuity.

### Occultism

Unlike Hecate, Hermes, the planetary gods, or named daemons, the Penates did not acquire a major fixed office in Renaissance ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn, or standard grimoire catalogues. Modern occult tables assigning them planets, colors, crystals, sigils, or fixed cardinal directions lack ancient evidence. **[MODERN INVENTION]**

Renaissance and early-modern authors did transmit late-antique cosmic explanations, especially through Varro as quoted by Augustine and Macrobius. That is philosophical reception, not a secret Penates magical tradition.

### Modern paganism

Roman reconstructionist practice usually treats *Penates* as a household category: individual practitioners select gods important to their family or honor anonymous powers of sustenance. This resembles one genuine feature of Roman religion—the openness of household divine assemblages—but exact modern liturgies are reconstructed.

Assertions that the Penates must always be exactly two male spirits, must be Castor and Pollux, or must receive a prescribed modern food are not established ancient rules.

### Political appropriation

Augustan use was itself political: the restored Velian temple and Virgilian epic linked the emperor’s renewed Rome with Trojan legitimacy. Later Italian nationalism and Fascist Romanità reused Aeneas and Augustan imagery, but evidence for a major independent Fascist cult or emblem of the Penates is lacking. They were subordinate elements within broader appropriation of Rome, family, fatherland, and Augustan restoration.

No well-documented modern extremist movement is distinctively organized around the Penates. Claims to that effect require case-specific evidence.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Ancient disagreement as evidence

The ancient sources did not know a single answer to “who are the Penates?” Arnobius’s catalogue is decisive: Neptune and Apollo; four Etruscan classes; Fortuna, Ceres, Genius, and Pales; cosmic principles. These contradictions should not be solved by choosing one as the hidden truth. They document centuries of antiquarian attempts to turn an open cult category into a named pantheon.

### Major modern approaches

**Georg Wissowa, *Religion und Kultus der Römer*, 1902; second edition 1912.**  
Wissowa’s institutional and philological model sharply distinguished Roman cult categories and treated *Penates* as an adjectival designation connected with *penus*. His reconstruction remains foundational, though its confidence in isolating an original “pure Roman” layer is now treated cautiously. [Bibliographic record and editions](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL23547386M/Religion_und_Kultus_der_R%C3%B6mer).

**Carl Thulin and Etruscan-religion scholarship.**  
Thulin used late Roman reports to reconstruct the Etrusca disciplina, including classified Penates. The central difficulty is chronological: most explicit testimony is Roman and late, mediated by antiquarians. Etruscan mirrors depicting divine twins or assemblies illuminate iconographic environments but do not, without labels, prove that a pair are “Etruscan Penates.”

**Georges Dumézil, *La religion romaine archaïque* / *Archaic Roman Religion*, 1966 and revisions.**  
Dumézil opposed simplistic pantry reduction and incorporated the Penates into Roman structures of social continuity and divine function. His Indo-European comparisons remain influential but are reconstructions, not primary attestations. [Book record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Archaic_Roman_Religion.html?id=_aoicpjjhyMC).

**Robert Schilling.**  
Schilling emphasized the distinction between domestic and public Penates and the historical layering of Lavinium, Troy, and Rome.

**Annie Dubourdieu, *Les origines et le développement du culte des Pénates à Rome*, 1989.**  
The fullest monograph argues for the antiquity, Latin character, collective indeterminacy, and gradual development of private Penates. It is especially important for disentangling lexical use, domestic practice, and public mythology.

**Mary Beard, John North, and Simon Price, *Religions of Rome*, 1998; John Scheid; Kurt Latte.**  
These historians situate the Penates within ritual systems rather than assuming that every Roman god possessed a Greek-style biography. Their approach emphasizes practice, public authority, household structure, and the hazards of treating late antiquarian explanations as transparent archaic fact.

**John Bodel.**  
Bodel’s contextual study of Cicero’s Minerva and domestic religion challenges rigid typologies. A household’s Penates were not necessarily two otherwise anonymous pantry figurines; named gods could serve within the domestic assembly.

### Principal controversies

1. **Pantry gods or inner-house gods?**  
Both meanings occur in ancient etymology. An exclusively economic definition is too narrow; an exclusively mystical definition ignores food and storage.

2. **Private cult first, or public cult first?**  
The private institution is structurally basic and first securely attested in Plautus, but Lavinian public Trojan claims are nearly as early in surviving testimony through Timaeus. Linear evolution from “pantry” to “empire” is plausible but not directly documented at every stage.

3. **Trojan origin: memory or construction?**  
Timaeus proves the claim existed by the early third century BCE. Archaeology proves archaic Greek contact and major cult at Lavinium. Neither proves that Aeneas transported objects from historical Troy. The canonical form is principally Hellenistic and Augustan.

4. **Were the Penates the Dioscuri?**  
They were identified with or represented like divine twins in some public contexts. That does not establish that every domestic Penate was Castor or Pollux, or that the original Latin category arose from their cult.

5. **Were they Samothracian Great Gods?**  
This is a learned genealogical and syncretistic construction. “Great Gods,” Penates, Cabiri, and Dioscuri overlap in ancient interpretation but are not universally interchangeable.

6. **What were the Lavinian sacred objects?**  
Timaeus reports caducei and a ceramic vessel; Dionysius reports publicly visible statues at Rome, not necessarily at Lavinium. Modern attempts to make all these descriptions refer to one pair of statues generate a false harmony.

7. **Were all lararium figures Penates?**  
No. Identification requires context. Generic cataloguing has produced many unsupported “Penate statuettes.”

8. **Did the cult involve human sacrifice?**  
No Penates-specific evidence. General Roman sacrifice controversies cannot be transferred to them.

9. **Did their cult survive secretly into modern folklore?**  
European house-spirit traditions offer comparisons, not demonstrated historical continuity. Similar domestic functions do not establish descent.

### State of the evidence

The losses are severe:

- Varro’s principal religious books survive only in fragments and hostile quotation.
- Nigidius Figulus and Caesius survive largely through later writers.
- Lavinium’s secret objects are not securely identified archaeologically.
- The Velian temple’s exact remains are disputed or destroyed.
- Domestic objects are often iconographically ambiguous.
- Literary evidence is richest precisely when political systematization was strongest.

The best-controlled record therefore combines securely datable texts, labelled coins, inscriptions, architectural testimony, and carefully bounded archaeological inference.
