# Vertumnus: Research Dossier

**Class:** Male god (*deus*) in Roman tradition; possibly—but not demonstrably—the Roman development or interpretive counterpart of an Etruscan deity associated with Voltumna/Veltune.

**Earliest secure surviving attestation:** Cicero, *In Verrem* 2.1.154, delivered in 70 BCE, uses the statue of Vertumnus as a familiar Roman topographical landmark: the route *a signo Vertumni in Circum Maximum*, “from the statue of Vertumnus to the Circus Maximus.” A cult and temple conventionally dated to 264 BCE are reported by substantially later calendrical and antiquarian evidence, not by a contemporary third-century document.

**Evidentiary key:** **[EA]** securely attested ancient evidence; **[LE]** later ancient elaboration; **[RV]** regional or source-specific variant; **[LS]** learned systematization or antiquarian inference; **[SI]** syncretistic identification; **[MA]** medieval or early-modern accretion; **[MSR]** modern scholarly reconstruction; **[MI]** modern invention or unsupported popular claim.

---

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Vertumnus is a Roman god whose best-attested ancient manifestations are a statue or shrine in the **Vicus Tuscus** beside the Roman Forum, an Aventine temple whose dedication was commemorated on 13 August, and an Augustan literary personality defined by seasonal produce, transferable dress, and the capacity to assume different forms. Propertius’ *Elegy* 4.2 makes the Vicus Tuscus statue speak; Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* 14 converts the god’s mutability into a sustained courtship narrative with Pomona. The familiar modern definition “god of change, seasons, gardens, fruit trees, and plant growth” is therefore partly documentary and partly synthetic: produce and changing appearances are explicit in Propertius and Ovid, but no surviving archaic ritual text defines a universal divine “department” of Change. The Oxford Classical Dictionary accordingly warns that Vertumnus’ original functions are unknown because the ancient explanations depend heavily on wordplay with Latin *vertere*, “to turn/change.” [OCD](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548214129)

### Names

- **Vortumnus** — probably the older or more conservative Latin spelling. It occurs in Varro and in inscriptions and calendars: *Vortumno sacrum* (“sacred to Vortumnus,” CIL VI 803) and *Vortumnus temporibus Diocletiani et Maximiani* (CIL VI 804). **[EA]**
- **Vertumnus** — the literary form dominant in Propertius and Ovid, readily assimilated to *vertere*. **[EA]**
- **Vertimnus** — a comparatively rare manuscript or lexical variant; it does not establish a separate cult. **[RV]**
- **Voltumna** — the Latin name in *Fanum Voltumnae*, the federal sanctuary repeatedly mentioned by Livy. Whether this was the same being as Vertumnus is disputed. **[EA for the sanctuary-name; SI/MSR for identity]**
- **Veltune** — an Etruscan divine name inscribed on the late Etruscan bronze mirror from Tuscania. Its bearer is often connected with Voltumna and sometimes with Vertumnus, but neither step is a secure bilingual equation. **[EA for the name; MSR/SI for the equation]**
- **Veltha, Velthum(e)na, Veltuna** — modernly assembled Etruscan forms or proposed relatives. They should not be printed as interchangeable ancient spellings without citing the individual inscriptions. **[MSR]**
- **Volturnus** — a separate Roman/Italic river or wind deity with a flamen and a festival on 27 August. Confusing Volturnus with Vortumnus is ancient or antiquarianly tempting but unsupported as a cultic identity. **[LS/SI, disputed]**

### Ancient etymologies

Propertius 4.2 parades and rejects several derivations:

1. from the “turned” or altered course of the Tiber;
2. from returning spring and the succession of produce;
3. from exchange or traffic in the commercial Vicus Tuscus;
4. finally, from the god’s capacity to “turn himself into every figure,” *formas…vertere*.

Ovid, *Fasti* 6.409–410, offers a hydrological derivation: the god had not yet taken his name *ab averso amne*, from the river turned back or diverted. Ovid immediately notes that the name suits his many forms. These are Augustan aetiological wordplays, not historical linguistics. [Ovid, *Fasti* 6](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidFasti6.html)

Varro, *De lingua Latina* 5.46, instead associates the Vicus Tuscus statue with Etruria: *ibi Vortumnum stare, quod is deus Etruriae princeps*—“Vortumnus stands there because he is the chief god of Etruria.” This is the source of much later confidence about an Etruscan origin, but Varro supplies no Etruscan text or linguistic derivation. **[EA as Roman antiquarian testimony; LS as an account of origins]**

Modern etymology remains unsettled. Derivation directly from Latin *vertere* neatly fits the Augustan god but may be secondary remodeling. Derivation from an Etruscan name is possible but lacks a decisive sequence of securely read inscriptions. The proposed connection with the Etruscan gentilicial name *ultimni*, Latinized *Veldumnius*, has been rejected in modern reference scholarship. **[MSR, disputed]**

### Epithets and cult titles

No extensive epigraphic catalogue of distinctive epithets comparable to those of Jupiter or Hercules survives.

- **deus Etruriae princeps** — “chief god of Etruria,” Varro, *Ling.* 5.46. This is an antiquarian description, not a demonstrated Etruscan cult title. **[LS]**
- **Vortumnus in Aventino** — calendrical localization, “Vortumnus on the Aventine.” **[EA]**
- **Vortumnus in Loreto maiore** — “Vortumnus in the Greater Laurel Grove,” in the *Fasti Vallenses*. **[EA]**
- **Vertumnus / Vortumnus** itself functions without a surviving fixed epithet at the Vicus Tuscus.

“God of autumn,” “god of commerce,” “god of gardeners,” and “patron of benevolence” are descriptive modern labels, not securely documented Roman epithets. **[MSR/MI]**

### Classification and identifications

Roman authors consistently make Vertumnus a male god. Propertius allows the statue to adopt female clothing and identities, but grammatical and narrative gender remain male. Claims that Vertumnus was originally an androgynous “Great Goddess” derive principally from early twentieth-century comparativism, notably Jean Przyluski’s 1935 argument, not from an Etruscan theological text. [Przyluski](https://www.persee.fr/doc/rhita_0080-2603_1935_num_3_21_1002)

No secure ancient *interpretatio graeca* equates Vertumnus with a Greek god. Comparisons with Proteus arise from metamorphosis; comparisons with Dionysus/Bacchus from fruit and wine; comparisons with Priapus or Silvanus from gardens; comparisons with Janus from turning and transitions. These are analogies or late learned identifications, not established Roman equations.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The evidentiary problem

The traditional cult history begins in Rome’s regal period or in 264 BCE, but the surviving sources are much later. The dossier must therefore distinguish:

- the date an ancient author assigns to an event;
- the date of the author;
- the date of the surviving manuscript or inscription.

### Earliest evidence

#### Cicero, *In Verrem* 2.1.154 — 70 BCE

**[EA]** Cicero defines a ceremonial route from the statue of Vertumnus to the Circus Maximus. He gives no myth, genealogy, festival, or divine function. What it proves is that by 70 BCE the *signum Vertumni* was a sufficiently well-known fixed landmark to identify a Roman route.

#### Varro, *De lingua Latina* 5.46 and 5.74 — c. 47–45 BCE

**[EA/LS]** At 5.46 Varro connects the statue, the Vicus Tuscus, Etruscan settlers, and an Etruscan “chief god.” At 5.74 he includes Vortumnus among gods whose Roman altars annalistic tradition attributed to the Sabine king Titus Tatius. The two notices preserve incompatible—or at least differently framed—origin stories:

- **Etruscan localization:** the statue belongs in the Tuscan Street because Vortumnus is Etruria’s principal god.
- **Sabine/regal introduction:** Tatius vowed an altar to Vortumnus among numerous Sabine-smelling cults.

Neither constitutes contemporary regal-period evidence. Both are late Republican antiquarian reconstructions. [Varro, *De lingua Latina* V](https://readancienttexts.com/varro/de_lingva_latina/1)

#### The Aventine foundation of 264 BCE

Calendars and Festus support an Aventine cult; modern scholarship generally attributes its temple to M. Fulvius Flaccus after his conquest of Volsinii in 264 BCE. Festus, epitomizing the Augustan scholar Verrius Flaccus, knew a painting of Fulvius in triumphal dress in the temple. No surviving contemporary foundation inscription explicitly says that Fulvius founded the temple or performed an *evocatio*. The inference is strong but not certain. The date and documentary issues are summarized in the archaeological treatment of the Aventine sanctuary. [École française de Rome](https://books.openedition.org/efr/59965)

### Principal corpus

#### Horace, *Satires* 2.7.14 — c. 30 BCE

The inconsistent Priscus is *Vertumnis…iniquis*—“born under every adverse Vertumnus.” The plural is comic: numerous “Vertumni” signify changes of dress, rank, place, and appetite. **[EA literary reception]** It proves that mutability was already an intelligible association by the 30s BCE, not that Romans worshipped a plurality of Vertumni.

#### Horace, *Epistles* 1.20.1 — c. 20 BCE

The poet’s book longs to go *Vertumnum Ianumque*, “to Vertumnus and Janus,” meaning the booksellers’ or market area. **[EA]** Vertumnus again functions as urban topography and commercial geography, not necessarily as a god of commerce. [Latin text](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/horace/epist1.shtml)

#### Propertius, *Elegies* 4.2 — c. 16 BCE

This is the richest single source for the Roman god. The Vicus Tuscus statue speaks in the first person. It claims:

- Tuscan birth and Volsinian origin;
- contentment with a modest site overlooking the Forum;
- offerings of first grapes, grain, cherries, plums, mulberries, pears, and apples;
- endless competence in assumed attire—girl, man, reaper, soldier, fisherman, charioteer, Bacchic celebrant, Apollo-like musician;
- association with shopkeepers and market exchange;
- origin as a maple-wood image and later recasting in bronze by the legendary craftsman Mamurius;
- power to assume forms fittingly: *omnia vertuntur; certe vertentis honorem / nomen ab eventu patria lingua dedit*, in the poem’s climactic etymological play.

Propertius is not a neutral cult manual. The poem’s contradictory aetiologies, erotic costume changes, talking statue, and self-conscious play with elegiac identity are literary constructions. Yet it remains primary evidence for what an Augustan audience could recognize or accept about the statue. [Translation](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/PropertiusBkFour.php)

#### Ovid, *Fasti* 6.395–416 — composed c. 2 BCE–8 CE, later revised

Ovid places the statue beside the former marshes and procession route near the Forum. He offers the diverted-river etymology and mentions the god’s many forms. **[LE]** This is a topographical aetiology, not evidence that Vertumnus historically engineered Rome’s drainage.

#### Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.622–771 — completed c. 8 CE

This is the sole full surviving Vertumnus myth. Vertumnus repeatedly enters Pomona’s orchard disguised as a reaper, haymaker, ploughman, vine-dresser, fruit picker, soldier, fisherman, and finally an old woman. The “old woman” praises Vertumnus, kisses Pomona, points to the mutually supporting vine and elm, and narrates the cautionary story of Iphis and Anaxarete. When persuasion fails, Vertumnus discards the disguise and displays his youthful beauty. Pomona is “captured” by the god’s form and feels reciprocal desire.

The poem does not explicitly narrate rape, marriage, or offspring. Nor does Vertumnus carry out the old woman’s threat that a rejected lover’s associates or gods might punish Pomona. [Latin text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0029%3Abook%3D14)

#### Calendars (*fasti*) — principally early Imperial copies

The *Fasti Amiternini*, *Allifani*, *Vallenses*, a calendar fragment associated with the Arval Brethren, and the calendar of Antium place Vortumnus’ temple anniversary on the Ides of August, 13 August, alongside Diana and several other gods. The *Fasti Vallenses* specify *in Loreto maiore*. **[EA]** These records establish a public calendrical commemoration, not the detailed modern “Vertumnalia” rituals often supplied online. [Calendar of Antium](https://www.attalus.org/docs/cil/add_7.html)

#### Festus, *De verborum significatu* 228 Lindsay — second century CE, preserving Verrius Flaccus

Festus reports a painted image of M. Fulvius Flaccus in triumphal dress in the Aventine temple. This is the basis for associating the temple with the victory over Volsinii in 264 BCE. **[LE preserving earlier antiquarian material]**

#### Servius and the expanded Servian tradition — late fourth/fifth century CE

Commentary on Virgil preserves a tradition that sacrifices to Vertumnus caused the Tiber to turn aside. **[LE/LS]** This is late antiquarian aetiology. It may explain or elaborate Ovid’s *averso amne*; it cannot independently establish an archaic hydraulic ritual.

#### John Lydus, *De mensibus* 4.49 — sixth century CE

Lydus preserves late learned material about the god and calendar. **[LS]** It is evidence for Byzantine antiquarian transmission, not direct evidence for early Roman practice.

#### Sources named in later handbooks but not direct Vertumnus witnesses

- Cicero’s *De natura deorum* does not supply a substantive Vertumnus theology; Cicero’s important direct reference is in *In Verrem*.
- Livy’s surviving text documents the *Fanum Voltumnae* and Etruscan assemblies, not an unambiguous equation of Voltumna with Roman Vertumnus.
- Virgil does not narrate Vertumnus’ mythology; later commentary on Virgil is the relevant witness.
- Augustine’s *City of God* discusses Varro’s proliferating functional gods, including **Vitumnus**, the giver of life. *Vitumnus* must not be silently emended or conflated with *Vertumnus*. [Augustine, *City of God* VII](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120107.htm)
- No securely identified passage of Arnobius supplies unique documentary information about Vertumnus. General lists of Roman gods in Christian apologetic must be checked against the Latin rather than cited by association.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

#### Seasonal produce and horticultural succession

**[EA]** Propertius associates the statue with first grapes, ripening grain, cherries, plums, mulberries, pears, apples, garlands, and grafting. Ovid makes Vertumnus familiar with orchards and Pomona’s cultivated trees. “God of seasons” is a reasonable synthesis, especially after Arcimboldo, but no early source assigns him the cosmic regulation of all four seasons.

#### Change, transformation, and appropriate appearance

**[EA/LE]** Horace treats “Vertumni” as instability; Propertius depicts an unlimited wardrobe and a form suitable to every role; Ovid turns costume change into physical metamorphosis. Maurizio Bettini interprets the god less as undifferentiated chaos than as mastery of *decus*: taking the appropriate form at the appropriate time. This is a modern literary reconstruction, not a recovered Roman creed. [BMCR review of Bettini](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2017/2017.08.03/)

#### Market and exchange

**[EA location; MSR for divine function]** The Vicus Tuscus was commercial, and Horace places books on sale “at Vertumnus and Janus.” Propertius refers to shopkeepers’ offerings. Vertumnus therefore belonged physically to a landscape of sale and exchange. Calling him “god of commerce” may overconvert location and poetic wordplay into a formal jurisdiction.

#### River-turning and urban drainage

**[LE/LS]** Ovid and Servius connect his name with a turned or diverted Tiber. This is aetiological lore, not a documented engineering cult.

### Form and recognition

The ancient god has no securely established, unique iconographic type. Propertius’ very point is that any costume fits him. He can appear:

- in Coan silk as a young woman;
- in a toga;
- with sickle and hay;
- with weapons;
- with fishing rod;
- as charioteer;
- carrying baskets or fruit;
- with Bacchic grapes or an Apollo-like instrument.

The original Vicus Tuscus image was, in Propertius’ account, maple wood and later bronze. Propertius attributes the bronze to Mamurius Veturius, Rome’s legendary ritual smith. **[LE/poetic antiquarianism]** No extant bronze can securely be identified as that statue.

Proposed identifications of ancient statuettes from Isola di Fano, Nemi, and Cortona have been rejected or judged ungrounded; the pieces are more plausibly Mercury, Silvanus, or another figure. [Treccani, ancient art](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/vertumno_%28Enciclopedia-dell%27-Arte-Antica%29/)

The labeled Etruscan mirror figure **Veltune** is iconographically important for Etruscan religion but becomes an image of “Vertumnus” only if the disputed theological equation is accepted.

### Symbols and associated objects

Securely source-based associations include fruit, grapes, grain, pruning or harvesting tools, agricultural dress, baskets, grafted trees, vine and elm, and mutable clothing. The later cornucopia is natural in European art but is not a securely exclusive ancient emblem.

No ancient source securely gives Vertumnus:

- a sacred animal;
- an exclusive color;
- a planet or zodiacal house;
- a mystical number;
- a weekly day;
- a canonical weapon;
- a magical seal.

Modern lists that do so are **[MI]** unless accompanied by an independently verifiable historical source.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No surviving ancient source gives Vertumnus parents, siblings, birth narrative, divine children, or a place in a genealogical dynasty. His “Tuscan birth” in Propertius is ethnic and civic, not biological.

Pomona is his desired partner in Ovid. The poem ends with mutual erotic attraction but does not explicitly call them husband and wife. Their routine description as a married divine couple is a later interpretive compression. No offspring are attested.

Vertumnus has no securely documented subordinate spirits, mount, priestly messenger, or divine enemy.

### Every documented divine interaction or joint episode

- **Pomona — courtship through disguises; final revelation and reciprocated desire.** Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.622–771, c. 8 CE. **[LE; later canonical literary myth]**
- **Pomona — shared orchard and horticultural setting, with Vertumnus admitted in disguise.** Same source and date. No marriage ceremony or child is narrated. **[LE]**
- **Silvanus — co-suitor of Pomona in Ovid’s introductory catalogue.** Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.639–641. No direct meeting or conversation with Vertumnus is described. **[LE; joint narrative context only]**
- **Picus — co-suitor of Pomona.** Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.320–396 and 14.622–641 place Picus within the larger Italian narrative and name him among Pomona’s admirers; no direct Vertumnus–Picus encounter occurs. **[LE]**
- **Pan — the rustic Pans are among Pomona’s unsuccessful suitors.** Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.638–641. No direct interaction with Vertumnus is recorded. **[LE; collective joint context]**
- **Satyrs/Fauns — unsuccessful suitors in the same catalogue.** No direct encounter with Vertumnus. **[LE; collective joint context]**
- **Tiber/Tiberinus — the river yields or is turned from the ground later occupied by the god’s statue.** Propertius 4.2.7–10; Ovid, *Fasti* 6.409–410; later Servian commentary. The degree of personification varies; this is topographical aetiology, not a narrated combat between gods. **[LE/LS]**
- **Bacchus/Iacchus — Vertumnus can be mistaken for a Bacchic figure when furnished with grapes.** Propertius 4.2.29–32. This is assumed iconography, not a meeting or identification. **[EA literary representation]**
- **Apollo/Phoebus — Vertumnus can look Apollo-like when given an instrument.** Propertius 4.2.31–32. No interaction or stable syncretism is attested. **[EA literary representation]**
- **Janus — their statues or districts are paired as booksellers’/market landmarks.** Horace, *Epistles* 1.20.1. No mythic encounter is recorded. Early-modern writers later argued that they were the same cosmic turning god; that is **[MA/SI]**, not ancient consensus.
- **Diana, Fortuna Equestris, Hercules Victor, Castor, Pollux, and the Camenae — share the calendrical date of 13 August in the calendar of Antium.** This is joint commemoration, not narrative interaction. **[EA]**

**Finding from absence:** There is no documented combat, divine council, genealogy, formal marriage, or exchange of gifts between Vertumnus and another individually named god beyond the Pomona courtship narrative.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### A. The speaking statue of the Vicus Tuscus

**Earliest full source:** Propertius 4.2, c. 16 BCE. **[EA literary myth/aetiology]**

A passer-by encounters not a narrator describing a god but the statue speaking for itself. Vertumnus insists that his many outward shapes belong to one divine body. He declares himself Tuscan and Volsinian but content with an unadorned Roman street-shrine. The Tiber once covered the locality; its recession made the Roman commercial and ceremonial landscape possible. The statue receives seasonal first fruits and claims every costume and occupation. It denies misleading popular etymologies, only to substitute further wordplay. At the end it credits Mamurius with its bronze body and commemorates the artisan.

The poem is simultaneously:

- a cult-place document;
- an Augustan aetiological experiment;
- a reflection on Etruscan incorporation into Rome;
- a catalogue of seasonal produce;
- a literary statement about Propertius’ own change of genres;
- a meditation on unstable gendered appearance.

The wooden predecessor, Mamurius’ workmanship, and Volsinian migration are not independently confirmed by contemporary evidence. They must remain **[LE/LS]** even though Propertius presents them in the god’s voice.

### B. Vertumnus and Pomona

**Earliest and only complete ancient account:** Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.622–771, c. 8 CE. **[LE]**

Pomona, a Latin orchard deity, loves cultivation rather than wild woodland and excludes men from her enclosed garden. Numerous rustic divinities desire her, but Vertumnus’ passion is greatest. Because he can assume any shape, he repeatedly approaches without revealing himself.

As an old woman he is finally admitted. He kisses Pomona more ardently than an old woman should, admires the fruit, and points to a vine supported by an elm: apart they are sterile or useless, together fruitful. He advises Pomona to accept a lover and recommends Vertumnus—himself—as youthful, faithful, agriculturally knowledgeable, and devoted only to her.

The disguised god then narrates the story of **Iphis and Anaxarete**. Iphis, a poor Cypriot, loves the aristocratic Anaxarete. She rejects him cruelly; he hangs himself at her door. When she coldly watches his funeral, Venus turns her to stone. The embedded tale is a coercive exemplum: accept love or face divine punishment.

Vertumnus then abandons the old-woman shape and appears radiant, compared to the sun emerging from clouds. He is prepared to compel Pomona but does not need to: his beauty affects her and she feels the same desire.

#### Variants and misstatements

- There is no pre-Ovidian surviving version of this romance. Claims that it was an archaic Etruscan myth are **[MI]**.
- Ovid does not say Pomona was won by the moral force of the Iphis story; her change follows Vertumnus’ bodily revelation.
- Ovid does not explicitly narrate sexual intercourse, rape, marriage, or children.
- The narrative nevertheless contains deception, unauthorized kissing while disguised, and an implicit threat of force. Sanitized children’s versions commonly omit these elements.
- Renaissance and Baroque art overwhelmingly selects the old-woman scene, making disguise and intimate persuasion visually definitive.

### C. The conquest of Volsinii and divine transfer

**Historical kernel:** Rome conquered and destroyed or forcibly relocated Volsinii in 264 BCE; M. Fulvius Flaccus celebrated a triumph. Later evidence associates his triumphal portrait with Vertumnus’ Aventine temple.

**Reconstructed cult narrative:** Fulvius allegedly transferred the patron god of conquered Volsinii to Rome by *evocatio* and founded the Aventine temple. **[MSR]**

This reconstruction is plausible because Roman generals sometimes vowed temples from war-spoils and sometimes ritually invited enemy gods to Rome. But no extant source explicitly narrates an *evocatio of Voltumna/Vertumnus at Volsinii*. The historical conquest, the temple, Varro’s Etruscan attribution, and the similarity of names have been combined into a smooth story that exceeds the direct evidence.

### D. Turning the Tiber

Ovid’s *Fasti* and late commentary connect Vertumnus with the river’s altered course. **[LE/LS]** No independent ritual or inscription confirms this as an archaic myth. It probably belongs to the continuing aetiological contest over the divine name and the formerly marshy Forum.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Vicus Tuscus statue and shrine

**[EA]** The statue stood near the Forum, behind or near the Temple of Castor and the Basilica Julia, on the street leading toward the Velabrum and Circus Maximus. Cicero makes it the northern marker of a route; Varro explains its presence through Etruscan settlement; Propertius makes it oversee the Forum; Pseudo-Asconius places it at the end of the Vicus Turarius beneath the basilica’s corner.

The structure is variously called a statue, sign, shrine, or *sacellum*. No securely excavated architectural plan can be assigned to it. The nearby late-antique base is stronger evidence than speculative reconstruction.

Propertius says shopkeepers made offerings and that the god received seasonal produce. These statements are literary but topographically plausible. There is no evidence of an organized Vicus Tuscus priesthood.

### Aventine temple

**[EA for existence and anniversary; MSR for exact foundation circumstances]**

The temple stood *in Loreto maiore*, in the Greater Laurel Grove on the Aventine. Several calendars mark its anniversary on 13 August. Festus says it contained a painting of Fulvius Flaccus in triumphal dress. The site has not been securely identified archaeologically.

The conventional foundation in 264 BCE rests on Fulvius’ Volsinian triumph and the temple painting. It was probably a *manubial* temple funded or adorned from military spoils, but the surviving evidence does not preserve the vow or dedication formula.

### Festival

The 13 August anniversary is secure. The name **Vertumnalia** is commonly applied, but the calendars principally record the temple dedication. No full ancient description survives of:

- a procession peculiar to Vertumnus;
- prescribed sacrificial animal;
- priestly costume;
- games;
- mystery initiation;
- sexual ritual;
- divination;
- human sacrifice.

Modern celebrations involving autumn equinoxes, colored candles, apples, “shape-shifting magic,” or Dionysian rites are **[MI]** unless explicitly presented as new reconstructionist practice. August 13, moreover, is not the autumn equinox.

### Priesthood

No *flamen Vertumnalis* is securely attested. Confusion arises with the **Flamen Volturnalis**, priest of Volturnus. The two offices and gods must not be merged.

### Sacrifice and offerings

Propertius’ fruit, grain, and garlands are the clearest evidence. Servius preserves unspecified sacrifices connected with turning the Tiber. The inscription *Vortumno sacrum* attests a dedication but not its donor or ritual circumstances. [CIL VI 803 record](https://lupa.at/35425)

There is no evidence for human sacrifice, temple prostitution, cultic mutilation, or cult violence in Vertumnus’ Roman worship. The violent context is political: the cult’s possible association with Rome’s conquest of Volsinii and appropriation of enemy sacred prestige.

### Epigraphy

- **CIL VI 803:** *Vortumno sacrum* — “Sacred to Vortumnus.” **[EA]**
- **CIL VI 804:** *Vortumnus temporibus Diocletiani et Maximiani* — “Vortumnus in the time of Diocletian and Maximian.” Found in the sixteenth century near the Vicus Tuscus and conventionally understood as recording restoration under the Tetrarchy, c. 286–305 CE. **[EA]**
- A recent reassessment proposes that the long-lost or misidentified base may survive among holdings of the Museo Nazionale Romano, catalogued under CIL VI 40841. This remains a modern archaeological identification requiring specialist confirmation. [CNR record](https://iris.cnr.it/handle/20.500.14243/526993)

### Theophoric names and cult economy

No substantial series of personal names derived securely from Vertumnus demonstrates broad popular devotion. Etruscan gentilicia resembling Voltumna cannot automatically be treated as theophoric evidence for Roman Vertumnus.

The Vicus Tuscus association with shopkeepers implies small-scale offerings embedded in commercial life. No temple estate, treasury, tariff, or priestly corporation is documented.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Possible Etruscan background: sixth–third centuries BCE

Livy’s *Fanum Voltumnae* was the meeting place of Etruscan councils and festivals. Archaeological work at Campo della Fiera below Orvieto has uncovered a major sanctuary used from the sixth century BCE through the Roman period, widely identified as the fanum. That strengthens the Volsinian context but has not produced an inscription reading “this is the sanctuary of Vertumnus.”

The Tuscania mirror inscribed **Veltune** provides authentic Etruscan evidence for a similarly named divine figure. Interpretations vary: a distinct deity, an aspect or epithet of Tinia, the figure Latin authors called Voltumna, or a name only secondarily related to Vertumnus. The evidence does not decide among them.

### Mid-Republican incorporation: 264 BCE

The conquest of Volsinii and probable establishment or monumentalization of an Aventine cult mark the strongest historical horizon for an Etruscan–Roman convergence. Yet the Vicus Tuscus statue was believed to be older, and Varro separately attributed a Vortumnus altar to Titus Tatius. Modern scholars therefore consider several possibilities:

1. an old Roman/Sabine Vortumnus later overlaid with conquered Voltumna;
2. an Etruscan deity present at Rome before 264;
3. a deity transferred or redefined after Volsinii’s defeat;
4. Augustan antiquarian invention joining formerly separate traditions.

No option is proven.

### Late Republic: 70–20 BCE

Cicero, Varro, and Horace show that the statue was a public landmark and that mutability, Etruscan associations, commerce, and literary play had accrued around it. This is the first securely documented phase.

### Augustan transformation: c. 20 BCE–8 CE

Propertius and Ovid turn a localized cult figure into a sophisticated literary god:

- ethnically Tuscan yet happily Roman;
- humble yet universal in appearance;
- agricultural yet urban and commercial;
- male yet able to embody female roles;
- a statue yet a speaking, desiring agent.

Ovid adds the Pomona romance. This literary phase, rather than any recoverable archaic theology, created the Vertumnus best known afterward.

### Imperial cult continuity: first–third centuries CE

Calendars continued to record the August dedication. The sparse epigraphy suggests survival but not empire-wide prominence. There is no evidence of a major mystery cult, military network, or extensive provincial temple system.

### Tetrarchic restoration: late third/early fourth century CE

CIL VI 804 records attention to “Vortumnus in the time of Diocletian and Maximian.” This is significant evidence that the Vicus Tuscus monument was restored or renewed during the Tetrarchy. The inscription is better evidence for monumental conservation than for the size or vitality of a worshipping community. [Archaeological summary](https://dipantichita.web.uniroma1.it/sites/default/files/allegati/2025-12/2023-74.pdf)

### Christianization and disappearance

No source records a targeted Christian destruction of Vertumnus’ shrine, a martyr conflict there, or a formal prohibition naming his cult. General anti-sacrificial legislation and the transformation of Rome’s public religion in the fourth and fifth centuries provide the wider context. The shrine disappears from the active cult record; antiquarian and poetic texts survive.

Claims that Vertumnus was “demonized,” converted into a saint, or continuously worshipped in rural folklore are **[MI]** unless supported by specific evidence.

### Medieval transmission

Ovid’s manuscripts preserved the Pomona narrative, while mythographic reading treated Vertumnus as a moralized figure of mutability, courtship, and seasonal abundance. There is no demonstrable continuous medieval cult.

### Renaissance revival onward

The recovery and circulation of Ovid, Propertius, and Roman antiquarian literature restored Vertumnus as an artistic and political emblem. This was a revival of texts and images, not uninterrupted pagan practice.

### Present-day worship

Some Roman reconstructionists and eclectic polytheists honor Vertumnus, often on 13 August or at harvest time. The scale is not reliably measurable, and no centralized cult authority exists. Autumn-equinox rites, Dionysian syncretisms, tarot correspondences, and spell systems are documented modern creations, not reconstructed ancient liturgies.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Renaissance and Baroque art

Pontormo’s **Vertumnus and Pomona**, painted c. 1519–1521 for the Medici villa at Poggio a Caiano, transforms Ovid’s orchard into a program of cultivated fertility and Medicean territorial abundance. **[MA]**

Hendrick Goltzius’ 1613 painting depicts Vertumnus as an old woman addressing a nude Pomona; the walking stick and physical intimacy reveal the disguise to the viewer. [Rijksmuseum](https://www.rijksmuseum.nl/en/collection/object/Vertumnus-and-Pomona--8abec3f775869264302ac24acaeb2ee6)

Rubens, Caesar van Everdingen, Ferdinand Bol, Nicolaes Maes, and numerous other seventeenth-century painters returned to the same scene. The image’s popularity made “old woman persuading Pomona” more visually canonical than the god’s public Roman statue.

### Arcimboldo’s *Vertumnus*

Giuseppe Arcimboldo painted **Emperor Rudolf II as Vertumnus** in 1591. The emperor’s face and body are assembled from flowers, fruits, grains, and vegetables. This is not an ancient portrait type but an early-modern political invention: Vertumnus’ ability to unite seasonal diversity becomes an allegory of Rudolf’s universal, fertile, and harmonious dominion. Gregorio Comanini supplied an explanatory poem.

The painting was taken from Prague as Swedish war booty in 1648, entered Swedish royal collections, and is now at Skokloster Castle. [Skokloster Castle](https://skoklostersslott.se/en/the-history-of-skokloster-castle/skokloster-castles-collections/vertumnus-en/) The museum catalogue dates it specifically to 1591. [Swedish History Museum catalogue](https://samlingar.shm.se/object/BD10473C-4D1B-4D89-82D9-4ADD890B7941)

Arcimboldo’s portrait has arguably shaped the modern image of Vertumnus more powerfully than any surviving ancient artwork: the god becomes literally a face composed of all seasons’ produce.

### Opera and decorative arts

**Pomone** by Robert Cambert, libretto by Pierre Perrin, first performed in 1671, is conventionally called the first French opera. Its central plot derives from Vertumnus and Pomona. François Boucher later designed *Vertumnus and Pomona* for the Beauvais tapestry series *Scenes from Operas*, woven in 1758–1776. [Metropolitan Museum of Art](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/219681)

### Early-modern learned syncretism

Annius of Viterbo and other Renaissance mythographers argued from *vertere* and cosmic turning that Janus and Vertumnus were the same deity and, further, aspects of heaven or the year. These arguments belong to early-modern synthetic mythology; some depended on Annius’ notorious pseudo-antique materials. **[MA/SI]**

### Occultism and esotericism

Vertumnus is not a major named power in the principal Renaissance planetary-magical systems, the *Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn*, or standard Solomonic grimoire hierarchies. Modern websites assigning him sigils, tarot cards, crystals, planets, or ritual “energies of transformation” generally provide no premodern source. These correspondences should be classified **[MI]**.

### Neopaganism

Contemporary devotees variously understand Vertumnus as:

- a Roman god honored through reconstructed prayer and offering;
- a seasonal or harvest god;
- a patron of personal transition, gender fluidity, or shape-changing;
- an autumnal form syncretized with Dionysus.

Only the first two have close ancient textual anchors, and even these require reconstruction because no ritual manual survives. The psychological and Dionysian forms are legitimate descriptions of modern practice but not ancient recoveries.

### Modern scholarship, gender, and identity

Late twentieth- and twenty-first-century literary scholarship has emphasized Vertumnus’ shifting gender presentation. Sara Lindheim argues that Propertius uses the god’s costumes to question the construction of gender and the relation between identity and representation. This is a modern critical reading grounded in Propertius’ text, not a claim that Rome possessed a cultic doctrine corresponding to modern gender categories. [Lindheim](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/ramus/article/abs/i-am-dressed-therefore-i-am-vertumnus-in-propertius-42-and-in-metamorphoses-146227711/F8829F4620F035BABECE13D57AD5B26A)

### Nationalist or extremist appropriation

No significant, specifically Vertumnian extremist or nationalist movement is documented. Arcimboldo’s image is imperial propaganda in its original Habsburg setting, but that is not modern extremist appropriation. Broad claims that Vertumnus was a racial “Aryan vegetation god” belong to obsolete comparativist or internet mythology and lack evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### The Etruscan-origin problem

Older scholarship, including Wissowa and many encyclopedia traditions, often treated Vertumnus as simply the Roman form of Etruscan Voltumna. The evidence cited is cumulative:

- Varro calls Vortumnus Etruria’s chief god;
- Propertius calls him Tuscan and Volsinian;
- Voltumna’s federal sanctuary belonged to the Volsinian sphere;
- Fulvius conquered Volsinii in 264 BCE;
- an Aventine temple is conventionally dated to that year;
- Veltune appears on an Etruscan mirror.

The objection is that every link requires interpretation. No bilingual inscription equates Veltune, Voltumna, and Vortumnus; no source narrates an *evocatio*; and Varro and Propertius wrote more than two centuries after the conquest. Rose and Scheid therefore call the Etruscan origin “supposed” and stress that nothing independently confirms it. [OCD](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548214129)

A productive modern model treats the names not as a timeless equation but as an evolving constellation: an older Roman cult, Etruscan names and memories, the political appropriation of Volsinii, and Augustan etymology may have converged gradually.

### Latin versus Etruscan etymology

- **Latin theory:** *Vortumnus/Vertumnus* belongs to *vertere*, with reference to turning, exchange, seasonal succession, or transformation.
- **Etruscan theory:** the Latin name adapts an Etruscan divine name.
- **Hybrid theory:** an Etruscan or Italic name was remodeled in Latin under the influence of *vertere*, producing the Augustan field of meanings.
- **Umbrian/Italic theory:** Radke proposed an “introducing” or transitional god resembling Janus, possibly of Umbrian origin. Modern summaries regard the proposal as interesting but unproven.

### What was Vertumnus god of?

Wissowa and later handbooks commonly reconstructed a vegetation, fruit, and seasonal god. Dumézil treated him within the structure of archaic Roman religion but remained cautious about simple Etruscan derivation. More recent work stresses that the sources document actions and associations rather than a neat portfolio.

Bettini’s *Il dio elegante* reads Propertius’ Vertumnus as the god of fitting transformation and “non-identity.” Reviewers have noted that this sometimes turns a poetic construction into too comprehensive a theology. [BMCR](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/2017/2017.08.03/)

### Temple and *evocatio*

The temple’s association with Fulvius’ triumphal image makes a 264 BCE foundation persuasive. But the often-repeated sentence “Fulvius evoked Voltumna from Volsinii and built Vertumnus’ temple” is a modern synthesis, not a quotation from an ancient source.

### Iconography

The absence of a fixed ancient type is itself significant. Attempts to identify anonymous bronzes by sickle, fruit, bifrontality, or Etruscan provenance have generally exceeded the evidence. The Roman statue’s appearance is known chiefly through Propertius’ poetic description, and that description systematically resists fixed identity.

### Primary evidence: survival and loss

- The Vicus Tuscus cult statue is lost.
- The alleged wooden predecessor is known only from Propertius.
- The Aventine temple has not been securely located.
- The Fulvius painting is lost and known through Festus.
- CIL VI 804 was found in the Renaissance, then considered lost or displaced; recent work proposes a surviving fragment in the Museo Nazionale Romano.
- The original manuscripts of all Republican and Augustan literary sources are lost; the works survive through medieval transmission.
- The Etruscan evidence is fragmentary and linguistically difficult.
- Excavation at Campo della Fiera has greatly improved knowledge of the federal sanctuary landscape without resolving the divine-name equation.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Vertumnus was certainly the supreme god of all Etruscans. **Unsupported simplification.**
- Voltumna was certainly his wife or feminine half. **Speculative antiquarian theory.**
- Vertumnus and Volturnus were the same god. **Unsupported conflation.**
- He had a priest called the Flamen Vertumnalis. **Confusion with Volturnus.**
- The Vertumnalia was an autumn-equinox mystery festival. **Modern invention.**
- Human sacrifice or temple prostitution formed part of his cult. **No evidence.**
- Ovid preserves a pre-Roman Etruscan marriage myth. **No evidence.**
- Arcimboldo reproduces ancient Vertumnus iconography. **False; it is a Mannerist court allegory.**
- Augustine’s *Vitumnus* is Vertumnus. **False conflation of distinct names.**
