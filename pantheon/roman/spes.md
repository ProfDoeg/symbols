# Spes: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Roman goddess (*dea*) and divine personification of hope, expectation, and anticipated benefit.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the state temple vowed and dedicated by Aulus Atilius Calatinus in the Forum Holitorium during the First Punic War, conventionally dated about **258–256 BCE**. Cicero explicitly attributes its consecration to Calatinus; Roman calendars preserve its dedication on **1 August**.  
> **Earlier but retrospective evidence:** Livy and Dionysius of Halicarnassus place a temple of Hope near the Esquiline/Praenestine approach in a narrative set in **477 BCE**. Because both authors wrote centuries later and no contemporary fifth-century evidence survives, this *Spes Vetus* cannot be treated as a securely attested archaic cult.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early/secure]** Contemporary or near-contemporary material, or later testimony confirmed by archaeology, inscriptions, calendars, or independent sources.
- **[Retrospective tradition]** A later source claims an earlier institution or event, without contemporary corroboration.
- **[Imperial development]** An innovation or reformulation securely belonging to the Roman imperial period.
- **[Regional variant]** A local form or association not necessarily valid throughout the Roman world.
- **[Learned identification]** An equation created or articulated by antiquarians, philosophers, mythographers, or modern scholars.
- **[Christian reuse]** Christian employment of the Latin word and personified image, not continued worship of the Roman goddess.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** Scholarly inference from incomplete evidence.
- **[Modern invention/reception]** Post-antique imagery, ritual, or narrative without evidence of ancient continuity.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

**[Early/secure]** *Spēs* is simultaneously an ordinary Latin common noun—“hope,” “expectation,” “prospect,” and sometimes the expected future itself—and the proper name of a goddess. This double reference is characteristic of Roman “divine qualities”: the named benefit, its divine power, its cult image, and the goddess who grants it are not cleanly separable categories.

Cicero’s *De legibus* 2.28, composed in the 50s BCE, explains the rationale of the cult: because expectation of good things raises the mind, “rightly also was Spes consecrated by Calatinus” (*quoniamque exspectatione rerum bonarum erigitur animus, recte etiam Spes a Calatino consecrata est*). This is the earliest surviving explicit theological interpretation of the goddess, though not her earliest cultic evidence. [Cicero, *De legibus* 2.28](https://www.latin.it/autore/cicerone/rhetorica/de_legibus/%2102%21liber_ii/28.lat)

Cicero’s Academic spokesman Cotta supplies an important countervoice in *De natura deorum* 3.61: *mens, spes, fides, virtus,* and *concordia* are things within human beings; their utility and consecrated images are evident, but their possession of divine power remains to be proved. **[Late Republican philosophical dispute]** Roman intellectuals could therefore acknowledge Spes’s temples and images while disputing whether an abstraction was literally a deity. [Cicero, *De natura deorum* 3.61](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cicero/de_Natura_Deorum/3B%2A.html)

### Name and etymology

- **Latin:** *Spēs*, genitive *Speī*; reconstructed Classical pronunciation approximately /speːs/.
- **Meaning:** hope, expectation, anticipation, prospect; also a promising person or thing, particularly a child or heir.
- **[Modern linguistic reconstruction]** De Vaan derives Latin *spēs* from Proto-Italic *spēs* and ultimately connects it with a Proto-Indo-European root conventionally reconstructed as *speh₁-* or a related formation meaning “prosper, succeed.” This is a linguistic ancestry for the noun, not proof that a named Proto-Indo-European goddess Spes existed. No such prehistoric goddess can be reconstructed securely.
- **Greek equivalent:** Ἐλπίς, *Elpis*. **[Learned/syncretistic identification]** Greek writers translated Spes as Elpis, and Roman authors could translate Greek *elpis* as *spes*. The lexical equation does not demonstrate that Rome imported the cult from Greece or that the two figures possessed identical histories.
- **Etruscan:** no securely attested Etruscan divine name has been shown to be the equivalent or source of Spes. The sometimes-invoked Etruscan mirror tradition offers many labeled Greek and Etruscan beings, but no securely identified “Etruscan Spes.” **The proposed Etruscan origin is unestablished.**

### Documented titles and formulations

The epithets are especially abundant on imperial coins. They identify the political subject whose hopes the goddess embodies rather than separate mythological personalities:

- **Spes Augusta / SPES AVGVSTA** — “Augustan Hope” or “Hope associated with the Augustus.” **[Imperial development, from Claudius]** It binds communal confidence to the reigning emperor and dynasty.
- **Spes Augusti / Augustorum** — “Hope of the Augustus/of the Augusti.” **[Imperial development]**
- **Spes Populi Romani / SPES P R** — “Hope of the Roman People.” **[Imperial development]**
- **Spes Publica / SPES PVBLICA** — “Public Hope,” the hope of the state or political community. **[Imperial development]**
- **Spes Reipublicae / SPES REIPVBLICAE** — “Hope of the Commonwealth.” **[Late imperial development]**
- **Bona Spes** — “Good Hope.” **[Cultic/epigraphic and numismatic formulation]**
- **Spes Firma** — “Steadfast Hope.” **[Imperial numismatic formulation]**
- **Spes Perpetua** — “Perpetual Hope.” **[Imperial numismatic formulation]**
- **Spes Felicitatis Orbis** — “Hope of the World’s Prosperity.” **[Imperial numismatic formulation]**
- **Spes Victrix** — “Victorious Hope.” **[Regional imperial formulation]** Attested particularly in the political language and coinage of eastern imperial cities.
- **Spes Vetus** — “Old Hope.” This is primarily a topographical designation for the ancient sanctuary or district near the later Porta Praenestina, not securely an original cult-title from the fifth century BCE.
- **Spes Romana / Spes Romanorum** — “Roman Hope/Hope of the Romans.” **[Late imperial and post-classical political formulation]**

Claudia Perassi’s numismatic study catalogues these legends and emphasizes that Spes could refer to emperor, imperial couple, designated heir, Roman people, or the prosperity of the empire. [Perassi, *Spes. Iconografia, simbologia, ideologia nella moneta romana*](https://publires.unicatt.it/en/publications/spesiconografia-simbologia-ideologia-nella-moneta-romana-i-iii-se-11/)

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The disputed archaic beginning: *Spes Vetus*

**[Retrospective tradition]** Livy 2.51.2 and Dionysius of Halicarnassus 9.24.4 locate fighting against the Etruscans in 477 BCE near a temple of Hope outside Rome. Dionysius says the first battle took place about eight stades from the city “near the temple of Hope,” followed by another near the Colline Gate. [Dionysius, *Roman Antiquities* 9.24](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Dionysius_of_Halicarnassus/9A%2A.html)

Later topography knew the area near the Praenestine Gate as *ad Spem Veterem*, “at Old Hope.” Frontinus, writing around 97 CE, uses it as a hydraulic landmark in *De aquaeductu* 1.5, 19–20. The place-name proves that a sanctuary remembered as old existed by the early imperial period; it does not independently date its foundation to 477 BCE. [Platner–Ashby, “Spes Vetus”](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Places/Europe/Italy/Lazio/Roma/Rome/_Texts/PLATOP%2A/Spes_Vetus.html)

The strongest responsible conclusion is:

- A sanctuary called *Spes Vetus* existed and generated a durable place-name.
- Augustan-era historians placed it in fifth-century events.
- No fifth-century inscription, cult object, or datable building confirms that chronology.
- The often-repeated assertion that the consul Gaius Horatius Pulvillus “founded” it after the battle goes beyond what the surviving passages securely establish.

### The Forum Holitorium temple

**[Early/secure institutional cult]** Cicero records that Calatinus consecrated Spes. Tacitus, *Annals* 2.49, adds that Germanicus rededicated the temple in 17 CE and that Atilius had vowed it in the same war in which he vowed the temple of Fides—that is, the First Punic War. Calatinus was consul in 258 and 254 BCE and censor in 247; 258 BCE is the conventional foundation date, while some reconstructions place dedication in 256 or associate it with a later office. No surviving foundation inscription settles the exact year. [Tacitus, *Annals* 2.49](https://www.ourdecline.com/smartboard/shop/tacitusc/annals/chap2.htm)

The calendars—the *Fasti Arvales, Vallenses, Antiates,* and *Praenestini* as assembled in *CIL* I² and *Inscriptiones Italiae* 13.2—give **1 August** as the temple’s anniversary. This is firmer than the exact foundation year. [Platner–Ashby, “Aedes Spei”](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Places/Europe/Italy/Lazio/Roma/Rome/_Texts/PLATOP%2A/Aedes_Spei.html)

### Republican historical notices

- **218 BCE — [secure literary attestation]:** Livy 21.62.4 reports that the temple in the vegetable market was struck by lightning among prodigies reported at the beginning of the Second Punic War. Livy himself warns that frightened minds readily credited such reports. The passage attests the temple in Rome’s remembered prodigy tradition, not the supernatural occurrence as a historical fact. [Livy 21.62](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Liv.+21.62&lang=original)
- **213 BCE — [secure historical tradition]:** a fire destroyed the temples of Spes, Fortuna, and Mater Matuta. Livy 24.47.15–16 records the fire; 25.7.5–6 records the appointment of commissioners in 212 to rebuild them.
- **179 BCE — [secure literary attestation]:** Livy 40.51.6 reports that the censor Marcus Fulvius Nobilior built a portico behind the Temple of Spes toward the Tiber and the Temple of Apollo Medicus. The manuscript wording has produced a topographical dispute, but it confirms Spes as a civic landmark.
- **110 BCE — [secure epigraphy; regional evidence]:** CIL I² 674 from Capua records magistri overseeing a wall belonging jointly to Spes, Fides, and Fortuna during the consulship of Marcus Minucius Rufus and Spurius Postumius Albinus. This is direct inscriptional evidence rather than literary recollection. [CIL I² 674 translation and record](https://www.attalus.org/docs/cil/dedicat2.html)

### Late Republican theology

- **Cicero, *De legibus* 2.28, c. 50s BCE:** approves consecration of beneficial powers such as Salus, Honos, Ops, Victoria, and Spes.
- **Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.61 and 3.61, 45 BCE:** the Stoic case treats useful divine powers as appropriately worshipped, while Cotta’s Academic critique questions whether internal or desired qualities are gods. The manuscript reading at 2.61 is sometimes disputed between *Spes* and *Fides*; Calatinus certainly appears in the tradition of both cults.
- **Varro, *Antiquitates rerum divinarum*, 40s BCE:** lost. Augustine and other Christian writers preserve fragments and summaries of Varro’s classification of Roman gods. No substantial surviving Varro fragment supplies an independent mythology of Spes.

### Augustan and early imperial poetry

Ovid, Virgil, and other poets use *spes* constantly, sometimes with capitalization supplied only by modern editors. This demands caution: grammatical personification is not automatically cultic invocation.

- **Ovid, *Heroides* 2.9, c. 20 BCE–8 CE:** Phyllis says that Hope is slow to abandon her. **[Literary personification]** This is vivid personification, but not a narrative about the cult goddess.
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 9.468–486, completed c. 8 CE:** *Spes* is personified in the emotional drama surrounding Byblis. **[Literary personification]**
- **Virgil:** uses *spes* as hope, prospect, offspring, or political expectation. No independent Virgilian myth of the goddess survives.
- **Ovid’s *Fasti*:** despite its extensive treatment of Roman festivals, its surviving six books do not preserve a substantial Spes festival narrative. The calendar evidence for 1 August is therefore epigraphic, not an Ovidian myth.

### Imperial and late-antique testimony

- **Tacitus, *Annals* 2.49, composed early second century CE:** principal source for Germanicus’s rededication in 17 CE.
- **Cassius Dio 50.10.3, early third century CE:** reports that the Temple of Hope burned in the disturbances of 31 BCE; his Greek is ναὸς Ἐλπίδος, “temple of Elpis/Hope.”
- **Festus, epitome of Verrius Flaccus:** useful for Roman antiquarian terminology, but no extant Festan passage provides a developed Spes myth.
- **Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, early fifth century CE:** uses *spes* in allegorical explanations—for example, the dog’s head on the image of time signifies the flattering uncertainty of the future—but does not preserve a distinctive cult legend of Spes. [Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 1](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Macrobius/Saturnalia/1%2A.html)
- **Augustine, *City of God*, 413–426 CE:** attacks the multiplication and rationalization of Roman divine powers and repeatedly mines Varro as a witness. His treatment is polemical and late, valuable for the classifications Roman antiquarians transmitted but not neutral evidence of early cult.
- **Arnobius, *Adversus nationes*, c. 300 CE:** likewise preserves details of pagan theology in order to ridicule it. No important independent Spes ritual is securely recoverable from him.

### Epigraphic corpus

The surviving inscriptions include:

- **CIL I² 46, Genzano:** a short Republican dedication, “[to] Spes; given as a gift.” Its brevity proves private or local devotion but provides no theology.
- **CIL I² 2689:** a damaged dedication presenting an object to Spes.
- **CIL I² 674, Capua, 110 BCE:** a public wall or precinct work for Spes, Fides, and Fortuna.
- **Early imperial calendar inscriptions:** record the August dedication anniversary.
- **Imperial dedications to Spes Augusta/Bona Spes:** establish cultic use beyond coin legends, although many isolated examples lack recoverable ritual context.

The online Attalus index conveniently gathers the principal Republican references but is an index and translation aid, not a substitute for the *CIL* editions. [Spes in ancient sources](https://www.attalus.org/names/s/spes.html)

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domain

**[Republican core]** Spes governs favorable expectation: the interval between present uncertainty and hoped-for success. In civic cult this meant military survival, victory, political security, fertility, and future prosperity. The First Punic War setting is significant: Calatinus’s temple monumentalized hope during an uncertain overseas war, although no source gives a detailed oracle or battlefield epiphany.

**[Imperial development]** The domain narrowed politically around dynastic continuity. Children and designated heirs could be called the emperor’s or the state’s *spes*. Spes consequently became a visual assertion that a youthful Caesar, imperial marriage, birth, adoption, or new reign promised future stability. Nicholas Purcell summarizes the imperial cult’s particular relation to the safety of the younger generation and succession. [Oxford Classical Dictionary, “Spes”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/550499328)

**[Regional evidence]** At Minturnae and other Italian sites, dedications show forms of hope rooted in family, local prosperity, and individual petition rather than exclusively imperial ideology. It is therefore unsafe to reduce all worship to state propaganda.

### Standard image

**[Imperial iconographic canon]** Spes normally appears as a youthful, fully clothed woman:

- standing or advancing, almost always toward the viewer’s left on coins;
- extending a flower or bud in her right hand;
- lifting a fold or hem of her garment with her left;
- rendered in an intentionally archaizing style, recalling an Archaic Greek *kore*.

The flower signifies the promise of fruit rather than fruit already obtained: potential, growth, birth, and futurity. Raising the skirt is primarily an archaizing garment gesture; eroticized modern descriptions of it as a coquettish display go beyond the image’s probable cultic meaning.

Perassi argues that Claudius’s coin engravers may have drawn upon the cult statue installed in the restored Forum Holitorium temple dedicated by Germanicus in 17 CE. This is a plausible but **[modern scholarly reconstruction]**, since the statue itself has not survived and no ancient author states that the coin copies it. [Perassi abstract](https://publicatt.unicatt.it/handle/10807/36229)

### Coinage

**[Secure material record]** Spes’s standard named appearance on Roman imperial coinage begins with Claudius, particularly sestertii of 41–54 CE bearing **SPES AVGVSTA**. A surviving example dated 50–54 CE shows her walking left, flower raised in the right hand, skirt lifted by the left. [Vassar College Claudian sestertius](https://emuseum.vassar.edu/objects/16112/sestertius-roman-empire-claudius-5054-ce)

The type then persisted for nearly three centuries. It appears under emperors and empresses, on coins for heirs, and in combinations such as:

- Spes alone as imperial or public hope;
- Spes facing or offering a flower to a Caesar;
- Spes with soldiers, linking hope to military loyalty;
- legends referring to public, dynastic, perpetual, or universal hope.

The Online Coins of the Roman Empire database documents the breadth of the type across reigns. [OCRE Spes results](https://numismatics.org/ocre/results?q=deity_facet%3A%22Spes%22)

The suggestion that an earlier coin of Drusus carried Spes is misleading if taken as a contemporary lifetime issue: relevant pieces belong to Claudius’s commemorative coinage. The securely named canonical image is Claudian.

### Sculpture and glyptic

Figures of archaizing maidens holding flowers are often identified as Spes, but attribution is strongest when supported by an inscription, coin-like attributes, or secure context.

The “Hope Dionysus” in the Metropolitan Museum contains a small archaizing female support that **may** represent Spes. The museum explicitly treats the identification as uncertain; the statue also underwent modern restoration by Vincenzo Pacetti. It cannot be used as uncomplicated evidence for a particular ancient myth involving Spes and Dionysus. [Metropolitan Museum, “Hope Dionysus”](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/255973)

### Attributes not securely ancient

- **Anchor:** standard for Christian and early-modern Hope, but not the defining attribute of the pagan Roman goddess.
- **Green clothing:** a medieval/early-modern color convention, not an established Roman cult prescription.
- **Cornucopia, grain, or children:** occasional or derivative expressions of hoped-for abundance and succession; they do not replace the flower-and-hem canonical type.
- **Crow:** sometimes repeated in modern summaries as a symbol of longevity, but the claim lacks a firmly identified early primary source and should not be treated as standard.
- **Sacred day:** 1 August is the temple anniversary. It does not make every 1 August observance a universal “festival of Spes.”
- **Sacred animal, number, planet, zodiac sign, or magical correspondence:** none is securely documented for ancient Spes.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

**[Absence of evidence]** No Roman source gives Spes parents, birth, consort, children, siblings, servants, mount, or a stable divine household. Roman divine qualities did not require genealogical embedding.

Greek *Elpis* appears in poetic and mythographic settings, most famously in Hesiod’s Pandora narrative, but Hesiod gives her no Roman genealogy. Equating every Greek statement about Elpis with Spes creates a synthetic biography unsupported by Roman evidence.

### Greek equation

**Elpis — [learned/syncretistic identification]:** Greek Ἐλπίς translates Latin *Spes*, and Cassius Dio uses the Greek name for Rome’s Temple of Hope. This is an ancient linguistic and interpretive equivalence, not evidence that the Forum Holitorium cult was imported from a Greek sanctuary.

### Every documented divine interaction or joint association

Because Spes has no surviving narrative mythology, the record consists chiefly of joint cult and iconographic association:

- **Fides — joint civic consecration:** Tacitus says Atilius vowed temples of Hope and Faith in the First Punic War; at Capua, Spes and Fides shared a precinct work in 110 BCE. Source: Tacitus, *Annals* 2.49; CIL I² 674. **[Republican cult association; secure]**
- **Fortuna — joint Capuan cult:** the 110 BCE Capuan inscription assigns a wall to Spes, Fides, and Fortuna. Source: CIL I² 674. **[Regional Republican cult association; secure]**
- **Fortuna and Mater Matuta — shared disaster and rebuilding, not mythic action:** their neighboring temples burned with Spes’s temple in 213 BCE and were assigned for reconstruction in 212. Source: Livy 24.47.15–16; 25.7.5–6. **[Historical joint occurrence; secure literary tradition]**
- **Victoria — shared political-semantic field and occasional imperial visual association:** imperial imagery could coordinate Hope and Victory, and calendar material places dedications to Spes and Victoriae on 1 August. No surviving myth stages a meeting or contest. **[Imperial cult association]**
- **Salus — conceptual and sometimes regional cult association:** both represent benefits expected from divine power; imperial imagery and local dedications could coordinate them. No narrative interaction survives. **[Cultic/ideological association]**
- **Felicitas — imperial ideological association:** Spes could appear in programs promising future prosperity. There is no story in which the two goddesses act together. **[Imperial learned and iconographic association]**
- **Dionysus/Bacchus — uncertain sculptural juxtaposition:** the small female support in the “Hope Dionysus” may be Spes, but no text explains an episode between them. **[Uncertain iconographic association]**
- **Pandora — no Roman interaction securely attested:** later Latin and Renaissance retellings translate the remaining *Elpis* as *Spes*, but no early Roman cult text makes Pandora an associate of the Roman goddess. **[Learned translation; later reception]**
- **Jupiter/Zeus — no Roman interaction:** Hesiod says Zeus’s will keeps Elpis inside Pandora’s jar. Applying this directly to Spes is Greek-to-Roman harmonization, not a Roman myth. **[Greek literary narrative only]**

No securely attested interaction exists between Spes and Janus or Juno Sospita merely because their temples stood beside hers.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### The central finding: Spes has no surviving Roman myth-cycle

**[Secure negative finding]** No extant Roman epic, hymn, ritual text, or antiquarian account tells of Spes’s birth, marriage, combat, journey, punishment, miracle, or intervention. Her documented history is cultic, topographical, political, and iconographic. Constructing a full divine biography by borrowing Elpis’s Greek appearances would erase precisely what is distinctive about Roman Spes.

### The Pandora story

**[Greek early tradition]** Hesiod’s *Works and Days* 90–105, late eighth or early seventh century BCE, says that when the woman later called Pandora opened the great storage jar, evils escaped; Elpis alone remained beneath its rim because the lid closed by Zeus’s will. Hesiod does not say unambiguously whether retaining Elpis protects humanity, deprives it of hope, or preserves deceptive expectation. That interpretive dispute is ancient in potential but largely modern in explicit form.

**[Roman/Latin reception]** Latin translators render *elpis* as *spes*, producing the impression that the Roman goddess participated in the story. Pseudo-Hyginus’s *Fabulae* 142 gives a compressed Latin Pandora account but does not furnish a developed Roman cult myth of Spes. [Hyginus, *Fabulae* 142](https://cal.byu.edu/macfarlane/OGCMA/PandoraANCIENT_Hyginus.htm)

**[Renaissance accretion]** Erasmus helped establish “Pandora’s box” by translating the Greek *pithos*, a large jar, as *pyxis*, box. Andrea Alciato’s *Emblemata* and Gabriele Faerno’s sixteenth-century fables then visualized or narrated Latin *Spes* remaining behind. Giulio Bonasone’s engraving made female Roman virtues fly from the vessel while flower-bearing Spes remained. These are important receptions, but not evidence for an ancient Roman Spes myth. [Pandora reception history](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pandora%27s_box)

### The temple vow as quasi-narrative

**[Republican historical cult tradition]** The closest Spes comes to an indigenous Roman “story” is the First Punic War vow:

1. Calatinus, campaigning during a dangerous war, vows a temple to Hope.
2. The temple is established in the Forum Holitorium.
3. It is repeatedly endangered—lightning in 218, fire in 213, fire in 31 BCE.
4. Civic authorities repeatedly restore it.
5. Germanicus rededicates it in 17 CE.
6. Claudius, Germanicus’s brother, subsequently makes Spes Augusta a major dynastic coin type.

The last ideological connection is a strong historical inference, not an ancient narrative explicitly stated by Tacitus.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Forum Holitorium

**[Secure]** Spes’s principal temple stood in the Forum Holitorium, Rome’s vegetable market, near the Carmental Gate and the Tiber. Its location integrated the goddess into commerce, food supply, military departure routes, and the monumental zone around the later Theatre of Marcellus.

The principal phases are:

- vowed and dedicated by Calatinus during the First Punic War;
- struck by lightning in 218 BCE;
- burned in 213 and rebuilt beginning in 212;
- affected by the fire of 31 BCE;
- restored under Augustus and rededicated by Germanicus in 17 CE.

### Which surviving temple is hers?

Three Republican temples are incorporated into or preserved beside San Nicola in Carcere. Their assignment to Janus, Juno Sospita, and Spes remains disputed.

- Platner and Ashby favored the central Ionic temple for Spes.
- Filippo Coarelli and other modern topographers identify Spes with the southernmost, Doric, hexastyle temple.
- The fire pattern of 213 BCE and the relationship to the Sant’Omobono sanctuary are used in support of the southern identification.

Thus the frequently reproduced photograph of “the Temple of Spes” rests on a persuasive but not universally accepted topographical reconstruction. [Digital Augustan Rome, “Spes, Aedes”](https://www.digitalaugustanrome.org/records/spes-aedes/)

### Festival and calendar

**[Secure]** The temple’s *dies natalis* was **1 August**, preserved in multiple fasti as *Spei in Foro Holitorio* or *Spei ad Forum Holitorium*. Victoria and Victoria Virgo also had anniversaries on that date. The calendars establish public commemoration but do not describe procession, sacrifice, officiant, victim, hymn, or public games.

### Priesthood

**[Absence of evidence]** No distinct permanent priesthood of Spes is securely documented. The temple would have operated within normal civic religious administration, involving magistrates, public slaves or attendants, and pontifical oversight as appropriate, but the surviving evidence does not authorize a detailed institutional reconstruction.

### Sacrifice and offerings

**[Secure in general, specific details absent]** The dedication formulae and temple status imply offerings and sacrifice according to Roman practice. CIL I² 46 and 2689 prove gifts to Spes. What those gifts were, who offered them, and whether a prescribed animal victim belonged to her cult cannot normally be recovered.

There is no evidence for:

- human sacrifice to Spes;
- temple prostitution;
- sexual initiation;
- ecstatic possession;
- an oracle of Spes;
- mystery rites;
- a distinct magical cult;
- ritual violence by her devotees.

Their absence should not be filled with generic practices borrowed from other Roman cults.

### Capua

**[Regional/secure]** CIL I² 674 shows civic or collegial magistri constructing a wall for Spes, Fides, and Fortuna in 110 BCE. The inscription documents a shared sacred facility or precinct but does not establish that the three formed a universal Roman triad. [Capuan dedication](https://www.attalus.org/docs/cil/dedicat2.html)

### Other cult sites

Literary, epigraphic, and antiquarian compilations report Spes at Ostia, Gabii, Antium, Aricia, Genzano, and other Italian communities. The evidence varies from temples or porticoes to short dedications. These demonstrate geographic diffusion but not uniform theology. Treccani’s older synthesis notes Ostia, a portico at Gabii associated with Salus, and evidence from Antium, Aricia, and Capua. [Treccani, “Spes”](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/spes_%28Enciclopedia-Italiana%29/)

### Economics and politics

The Forum Holitorium location placed the temple within a market and port district, but it does not prove that Spes began as a vegetable or garden goddess. Wissowa rejected the older agricultural-origin theory and interpreted her more generally as the power petitioned for fulfillment of desire. The flower could support agricultural interpretation, but the canonical image is imperial and centuries later than the original temple.

Calatinus’s military career, the First Punic War, and later imperial coinage make political expectation central. Yet private dedications show that the goddess was not merely an official slogan.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Phase I: alleged archaic cult, fifth century BCE

**[Retrospective tradition]** Livy and Dionysius locate a temple of Hope in events of 477 BCE. The later name *Spes Vetus* supports antiquity but cannot verify the precise date, founder, or original form.

### Phase II: secure Republican state cult, mid-third century BCE

**[Early/secure]** Calatinus’s Forum Holitorium temple is the first reconstructable institutional cult. Its emergence belongs to a larger Middle Republican expansion of temples to divine qualities—Victoria, Spes, Fides, Honos, and related powers—during Rome’s Italian and Mediterranean wars.

### Phase III: resilience during the Punic Wars, 218–212 BCE

The lightning strike of 218 and fire of 213 were treated within the Roman prodigy and restoration system. Rebuilding was a civic obligation. Hope’s temple was materially vulnerable precisely while “hope” carried intense wartime resonance.

### Phase IV: Italian diffusion and civic association, second–first centuries BCE

Capua’s 110 BCE dedication establishes Spes beyond Rome and associates her with Fides and Fortuna. Short Republican dedications demonstrate individual and local engagement. Philosophers meanwhile debated whether such qualities were genuinely divine or human conditions honored by images.

### Phase V: Augustan rebuilding and Julio-Claudian dynastic transformation, 31 BCE–54 CE

After the temple fire of 31 BCE, Augustan reconstruction restored the sanctuary. Germanicus rededicated it in 17 CE. Under Claudius, Spes appears as **Spes Augusta** on imperial bronze coinage in the flower-and-raised-hem form.

**[Imperial development]** This was not simply the old cult unchanged. Hope was now increasingly directed toward the emperor, his children, designated successors, and continuation of the ruling house. Claudius’s relationship to the popular Germanicus gave the type special dynastic force.

### Phase VI: empire-wide political language, first–third centuries CE

Spes became a flexible coin type under successive dynasties. She could signal:

- the promise of a new reign;
- the emperor’s children or successor;
- restoration after civil war;
- military expectation;
- public security and abundance;
- continuity of the Roman order.

Because coins circulated widely, their imagery is the largest surviving visual corpus for Spes. It is evidence for official messaging, not a direct measure of private belief.

### Phase VII: late-imperial abstraction and Christian recoding, fourth century

The conventional figure gradually yielded to legends such as *spes publica* and *spes rei publicae*. A bronze issue of Constantine from Constantinople, around 327 CE, paired **SPES PVBLICA** with a chi-rho. Here the familiar Roman political vocabulary is recoded through an explicitly Christian emblem: public hope remains, but the pagan goddess need not. [Stanford Humanities Center, “Worshipping Hope”](https://shc.stanford.edu/arcade/interventions/worshipping-hope-versions-spes-roman-empire)

### Phase VIII: cultic extinction and semantic survival, fourth–sixth centuries

Imperial anti-sacrifice and anti-temple legislation progressively deprived traditional cults of public support. No surviving decree singles out Spes’s temple, and there is no documented dramatic destruction or forced conversion of a Spes congregation. The correct finding is institutional disappearance without a Spes-specific martyrdom or suppression story.

The Latin noun survived everywhere in Christian scripture, theology, preaching, inscriptions, and liturgy. This was not straightforward continuity of worship. Christian *spes* was redirected from military victory and dynastic futurity toward resurrection, salvation, and eternal life.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Christian virtue

**[Christian reuse]** In the Latin New Testament and patristic tradition, *spes* translates Greek ἐλπίς. Paul’s triad of faith, hope, and love—*fides, spes, caritas*—made hope one of the three theological virtues. Romans 5:5, “hope does not disappoint,” and 1 Corinthians 13:13 became central texts.

Christian Hope is not the pagan goddess baptized under another name. Its object and theology are different: God, Christ’s promises, resurrection, and eternal life. The lexical continuity nevertheless allowed artists to retain a female personification.

The modern Catholic formulation defines hope as the theological virtue by which believers desire heaven and eternal life and rely on divine grace. [Catechism of the Catholic Church §§1812–1821](https://www.vatican.va/content/catechism/en/part_three/section_one/chapter_one/article_7/ii_the_theological_virtues.html)

### Anchor iconography

**[Late-antique and medieval Christian development]** Hebrews 6:19 calls hope “an anchor of the soul.” The anchor consequently became the dominant Christian attribute of Hope. It sometimes appears with a cross or proper names derived from *spes* or *elpis*. This is a biblical-Christian symbol, not a demonstrated attribute of the Republican goddess. [Catholic Encyclopedia, “The Anchor”](https://www.newadvent.org/cathen/01462a.htm)

### Renaissance emblem books

**[Early-modern systematization]** Cesare Ripa’s *Iconologia*, first published in 1593 and repeatedly expanded, codified Hope as a woman with flowers, plants, distinctive colored clothing, and an anchor. Ripa fused classical personification, Christian theology, natural symbolism, and emblematic invention. The combination shaped European art but must not be projected back onto the Forum Holitorium cult. [Ripa, “Speranza”](https://www.dbnl.org/tekst/pers001cesa01_01/pers001cesa01_01_0379.php)

The Renaissance Pandora tradition similarly transformed Greek Elpis into flower-bearing Latin Spes and supplied a visual “myth” more elaborate than anything surviving from Roman cult.

### Neoclassicism

Bertel Thorvaldsen modeled his *Goddess of Hope* in 1817 after working with archaic sculpture. The frontal, stylized figure holds a pomegranate blossom, combining the ancient flower attribute with modern neoclassical ideas of fertility and eternal life. The marble in the Thorvaldsens Museum was executed posthumously in 1851–1859 from his model. **[Modern reception]** It is not a copy of an identified ancient cult statue. [Thorvaldsens Museum catalogue](https://kataloget.thorvaldsensmuseum.dk/en/A46)

### Literature, political language, and public monuments

*Spes* remains common in Latin mottos:

- *spes mea* — “my hope”;
- *dum spiro spero* — “while I breathe, I hope”;
- *ave crux, spes unica* — “hail, cross, sole hope”;
- *spes non confundit* — “hope does not disappoint.”

These usually belong to Christian, civic, familial, or educational traditions rather than worship of Spes.

Female Hope figures with anchors became common on tombs, civic memorials, churches, and allegorical paintings. Their appearance often owes more to Hebrews and Ripa than to Roman coins.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist practice

**[Modern revival]** Contemporary practitioners of *religio Romana* may honor Spes on 1 August, reproduce Roman prayer structures, or invoke her for personal and communal hope. Such worship is a revival reconstructed from calendars, inscriptions, and general Roman ritual—not an unbroken lineage.

No reliable census isolates Spes worshippers, and she is not demonstrably central to large modern pagan organizations. Elaborate online correspondences assigning her crystals, tarot cards, planets, chakras, colors, or spells are **modern inventions** unless their creators explicitly identify them as personal practice.

### Occultism and the Golden Dawn

The word *Spes* has occurred as an initiatory motto—“Hope”—in modern esoteric orders, but no substantial Golden Dawn ritual system centered on the Roman goddess has been demonstrated. Claims that Spes possessed an ancient planetary magic, grimoire seal, or fixed ceremonial correspondence are unsupported.

### Political and extremist appropriation

Roman imperial formulas such as *spes publica* and *spes rei publicae* naturally recur in nationalist and authoritarian rhetoric because they promise collective renewal. No well-documented modern extremist cult specifically centered on Spes emerged from the contested-material search. Generic use of Roman imagery should not be relabeled a cult of this goddess without direct evidence.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Spes originally agricultural?

An older interpretation made her a vegetation or garden goddess: the flower promised fruit, and hope was linked with crops and children. Wissowa argued instead that the core was the broader fulfillment of desired outcomes. The evidence favors caution:

- the name transparently means hope;
- the First Punic War temple has a military-political context;
- the flower-bearing canonical image does not appear securely until Claudius;
- ordinary *spes* could describe crops, offspring, victory, liberty, or security.

Agricultural expectation is therefore one application, not a demonstrated prehistoric essence.

### Is *Spes Vetus* proof of a fifth-century cult?

Purcell and other reference works accept that Spes was worshipped by the fifth century on the authority of Livy 2.51.2. More skeptical treatments emphasize that Livy and Dionysius are Augustan authors narrating the fifth century and may retroject later topography. The defensible formulation is “fifth-century according to annalistic tradition; materially secure only later.” [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/550499328)

### Which temple survives?

Platner–Ashby’s central-temple identification competes with Coarelli’s southern Doric identification. Modern maps often present the latter as settled, but the disagreement should remain visible. The archaeological remains prove the monumental context; their assignment among Spes, Janus, and Juno Sospita is reconstructed.

### Was Claudius’s Spes copied from the cult statue?

Perassi argues yes, linking the coin type to the image installed after Germanicus’s restoration. Its archaizing style makes this attractive, and the dynastic connection is powerful. Nevertheless, no inscribed statue or ancient testimony confirms the copying. It remains an influential modern reconstruction.

### Greek and Etruscan origins

The equation Spes = Elpis is ancient and linguistically obvious. Deriving the Roman cult itself from Greek Elpis is harder: the Roman goddess had a stronger civic cult and more positive public profile than Greek literary Elpis.

No labeled Etruscan mirror securely supplies Spes’s predecessor. Etruscan mirrors are valuable for tracing Greek myth in Italy, but an unlabeled archaizing woman or flower-holder cannot automatically be called Spes. Claims of a specific Etruscan mother, lover, or equivalent are speculative.

### Primary evidence and its limitations

What survives:

- architectural remains beneath and beside San Nicola in Carcere;
- Republican and imperial inscriptions, especially in the *CIL*;
- Roman calendars;
- extensive imperial coinage;
- scattered sculpture and glyptic;
- literary testimony from Cicero, Livy, Tacitus, Dio, Frontinus, and later antiquarians or Christian critics.

What is lost:

- the original dedication inscription;
- the cult statue;
- priestly books describing the temple ritual;
- Varro’s *Antiquitates rerum divinarum*;
- most of Livy’s books covering the temple’s foundation period;
- any hymns or myths that may once have accompanied worship.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Spes was certainly born from, married to, or mother of another god.
- Her Roman cult was simply borrowed from Greek Pandora mythology.
- The anchor was her standard ancient Roman attribute.
- A fixed sacred color, crystal, planet, tarot card, or weekday belonged to her.
- She had an Etruscan counterpart securely identifiable on mirrors.
- Her cult practiced temple prostitution or human sacrifice.
- Christian veneration of hope was an uninterrupted continuation of her temple cult.
- Every literary occurrence of capitalized “Hope” refers to the goddess; capitalization is usually editorial.

## 10. Dedicated contested-material findings

### Etymology and origin

**Finding:** the Latin name is inherited Italic vocabulary; the goddess’s institutional origin remains obscure. Linguistic antiquity does not prove a prehistoric personal deity.

### Syncretism

**Finding:** equation with Greek Elpis is secure as translation and interpretatio. Identity of cult history is not. An Etruscan derivation is unproved.

### Human sacrifice

**Finding:** no evidence connects human sacrifice with Spes. Roman accounts of human sacrifice concern other crises and deities; importing them into her cult would be fabrication.

### Temple prostitution

**Finding:** no evidence. The category itself is heavily contested in ancient Mediterranean scholarship and has no documentary connection to Spes.

### Cult violence

**Finding:** the temple suffered lightning and urban fires, but there is no recorded violence conducted by a Spes priesthood or congregation.

### Suppression

**Finding:** traditional public cult was displaced under Christian emperors, but no Spes-specific closure, demolition, massacre, forced conversion, or transfer into a named church is documented. San Nicola in Carcere’s incorporation of the remains is medieval architectural reuse, not evidence for seamless cult continuity.

### Occultism

**Finding:** *Spes* appears as a Latin motto and allegory. No demonstrably ancient grimoire cult, planetary seal, demonological transformation, or major Golden Dawn working belongs to her.

### Neopaganism

**Finding:** present worship is revivalist and reconstructionist. Its existence is real; claims of uninterrupted transmission are not documented.

### Nationalist or extremist appropriation

**Finding:** Roman “public hope” formulas have obvious political adaptability, but no distinct, historically consequential extremist Spes movement was identified. Assertions of one require case-specific evidence.

Her fullest reconstructable “life” is therefore the changing public location of hope—from a Republican wartime sanctuary, through imperial dynasty and coinage, to Christian salvation and post-antique allegory.
