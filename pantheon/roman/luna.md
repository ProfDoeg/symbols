# Luna: Research Dossier

**Class:** goddess; more precisely, the Roman divine personification of the visible Moon, later also a celestial member or “aspect” of composite Diana–Hecate theology.

**Earliest secure attestation:** Livy’s record for 182 BCE of the *aedes Lunae* on the Aventine, whose door was torn away by a storm and carried against the rear wall of the Temple of Ceres (*Ab urbe condita* 40.2.2). Livy wrote in the late first century BCE, but dated the reported prodigy to 182. The temple’s existence by that year is therefore the earliest securely datable evidence; its precise foundation date is unknown. Varro’s attribution of Luna’s Roman cult to the legendary Sabine king Titus Tatius and Tacitus’ attribution of the Aventine temple to Servius Tullius are much later antiquarian traditions, not contemporary evidence for the regal period. A Roman Republican denarius depicting Luna in a two-horse chariot is securely dated about 150 BCE.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Securely attested early material:** Republican-era evidence or a securely datable Republican institution.
- **[LC] Later canonical elaboration:** Roman literary or artistic treatment that became standard.
- **[RV] Regional or medium-specific variant:** Local, provincial, funerary, Mithraic, numismatic, or otherwise restricted evidence.
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** Philosophical, antiquarian, grammatical, or priestly classification rather than necessarily popular cult.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** Historical equation or fusion with another deity.
- **[MA] Medieval or early-modern accretion.**
- **[F] Folklore:** Post-antique vernacular tradition not demonstrably continuous with Luna’s ancient public cult.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** A reasoned inference, not an ancient statement.
- **[MI] Modern invention or modern religious creation.**
- **[U] Unestablished:** Asserted but unsupported, or evidence insufficient.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

**[EA]** *Lūna* is simultaneously the ordinary Latin word for the Moon and the proper name of its goddess. Roman evidence does not sustain a clean modern division between “the astronomical Moon” and “a person who controls the Moon”: celestial body, visible light, calendrical regulator, and divine presence overlap. Cicero’s Stoic spokesman describes the Moon’s phases, monthly path, and effects on living things as evidence of divine cosmic order (*De natura deorum* 2.50); elsewhere he says that “Diana and Luna are thought to be the same,” and derives *Luna* from *lucere*, “to shine” (2.68–69). This is philosophical and etymological systematization, not proof that all Roman worshippers always treated Luna and Diana as one deity. [Cicero, *De natura deorum* II](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cicero/de_Natura_Deorum/2A%2A.html)

### Names and linguistic forms

- **Lūna** — Latin, “Moon; Moon-goddess.” **[EA]** The divine name is formally identical to the celestial noun.
- **Old Latin reconstructed \**louksnā / \**leuksnā** — **[MSR]** conventionally derived from Proto-Indo-European *leuk-*/*lou̯k-*, “light, brightness.” Latin loss of the consonant cluster produced *lūna*. The ancient derivation from *lucere* is not sound historical linguistics in Cicero’s form, but it recognizes the same semantic field.
- **Noctilūca**, “night-shiner” or “she who shines by night.” **[EA/LS]** Varro, *De lingua Latina* 5.68, says: *Luna, vel quod sola lucet noctu. Itaque ea dicta Noctiluca in Palatio; nam ibi noctu lucet templum*—approximately, “Luna, perhaps because she alone shines at night. Therefore she was called Noctiluca on the Palatine, for there her temple shines at night.” The passage may describe a continuously illuminated Palatine shrine rather than an entirely separate goddess. [Varro text and translation](https://classicalliberalarts.com/resources/varro_de_lingua_latina_1.pdf)
- **Luna Lucifera**, “Light-bearing Luna.” **[LC/SI]** Attested particularly in imperial iconography and coin legends; *Lucifera* also belongs to Diana. A Julia Domna antoninianus under Caracalla carries *LVNA LVCIFERA* and shows the crescent-crowned goddess in a biga. The epithet is secure in imperial material, but should not be projected onto the early Aventine cult without evidence.
- **Luna Aeterna**, “Eternal Luna.” **[RV/SI]** Appears in imperial dedications paired with *Sol Aeternus*; it belongs to late-imperial celestial and universalizing theology rather than the securely known Republican cult.
- **Luna Augusta** or lunar imagery assimilated to an Augusta — **[RV/SI]** Imperial coinage used crescent, stars, torches, and lunar chariot imagery for empresses, especially in consecration and eternity types. Identification of an empress with Luna could be implicit rather than supplied by a literal *Luna Augusta* legend.
- **Selēnē, Σελήνη** — Greek Moon-goddess. **[SI]** In Greek accounts of Roman institutions, Luna can simply be rendered Σελήνη: Appian, for example, calls the Aventine shrine *τὸ τῆς Σελήνης ἱερόν*. In Roman poetry and art, Greek Selene’s myths were transferred to Luna.
- **Mēnē, Μήνη** — Greek poetic name for the Moon. **[SI]** It is relevant to Greek lunar tradition, but no evidence establishes it as an indigenous Latin cult title of the Aventine Luna.
- **Losna** — an Italic name written on a Praenestine mirror, long called “Etruscan Luna.” **[RV/contested]** The figure watches the contest between Pollux and Amykos and holds a sceptre; the name has been interpreted as the local form cognate with Latin *Luna*. Older scholarship often treated the mirror as straightforward evidence for an Etruscan moon-goddess. More cautious classification recognizes the mirror as Praenestine/Italic and does not make *Losna* the normal Etruscan name for the Moon. [Treccani, “Losna”](https://www.treccani.it/enciclopedia/losna_%28Enciclopedia-dell%27-Arte-Antica%29/)
- **Tiur** — the securely attested Etruscan lunar deity. **[RV]** An inscribed mirror with Tiur, Lasa, and Turan has been described as the only known image explicitly labelled with the Etruscan lunar deity’s name. Tiur is not simply an alternative spelling of Luna. [Study of the inscribed Tiur mirror](https://doi.org/10.13140/RG.2.2.19924.01929)

### Diana, Lucina, and the problem of identity

**[SI/LS]** Cicero identifies Diana with the Moon and links *Luna*, *Lucina*, and *Lucifera* through light and childbirth (*De natura deorum* 2.68–69). Servius later systematizes a three-level divinity: Luna in heaven, Diana on earth, and Proserpina or Hecate below (*ad Aen.* 4.511; 6.118). Arnobius reports a Stoic doctrine that Luna, Diana, Ceres, Juno, and Proserpina are one (*Adversus nationes* 3.33). These texts document real ancient identifications, but their disagreement warns against treating “Luna = Diana = Hecate” as a timeless Roman dogma. [Arnobius, *Against the Heathen*](https://ccel.org/ccel/arnobius/against_heathen/anf06.xii.iii.iii.xxxiv.html)

The Aventine temples of Luna and Diana were separate institutions. Tacitus’ statement that Servius Tullius founded Luna’s sanctuary may be a conflation with Servius’ much better-attested foundation of the Aventine Diana temple; the manuscript tradition also permits *Lucinae* in place of *Lunae*. [Commentary on Tacitus, *Annals* 15.41](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/ar/tacitus-annals/15-41)

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The earliest evidence

1. **182 BCE — Aventine temple. [EA]** Livy 40.2.2 records a violent storm that tore a door from the *aedes Lunae, quae in Aventino est*—“the temple of Luna, which is on the Aventine”—and drove it against the rear of Ceres’ temple. This establishes the sanctuary by 182 BCE. It does not establish who founded it or how long it had stood.

2. **Possible foundation between 292 and 219 BCE. [MSR]** Adam Ziolkowski proposed this interval from the structure of Roman temple-founding evidence. It remains a scholarly reconstruction; there is no surviving foundation inscription.

3. **About 179–150 BCE — numismatic image. [EA]** Republican denarii show a female lunar charioteer in a biga. The British Museum’s denarius of Decimius Flavus, dated 150 BCE, depicts Luna holding reins and whip. It is among the earliest securely dated surviving images explicitly catalogued as Selene/Luna. [British Museum R.7470](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_R-7470)

4. **31 March calendars. [EA/LC]** The Fasti Caeretani and Fasti Praenestini mark the Aventine Luna observance on the day before the Kalends of April. The surviving Praenestine marble calendar was designed by Verrius Flaccus in the Augustan age; it documents an established festival, not necessarily its date of origin. [Fasti Praenestini](https://www.attalus.org/docs/cil/add_8.html)

### Principal literary and documentary corpus

- **Varro, *De lingua Latina* 5.68, 5.74; composed about 47–45 BCE. [EA as evidence for late-Republican antiquarian knowledge; LS for regal claims]**  
  At 5.68 Varro explains Luna/Noctiluca and the illuminated Palatine shrine. At 5.74 he lists Luna among deities supposedly introduced by Titus Tatius. Only six of the original twenty-five books survive; modern critical editions include those of R. G. Kent and Wolfgang de Melo. [Varro guide and Loeb links](https://www.attalus.org/info/varro.html)

- **Cicero, *De natura deorum*; 45 BCE. [LS/SI]**  
  Gives Stoic astronomical theology, the Moon’s effects on growth, the Diana–Luna identification, and skeptical counterarguments: if the Moon is divine, Cotta asks, why not every star? The dialogue records competing philosophical positions, not a Roman creed.

- **Virgil, *Georgics* 1 and 3; 29 BCE. [LC]**  
  Sol and Luna are the world’s brilliant lights and agricultural regulators. At 3.391–393 Virgil tells how Arcadian Pan deceived Luna with a snowy fleece and lured her into the woods, a Greek story attributed by Servius to Nicander. [Virgil, *Georgics* III](https://www.poetryintranslation.com/PITBR/Latin/VirgilGeorgicsIII.php)

- **Ovid, *Fasti* 3.883–884; composed about 2–8 CE. [LC supported by calendar evidence]**  
  “The Moon rules the months; the term of this month too ends with Luna worshipped on the Aventine ridge.” Ovid confirms the 31 March observance. [Ovid, *Fasti* III](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidFasti3.html)

- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses*, *Heroides*, and other poems; late first century BCE–early first century CE. [LC/SI]**  
  Ovid uses Luna, Phoebe, Diana, and lunar circumlocutions fluidly. His Phaethon story shows the Moon astonished at her brother’s runaway horses; *Heroides* 18 invokes the Moon’s sympathy for Leander by recalling Latmos and Endymion. These are Latin adaptations of Greek celestial mythology, not documentation of the Aventine ritual.

- **Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 40.2.2; written late first century BCE. [EA]**  
  Primary literary witness to the temple in 182 BCE.

- **Vitruvius, *De architectura* 5.5.8; late first century BCE. [EA]**  
  Says Lucius Mummius brought bronze acoustic vessels from the demolished theatre at Corinth in 146 BCE and dedicated them from his war-spoils at Luna’s temple. [Vitruvius 5.5.8](https://lexundria.com/vitr/5.5.8/cf)

- **Appian, *Civil Wars* 1.78; Greek, second century CE, narrating 84 BCE. [LC preserving Republican event]**  
  Lightning struck the temples of Luna and Ceres during disrupted consular elections after Cinna’s death, causing the augurs to postpone the assemblies. [Appian 1.78](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.01.0232%3Abook%3D1%3Achapter%3D9)

- **Tacitus, *Annals* 15.41; about 110–120 CE, narrating 64 CE. [LC; manuscript dispute]**  
  Lists the sanctuary supposedly dedicated by Servius Tullius among the ancient monuments destroyed in Nero’s fire. The manuscripts divide between *Lunae* and *Lucinae*. The context and other evidence favor an Aventine Luna temple existing until 64, but the Servian foundation remains uncertain. [Tacitus, *Annals* XV](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/e/roman/texts/tacitus/annals/15b%2A.html)

- **Tertullian, *De spectaculis* 8; about 197–202 CE. [hostile witness]**  
  Calls the Circus principally consecrated to Sol and interprets its ornaments as demonic idolatry. His hostility is explicit, but his inventory remains evidence for how a late-second-century Christian understood the circus’ divine topography. [Tertullian, *De spectaculis*](https://ccel.org/ccel/tertullian/spectaculis/anf03.iv.v.viii.html)

- **Roman calendars and regional catalogues; Republican traditions preserved in imperial inscriptions. [EA/LC]**  
  Beside 31 March, 28 August carries a sacrifice or games for Sol and Luna, associated in later topographical catalogues with the Circus Maximus. This is a distinct cult-place from the Aventine temple.

- **CIL and other inscriptions. [EA–RV]**  
  Dedicatory formulas include *Lunae*, *Soli et Lunae*, and, especially in imperial provinces, *Soli Aeterno et Lunae Aeternae*. Many epigraphic occurrences belong to paired celestial theology rather than to the Aventine cult. CIL references require case-by-case checking because “Luna” can also occur in personal names, place-names, calendar dating, or restorations. [CIL portal and concordance](https://www.attalus.org/docs/cil.html)

- **Servius, commentaries on Virgil; late fourth/early fifth century CE. [LS]**  
  Preserves variant mythographic learning and explicitly organizes Luna, Diana, Hecate, and Proserpina into terrestrial and cosmic levels. His explanations are late-antique scholarship, not transparent evidence for archaic belief. [Servius on *Aeneid* 3.73](https://www.monumenta.ch/latein/text.php?domain=&hide_apparatus=&inframe=1&lang=1&level=&links=&rumpfid=Servius%2C+in+Vergilii+Aeneidos+Commentarius%2C+03%2C+++73&tabelle=Servius)

- **Macrobius, *Saturnalia* and commentary on the *Dream of Scipio*; early fifth century CE. [LS]**  
  Treats celestial divinities through late-Platonic, solar, and cosmological synthesis. Useful for late-antique reception, not for reconstructing the Aventine foundation.

- **Augustine, *City of God* 4–7; 413–426 CE. [hostile witness/LS]**  
  Preserves and attacks material from Varro’s lost *Antiquitates rerum divinarum*, including the reduction of gods to world-parts and the equation of Sol/Luna with Apollo/Diana. Augustine is indispensable but selective and polemical. [Augustine, *City of God* VII](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120107.htm)

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and powers

- **Visible Moon and nocturnal illumination. [EA]** Varro’s *Noctiluca* passage gives the clearest cultic expression.
- **Months and calendrical measurement. [LC]** Ovid says *Luna regit menses*, “Luna rules the months.” The Latin *mensis* belongs to a different Indo-European word-family from *luna*, although ancient authors naturally connected lunar courses and months conceptually.
- **Growth, moisture, reproduction, and maturation. [LS]** Cicero’s Stoic spokesman says influences flow from Luna by which living beings are nourished and mature. Pliny attributes powerful effects on plants, moisture, bodies, and agriculture to waxing and full Moon. These claims represent ancient natural philosophy as well as religion.
- **Childbirth. [SI/LS]** Through identification with Diana Lucifera and Juno Lucina, lunar courses were connected with gestation. This is stronger evidence for learned Diana/Lucina theology than for a specialized Aventine obstetric cult.
- **Cosmic pairing and imperial eternity. [LC/RV]** Paired Sol and Luna can signify the totality of time, heaven, or imperial dominion.
- **Circus and chariot imagery. [LC]** The Moon’s biga mirrors Sol’s quadriga. The association is visual, cosmological, and connected with the Circus’ racing space.
- **Funerary hope or perpetual sleep. [RV]** Selene/Luna’s visit to the eternally sleeping Endymion became especially popular on Roman sarcophagi; death could be represented as beautiful sleep and repeated divine visitation.

### Appearance and identifying attributes

**[LC]** Luna is normally a young woman, dressed in a long chiton or Romanized drapery, with one or more of:

- a crescent above the forehead or behind the shoulders;
- a lunar disc or billowing, crescent-shaped mantle;
- a torch, or paired torches;
- reins and whip;
- a two-horse or two-ox chariot, the *biga*;
- stars surrounding the crescent;
- a descending trajectory, paired with the ascending Sol.

A Republican denarius of 150 BCE shows the biga without requiring a surviving inscription naming the driver; typology and the celestial convention identify her. [British Museum R.7470](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_R-7470)

**[LC/RV]** On imperial coins, a crescent beneath an empress’s bust can mark the antoninianus denomination and should not automatically be read as a literal portrait of Luna. Conversely, legends such as *LVNA LVCIFERA* make the identification explicit.

**[RV]** Early Severan Endymion sarcophagi show Selene descending from her chariot toward the sleeping youth, often assisted by cupids, while Sleep stands nearby and Sol appears at the opposite end. A Metropolitan Museum sarcophagus from Ostia, dated 190–210 CE, preserves the full funerary program. [Metropolitan Museum catalogue](https://resources.metmuseum.org/resources/metpublications/pdf/Roman_Sarcophagi_in_The_Metropolitan_Museum_of_Art.pdf)

**[RV/SI]** In Mithraic tauroctonies, Luna usually occupies an upper corner opposite Sol, recognizable by crescent and descending biga. This is not evidence that Luna participated in the bull-slaying as an actor; she frames the cosmic scene as the Moon. [Metropolitan Museum overview of mystery cults](https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/mystery-cults-in-the-greek-and-roman-world)

### Animals, plants, colors, numbers, and days

- **Horses and oxen:** securely iconographic draught animals of the biga. **[LC]**
- **Mules:** reported in some literary antiquarian descriptions of the lunar chariot, but not universal. **[RV]**
- **White and black horses:** late explanations treat the pair as day/night or visible/invisible courses; the S. Maria Capua Vetere Mithraic painting gives differently colored animals. **[LS/RV]**
- **White fleece:** belongs to Virgil’s Pan episode, not a documented cult offering. **[LC]**
- **Silver:** alchemical Luna’s metal from medieval and early-modern planetary correspondence. **[MA]** It must not be retrojected into Republican ritual without evidence.
- **Monday, *dies Lunae*:** planetary weekday, established in the Roman imperial period through the seven-day planetary week. **[LC]** Modern Romance forms such as Italian *lunedì* and French *lundi* preserve it.
- **Number two:** associated by Isidore of Seville with Luna’s biga and dual visibility by day and night. **[MA/LS]** This is a seventh-century allegorical explanation, not demonstrably ancient cult doctrine.
- **Colors:** no securely attested official Roman cult color for Luna. Claims that silver, white, blue, or black were her ancient mandatory colors are **[U/MI]** unless tied to a specific object or later magical system.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other beings

### Roman cult identity versus Greek genealogy

**[EA]** The early Roman cult evidence gives Luna no parentage, birth story, consort, or children. Varro’s placement of her among Titus Tatius’ imported gods is an origin-of-cult legend, not a divine genealogy.

**[SI/LC]** Once Luna becomes Latin Selene, Greek genealogies attach to her:

| Tradition | Parentage or relation | Source and date | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Daughter of Hyperion and Theia; sister of Helios and Eos | Hesiod, *Theogony* 371–374, c. 700 BCE; repeated in Roman mythography | **[SI/LC]** Greek genealogy transferred to Luna |
| Daughter of Pallas | Homeric Hymn to Hermes 99–100, probably sixth century BCE | **[SI/RV]** competing Greek genealogy |
| Daughter of Hyperion and Euryphaessa | Homeric Hymn to Selene 4–7, date disputed, often archaic | **[SI/RV]** |
| Identical with Diana | Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.68, 45 BCE | **[SI/LS]** |
| Heavenly member of Luna–Diana–Proserpina/Hecate triad | Servius, late fourth/early fifth century CE | **[SI/LS]** late-antique system |
| Lover of Endymion | Greek lyric and Hellenistic sources; Latin allusions in Ovid and Roman art | **[SI/LC]** |
| Seduced or deceived by Pan | Virgil, *Georgics* 3.391–393, 29 BCE, following Greek material | **[LC]** |
| Mother of fifty daughters by Endymion | Pausanias 5.1.4, second century CE, reporting Elean tradition | **[RV/SI]** Greek regional tradition |
| Mother of Pandia by Zeus | Homeric Hymn to Selene 14–16 | **[RV/SI]** Greek, not an Aventine doctrine |
| Mother of Herse/Dew by Zeus | Later Greek sources | **[RV/SI]** |

### Every documented divine interaction

The following list separates actual episodes or joint appearances from mere membership in the same pantheon.

- **Sol:** paired worship and sacrifice; a joint Sol–Luna cult is associated with the Circus Maximus and 28 August in Roman calendars and late topographical catalogues. **Source:** Fasti and regional catalogues; imperial inscriptions. **Date:** evidence principally late Republican–imperial. **[EA/LC]**
- **Sol:** paired cosmic charioteers, with Sol rising in a quadriga and Luna descending in a biga. **Source:** coins, sarcophagi, Mithraic reliefs, imperial period. **[LC/RV]**
- **Sol/Phoebus:** Luna watches in astonishment as her brother’s horses run out of control during Phaethon’s disastrous drive. **Source:** Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 2, c. 8 CE. **[LC/SI]**
- **Diana:** explicit identification—“Diana and Luna are thought to be the same.” **Source:** Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.68, 45 BCE. **[SI/LS]**
- **Diana:** differentiated cultically despite identification; the Aventine temples of Luna and Diana were separate. **Source:** calendars, Livy, Tacitus, topographical evidence. **[EA]**
- **Juno Lucina:** linked through the shared light/childbirth complex, not through a narrative meeting. **Source:** Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.68–69. **[SI/LS]**
- **Proserpina:** Luna in heaven and Proserpina below are forms or levels of one triune divinity. **Source:** Servius on *Aeneid* 4.511 and 6.118, late fourth/early fifth century CE. **[SI/LS]**
- **Hecate:** identified with the terrestrial or underworld phase of the same composite lunar goddess. **Source:** Servius; late-antique commentary. **[SI/LS]**
- **Ceres:** Luna, Diana, Ceres, Juno, and Proserpina are reported as one according to Stoic theology. **Source:** Arnobius, *Adversus nationes* 3.33, early fourth century CE. **[SI/LS]**
- **Ceres:** no mythic encounter, but the Aventine temples were physical neighbors; Luna’s door struck Ceres’ temple in the storm of 182 BCE, and both shrines were struck by lightning in 84 BCE. **Source:** Livy 40.2.2; Appian, *Civil Wars* 1.78. **[EA]**
- **Pan:** Pan seduces or deceives Luna, using a snowy fleece or disguising himself in one. **Source:** Virgil, *Georgics* 3.391–393; Servius attributes the story to Nicander. **Date:** Virgil 29 BCE; earlier Greek source fragmentary. **[LC/SI]**
- **Jupiter/Zeus:** father of Pandia by Selene in the Homeric Hymn; transferred to Luna only through Greco-Roman equivalence. **Source:** Homeric Hymn to Selene. **[SI/RV]**
- **Hyperion and Theia:** parents of Selene/Luna in the Hesiodic genealogy. **Source:** Hesiod, *Theogony* 371–374. **[SI/LC]**
- **Aurora/Eos:** sister in the Hesiodic family and a complementary celestial figure; no substantial independent Roman Luna–Aurora episode is securely attested. **[SI; absence finding]**
- **Mithras:** Luna observes or frames the tauroctony from the celestial margin. **Source:** imperial Mithraic reliefs and paintings, mainly second–fourth centuries CE. **[RV/SI]** No surviving Mithraic narrative text explains a direct conversation or action between them.
- **Cybele:** Luna appears in the cosmic frame of the late-antique Parabiago plate’s Cybele–Attis composition. **Source:** silver plate, conventionally fourth century CE. **[RV]** This is joint iconography, not a textual meeting.
- **Castor and Pollux:** Losna watches Pollux’s contest with Amykos on a Praenestine mirror. **Source:** inscribed Italic mirror. **[RV]** Whether Losna should be identified without qualification as Roman Luna remains disputed.
- **Lasa and Turan:** the Etruscan lunar deity Tiur appears with these goddesses on an inscribed mirror. **Source:** Etruscan mirror. **[RV]** This concerns Tiur, not direct evidence for Roman Luna.

No securely attested Roman myth records Luna fighting, judging, rescuing, or being punished by a major Roman god. Many online catalogues manufacture such interactions by freely combining Selene, Artemis, Diana, Hecate, and modern “Triple Goddess” material.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### 5.1 Luna and Endymion

**Earliest core. [SI]** The story is Greek before it is Roman. Archaic and classical Greek sources know Endymion in inconsistent forms: king of Elis, shepherd, hunter, astronomer, or beautiful sleeper. The lunar love-story is associated especially with Mount Latmos in Caria. Apollonius Rhodius in the third century BCE alludes to Selene’s love for him; later sources elaborate nocturnal visits and perpetual sleep.

**Roman form. [LC]** Latin poets generally allude rather than narrate the whole tale. In Ovid’s *Heroides* 18.59–74, Leander appeals to the Moon and reminds her of Latmos: because she knows secret nocturnal love, she should aid him. Hyginus, *Fabulae* 271, simply lists Endymion among beautiful youths and says Luna loved him.

**Variants.**

- Endymion chooses perpetual sleep or agelessness.
- Zeus imposes sleep as a punishment.
- Selene asks Zeus to preserve Endymion unchanged.
- The goddess visits and kisses him while he sleeps.
- In the Elean genealogy they have fifty daughters, sometimes interpreted by later scholars as fifty lunar months in an Olympiad cycle.
- The beloved is a Carian shepherd or hunter at Latmos; in another tradition he is a king of Elis.

No single ancient source supports the familiar seamless modern version in which Luna herself asks Jupiter to make Endymion sleep forever so she can visit him nightly. That narrative combines distinct sources. **[MI when presented as one canonical Roman account]**

**Roman function. [RV/MSR]** From the second century CE, the scene became extremely popular on sarcophagi. The sleeping, eternally beautiful youth offered a visual analogy for death, while the goddess’ recurring descent suggested continued visitation or cosmic renewal. This funerary reading is contextually strong, though individual patrons’ beliefs cannot be recovered.

### 5.2 Pan and Luna

**[LC/SI]** Virgil’s *Georgics* 3.391–393 says that Pan, god of Arcadia, captured or beguiled Luna with the gift or lure of snowy wool and called her into the forest; she did not refuse. Servius says Nicander told the story with Pan wrapping himself in a fleece and disguising himself as a sheep. The episode serves Virgil’s discourse on breeding white sheep and fine wool. It is Greek pastoral mythology translated into Latin, not a known ritual myth of the Aventine.

The sexual vocabulary varies by translation: “charmed,” “beguiled,” “ensnared,” or “deceived.” The surviving lines do not justify sanitizing the deception, but they also do not supply a full narrative of force.

### 5.3 The Phaethon catastrophe

**[LC/SI]** In Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* 2, Phaethon loses control of Sol’s chariot and scorches the world. Luna looks down in amazement at her brother’s horses running below their accustomed path. She is a witness rather than an independent actor. Her presence completes the celestial family and emphasizes cosmic disorder.

### 5.4 Luna, Diana, and the three-world goddess

**[LS/SI]** This is not originally a narrative cycle. Cicero equates Diana and Luna through lunar light. Virgil’s *Aeneid* and magical scene in Book 4 use Diana, Hecate, Phoebe, and Trivia in a fluid poetic register. Servius then supplies a systematic explanation: Luna above the earth, Diana on earth, Proserpina or Hecate below it.

**Development:** celestial identification in late-Republican literature → poetic triform imagery → late-antique grammatical and theological systematization → Renaissance occult correspondence → modern Maiden–Mother–Crone doctrine. The last step is not ancient. Robert Graves’ phase-based Maiden–Mother–Crone “Triple Goddess” is a twentieth-century synthesis. [Modern Triple Goddess historiography](https://www.gresham.ac.uk/watch-now/modern-goddess)

### 5.5 Luna in Mithraic cosmology

**[RV]** Mithraic monuments normally show Luna opposite Sol around the bull-killing. The image documents her as a cosmic boundary-marker, but no surviving Mithraic scripture explains her exact function. Older claims that Mithraic images straightforwardly reproduce a Persian creation story are now treated cautiously; Roman Mithraism’s relation to Iranian religion is a major scholarly dispute.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### 6.1 The Aventine temple

**Location. [EA/MSR]** Literary evidence places the temple on the Aventine, probably toward its northern end above the Porta Trigemina and near the temple of Ceres, Liber, and Libera. No archaeological remains have been securely identified. Livy’s storm story has been used both to place Luna very near Ceres and to argue that the distance made the door’s flight a prodigy; topography therefore remains disputed. [Digital Augustan Rome](https://www.digitalaugustanrome.org/records/luna-aedes/)

**Foundation.**

- Servius Tullius: Tacitus 15.41, but nowhere independently secure and possibly a textual or antiquarian confusion with Diana. **[LC/U]**
- Between 292 and 219 BCE: Ziolkowski’s reconstruction. **[MSR]**
- Existence by 182 BCE: secure. **[EA]**

**Architecture. [contested]** Vitruvius 1.2.5 has been read as placing Luna among deities suited to open-air or hypaethral temples. Modern reconstruction therefore sometimes calls the Aventine temple roofless. Since the precise application and physical remains are uncertain, a completely open structure should not be stated as archaeological fact.

**Recorded history.**

- 182 BCE: storm removes a door. **[EA]**
- 146 BCE: Mummius dedicates Corinthian bronze acoustic vessels or their proceeds as war-spoils. **[EA]**
- 121 BCE: Gaius Gracchus’ associate Marcus Fulvius Flaccus and his son flee through or past Luna’s temple before being killed; preserved in later historical sources. **[LC preserving Republican topography]**
- 84 BCE: Luna and Ceres temples struck by lightning amid the political crisis following Cinna’s death. **[EA through Appian]**
- 31 March annually: dedication observance. **[EA/LC]**
- 64 CE: destroyed in the Great Fire. **[LC]**
- After 64: no secure record of rebuilding. **[absence finding]**

A useful consolidated ancient-source topography is in Platner and Ashby. [*A Topographical Dictionary of Ancient Rome*, “Aedes Lunae”](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Places/Europe/Italy/Lazio/Roma/Rome/_Texts/PLATOP%2A/Aedes_Lunae.html)

### 6.2 The Palatine Noctiluca

**[EA]** Varro attests a Palatine *templum* called Noctiluca because it shone at night. It may have been a shrine kept continuously illuminated. The text gives no foundation date, priestly college, sacrifice, or festival. It is not safe to merge it with the Aventine temple.

The claim that *Noctiluca* was a wholly separate goddess is possible but not demanded by Varro’s syntax; he appears to explain it as a title of Luna.

### 6.3 Sol and Luna at the Circus

**[EA/LC]** The Circus Maximus contained a cult-place or temple of Sol and Luna according to later regional catalogues, while calendars place a Sol–Luna observance on 28 August. Tertullian calls the circus chiefly sacred to Sol and describes its entire decorative program as religious. The paired celestial chariots closely suit a racecourse.

This sanctuary must not be identified with the Aventine temple: the calendars distinguish the Aventine Luna on 31 March from Sol and Luna’s Circus observance. Tertullian is a hostile Christian witness, but hostility does not erase the topographical information; it does require caution about his language of demons and pollution.

### 6.4 Personnel and offerings

No independent priesthood uniquely titled *sacerdos Lunae* is securely documented for the Republican Aventine cult. No recruitment rules, privileges, temple treasury, oracle, or initiation system can be reconstructed. **[absence finding]**

Documented or plausible offerings include:

- public sacrifice at calendar observances, though no surviving prescription identifies the victim; **[EA/U for details]**
- Mummius’ *manubiae*, the Corinthian bronze vessels or proceeds, as a political-military dedication; **[EA]**
- private imperial dedications to Luna or Sol and Luna, often under celestial epithets; **[RV]**
- images, reliefs, coins, and lamps bearing crescents or Luna’s biga, though not every lunar symbol is a votive to the goddess. **[RV]**

### 6.5 Alleged sexual rites, human sacrifice, and temple prostitution

**Human sacrifice:** no source securely associates Luna’s Roman cult with human sacrifice. **[absence finding]**

**Temple prostitution:** no source securely associates the Aventine or Palatine cult of Luna with institutional “sacred prostitution.” **[absence finding]** General internet claims arise from indiscriminate transfer of stories about other Mediterranean goddesses and from outdated universal-goddess theories.

**Cult violence:** the deaths near the temple during the Gracchan crisis were political violence in the temple’s vicinity, not a Luna ritual. **[EA distinction]**

**Spoils and imperial politics:** Mummius’ dedication after Corinth’s destruction is a documented instance of imperial violence and plunder converted into Roman sacred capital. The dedication honored Luna while publicly displaying conquest. **[EA]**

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the secure record

**[LS/U]** Varro makes Luna one of the gods introduced by Titus Tatius, thereby classifying her cult as Sabine. The story belongs to Roman antiquarian reconstruction of the regal past. Wissowa objected that archaic cult evidence was lacking; later scholars have judged an early Italic lunar cult plausible but unprovable. [Oxford Classical Dictionary, “Luna”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/549652335)

The linguistic cognate *Losna* and Italic/Etruscan lunar figures demonstrate wider central-Italian moon religion, but they do not prove a direct institutional descent from an Etruscan or Sabine cult into the Aventine temple.

### Middle Republic, by 182–146 BCE

**[EA]** The Aventine sanctuary is functioning, participates in the state prodigy system, and receives prestigious war-spoils. Luna is sufficiently legible iconographically to appear driving a biga on Republican coinage.

This is the strongest evidence for Luna as an independent Roman cult figure rather than merely a poetic name for Diana.

### Late Republic, 84–27 BCE

**[EA/LS]** The temple’s lightning strike enters political-prodigy history. Varro documents Noctiluca and creates a Sabine-origin catalogue. Cicero assimilates Luna to Diana within Stoic cosmic theology. Virgil imports or translates the Pan story. Greek Selene increasingly supplies Luna’s literary genealogy and myths.

### Augustan and Julio-Claudian periods

**[LC]** Ovid anchors the Aventine festival in the calendar while adapting Selene’s poetic associations. Lunar imagery participates in Augustan cosmic and imperial symbolism. The Fasti Praenestini publicly monumentalize calendrical religion.

**64 CE:** the Aventine temple is destroyed in Nero’s fire. No secure rebuilding is recorded. Luna nevertheless continues vigorously in poetry, art, coinage, astrology, and paired celestial religion.

### High Empire

**[LC/RV]** Luna’s independent urban temple becomes less visible, while her image expands:

- on coins and imperial apotheosis types;
- in Endymion funerary art;
- beside Sol in imperial cosmology;
- throughout provincial dedications;
- in Mithraic monuments;
- in the seven-day planetary week.

The apparent paradox—weak institutional evidence but abundant imagery—reflects different evidence streams rather than cultic disappearance.

### Late Empire

**[SI/LS]** Philosophers, mythographers, and commentators increasingly place Luna inside universal systems: Diana/Hecate/Proserpina, cosmic soul, planetary hierarchy, or Sol–Luna eternity. Christian apologists attack these identifications as inconsistent polytheism but preserve otherwise lost pagan classifications.

From 391–392 CE, Theodosius I’s legislation prohibited sacrifice and public pagan cult. The laws applied generally; no surviving decree or destruction account singles out a Luna sanctuary. Mithraea and other cult sites were abandoned, repurposed, or destroyed unevenly. [Metropolitan Museum, late-antique suppression context](https://www.metmuseum.org/de/essays/mystery-cults-in-the-greek-and-roman-world)

### Medieval period

**[MA]** Luna survives primarily as:

- the Latin name and personification of the Moon;
- a planetary power in astrology;
- the ruler of Monday;
- the alchemical planet corresponding to silver;
- a female charioteer in manuscripts derived from classical and late-antique cosmography.

This is transmission and reinvention, not evidence for continuous institutional worship of the Aventine goddess.

### Renaissance to nineteenth century

**[MA]** Humanists recovered Ovid, Virgil, Servius, mythography, and Roman art. Luna entered emblem books, planetary image magic, painting, sculpture, and alchemical diagrams. Renaissance “Luna” often combines Selene’s crescent and Endymion, Diana’s bow, Hecate’s magic, and astrological silver. The hybrid is historically important but not a restoration of one ancient cult form.

Romantic treatments of Endymion, particularly John Keats’ *Endymion* (1818), made the Moon’s beautiful sleeping lover central to modern reception, often calling the goddess Cynthia or Diana rather than Luna.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

**[MI/MSR]** Wicca, Goddess spirituality, Roman reconstructionism, and eclectic Paganism have revived worship of Luna. There is no evidence of an unbroken initiatory or family cult descending from ancient Rome. Reconstructionists may deliberately use the 31 March festival and Roman prayer forms; this is a modern revival based on texts.

The widespread Maiden–Mother–Crone lunar scheme is not an ancient Roman Luna doctrine. It develops through nineteenth- and twentieth-century ritualism, Margaret Murray’s discredited witch-cult hypothesis, Robert Graves’ *The White Goddess* (1948), and modern Pagan liturgy. Ronald Hutton’s work traces this modern genealogy. Individual contemporary worship remains a living religious practice, but historical continuity claims require separate evidence.

No reliable evidence establishes Luna-specific nationalist or extremist appropriation comparable to that involving some Germanic or Indo-European gods. Generic lunar, Roman, or “European pagan” symbolism can occur in political subcultures, but attributing it specifically to Luna without a named group and document would be **[U]**.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Esotericism and magic

**[MA]** Medieval Arabic and Latin astral magic transmitted planetary Moon correspondences—silver, moisture, generation, dreams, travel, and Monday—to Renaissance Europe. When Latin authors name the planetary power *Luna*, that figure need not be the civic Roman goddess in any narrow sense.

**[MA/MI]** Renaissance magicians, later ceremonial occultists, and nineteenth-century orders systematized lunar colors, metals, numbers, angels, incense, and talismans. These systems are datable occult constructions. They should not be advertised as recovered rites of the Aventine temple.

**[MI]** The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn and successor traditions use a lunar magical sphere associated with Yesod and with a range of Greco-Roman goddesses. “Luna” in modern planetary magic is normally a syncretic emblem rather than a historically reconstructed Roman cult personality.

### Neopagan worship

Modern Roman reconstructionists variously:

- honor Luna on 31 March;
- honor Sol and Luna on 28 August;
- use incense, wine, milk, cakes, lamps, or hymns composed from Roman formulas;
- distinguish Luna from Diana;
- or treat them as aspects of one goddess.

Only the dates and some names have ancient documentary bases. Specific modern altar colors, crystals, tarot correspondences, “moon water,” menstrual doctrines, and phase-by-phase personality systems are **[MI]** unless their modern source is acknowledged.

The scale of Luna-specific worship cannot be established reliably: modern Pagan populations are decentralized, and survey categories rarely isolate this goddess.

### Literature and visual art

- Renaissance and Baroque Endymion paintings commonly identify the visitor as Diana, Selene, or Luna interchangeably. **[MA]**
- Keats’ *Endymion* (1818) profoundly reshaped the myth in Romantic literature. **[MI as adaptation]**
- Modern fantasy, comics, games, anime, and commercial astrology use “Luna” primarily because the Latin name has become an international feminine name and shorthand for moon-magic. **[MI]**
- Astronomical usage calls Earth’s Moon “Luna,” especially in science fiction, but “Luna” is not the International Astronomical Union’s uniquely mandated proper name. **[modern linguistic convention]**
- The personal name Luna has become widely popular in numerous countries; this is a modern onomastic afterlife rather than continuity of cult.

### Modern misconceptions

- “Luna was one member of the ancient Maiden–Mother–Crone trinity”: **[MI]**.
- “Luna was always simply another name for Diana”: **[U/overstatement]**; philosophy identifies them, but the temples and calendar observances distinguish them.
- “Luna’s ancient sacred color was blue or silver”: **[U]**.
- “Roman witches maintained Luna worship continuously into Wicca”: **[MI]**.
- “The crescent and star everywhere represent Luna”: **[U]**; context is required.
- “Losna proves Luna was borrowed directly from Etruria”: **[contested/MSR]**.
- “The Aventine temple was certainly founded by Servius Tullius”: **[LC/U]**.
- “Luna ordered Endymion’s eternal sleep in the canonical Roman version”: **[MI composite]**.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Wissowa and the Roman-cult model

Georg Wissowa’s *Religion und Kultus der Römer* emphasized securely attested Roman institutions and was skeptical of claims for an archaic Luna cult because the evidence is late and sparse. His approach usefully resists turning Greek mythography into Roman cult history, although its rigid separation of “genuine Roman” and “foreign Greek” elements is now treated with caution.

### Latte, Dumézil, and comparative approaches

Kurt Latte accepted a broader history of Roman religious change and discussed Sol and Luna among developing forms of worship. Georges Dumézil interpreted Roman religion through Indo-European structural comparison. Comparative models can illuminate inherited categories, but none supplies missing evidence for Luna’s actual temple ritual. An Indo-European etymology is not equivalent to proof of an unchanged Indo-European goddess.

### Ziolkowski and temple chronology

Adam Ziolkowski’s *The Temples of Mid-Republican Rome and Their Historical and Topographical Context* argued for a foundation between 292 and 219 BCE. The principal debate concerns:

- whether Tacitus really read *Lunae* or *Lucinae*;
- whether Servius Tullius is a credible founder;
- how Livy’s flying door locates Luna relative to Ceres;
- whether the temple’s absence from earlier evidence is accidental;
- whether it was restored before 64 CE.

No excavated building settles the matter.

### Scheid, Beard–North–Price, and practice-centered religion

John Scheid and Mary Beard, John North, and Simon Price emphasize that Roman religion was embedded in civic acts, calendars, priesthoods, space, and political authority. Applied to Luna, this favors the March festival, prodigy reports, dedications, and temple history over attempts to construct a personality from Greek myth alone.

### Selene/Luna iconography

The *Lexicon Iconographicum Mythologiae Classicae* appropriately catalogues “Selene, Luna” together because many images cannot be assigned securely to a Greek or Roman cult identity. This is useful for visual typology but dangerous if interpreted as proof that all Greek Selene evidence describes Roman Luna’s civic cult.

### Etruscan and Italic origins

Older writers readily called Losna an Etruscan Luna. Current evidence complicates that claim:

- the *Losna* mirror is Praenestine/Italic;
- Tiur is the securely labelled Etruscan lunar deity;
- central-Italian contact makes borrowing and cognate development plausible;
- no surviving document narrates Luna’s transfer from Etruria to Rome.

Thus “Italic background” is safer than “Etruscan origin.” **[MSR]**

### Christian authors as hostile witnesses

Tertullian, Arnobius, Augustine, and other apologists aim to discredit traditional religion. Their claims about absurdity, demons, or immorality cannot be treated as neutral description. Yet they quote, paraphrase, or catalogue cults and pagan scholarship now otherwise lost. The correct procedure is neither to discard them nor to accept their framing: isolate the recoverable datum and mark the polemical context.

### Primary-evidence condition

What survives:

- literary manuscripts copied centuries after composition;
- inscribed calendars;
- scattered dedications;
- coins;
- sarcophagi, reliefs, mirrors, and Mithraic monuments;
- late topographical catalogues.

What does not survive:

- the Aventine temple itself;
- its foundation inscription;
- its cult statue;
- a Luna-specific ritual book;
- a securely documented priesthood archive;
- Varro’s complete *Antiquitates rerum divinarum*;
- any indigenous Roman narrative cycle comparable to Greek Selene’s myths.
