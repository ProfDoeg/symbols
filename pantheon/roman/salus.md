# Salus: Research Dossier

**Class:** Roman goddess—more precisely, a female conceptual or “deified quality” (*salus*: safety, preservation, welfare, deliverance, later bodily health). “Personification” is a useful modern description but can obscure the fact that Romans addressed her as a deity, maintained a temple and altar, sacrificed to her, and represented her anthropomorphically.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the historically transmitted vow of a temple to Salus by the consul Gaius Junius Bubulcus Brutus in **311 BCE**, during the Second Samnite War. Livy, writing in the Augustan age, mentions the vow retrospectively when recording the contracting of the temple during Bubulcus’s censorship and its dedication during his dictatorship. The literary record is therefore much later than the event, but the institutional sequence is generally accepted. Later chronological systems place the dedication in **303 or 302 BCE**; 5 August was its securely attested anniversary. Earlier “archaic” origins are scholarly reconstructions, not securely dated evidence. Daniele Miano’s reassessment concludes that neither the *collis Salutaris*, the title *Semonia*, nor the surviving Italic inscriptions demonstrates a cult before the temple vow of 311 BCE. [Miano, “From saviours to salvation”](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/156645/8/Miano%20From%20saviours%20to%20Salvation%20Salus%20in%20Republican%20Italy%20%28accepted%29.pdf)

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identity

**[Secure Republican attestation]** Salus is the divine power of being or remaining *salvus*: intact, unharmed, secure, prosperous, and preserved from danger. In Republican civic religion her defining concern was principally the safety and continuing strength of Rome and the Roman people—not narrowly medical “health.” This political sense remained active even after Greek Hygieia’s medical iconography transformed Salus’s appearance.

The Latin common noun *salūs*, genitive *salūtis*, covers “safety,” “deliverance,” “well-being,” “prosperity,” and “health.” It belongs with *salvus*, “safe, intact, sound.” The divine name is therefore not separable from ordinary Latin: context, capitalization in modern editions, cult title, syntax, and iconography determine whether a text refers to the goddess or merely to “safety.” Lexicographical evidence distinguishes the abstract noun from the personified Roman divinity. [Logeion, *salus*](https://logeion.uchicago.edu/salus)

### Documented forms and titles

- **Salus** — “Safety,” “Welfare,” “Preservation,” subsequently “Health.” The normal Latin divine name.

- **Salus Publica** — “Public Safety/Welfare.” **[Secure Republican and imperial cult title]** It identifies the beneficiary as the political community.

- **Salus Publica Populi Romani Quiritium** — “The Public Safety of the Roman People, the Quirites.” **[Secure imperial epigraphic formula preserving a Republican civic conception]** The Arval records employ this full title in annual vows and prescribe a gilded white cow for her.

- **Salus Romana** — “Roman Safety.” **[Augustan poetic/calendar formulation]** Ovid, *Fasti* 3.879–882, places *Romana Salus* beside Janus, Concordia, and the Ara Pacis in the observances of 30 March. [Ovid, *Fasti* 3](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidFasti3.html)

- **Salus Augusta** — grammatically “August/Augustan Salus.” **[Imperial systematization]** It may denote a sacralized imperial quality, the welfare created by Augustus or the imperial order, or—in particular images—an imperial woman assimilated to Salus. Tiberian bronze issues of 22–23 CE label Livia’s portrait *SALVS AVGVSTA*.

- **Salus Augusti / Augustorum** — “Safety/Health of the emperor / emperors.” **[Imperial development]** This makes the ruler’s personal survival and health the condition of public welfare. It is not identical in nuance to *Salus Augusta*.

- **Salus Caesaris** — “Safety of Caesar.” **[Late Republican/early imperial political language]** It belongs to the developing practice of vows for the ruler’s preservation.

- **Salus Generis Humani** — “Safety of the Human Race.” **[Civil-war imperial ideology, first securely prominent in 68–69 CE coinage]** Galban issues used it to present the new regime as deliverance from tyranny; Trajan later revived it.

- **Salus Exercitus / Militum** — “Safety of the Army / Soldiers.” **[Imperial numismatic or epigraphic variant]**

- **Salus Provinciarum, Italiae, Orbis, Rei Publicae** — “Safety of the Provinces, Italy, the World, the Commonwealth.” **[Imperial regional and political variants]**

- **Salus Semonia** — **[Late, contested title]** Macrobius (*Saturnalia* 1.16.8) and a damaged Festus/Verrius tradition associate this form with a festival. CIL VI 30975, an altar inscription of 1 CE, has *Salus Semonia* added in a different hand and grammatical construction at the end of a divine list. Wissowa, Norden, and Latte used it to reconstruct an archaic Salus belonging to the *Semones* and connected with Semo Sancus/Dius Fidius. Against this, the title is late and isolated; the antiquity and even original unity of the name Semo Sancus are disputed. It cannot securely prove either an archaic oath-function or pre-fourth-century cult. [Miano, pp. 7–9](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/156645/8/Miano%20From%20saviours%20to%20Salvation%20Salus%20in%20Republican%20Italy%20%28accepted%29.pdf)

- **Salus Umeritana** — **[Regional imperial manifestation]** Known from a silver vessel found near Santander and interpreted as the beneficial or healing power of otherwise unidentified waters at Umeri. This is a localized Salus, not evidence for a universal ancient epithet.

### Greek translations and identifications

Salus did not possess one perfect Greek equivalent.

- **Ὑγίεια / Hygieia**, “Health”: **[Syncretistic identification]** Greek writers normally translated Roman Salus as Hygieia, and Roman imagery eventually borrowed Hygieia’s serpent and feeding bowl. Livy 40.37 supplies a crucial transition: during the plague of 180 BCE, golden statues were ordered for Apollo, Aesculapius, and Salus. In that medical setting Salus fills Hygieia’s expected position. This is strong evidence for functional translation, but not proof that early Republican Salus began as Hygieia.

- **Σωτηρία / Sōtēria**, “Preservation/Deliverance”: **[Conceptual translation]** This better expresses civic rescue and safety. Modern scholarship increasingly treats Hygieia and Sōtēria as overlapping but incomplete translations rather than timeless equivalents.

- **Valetudo**, “Health/physical condition”: **[Roman learned and numismatic differentiation]** On the denarius of Manius Acilius Glabrio, conventionally dated 49 BCE, the obverse head labeled *SALVTIS* is paired with a standing *VALETVDO*. Rather than proving that the figures were always identical, the coin can distinguish civic Salus from bodily health while linking them.

### Alleged Sabine or Etruscan origin

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction, unproved]** Older scholars often called Salus “Sabine” because her temple stood on the Quirinal, a hill traditionally associated with Sabines. This is circular unless independent evidence predating the temple is supplied.

Etruscan and Latin evidence shows a multilingual central-Italian environment of “saving” divinities around 300 BCE, but no securely named Etruscan goddess can simply be equated with Salus. Greek-inscribed strigils from Etruria and Latium bear *sōteira* or *sōizousa* (“saviour/saving”), while an early-third-century Latin vessel from the Etruscan necropolis at Horta reads *Salutes pocolom*, “cup of Salus.” These demonstrate translation and mobility, not descent from a particular Etruscan original. [Miano, pp. 12–14](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/156645/8/Miano%20From%20saviours%20to%20Salvation%20Salus%20in%20Republican%20Italy%20%28accepted%29.pdf)

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

1. **Temple vow, Rome, 311 BCE.** **[Secure historical tradition; retrospective text]** Livy 9.43.25 says Bubulcus, as censor, contracted for the Temple of Salus which he had vowed as consul. Livy 10.1.9 reports its dedication during his dictatorship. Since Livy does not narrate the vow in his account of 311, the common claim that it was made specifically during the army’s escape from a Samnite ambush is plausible reconstruction, not an attested episode.

2. **Temple dedication, 303/302 BCE.** **[Secure institution; chronological variance]** The Roman annalistic chronology and modern conversion differ by a year. Livy’s consular framework is commonly rendered 302 BCE in recent discussions; Platner and Ashby render it 303. The cult anniversary—5 August—is more secure than the converted year. [Platner–Ashby, “Aedes Salutis”](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Places/Europe/Italy/Lazio/Roma/Rome/_Texts/PLATOP%2A/Aedes_Salutis.html)

3. **Praeneste inscription, late fourth or third century BCE.** **[Secure epigraphy; location of Salus’s altar uncertain]** CIL I² 62 = ILLRP 132 = EDR110696 records a dedication to Hercules made *ede leigibus ara Salutus*—“according to the same rules as the altar of Salus.” The inscription was found in Praenestine territory, but whether the altar stood at Praeneste or referred to Rome cannot be established.

4. **Horta vessel, first quarter of the third century BCE.** **[Secure object]** CIL I² 450 = ILLRP 254: *Salutes pocolom*, “cup of Salus.” Found in an Etruscan necropolis but written in Latin.

These inscriptions are early, but none securely antedates the Roman vow of 311 BCE.

### Principal ancient texts

- **Varro, *De lingua Latina* 5.52–53, 40s BCE.** **[Late Republican antiquarian evidence]** Preserves the *sacra Argeorum* topography, including the *collis Salutaris* and Salus’s temple. Because the document itself mentions the temple, it cannot establish that the hill-name predates 311 BCE.

- **Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.61–62, 45 BCE.** **[Late Republican learned systematization]** Includes Salus among beneficial abstractions whose usefulness led to their deification. Cicero’s philosophical explanation is evidence for elite interpretation, not necessarily the cult’s origin.

- **Cicero, *De legibus* 2.21 and related fragments.** **[Normative religious formulation]** The augurs are to take auspices concerning the health or safety of the people. The text does not unambiguously say that the goddess Salus herself is the object of the preliminary augury.

- **Cicero, *Pro Sestio* 131 and *Ad Atticum* 4.1.4, 57 BCE.** **[Contemporary personal-political appropriation]** Cicero stresses that his return from exile on 5 August coincided with his daughter Tullia’s birthday, the anniversary of Brundisium, and Salus’s festival. His private *salus* is rhetorically assimilated to public welfare.

- **Terence, *Hecyra* 338, 160 BCE.** **[Literary invocation]** “I pray to you, Aesculapius, and to you, Salus.” This explicitly joins Salus to the Greek healing god in a request concerning bodily health.

- **Livy 9.43.25; 10.1.9; 28.11.4; 40.37, composed chiefly 20s BCE–early first century CE.** **[Augustan historiography]** Supplies the temple’s institutional history, the 206 BCE lightning strike, and the plague rite of 180 BCE.

- **Ovid, *Fasti* 3.879–882, composed circa 2–8 CE and revised later.** **[Augustan calendrical poetry]** Names Janus, Concordia, Roman Salus, and the Ara Pacis for 30 March. This is a joint calendrical observance, not a myth of divine friendship. [Latin text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A2008.01.0547%3Abook%3D3)

- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses*.** Salus is not a developed mythological character. Occurrences of *salus* normally mean safety or deliverance. Claims that Ovid transmits a Salus myth should identify a passage; none has been established.

- **Virgil, *Aeneid* 2.354:** *una salus victis nullam sperare salutem*, “the only safety for the defeated is to hope for no safety.” **[Literary common noun, not secure deity]** It documents the semantic force of *salus*, not an appearance by the goddess.

- **Valerius Maximus 8.14.6, early first century CE.** **[Imperial anecdotal tradition]** Says Gaius Fabius painted and signed the walls of the temple. [Valerius Maximus 8.14.6](https://www.attalus.org/translate/valerius8b.html)

- **Pliny, *Natural History* 35.19, 77 CE.** **[Imperial antiquarian evidence]** Dates Fabius’s paintings to AUC 450 and says that they survived until the temple burned under Claudius. [Pliny, *Natural History* 35.19](https://www.attalus.org/pliny/hn35a.html)

- **Tacitus, *Annals* 15.74, early second century CE.** **[Hostile senatorial interpretation of imperial cult politics]** After the Pisonian conspiracy of 65 CE, the Senate voted that a temple to Salus be erected where Scaevinus had taken up the dagger. Tacitus presents this with other extravagant Neronian honors. It is uncertain whether the proposed temple was completed. [Tacitus, *Annals* 15.74](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/thayer/e/roman/texts/tacitus/annals/15c%2A.html)

- **Cassius Dio 37.24, early third century CE, describing 63 BCE.** **[Late narrative preserving ritual explanation]** Records an *augurium salutis* after a long interruption during a rare respite from war. It asked whether the gods permitted prayer for the people’s prosperity. [Cassius Dio 37.24](https://lexundria.com/dio/37.24.1/cy)

- **Festus/Paul the Deacon, ultimately from Verrius Flaccus.** **[Late epitome of Augustan antiquarian material]** Preserves the Porta Salutaris and damaged information about Salus Semonia. The transmission is fragmentary and cannot be treated as verbatim archaic ritual evidence.

- **Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 1.16.8, early fifth century CE.** **[Late learned systematization]** Lists a festival of Salus Semonia; valuable for antiquarian reception but not independent proof of archaic conditions.

- **Martianus Capella, *De nuptiis* 1.45–61, fifth century CE.** **[Late learned/Etruscanizing system]** Places Salus among beings assigned to the first celestial region. This may preserve Etruscan divinatory classification, but its distance from Republican evidence forbids assuming continuity.

- **Arnobius, *Adversus nationes* 4.1–3, circa 300 CE.** **[Hostile Christian witness]** Lists *Pietas, Concordia, Salus, Honor, Virtus,* and *Felicitas* as concepts for which Romans built altars and temples, then attacks their divinity as empty verbal abstractions. The polemic nevertheless confirms that pagans treated Salus as a deity. [Arnobius 4](https://www.ancienttexts.org/library/latinlibrary/arnobius4.html)

- **Augustine, *City of God* 4–7, 413–427 CE.** **[Hostile Christian witness drawing on lost Varro]** Uses Varro’s theological classifications to criticize Rome’s multiplication of functional and conceptual gods. Augustine is indispensable for fragments of Varro, but his selection serves Christian argument and must not be mistaken for an unmediated transcript.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains by period

- **[Secure Republican core]** Preservation, military-political safety, the continuity and success of the Roman community.
- **[Second-century BCE expansion]** Bodily health and recovery, especially when associated with Apollo and Aesculapius.
- **[Late Republican political elaboration]** The welfare of the commonwealth as secured by a statesman or “saviour.”
- **[Imperial systematization]** Public welfare embodied in the ruler’s safety, health, succession, return, and dynasty.
- **[Provincial/regional variation]** Military security, healing waters, household or neighborhood safety, provincial welfare.
- **[Late antique semantic transformation]** *Salus* increasingly also signifies Christian salvation, while pagan Salus imagery and coin legends are displaced or reinterpreted.

### Iconography

No securely identified cult image from the Quirinal temple survives.

**Republican coinage:**

- A denarius of the moneyer Decimus Junius Silanus, 91 BCE, carries a female head usually identified as Salus because of the moneyer’s ancestral connection with Bubulcus and the temple. **[Scholarly identification, probable but not inscriptionally certain]**
- The denarius of Manius Acilius Glabrio, 49 BCE, labels a head *SALVTIS* and depicts *VALETVDO* standing with a serpent. **[Secure inscription and iconographic linkage]** This is among the earliest explicit numismatic bridges between Roman Salus and Hygieian health imagery.

**Imperial standard type:** a draped woman standing or seated, holding a patera from which she feeds a serpent rising from an altar; frequently she also holds a scepter. The serpent-and-bowl type is derived from Hygieia, not demonstrated for Salus’s fourth-century BCE cult. A British Museum Hadrianic coin provides a representative example. [British Museum coin](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_2014-4035-58)

Other types show Salus:

- seated with patera;
- holding or embracing a serpent;
- resting against a column;
- standing with scepter;
- paired with Aesculapius;
- offering ears of grain or poppies to a serpent;
- standing with Fortuna, Victory, Mars, or imperial personifications;
- represented by an imperial woman, especially Livia.

The stable diagnostic combination is the legend *SALVS* plus the feeding of a serpent. Without a legend, a Hygieia-type female may not be distinguishable as Greek Hygieia, Roman Salus, or a localized healing personification.

### Symbols securely supported

- **Serpent:** healing and Aesculapian/Hygieian affiliation; dominant from the later Republic and especially the Empire.
- **Patera:** ritual offering and feeding of the serpent.
- **Altar:** cult and sacrifice.
- **Scepter:** authority and public character.
- **Cornucopia:** prosperity in some local or political representations.
- **Throne/chair:** sovereign stability or personified public welfare.
- **Laurel and sacrificial animals:** belong to rituals for health and public vows, not exclusively to Salus.

No securely ancient evidence assigns Salus a distinctive sacred color, number, planet, zodiacal sign, or weekday. Modern tables that do so are **modern invention or occult correspondence-building** unless they cite a particular ancient source.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

**[Absence of evidence]** Salus has no securely attested birth narrative, parents, siblings, consort, or offspring. She is not a narrative member of a divine family in the manner of Olympian gods. Modern claims that she is literally Hygieia, a daughter of Aesculapius, import Hygieia’s Greek genealogy after the identification and should not be retrojected onto Republican Salus.

### Every documented named divine interaction or joint appearance

- **Apollo — joint plague cult.** Golden statues of Apollo, Aesculapius, and Salus were ordered during the epidemic of 180 BCE; Livy 40.37, written in the Augustan period. **Tier: securely transmitted Republican ritual; medical syncretism.**

- **Aesculapius — joint healing invocation.** Terence, *Hecyra* 338 (160 BCE), invokes Aesculapius and Salus together for a person’s health. **Tier: securely attested Republican literary/cultic association.**

- **Aesculapius — joint plague statues.** Livy 40.37. **Tier: securely transmitted Republican ritual; syncretistic identification with the Hygieian sphere.**

- **Hygieia — iconographic and translational identification.** Greek authors render Salus as Ὑγίεια; from the late Republic and especially Flavian period Salus takes Hygieia’s serpent-feeding image. **Tier: syncretistic identification, gradual rather than a single event.**

- **Valetudo — differentiated but associated personification.** On the Acilius Glabrio denarius of 49 BCE, *SALVTIS* and *VALETVDO* occupy opposite faces. **Tier: secure Republican numismatic association; relationship disputed.**

- **Janus — joint calendrical worship.** Ovid, *Fasti* 3.879–882, for 30 March. **Tier: Augustan canonical calendrical poetry; no narrative interaction.**

- **Concordia — joint calendrical worship.** Same Ovidian passage; Concordia and Salus also appear together in imperial political ideology and Arval ceremonies held in Concordia’s temple. **Tier: Augustan/imperial cult association.**

- **Pax — joint calendrical worship.** Ovid joins Roman Salus with the Ara Pacis. **Tier: Augustan political-religious systematization.**

- **Jupiter Optimus Maximus — joint annual vows.** Arval records place Salus Publica after Jupiter, Juno Regina, and Minerva in vows for the emperor and state. **Tier: securely attested imperial ritual.**

- **Juno Regina — same annual-vow sequence.** Salus’s vow is formulated by reference to the vow made to Juno; CIL VI 2064 and related Arval acts. **Tier: securely attested imperial ritual.**

- **Minerva — same annual-vow sequence.** Salus follows the Capitoline gods in sacrificial prescriptions. **Tier: securely attested imperial ritual.**

- **Victoria — political association on coinage.** Salus and Victoria jointly articulate rescue through victory; especially civil-war and imperial types. **Tier: secure numismatic ideological association; not narrative myth.**

- **Fortuna / Fortuna Redux — political-numismatic association.** Salus Generis Humani was linked to the victor’s fortune and safe return in 68–69 and later imperial issues. **Tier: imperial ideological association.**

- **Mars Ultor — coordinated civil-war imagery.** Anonymous/Galban coin programs juxtaposed vengeance on the tyrant with public salvation. **Tier: imperial numismatic program; not a narrated meeting.**

- **Sol — adjacent but distinct Neronian thanksgiving.** Tacitus 15.74 reports honors to Sol for exposing the Pisonian conspiracy and a proposed temple to Salus at the dagger’s location. **Tier: securely attested senatorial decree as reported by Tacitus; coordinated honors, not direct interaction.**

- **Semo Sancus/Dius Fidius — hypothesized relation through *Semonia*.** **Tier: contested modern reconstruction based on late and isolated evidence.** No narrative interaction is attested.

- **Hercules — legal/cultic cross-reference.** The Praeneste dedication to Hercules follows the rules of an “altar of Salus.” **Tier: secure epigraphic association, not divine interaction.**

No surviving source records Salus fighting, marrying, seducing, deceiving, judging, rescuing, or conversing with another god. Assertions that Janus, Pax, or Concordia were her “friends” are modern narrative embellishments of joint worship.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Absence of a mythic cycle

Salus has no securely attested narrative mythology. This absence is historically significant. Her record is made of vows, temple foundations, ritual coordination, political language, images, and sacrifice—not divine adventures.

### The temple vow and Samnite victory

**[Historical cult legend]** The closest thing to an origin story is Bubulcus’s vow in the Second Samnite War. Livy establishes the vow but does not say precisely when or why it was made. Modern reconstructions connect it with the Roman escape from a Samnite ambush described at Livy 9.31. The temple and its paintings may have monumentalized Bubulcus’s victory and triumph, but the subjects of the lost murals are not described by Pliny or Valerius Maximus. The popular statement that the frescoes unquestionably depicted “the Samnite battle and triumph” is therefore an inference.

### Aesculapius’s arrival and Salus

Arnobius, *Adversus nationes* 7.44/47, retells and attacks the tradition that Aesculapius came to Rome in serpent form and ended a plague. Salus appears there chiefly as the common noun “health restored to the Roman people,” not as a participating goddess. **[Modern conflation warning]** It is misleading to turn Aesculapius’s arrival into a myth in which Salus personally welcomes or assists him.

### Fabius Pictor’s signature

Valerius Maximus says the noble Gaius Fabius painted and signed the temple walls, seeking lasting fame despite painting’s low artisanal status. Pliny says the earliest bearer of the surname *Pictor* painted the temple and that the work survived until the Claudian fire. **[Imperial anecdotal tradition]** Jerome later confused the painter with—or telescoped him into—the historian Quintus Fabius Pictor. The painter’s identity and the derivation of the cognomen are traditions, not contemporary documentation.

### Nero’s “Salus”

After the failure of the Pisonian conspiracy in 65 CE, the Senate proposed a temple to Salus where the conspirator Scaevinus had taken his dagger. In Tacitus’s narrative, *salus* has become the ruler’s personal survival, treated as identical with the state’s welfare. **[Imperial political episode, not myth]** Later retellings that say Nero built a major new Salus temple exceed the evidence: Tacitus records the decree, not its completion.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Temple on the Quirinal

**Foundation sequence:**

- vowed by Gaius Junius Bubulcus Brutus as consul in 311 BCE;
- contracted while he was censor, conventionally 306 BCE;
- dedicated while dictator in 303/302 BCE;
- anniversary: **5 August**, attested by multiple calendars and Cicero.

The temple stood on the portion of the Quirinal called *collis Salutaris*, near the Temple of Quirinus, Atticus’s house, and the later Clivus Salutis. Two broad modern placements—near the Palazzo del Quirinale or closer to Palazzo Barberini—depend on topographical reconstruction. No structural remains have been securely identified. [Platner–Ashby](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Places/Europe/Italy/Lazio/Roma/Rome/_Texts/PLATOP%2A/Aedes_Salutis.html)

The building was struck by lightning in 276 and 206 BCE, according to prodigy traditions. It burned under Claudius, destroying Fabius’s paintings, but was restored or rebuilt because the fourth-century *Regionary Catalogues* still list it in Regio VI. A senatorial statue of Cato the Elder stood there; Plutarch calls it a temple of Hygieia, illustrating Greek translation of Salus.

### Fabius Pictor’s paintings

Pliny dates the decoration to AUC 450, conventionally 304 BCE; Valerius Maximus names C. Fabius and says he signed it. The paintings are important as one of the earliest recorded monumental Roman painting programs. Their exact subjects, style, and patronal arrangement cannot be reconstructed. They survived, perhaps with repairs, until the Claudian fire.

### Festivals

- **5 August:** **[Secure temple anniversary]** The *dies natalis* of the Quirinal temple.
- **30 March:** **[Secure Augustan calendrical observance]** Ovid’s joint worship of Janus, Concordia, Roman Salus, and Pax.
- **Salus Semonia festival:** **[Late antiquarian evidence]** Macrobius mentions it; its original date and ritual cannot be reconstructed with confidence.
- Claims of a universal annual “Salus festival on 30 April” often derive from older reference works or calendrical confusion and should not displace the better-attested 30 March and 5 August evidence.

### The *augurium salutis*

The ritual has often been described as an annual sacrifice “to the goddess Salus,” but that is not what the clearest source says.

Cassius Dio 37.24 reports that in 63 BCE, after a long interruption, the Romans conducted the *augurium salutis*: an augural inquiry asking whether the god or gods permitted them to pray for the people’s welfare. Because it could be conducted only when no Roman army was at war, it was easily suspended during prolonged expansion. Augustus revived it in 29 BCE; imperial performance continued intermittently, including under Claudius.

**[Contested interpretation]**

- **Traditional view:** the name refers to the goddess and the rite authorized prayer or sacrifice to Salus.
- **Stricter philological view:** *salutis* is an objective genitive—an augury “concerning public safety.” Dio describes permission to ask for the people’s prosperity and does not name the goddess as recipient. The Oxford Classical Dictionary therefore warns that the ceremony “does not appear to be connected” directly with Salus. [Oxford Classical Dictionary, “Salus”](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/550498270)

The evidence permits a conceptual relationship but not identity of ritual and goddess.

### Sacrifice and vows

Arval records from the Flavian period document annual vows for the emperor, imperial household, and state. Salus Publica Populi Romani Quiritium received a **female bovine**, specified in some formulations as white and gilded. Incense, wine, sacrificial meal, knife, and cooked entrails occur in the larger ritual sequence. This is direct epigraphic evidence, unlike generalized descriptions of “offerings to Salus.”

Livy’s plague rite of 180 BCE involved golden statues, consultation of the Sibylline books, and public supplication by everyone over twelve wearing garlands and laurel. It was a state expiation, not private medical treatment.

### Local and private worship

- **Praeneste:** an altar of Salus governed cultic rules followed in a dedication to Hercules.
- **Horta:** the inscribed *pocolom* indicates a vessel dedicated to or belonging to Salus.
- **Pompeii IX.8.8:** painted inscriptions *Salutis* and *Salutei sacrum* stood above cornucopias at a street or entrance shrine. The shrine’s earliest phase belongs to the late second century BCE, while the precise date of the surviving inscriptions is debated. [Pompeii in Pictures](https://www.pompeiiinpictures.com/pompeiiinpictures/r9/9%2008%2008.htm)
- **Ferentinum, Pisaurum, Bagnacavallo, and other Italian sites:** inscriptions attest locally specified forms of Salus, but fragmentary texts often do not reveal whether civic, bodily, agricultural, or household safety was intended.
- **Provinces:** dedications to Salus Augusta, Salus Augusti, military Salus, and healing manifestations show both loyalty and requests for protection. The intended balance must be assessed inscription by inscription.

### Human sacrifice, sexual rites, violence

**[Negative finding]** No primary evidence associates Salus’s cult with human sacrifice, temple prostitution, ritual sexual service, self-mutilation, or cult-directed violence. Animal sacrifice—especially cattle in state vows—is documented. Rome’s wars and imperial coercion supplied the political context of her cult, but that is not evidence that Salus received human victims.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 311 BCE: no secure Salus

**[Disputed reconstruction]** Wissowa and followers reconstructed an archaic Quirinal or Sabine Salus, connected to Semo Sancus, oaths, and a supposedly ancient *collis Salutaris*. Recent criticism observes that all decisive evidence may postdate the temple. The correct finding is agnosticism: an earlier cult is possible but not demonstrated.

### 311–third century BCE: victory temple and Italian diffusion

The temple emerged among the “victory temples” founded by Republican commanders. Salus initially expressed preservation of the civic body through military danger. Early inscriptions from Praeneste and Horta show that the Latin concept circulated beyond Rome. These do not prove centralized Roman imposition; Italian communities could give Salus locally useful meanings.

### 293–180 BCE: encounter with Greek healing cult

Aesculapius was imported from Epidaurus in 293 BCE following plague and Sibylline consultation. By Terence and Livy’s account of 180 BCE, Salus could occupy Hygieia’s healing position beside Aesculapius. This did not erase public welfare. It added bodily health to a broader field of safety and wholeness.

### Late Republic: public salvation and political saviours

Coinage made Salus visible and manipulable. The Acilius coin related her to Valetudo; Junian family issues recalled Bubulcus’s temple. Cicero made *salus rei publicae* a central political value and assimilated his return from exile to Rome’s salvation. Hellenistic *sōtēr* language and Italian traditions interacted, but the thesis that Salus’s cult originated as imitation of ruler-saviour cult lacks adequate evidence.

### Augustus and Tiberius: ruler, dynasty, and public welfare

Augustus revived the *augurium salutis* in 29 BCE and placed Salus within a program of Pax and Concordia. Vows shifted in wording from the ruler’s *valetudo* toward his *salus*. Under Tiberius, coins of 22–23 CE represented Livia as *Salus Augusta*, accompanied in the larger series by *Pietas* and *Iustitia*. Public welfare was thus embodied in dynastic continuity.

### Nero and 68–69 CE: imperial survival and deliverance from tyranny

After 65, senatorial decrees transformed Nero’s escape from assassination into public Salus. Following Nero’s fall, rival regimes reversed the claim: *Salus Generis Humani*, *Salus et Libertas*, Victoria, Mars Ultor, Pax, and Concordia represented liberation from Nero and legitimate restoration. This ideological reversal is one of the clearest demonstrations that Salus was politically contested rather than doctrinally fixed.

### Flavian through Antonine periods: Hygieian Salus becomes standard

Under the Flavians, *Salus Augusti* and the serpent-feeding Hygieia type became standard. Hadrian applied comparable imagery to *Salus Publica*, incorporating provincial well-being into public welfare. From Hadrian onward a Salus coin need not respond to a particular illness; it became routine imperial reassurance. Winkler’s archaeological study traces this evolution from state cult to political idea. [BMCR review of Winkler](https://bmcr.brynmawr.edu/1997/1997.03.07/)

### Third century: ubiquity amid instability

During military and political crisis, *SALVS AVG*, *SALVS AVGG*, *SALVS EXERCITI*, and related legends recur across imperial and breakaway coinages. The image promised that emperor, army, and empire remained intact. Frequent minting does not demonstrate intensified temple worship; coins are evidence for official representation.

### Constantine and late antiquity

Traditional Salus types continued into the Constantinian age. Simultaneously, *salus* became a central Christian Latin term for salvation, Christ’s saving work, and the welfare granted by the Christian God. Late coins could use *salus rei publicae* with Christian signs, transferring the political vocabulary rather than necessarily preserving worship of the goddess. The fourth-century Regionary Catalogues still list the Quirinal temple, but continued listing does not establish active sacrifice.

### Suppression and disappearance

Imperial anti-sacrifice legislation in the later fourth century and the closure or abandonment of temples ended Salus’s public cult with Roman civic religion generally. No source records a distinctive violent suppression of her temple or priesthood. Nor is there evidence of continuous clandestine Salus worship through the Middle Ages. The name survived primarily as Latin vocabulary, allegory, medical symbolism, and antiquarian knowledge.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Renaissance and early-modern allegory

Humanist artists and physicians recombined Salus, Hygieia, and medical allegory. A particularly instructive Renaissance image analyzed by Washington University’s Becker Medical Library is not a straightforward copy of an ancient Salus but a new composite of Salus, Hygieia, and Pythagorean medicine. **Tier: documented early-modern invention.** [“A Goddess for the Renaissance”](https://becker.wustl.edu/news/goddess-for-the-renaissance/)

### Medicine and pharmacy

The woman feeding a serpent from a bowl became a durable emblem of health and pharmacy. In modern usage it is frequently called the Bowl of Hygieia; where labeled Salus, the image reflects their imperial Roman fusion. Claims that the serpent was Salus’s primordial Roman attribute are historically inaccurate.

### Political and commercial vocabulary

*Salus populi suprema lex esto*—“let the welfare of the people be the supreme law,” from Cicero, *De legibus* 3.8—has entered legal, civic, and emergency rhetoric. Here *salus* is a common noun, not an invocation of the goddess. Institutions named Salus, including medical, insurance, security, and educational organizations, generally exploit the Latin meaning rather than practice cult.

### Modern paganism

Contemporary Roman reconstructionists and eclectic polytheists sometimes worship Salus, commonly using domestic offerings, reconstructed Roman prayer forms, 30 March or 5 August observances, and health-related petitions. **Tier: modern revival, not continuous survival.** No reliable population figures isolate Salus worshippers, and there is no evidence of institutional continuity from antiquity.

Some online prayers address her with phrases modeled on Catholic Marian language or assign crystals, colors, planets, chakras, or tarot correspondences. Unless an identifiable modern author and date are given, these are best classified as **modern eclectic invention**, not ancient tradition.

### Occult and esoteric movements

The dedicated search found no significant role for Salus in the canonical rituals or grade systems of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, Wicca, or classical chaos-magic literature. General statements that she was “important to the Golden Dawn” are unsupported. Modern invocations may occur in individual eclectic practice but do not establish a transmitted Salus current.

### Popular culture

Salus has not acquired a major modern narrative persona comparable to Mars, Venus, or Hades. Her strongest modern afterlife is lexical and emblematic: public health, safety, medicine, insurance, and coins. The lack of an ancient narrative cycle limited her adoption as a character in novels, films, comics, and games.

### Nationalist or extremist appropriation

No sustained, specifically Salus-centered extremist cult or political movement was identified. Roman imperial and fascist-influenced movements have employed general Roman vocabulary of national *salus*, but using the noun “salvation/welfare” is not evidence of devotion to the goddess. Specific claims require evidence case by case.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### “Abstract personification” versus deity

Nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century scholars often arranged Roman conceptual gods in an evolutionary sequence: common noun → literary personification → deity under Greek influence. Axtell’s *Deification of Abstract Ideas*, Wissowa’s *Religion und Kultus der Römer*, and related scholarship exemplify this approach.

Recent work by Anna Clark and Daniele Miano objects that “abstract deity” is a modern analytical category and that Romans did not need to resolve whether Salus was an “idea” or a “real goddess.” The temple, vows, sacrifices, and direct address are religious facts; rhetorical personification and cult were not mutually exclusive. [Miano research record](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/156645/)

### Archaic Salus and *Semonia*

- **Wissowa/Latte reconstruction:** Salus was ancient, possibly Sabine, part of a Quirinal complex, related to Semo Sancus through *Semonia*, and originally concerned with comprehensive civic welfare.
- **Recent critique:** *collis Salutaris* may be named after the fourth-century temple; Festus and Macrobius are late; CIL VI 30975 is an altered inscription of 1 CE; and *Semo Sancus* itself has a complex name-history. The evidence does not securely carry Salus before 311 BCE.

The second position presently rests on firmer source criticism.

### Greek influence

Older accounts sometimes portray Salus as a Roman abstraction later “turned into” Hygieia. The evidence is more complex:

- public-safety Salus is distinctively Roman;
- Greek conceptual personification and ruler-saviour language may have influenced her public articulation;
- Hygieia supplied medical function and imagery;
- Italian communities participated actively in translation;
- *Sōtēria*, *Hygieia*, *Valetudo*, and *Salus* never became perfectly interchangeable.

### The *augurium salutis*

This is the most persistent categorical error. The ceremony concerns permission to seek public *salus*. Direct dedication to the goddess is not stated by Dio. Its conceptual proximity to her cult is obvious, but identifying it as “Salus’s annual sacrifice on 5 August” combines distinct evidence.

### Temple chronology and the year 302

Ancient sources use consular and *ab urbe condita* dating, while modern chronological tables differ. Consequently, dedication dates appear as 303, 302, or occasionally 304 BCE. Livy’s sequence and the 5 August anniversary matter more than false precision. Pliny’s AUC 450 concerns the paintings and is itself not perfectly aligned with Livy’s scheme.

### Fabius Pictor

Ancient evidence supports a noble C. Fabius as painter and signatory. It does not securely describe his subjects. Confusion with the historian Q. Fabius Pictor is late. Attempts either to reject the painter entirely or to reconstruct a detailed Samnite panorama go beyond the surviving record.

### State welfare versus bodily health

Marwood emphasized multiple Salus traditions; Winkler traced the increasing imperial fusion of ruler’s health, public welfare, and Hygieian imagery. Miano emphasizes flexible conceptual translation and local agency. The current evidence supports chronological layering rather than a choice between “goddess of health” and “goddess of the state.”

### State of the evidence

- The Quirinal temple has not been securely excavated.
- Its paintings and cult statue are lost.
- Literary notices are predominantly centuries later than the foundation.
- Republican inscriptions are brief and context-poor.
- Imperial coinage is abundant but represents official messages, not a census of belief.
- CIL, ILLRP, the Arval records, calendar fragments, coin catalogues, and museum collections constitute the chief documentary corpora.
- The *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum* remains fundamental and continues to provide archival and digital resources. [CIL resources](https://cil.bbaw.de/en/home/archive/resources)

## 10. Chronology

- **Before 311 BCE:** No secure attestation. Archaic/Sabine Salus is a disputed reconstruction.

- **311 BCE:** Gaius Junius Bubulcus Brutus vows a temple to Salus during his consulship and the Second Samnite War. **Evidence:** Livy 9.43.25 retrospectively.

- **306 BCE:** Bubulcus, as censor, contracts for construction. **Evidence:** Livy 9.43.25.

- **303/302 BCE, 5 August:** Bubulcus, as dictator, dedicates the Quirinal temple. **Evidence:** Livy 10.1.9 and calendars; exact converted year varies.

- **Circa 304–302 BCE:** Gaius Fabius Pictor decorates and signs the temple. **Evidence:** Pliny 35.19; Valerius Maximus 8.14.6.

- **Late fourth–third century BCE:** Praenestine inscription refers to the rules of an *ara Salutus*. **Evidence:** CIL I² 62.

- **First quarter of third century BCE:** *Salutes pocolom* deposited at Horta. **Evidence:** CIL I² 450.

- **293 BCE:** Aesculapius’s Roman cult is introduced; this creates the institutional setting for later Salus–Hygieia translation.

- **276 BCE:** Temple reportedly struck by lightning. **Evidence:** Orosius/prodigy tradition.

- **180 BCE:** During plague, golden statues ordered for Apollo, Aesculapius, and Salus; public health supplication follows. **Evidence:** Livy 40.37.

- **160 BCE:** Terence invokes Aesculapius and Salus together. **Evidence:** *Hecyra* 338.

- **Late second/first century BCE:** Pompeian *Salutis / Salutei sacrum* shrine inscription.

- **91 BCE:** Probable Salus head on Junian family coinage.

- **63 BCE:** *Augurium salutis* held after long interruption. **Evidence:** Cassius Dio 37.24.

- **57 BCE, 5 August:** Cicero returns to Italy on Salus’s festival and exploits the coincidence politically. **Evidence:** *Pro Sestio* 131; *Ad Atticum* 4.1.4.

- **49 BCE:** Acilius Glabrio coin explicitly pairs *SALVTIS* and *VALETVDO*.

- **29 BCE:** Augustus revives the *augurium salutis*. **Evidence:** imperial historical tradition.

- **Circa 2–8 CE:** Ovid records 30 March worship of Janus, Concordia, Roman Salus, and Pax.

- **1 CE:** CIL VI 30975 receives the added phrase *Salus Semonia*.

- **22–23 CE:** Tiberian coins portray Livia as *SALVS AVGVSTA*.

- **Claudian reign, 41–54 CE:** Quirinal temple burns; Fabius’s paintings are destroyed. It is subsequently restored.

- **49 CE:** Claudius performs the *augurium salutis* according to later historical testimony.

- **65 CE:** Senate decrees a temple to Salus at the site associated with the Pisonian conspirators’ dagger. Completion is unestablished.

- **68–69 CE:** *Salus Generis Humani*, *Salus et Libertas*, and related types redefine salvation as deliverance from Nero.

- **Flavian period:** Hygieian serpent-feeding iconography and *Salus Augusti* become standard; Arval acts document cattle sacrifice to Salus Publica.

- **Second century CE:** Salus becomes a regular imperial coin type; Hadrianic *Salus Publica* expands the political imagery to the provinces.

- **Third century CE:** *SALVS AVG/AVGG*, military Salus, and related legends proliferate across imperial and breakaway regimes.

- **Circa 300 CE:** Arnobius attacks Salus and other conceptual deities as deified words, incidentally confirming their temples and cult.

- **Fourth century CE:** The Regionary Catalogues still list the Quirinal temple. Traditional Salus coin-types persist into the Constantinian period.

- **Late fourth–fifth centuries:** Public pagan sacrifice is suppressed; *salus* becomes increasingly Christianized as the standard Latin term for salvation. Macrobius and Martianus preserve antiquarian systems involving Salus.

- **Middle Ages:** No evidence of continuous worship. Salus survives as Latin vocabulary and in manuscripts of Roman authors.

- **Renaissance–early modern period:** Humanist medical allegory recombines Salus and Hygieia.

- **Nineteenth–twentieth centuries:** Wissowa, Axtell, Latte, Dumézil, Marwood, and Winkler construct competing historical models of Salus.

- **Twenty-first century:** Conceptual and translation-centered studies revise the “abstract personification” and archaic-Semonia models; small modern polytheist communities revive worship without institutional continuity.
