# Quirinus: Research Dossier

**Class:** Roman male god (*deus*); later identified with the deified founder Romulus.  
**Earliest secure attestation:** Quirinus’s cult is securely historical by **293 BCE**, when the consul Lucius Papirius Cursor dedicated his temple on the Quirinal, according to Livy 10.46.7–8. The cult must be older than that building: Roman antiquarian tradition assigns Quirinus a major flamen and an earlier shrine, but neither can be independently dated to the regal period. The earliest surviving direct representations and calendar records are late Republican—the *Fasti Antiates Maiores* and a denarius of 56 BCE—while literary evidence beginning with Ennius preserves a second-century-BCE tradition of Romulus’s apotheosis.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

Quirinus was one of the most prestigious but least narratively defined gods of Rome. His priest, the **Flamen Quirinalis**, belonged with the flamines of Jupiter and Mars among the three *flamines maiores*. Ancient ritual classifications and later Roman antiquarians consequently placed Jupiter, Mars, and Quirinus together in what modern scholarship calls the “archaic triad.” By the late Republic, Quirinus was commonly—but not without residual ambiguity—identified with the apotheosized Romulus. The combination made him simultaneously an inherited civic deity, a god associated with the Roman citizen body, a martial or spear-bearing power, and the divine form of Rome’s founder.

### Names and spellings

- **Latin:** *Quirīnus*, conventionally pronounced approximately /kʷiˈriː.nus/ in Classical Latin.
- **Epigraphic:** **QVIRINVS**, dative **QVIRINO**.
- **Recorded variants:** *Curinus, Corinus, Querinus,* and *Queirinus*. Some are dialectal or antiquarian spellings; others may be scribal.
- **Greek:** Κυρῖνος or Κουῖρινος in Greek-language discussions of Roman religion; the transliteration varies.
- **Romulus Quirinus:** the standard late Republican and Augustan divine identity of Rome’s founder.
- **Pater Quirinus:** “Father Quirinus,” a civic and ancestral style.
- **Mars Quirinus:** an identification or epithet found in antiquarian interpretation, not proof that Quirinus was always merely a form of Mars.
- **Janus Quirinus:** an ancient epithet of Janus. It does not make Janus and Quirinus identical; its meaning—Janus connected with the Quirites, peace, arms, or the ritual transition between war and peace—is disputed.
- **Hora Quirini:** “Hora of Quirinus,” the female associate whom later literary mythology identifies as the deified Hersilia.
- **Virites Quirini:** “powers/strengths of Quirinus,” an obscure divine abstraction preserved by antiquarian testimony.

### Ancient etymologies

**[Later canonical elaboration]** Ovid, *Fasti* 2.475–480 (published about 8 CE), presents three alternatives:

1. Sabine *curis*, “spear”: Romulus became Quirinus as a spear-bearing or martial god.
2. *Quirites*, the Roman citizens: the king took his name from his people.
3. *Cures*, the Sabine town: the name commemorated Romulus’s incorporation of the Sabines.

Festus/Paulus preserves the gloss: *Curis est Sabine hasta*—“*curis* is ‘spear’ in Sabine”—and derives both Quirinus and Quirites from it. Plutarch, *Romulus* 29, likewise records the “Mars,” “citizen,” and “spear” explanations. Servius adds fanciful derivations from Greek κοίρανος, “ruler,” and from ideas of nobility. These are ancient etymological traditions, not linguistic demonstrations.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction: disputed]** The influential reconstruction *\*co-virī-no-* or *\*co-virium*, “of the assembled men,” connects Quirinus, *Quirites*, and perhaps *curia* with the organized citizen body. It underlies the interpretation of Quirinus as “god of the assembled citizens.” The *Oxford Classical Dictionary* calls it the most plausible traditional explanation, but Michiel de Vaan objects that it is phonetically and semantically unconvincing. The surviving evidence does not decide between an indigenous civic formation and borrowing from a Sabellic language.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction: contested]** Patrice Lajoye has proposed that Quirinus was originally a thunder god, reconsidering the theonym and the storm attending Romulus’s disappearance. This is a modern hypothesis, not an ancient classification.

### Quirites

*Quirites* was a formal designation for Roman citizens, especially when addressed as a civic body rather than as soldiers. The precise relation between *Quirites* and Quirinus remains disputed:

- the people named the god;
- the god gave his name to the people;
- both derive from Cures;
- both derive from a word for spear;
- or both belong to a linguistic complex involving assembled *viri* and the *curiae*.

No surviving archaic document establishes the direction of derivation.

### Sabine god?

**[Ancient learned systematization]** Varro, Ovid, Plutarch, Festus, and later commentators repeatedly relate Quirinus to Sabine Cures, Sabine vocabulary, or the Sabine component of Rome.

**[Modern reconstruction]** Many historians have consequently called him a Sabine god brought to Rome by the settlement on the Quirinal or by the Romulus–Titus Tatius union. Others caution that “Sabine” was itself an antiquarian explanatory category and that no securely identified pre-Roman sanctuary or Sabine inscription names Quirinus. Michel Humm summarizes major competing interpretations: Quirinus as original Romulus/founder; god of the citizens; juridical protector of citizen rights; or local deity of the Sabine community on the Quirinal.

Accordingly, “Quirinus was a Sabine god” should be labeled **probable ancient and modern reconstruction**, not securely attested prehistoric fact.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Earliest evidence

**293 BCE: temple dedication.** Livy 10.46.7–8 reports that the consul Lucius Papirius Cursor dedicated and decorated with enemy spoils a temple of Quirinus vowed earlier by his father while dictator. Livy expressly doubts a claim that the vow occurred in the final battle, because the building could not have been completed so quickly. This is retrospective Augustan historiography, but it records a public monument whose later existence is independently secure. It is therefore the earliest securely dated event in Quirinus’s cult history.

**Earlier shrine and priesthood.** Festus and other antiquarian sources knew a *sacellum Quirini* near the Porta Quirinalis. The major flamen’s antiquity and Quirinus’s position in inherited ritual strongly imply a cult older than 293 BCE, but neither the shrine’s foundation nor the priesthood can be securely dated from contemporary evidence.

**Second century BCE: literary apotheosis.** Ennius’s fragmentary *Annales*, composed during the first half of the second century BCE, said that Romulus lived in heaven with the gods and helped establish the Roman apotheosis tradition. The surviving Ennian fragments do not securely demonstrate every detail of the later Romulus–Quirinus equation; reconstruction of their context depends on later quotations.

**Before 55 BCE: calendrical evidence.** The *Fasti Antiates Maiores*, the oldest substantially preserved Roman calendar, records the **Quirinalia on 17 February**. Although the inscribed calendar is late Republican, its festival system incorporates considerably older material.

**56 BCE: direct image and name.** A denarius issued by Gaius Memmius shows a laureate male head labeled **QVIRINVS**. This is the earliest securely dated surviving named image normally identified as Quirinus.

### Principal literary corpus

- **Ennius, *Annales* 1** — Latin; early second century BCE; survives only in later quotations. Romulus’s celestial existence is part of Rome’s earliest surviving national epic tradition. The exact wording and extent of his identification with Quirinus remain fragmentary.
- **Cicero, *De re publica* 2.17–20** — Latin; composed 54–51 BCE, incompletely preserved. Proculus Julius announces that the ascended Romulus is a god named Quirinus and asks for a shrine on the hill.
- **Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.62 and 3.39–41** — Latin; 45 BCE. Romulus/Quirinus is cited among humans elevated to divine status. The philosophical dialogue does not simply endorse every example; it exposes disagreement about the grounds of divinity.
- **Cicero, *Tusculan Disputations* 1.28** — Latin; 45 BCE. Romulus is presented among mortals believed to have reached heaven.
- **Varro, *Antiquitates rerum divinarum*** — Latin; first century BCE; lost, surviving through Augustine, Tertullian, and other quotations. Varro’s systematic Roman theology is indispensable but recoverable only through selective, frequently hostile Christian witnesses.
- **Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 1.16; 10.46.7–8** — Latin; written principally in the late first century BCE. Book 1 gives Romulus’s disappearance, the suspicion that senators dismembered him, and Proculus’s vision. It speaks of his divinity without preserving in the surviving speech the explicit name Quirinus. Book 10 records the temple dedication.
- **Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.291–296; 6.777–787** — Latin; 29–19 BCE. Jupiter foretells that “Quirinus with his brother Remus” will give laws; Anchises later invokes Romulus’s divine destiny. “Quirinus with Remus” is a politically charged poetic reconciliation of the murdered twins, not a cult genealogy.
- **Ovid, *Fasti* 2.475–532; 4.807–862; 6.795–796** — Latin; largely complete by 8 CE. The fullest calendar narrative of the Quirinalia, etymologies of the name, Romulus’s apotheosis, Proculus’s encounter, the *Feriae Stultorum*, and the temple’s June anniversary.
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.805–851** — Latin; completed about 8 CE. Mars requests Romulus’s promised apotheosis from Jupiter; Romulus becomes Quirinus; Juno and Iris arrange Hersilia’s ascent; Quirinus receives and renames her Hora.
- **Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities* 2.48, 2.56, 2.63** — Greek; late first century BCE. Supplies Greek antiquarian accounts of Rome’s Sabine component, Quirinus, Enyalius, priesthood, and early institutions.
- **Plutarch, *Romulus* 27–29; *Numa* 7; *Roman Questions* 27, 46** — Greek; late first/early second century CE. Preserves alternative death stories, Proculus’s testimony, competing etymologies, the spear/Juno Quiritis complex, and Hora.
- **Festus, *De verborum significatu*, epitomized by Paulus** — Latin; Festus probably second century CE, abridging Augustan Verrius Flaccus; surviving manuscript medieval and defective. Essential for *curis*, the Quirinalia, ritual vocabulary, and the shrine.
- **Pliny, *Natural History* 7.60 and 34.23** — Latin; 77 CE. Supplies notices about Papirius’s temple, spoils, and statuary.
- **Cassius Dio 41.14; 43.45; 54.19** — Greek; early third century CE. Records the temple fire, Caesar’s statue beside Quirinus, and Augustus’s reconstruction.
- **Servius, commentary on *Aeneid* 1.292** — Latin; late fourth/early fifth century CE. Says Mars raging is Gradivus and at peace is Quirinus, while compiling variant etymologies. This is late learned exegesis, not a transparent archaic doctrine.
- **Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 1.9.16** — Latin; early fifth century CE. Preserves the armed and martial interpretation and the Janus Quirinus complex.
- **Augustine, *City of God* 4.15; 4.23; 6.5 and elsewhere** — Latin; 413–426 CE. A hostile Christian witness using Varro. He emphasizes that Romans granted Romulus-Quirinus a major flamen while denying comparable honor to supposedly greater gods.
- **Arnobius, *Adversus nationes*** — Latin; about 300 CE. Treats Romulus’s divinization polemically among human beings made gods. Valuable as evidence for late-antique discourse, not as an impartial account of archaic cult.
- **Polemius Silvius, calendar** — Latin; 448/449 CE. Associates the Quirinalia with Romulus’s departure. This is the only calendar explicitly placing that event on 17 February; most narratives locate it in July or leave the date unstated.

### Manuscript caution

No autograph survives for any of these literary texts. Ennius and Varro are fragmentary and mediated by quotations; Festus survives through a damaged eleventh-century manuscript and Paulus’s eighth-century epitome. The apparently “archaic” theology in late authors may therefore reflect several stages of antiquarian reconstruction.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

**[Securely attested institutional core]**

Quirinus was:

- recipient of a fixed public festival;
- served by one of the three major flamines;
- associated with a Quirinal sanctuary, temple, and Salian priestly group;
- linked by name or ancient interpretation with the *Quirites*, the Roman citizen body.

**[Ancient interpretation]** The spear etymology and comparison with Mars made him martial. Servius’s formula—Mars violent as Gradivus, peaceful as Quirinus—casts Quirinus as martial power returned to civic peace.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** Because the Flamen Quirinalis officiated at the Robigalia, Consualia, and Larentalia, scholars have interpreted Quirinus as concerned with grain, agricultural abundance, storage, the settled community, or civic productivity. The rites themselves are attested; the conclusion that Quirinus was fundamentally an “agricultural god” is inferential.

**[Modern scholarly reconstruction: Dumézil]** Georges Dumézil placed Quirinus in the Indo-European “third function”—fertility, production, wealth, and the organized mass of cultivators—below Jupiter’s sovereignty and Mars’s force. This elegantly relates the three major flamines and the Jupiter–Mars–Quirinus sequence, but it is a comparative model, not an explicit Roman doctrine.

### Powers and characteristic actions

In the developed Romulus tradition Quirinus:

- receives Romulus’s mortal substance and divine identity;
- protects Rome and promises its dominion;
- becomes an exemplar for later Roman apotheosis;
- receives Hersilia/Hora into divine union;
- embodies the founder as civic guardian.

No ancient source gives him an independent adventure-cycle comparable to Jupiter, Mars, or Hercules. His obscurity is not evidence of a lost, fully developed mythology.

### Iconography

The securely identifiable iconography is sparse.

- **Memmian denarius, 56 BCE:** laureate male head labeled **QVIRINVS**. Recognition depends on the inscription, not distinctive attributes.
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.827–828:** Quirinus appears in the *trabea*, the striped ceremonial garment associated with Roman kings, augurs, and high magistracy. This is literary iconography shaped by the Romulus equation.
- **Augustan temple:** Cassius Dio describes a monumental rebuilding with seventy-six columns; Vitruvius’s description of a dipteral, octastyle Doric temple probably reflects the late Republican predecessor. A Roman relief, dated variously to the Flavian or second-century period, appears to depict its façade, with Romulus and Remus taking auspices in the pediment.
- **Cult statue:** ancient sources imply one, especially when Caesar’s image was placed beside Quirinus, but no securely identified original survives.
- **Weapons:** Festus preserves a ritual in which the Flamen Portunalis greased Quirinus’s arms on the Portunalia. The precise weapons and ritual meaning are not recoverable.
- **Spear:** strongly present in ancient etymology and comparison with Juno Quiritis and Mars; it is not securely attached to every surviving visual representation.
- **Sacred animals, colors, planets, numbers:** none is securely and distinctively documented for Quirinus. Modern lists assigning him wolves, particular colors, planets, crystals, or weekdays are modern constructions unless they explicitly derive them from Romulus or Mars.

### The Quirinal and possible Italic/Etruscan evidence

The **collis Quirinalis** originally designated one summit and later the broader Quirinal hill. Ancient writers derived it from Quirinus or from Sabine settlers from Cures. The direction of naming cannot be established.

No Etruscan bronze mirror is securely labeled with an Etruscan Quirinus. Comparisons with Etruscan armed, double, or civic gods—and especially with Culsans—are comparative proposals, not iconographic attestations of Quirinus. Thulin’s work on Etruscan religion is relevant to the broader Italic environment but does not supply a demonstrably Etruscan Quirinus.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogical traditions

**As an autonomous old god:** Quirinus has no securely attested parents, birth, or childhood. This is likely the older cultic condition, although it cannot be turned into a dated biography.

**As Romulus deified:** Quirinus inherits Romulus’s genealogy:

- **Father:** Mars, in the dominant Roman version.
- **Mother:** Rhea Silvia/Ilia.
- **Twin brother:** Remus.
- **Remote ancestry:** through Rhea Silvia, the Alban royal line and ultimately Aeneas.
- **Wife:** Hersilia, subsequently Hora.
- **Children:** Plutarch reports disputed traditions assigning Romulus a daughter Prima and son Aollius/Avillius; these do not become important parts of Quirinus’s cult.

### Every documented divine interaction

- **Jupiter — grants apotheosis.** Mars reminds Jupiter of his promise; Jupiter assents and Romulus’s mortal body dissolves. Ovid, *Fasti* 2.481–512 and *Metamorphoses* 14.805–828, about 8 CE. **[Later canonical elaboration]**
- **Mars — father and advocate.** Mars calls Romulus his son and petitions Jupiter to raise him to heaven. Ovid, *Fasti* 2.481–488; *Metamorphoses* 14.805–816. **[Later canonical elaboration]**
- **Remus — restored co-legislator.** Virgil foretells Quirinus and his brother Remus giving laws together. *Aeneid* 1.292–293, 20s BCE. **[Augustan poetic elaboration]**
- **Hora/Hersilia — divine spouse.** Quirinus receives the ascended Hersilia, changes her body and name, and is thereafter worshipped with Hora. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.829–851. **[Later canonical elaboration]**
- **Juno — facilitates reunion with Hersilia.** Juno sends Iris to lead Hersilia toward Quirinus’s grove and temple. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.829–841. Quirinus and Juno do not converse directly. **[Later canonical elaboration]**
- **Iris — conducts Hersilia toward him.** Iris carries Juno’s message and guides Hersilia; Quirinus then receives her. Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.830–851. **[Later canonical elaboration]**
- **Mars/Gradivus — theological equation or contrast.** Servius states that Mars when raging is Gradivus and when tranquil is Quirinus. Commentary on *Aeneid* 1.292, late fourth/early fifth century CE. This is not a narrative encounter. **[Late learned systematization/syncretistic identification]**
- **Enyalius — Greek equation.** Dionysius of Halicarnassus reports an equation of Quirinus with Enyalius, a Greek martial deity sometimes distinguished from and sometimes identified with Ares. *Roman Antiquities*, late first century BCE. **[Interpretatio Graeca]**
- **Janus — conjunction in the title Janus Quirinus.** Ancient ritual language joins the names but records no meeting or shared myth. Macrobius 1.9.16 and related antiquarian testimony. **[Learned/ritual association; no narrative interaction]**
- **Juno Quiritis — parallel spear terminology.** Plutarch uses her spear and title to explain Quirinus’s name. *Romulus* 29, early second century CE. **[Ancient etymological comparison; no narrative interaction]**
- **Consus, Robigus, and Acca Larentia — ritual proximity only.** The Flamen Quirinalis served in their festivals or rites. This establishes priestly linkage, not interaction between the gods. **[Secure ritual association; no mythic interaction]**
- **Portunus — priestly mediation.** The Flamen Portunalis greased Quirinus’s weapons. Festus/Paulus. The source records an inter-cult ritual, not a divine encounter. **[Attested ritual association]**

No securely attested narrative records Quirinus fighting another god, fathering a god, stealing a divine object, judging a divine contest, or participating in a divine council independently of the Romulus apotheosis.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### A. Romulus’s disappearance and apotheosis

**Earliest recoverable core:** Ennius in the second century BCE had Romulus living among the gods. How much of the later storm, Proculus, and name Quirinus appeared in Ennius cannot be established.

**Livian version:** During an army review at the Caprae Palus in the Campus Martius, a violent storm hides Romulus. When it clears, the throne is empty. Senators say he was carried to heaven. The people salute him as god, son of a god, king, and father of Rome. Livy also reports the darker alternative: senators may have torn him limb from limb. Proculus Julius later claims that Romulus descended from heaven and commanded Rome to cultivate military power and rule the world. Livy openly calls attention to the political convenience and remarkable acceptance of this testimony.

**Ciceronian version:** In *De re publica* 2.20, Proculus says Romulus appeared on the hill, declared himself the god Quirinus, and requested a sanctuary. This version more explicitly connects the apparition, name, and cult site.

**Ovidian *Fasti* version:** Mars asks Jupiter to fulfill his promise to deify his surviving son. Jupiter assents; a storm surrounds Romulus, Mars carries him heavenward, and his mortal substance falls away. Romulus becomes Quirinus. Proculus encounters the enlarged, splendid god, who commands the Romans to bring incense and practice ancestral and martial arts.

**Ovidian *Metamorphoses* version:** Mars descends in his chariot and carries Romulus upward. The mortal body dissolves “like a leaden bullet” melting in the sky; a beautiful divine form in the *trabea* replaces it.

**Plutarchan version:** Plutarch preserves both murder and assumption traditions. He notes suspicion that senators cut Romulus to pieces and carried the fragments away under their clothing. Proculus then swears that Romulus appeared in supernatural majesty, directed the Romans toward temperance and valor, and announced that he would be their gracious god Quirinus.

**Function:** The myth legitimized Rome’s founder cult, transformed unexplained royal disappearance into divine guardianship, and supplied a Roman precedent for the apotheosis of Caesar and emperors. Livy’s acknowledgment of murder and managed testimony shows that skeptical and sacral readings coexisted.

### B. Hersilia becomes Hora

In Ovid, Hersilia mourns Romulus. Juno sends Iris, who directs her to Quirinus’s grove and temple. A star descends, ignites her hair with light, and carries her upward. Quirinus embraces her, transforms her body, and names her Hora. She becomes a goddess joined to him.

**[Later canonical elaboration]** This is securely attested as Augustan literature, but its relationship to older cult is uncertain. *Hora Quirini* may have existed as an old divine abstraction or consort title; the identification with Romulus’s wife Hersilia may be literary myth-making.

### C. Quirinus and Remus as reconciled founders

Virgil’s Jupiter predicts an age in which “Remus with his brother Quirinus” will give laws. The line overturns the fratricidal foundation story and projects restored concord into Rome’s Augustan future.

**[Augustan political-poetic elaboration]** There is no evidence for an old joint cult of Quirinus and a deified Remus.

### D. Sabine incorporation

Ancient etymology made Quirinus’s name commemorate Romulus’s union of Rome with Sabine Cures. In the broader foundation story, the abducted Sabine women stop the war between Romulus and Titus Tatius; Romans and Sabines combine, and the united citizens become Quirites.

**[Ancient learned systematization]** The story explains civic institutions and ethnic mixture. It does not independently prove that Quirinus was imported from historical Cures.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Sanctuary and temple

**The archaic shrine:** Festus knew a *sacellum Quirini* near the Porta Quirinalis. Its physical remains have not been securely isolated.

**Republican temple:** Livy dates the dedication to **293 BCE**. It stood on the Quirinal, probably north of the Alta Semita in or near the eastern part of the modern Quirinal Palace gardens. Inscriptions CIL VI 475 and 565 and early-modern discovery records help establish the approximate area.

The temple reportedly contained:

- spoils captured from the Samnites;
- a shrine or cult-place of Hora;
- an early sundial attributed to Papirius;
- Quirinus’s cult image.

The building burned in **49 BCE** and was restored by **45 BCE**. Julius Caesar accepted the provocative honor of a statue placed beside Quirinus bearing language rendered as “to the unconquered god.” Cassius Dio treats this among honors that approached divine status.

Augustus completely rebuilt and rededicated the temple in **16 BCE**, listing the *aedes Quirini* among his works in *Res Gestae* 19. Cassius Dio describes a monumental structure with seventy-six columns. Its reconstruction linked Augustus with Rome’s founder while avoiding a simple declaration that Augustus himself was Quirinus.

The temple remained known into late antiquity. Its date of abandonment or destruction is not documented. No source records a specific Christian seizure, execution, or forced-conversion episode at the sanctuary.

### Flamen Quirinalis

The Flamen Quirinalis was one of the three patrician *flamines maiores*, traditionally appointed under Numa. The office was for life unless forfeited through ritual fault. The threefold rank of the Dialis, Martialis, and Quirinalis is the strongest evidence for Quirinus’s archaic prestige.

Attested or reported duties include:

- **Robigalia, 25 April:** prayer and sacrifice against crop rust; Ovid describes a dog and sheep entrails offered to Robigo. The Quirinal flamen’s presence links Quirinus institutionally with crop protection but does not make the sacrifice one “to Quirinus.”
- **Consualia, 21 August:** participation in rites of Consus, connected with stored grain, horses, and the mythic Sabine abduction.
- **Larentalia, 23 December:** rites for Acca Larentia.
- possible supervision of rites associated with the curiae and the Quirinalia.

The range of duties supports a relationship with civic integration and agricultural reproduction. Their original theological rationale is not preserved.

### Salii Collini

A second Salian college, the **Salii Collini** or **Agonenses**, was associated with the Quirinal, in contrast with the Palatine Salii of Mars. Tradition attributed its establishment to Tullus Hostilius. The connection suggests parallel martial and civic ritual systems, but the detailed liturgy is lost.

### Quirinalia

The **Quirinalia** fell on **17 February** (*XIII Kalendas Martias*). It appears in the old festival cycle and in the *Fasti Antiates Maiores*.

Ovid identifies the day with the **Feriae Stultorum**, “festival of fools.” The *Fornacalia*—curial rites for roasting emmer—were celebrated on dates separately announced for each curia. Anyone who did not know their curia or missed its appointed day could perform the rite collectively on 17 February. The “fools” were therefore the forgetful or civically disoriented.

Whether this catch-up rite was Quirinus’s original festival is uncertain. W. Warde Fowler and later scholars caution that the calendar coincidence need not prove an original theological identity between Quirinus and the *curiae*. Dumézil likewise noted that Quirinus’s own ritual had become obscure enough for the concluding Fornacalia observance to dominate the date.

A second temple anniversary appears on **29 June**, probably connected with a later rededication rather than the original 293-BCE dedication. Calendrical evidence also records a Quirinus observance on **23 August**, near the Consualia, possibly accompanied by Hora Quirini.

### Portunalia and the weapons

Festus records that on the **Portunalia, 17 August**, the Flamen Portunalis anointed Quirinus’s arms with a preparation associated with parsley. The precise Latin is textually difficult. The rite documents material cult equipment and cooperation between priesthoods; explanations involving rust-prevention, peace, or storing weapons remain speculative.

### Sacrifice, divination, and human sacrifice

No source securely records:

- human sacrifice to Quirinus;
- temple prostitution;
- a Quirinal oracle;
- mystery initiation;
- a specialized magical or funerary cult;
- possession or ecstatic prophecy by his priests.

Animal sacrifice and incense belonged to Roman public worship generally, and Ovid explicitly tells worshippers to offer incense to the “new Quirinus.” The dog and sheep at the Robigalia were offered within a ceremony led by his flamen but addressed to Robigo.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before 293 BCE: reconstructed archaic phase

The rank of the Flamen Quirinalis, the Salian organization, the old festival, and the topography of the Quirinal point to an old civic cult. The dates assigned to Numa or Tullus Hostilius are legendary chronology.

Quirinus may have been:

- the god of an early Quirinal community;
- a Sabine or Sabellic deity;
- protector of the assembled citizen body;
- a peaceful counterpart of Mars;
- an agricultural/civic deity;
- or a divine founder already connected with Romulus.

The evidence cannot select one original essence.

### 293–second century BCE: monumental republican cult

Papirius’s temple made Quirinus a visible public recipient of military spoils. The dedication after Samnite warfare complicates any simple opposition between peaceful Quirinus and martial Mars.

By Ennius’s lifetime, Romulus’s heavenly immortality belonged to Roman national epic. The exact date when “Romulus” and “Quirinus” became unambiguously identical remains disputed.

### First century BCE: consolidation of Romulus-Quirinus

Cicero treats Romulus’s elevation as a prominent case of human divinization. The Memmian coin names and depicts Quirinus. Livy, Virgil, Varro, and the late Republican calendar place the god at the intersection of citizenship, foundation history, and antiquarian theology.

Caesar’s statue beside Quirinus exploited this symbolism. Whether it presented Caesar as a second founder, a companion of Romulus, or a god in his own right was politically explosive precisely because the categories overlapped.

### Augustan age

Augustus rebuilt the temple in 16 BCE. Virgil and Ovid produced the fullest surviving mythic constructions:

- Quirinus as deified Romulus;
- son of Mars;
- admitted by Jupiter;
- reconciled poetically with Remus;
- joined with Hersilia/Hora;
- model of founder apotheosis.

This is the period when Quirinus is most narratively visible, even though his independent cult personality was already difficult for writers to reconstruct.

### Imperial period

The temple and priesthood survived, and inscriptions continued to attest the cult and its personnel. Yet Quirinus did not develop a large empire-wide devotional following. His principal importance was Roman, civic, priestly, antiquarian, and ideological.

Imperial apotheosis made Romulus-Quirinus a useful precedent. He could legitimate elevation of rulers while also revealing its political construction.

### Late antiquity and suppression

Arnobius, Augustine, and other Christian apologists used Romulus-Quirinus to attack pagan divinization: if a violent or merely human king could become a god through civic enthusiasm, they argued, Roman theology was arbitrary. These writers are hostile witnesses, but they preserve Varro and evidence that the identification remained intelligible.

Christian emperors progressively restricted sacrifice and temple institutions during the fourth century. No surviving decree singles out Quirinus’s temple, and no documented scene records its violent closure. The public cult disappeared with the wider Roman sacrificial system. Claims of a secret continuous medieval cult are unsupported.

### Medieval to early modern period

Quirinus persisted in manuscripts, grammatical glosses, histories of Rome, and the name of the Quirinal hill. Christian saints named Quirinus are not continuations or baptizations of the god merely because they share the Latin personal name.

The Quirinal later became a papal and royal center of power. That is topographical and political continuity of the name, not continuous worship.

### Present

Modern Roman polytheists and reconstructionists sometimes honor Quirinus, including at the Quirinalia, but practice is reconstructed from Ovid, calendars, antiquarian fragments, and general Roman sacrificial forms. There is no demonstrated institutional continuity from antiquity.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship as reinvention

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century historians repeatedly supplied Quirinus with a clearer “original nature” than the sources permit:

- **Wissowa:** institutional and historically stratified Roman cult.
- **Mommsen and earlier comparativists:** close relation or original identity with Mars.
- **Dumézil:** third-function god of producers and the organized citizen mass.
- **Brelich:** Romulus and Quirinus as originally one figure, later separated through Roman “demythicization.”
- **Radke:** Quirinus as founder god from the beginning.
- **Porte and Magdelain:** civic and juridical protector of the Quirites.
- **Rosivach:** deity of the Sabine Quirinal community.
- **Lajoye:** possible thunder-god background.

These are historiographical constructions of varying evidentiary strength, not rediscovered dogma.

### Occultism and esotericism

No important Renaissance grimoire, Hermetic order, Golden Dawn ritual, or classical planetary-magic system gives Quirinus a stable independent office comparable to Mercury, Mars, Jupiter, or the Olympian gods. Modern correspondence tables that assign him planets, tarot cards, crystals, “energies,” or magical colors are **modern inventions** unless transparently presented as contemporary devotional choices.

### Reconstructionist religion

Late twentieth- and twenty-first-century *cultus deorum Romanorum* and Roman-polytheist practitioners have revived the Quirinalia and prayers to Quirinus. Surviving online testimony shows both worship and acknowledged difficulty finding historical ritual material. The scale of specific Quirinus worship is not measurable from reliable demographic data.

### Literature, games, and popular culture

The largest recent popular transformation is **Romulus=Quirinus** in the Japanese game and media franchise *Fate/Grand Order*. It turns the sparse Roman god into a cosmic, supreme embodiment of Roman expansion and civilization. That characterization is a **modern fictional invention**, though it consciously uses the ancient apotheosis equation.

The name “Quirinus” also appears in modern fiction as an erudite Romanizing name. Proposed connections between Quirinus and J. K. Rowling’s “Quirinus Quirrell” are plausible onomastic speculation but have not been securely documented by an authorial statement.

### National and political appropriation

Italian nationalism and Fascism extensively appropriated Romulus, Roman foundation, imperial expansion, and the symbols of *romanità*. Quirinus could be absorbed into that ideological field through his identity with Romulus, but evidence for a distinct state-sponsored “cult of Quirinus” under Fascism is lacking. It is more accurate to speak of appropriation of **Romulus and Roman antiquity generally** than to claim a major specifically Quirinal movement.

No well-documented modern extremist organization appears to have made Quirinus its central divine patron. Assertions of such a cult require case-specific evidence.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### The “archaic triad”

Jupiter, Mars, and Quirinus are linked by:

- their three major flamines;
- the hierarchy of *spolia opima*, in which ancient tradition assigned first-rank spoils to Jupiter Feretrius, second to Mars, and third to Quirinus;
- parallel Salian institutions;
- repeated antiquarian classification.

Calling them the **archaic triad** is modern shorthand. There is no surviving archaic inscription saying “these three constitute the supreme triad,” nor secure proof that their grouping was the direct predecessor of Jupiter–Juno–Minerva in every respect.

### Dumézil’s third function

Dumézil interpreted:

- Jupiter as juridical-sacral sovereignty;
- Mars as warrior force;
- Quirinus as fertility, production, wealth, and the organized civic multitude.

Supporting evidence includes the priestly hierarchy, Quirinus–Quirites association, and the agricultural ceremonies involving his flamen. Objections include:

- Quirinus’s martial spear associations;
- the temple’s military spoils;
- his founder and royal identity;
- uncertainty about whether the threefold institutions ever embodied a unified ideology;
- the danger of fitting fragmentary Roman data to an Indo-European template.

Dumézil’s scheme remains heuristically influential but is not consensus fact.

### Was Quirinus originally Romulus?

- **For early identity:** Romulus’s divine afterlife appears in Ennius; later Romans regarded the equation as established; the Quirinal temple could be interpreted as the founder’s shrine.
- **Against:** Quirinus possessed a major flamen and fixed festival whose structures appear older than the surviving Romulus apotheosis narrative; his priest performed rites unrelated to Romulus; the narrative equation is clearest only in late Republican and Augustan literature.
- **Intermediate model:** an old god was progressively reinterpreted as Romulus, while traits of both figures then reshaped the combined identity.

The intermediate model best accommodates the evidence but cannot date each stage precisely.

### Sabine versus Roman origin

Ancient writers repeatedly say “Sabine,” but the linguistic argument is unsettled and no independent Sabine dedication has been securely identified. The Sabine explanation may preserve historical memory, Roman ethnic ideology, or antiquarian inference from Cures and the Quirinal.

### Mars and Quirinus

The evidence permits several relationships:

- distinct gods with parallel priesthoods;
- martial god and peaceful/civic god;
- two phases or epithets of Mars;
- Roman and Sabine counterparts;
- youth-warrior force versus mature citizen community.

Servius’s neat “raging Mars / peaceful Quirinus” formulation is centuries later than the early cult and should not be projected backward without qualification.

### Iconographic uncertainty

Older handbooks sometimes state that no image of Quirinus exists. That is too strong: the labeled Memmian coin is a secure depiction, and literary descriptions and temple representations exist. What is absent is a securely preserved cult statue or a rich, unmistakable independent iconographic tradition.

### Primary evidence and losses

- The temple is not standing and its exact footprint remains debated.
- The cult statue is lost.
- Varro’s divine antiquities are lost.
- Ennius survives in fragments.
- Festus is damaged and epitomized.
- Calendar inscriptions are incomplete.
- Many Quirinal discoveries made during papal construction are known only through early-modern records or lost inscriptions.

The result is an unusually high dependence on late witnesses reconstructing earlier religion.

## 11. Sources

### Primary and material sources

The most important primary dossiers are Livy 1.16 and 10.46; Cicero, *De re publica* 2 and *De natura deorum* 2–3; Virgil, *Aeneid* 1 and 6; Ovid, *Fasti* 2 and 6 and *Metamorphoses* 14; Dionysius 2; Plutarch, *Romulus* 27–29; Festus/Paulus; Pliny; Cassius Dio; Macrobius; Servius; Augustine; Arnobius; the *Fasti Antiates Maiores*; CIL I² calendar material; CIL VI 475 and 565; Augustus, *Res Gestae* 19; and the Memmian denarius of 56 BCE.

### Modern critical scholarship

Essential modern starting points include:

- Georg Wissowa, *Religion und Kultus der Römer*.
- Kurt Latte, *Römische Religionsgeschichte*.
- Georges Dumézil, *La religion romaine archaïque* / *Archaic Roman Religion*.
- Mary Beard, John North, and Simon Price, *Religions of Rome*.
- John Scheid, studies of Roman ritual and priesthood.
- Dominique Briquel and Michel Humm on Roman and Sabine identity.
- Danielle Porte, “Romulus-Quirinus, prince et dieu, dieu des princes.”
- André Magdelain on Quirinus, citizenship, and law.
- Gary Forsythe, *A Critical History of Early Rome*.
- Adam Ziolkowski, *The Temples of Mid-Republican Rome*.
- *Lexicon Iconographicum Mythologiae Classicae* and *Brill’s New Pauly*, “Quirinus.”
- Goldberg and Manuwald’s modern edition of Ennius’s fragments; Skutsch’s *Annals of Q. Ennius*.
- Degrassi’s editions of the Roman calendars and the *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum* for the epigraphic record.

### Evidentiary conclusions

The evidence securely establishes a prestigious and old Roman public cult, a major priesthood, a fixed festival, a Quirinal sanctuary, and a monumental temple by 293 BCE. It does **not** securely establish Quirinus’s original ethnicity, etymology, or domain. The deified-Romulus identity is unmistakable by the late Republic and becomes canonical under Augustus, but its exact beginning is unknown. Agricultural, martial, civic, Sabine, juridical, and trifunctional interpretations each explain part of the record; none exhausts it.
