# The Lares: Research Dossier

**Class:** Collective Roman guardian divinities or place-spirits; individual manifestations may be called a *Lar*. Ancient authors variously classified them as *dei*, *dii familiares*, *genii*, *daemones*, *heroes*, spirits of the dead, or gods of particular bounded places. No surviving Roman source supplies a universally accepted ontology.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The oldest securely datable physical text naming them is the Arval Brethren’s inscribed record of 29 May 218 CE, which preserves the archaic invocation *enos Lases iuvate* (“help us, Lases/Lares”). Linguistic archaism suggests that the hymn itself was composed no later than the fourth century BCE, but its exact original date cannot be proved. The earliest surviving contemporary literary evidence is Plautus, especially the speaking *Lar Familiaris* in *Aulularia*, composed in the late third or early second century BCE. [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548547961); [Plautus, *Aulularia*, prologue](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0094%3Aact%3Dprologue%3Ascene%3D0)

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EARLY ATTESTED]** Archaic or Republican evidence, or a demonstrably archaic formula preserved later.
- **[IMPERIAL ATTESTED]** Literary, epigraphic, or archaeological evidence from the Roman imperial period.
- **[LATER SYSTEMATIZATION]** Ancient antiquarian, philosophical, or poetic explanation imposed upon older rites.
- **[REGIONAL VARIANT]** A locally documented form that should not automatically be generalized.
- **[SYNCRETISTIC IDENTIFICATION]** An equation made with another culture’s divine category.
- **[LATE ACCRETION]** Late-antique or post-classical development.
- **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** Scholarly inference rather than an ancient explanation.
- **[MODERN INVENTION/REVIVAL]** A modern ritual, doctrine, image, or identification without demonstrable ancient continuity.
- **[UNESTABLISHED]** Proposed but not demonstrable from the surviving evidence.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Basic identity

**[EARLY ATTESTED]** The Lares are best described as divine guardians attached to socially used places: cultivated land, a farm, house, crossroads, neighborhood, road, sea-route, army, or city. Their identity is relational rather than biographical. A Lar belongs to or protects a particular sphere; the evidence does not establish one primordial Lar later divided into specialized forms.

**[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** “Household gods” is accurate for the *Lar Familiaris* but misleading as a definition of the entire class. Harriet Flower’s synthesis emphasizes that Lares operated across domestic, neighborhood, civic, and imperial settings; their cult cannot be reconstructed by treating all Lares as ancestors or all shrines as private household altars. [Flower, *The Dancing Lares and the Serpent in the Garden*](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Dancing_Lares_and_the_Serpent_in_the.html?hl=en&id=H3GYDwAAQBAJ&output=html_text)

### Forms of the name

- **Classical Latin:** *Lar*, genitive *Laris*, plural *Lares*.
- **Archaic Latin:** *Lases*, in *enos Lases iuvate*. The intervocalic change *s* > *r*, known as rhotacism, explains *Lases* → *Lares*.
- **Greek renderings:** Greek-language authors ordinarily explain Lares through functional equivalents such as ἥρωες, “heroes,” or δαίμονες, “divine/spiritual powers.” These are interpretive translations, not proof that Lares and Greek heroes were historically identical.
- **House/shrine terms:** Latin could use *lar* metonymically for home; *lararium* is ancient but modern archaeological usage applies it broadly to shrines that may have contained the Genius, Penates, snakes, and other gods as well as Lares.

### Etymology

**[UNESTABLISHED]** No etymology is secure.

1. **Etruscan hypothesis.** Older scholarship connected *Lar* with Etruscan personal names or titles *Lars, Laris,* or *Larth*, conventionally glossed “lord.” The phonetic resemblance is real; a semantic and historical derivation has not been demonstrated. Nineteenth-century reference works often stated the derivation as fact, but modern treatments are more cautious. [Perseus, “Lares”](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0104%3Aentry%3Dlares-bio-1)

2. **Italic/Latin inheritance.** The archaic *Lases* and the absence of an identifiable Etruscan Lar-class permit an Italic origin, but do not prove it.

3. **Ancestor/hero derivation.** Some scholars have linked the word and category to a supposed fourth-century-BCE dedication to Aeneas as *Lare*. The reading and its implications remain disputed; even if correct, it establishes that one heroic founder could be styled *Lar*, not that all Lares originated as dead ancestors.

### Documented titles

- **Lar Familiaris / Lares Familiares:** guardian of a *familia*, house, farm, or household community. Singular usage is especially clear in Plautus and Cato; plural domestic pairs become visually dominant in the late Republic and Empire.
- **Lares Compitales / Compitalicii:** Lares of the *compita*, crossroads and neighborhood junctions; protectors of the local *vicus*.
- **Lares Augusti:** Augustan-era civic form administered by neighborhood officials. The adjective may mean “august/venerable” while also unmistakably associating the cult with Augustus and the imperial house.
- **Lares Praestites:** guardians “standing before,” “presiding,” or “present to help” the city. Ovid’s etymology is poetic and antiquarian, not independent linguistic proof.
- **Lares Publici:** public Lares, epigraphically distinct from private cult.
- **Lares Rurales / Agrestes:** rural or agricultural guardians.
- **Lares Viales:** guardians of roads and travelers.
- **Lares Permarini:** guardians “across the sea”; Livy records a temple vowed by L. Aemilius Regillus in the war against Antiochus and dedicated in 179 BCE by M. Aemilius Lepidus.
- **Lares Militares:** military Lares, chiefly imperial epigraphic evidence.
- **Lares Grundules/Grundiles:** obscure title connected by later tradition with the sow that produced thirty piglets at Lavinium. Both spelling and interpretation are disputed; it is unsafe to use the title as evidence for the general origin of the Lares.
- **Lares Hostilii, Urbani, Civitatum, Vicorum:** specialized or local titles encountered in literary, antiquarian, or epigraphic catalogues. Not all were parts of an official, coherent Roman taxonomy.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Arval hymn

**[EARLY CORE PRESERVED IMPERIALLY]** The *Carmen Arvale* survives in the *Acta Fratrum Arvalium* inscribed in 218 CE at the sanctuary of Dea Dia outside Rome:

> *enos Lases iuvate*  
> “Help us, Lases.”

The formula invokes the Lares three times, followed by Semones and Mars. In its recorded ritual context, the hymn protects fields and their produce so that Dea Dia may exercise her function. The inscription proves use in 218 CE; archaic morphology and vocabulary support a much earlier composition, conventionally fourth century BCE or earlier, but do not date its first performance. [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548547961)

### Plautus

**[EARLY ATTESTED]** In *Aulularia*—probably staged around the turn of the second century BCE—the prologue is spoken by the household’s *Lar Familiaris*. He says that he has possessed and inhabited the house for the present owner, his father, and grandfather. A grandfather entrusted buried gold to him. Because the present daughter offers incense, wine, and garlands daily, the Lar arranges discovery of the treasure and the marriage plot.

This is unusually rich evidence. The Lar:

- persists across generations without being explicitly identified as any one ancestor;
- guards house, hearth, family secret, and property;
- responds reciprocally to neglect and offerings;
- can influence human events;
- favors a pious daughter rather than merely the male lineage.

The theatrical character may adapt a Greek comic model, so it cannot simply be read as a ritual manual. It nevertheless presupposes that a Roman audience recognized the domestic Lar. [Plautus, *Aulularia*, prologue](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0094%3Aact%3Dprologue%3Ascene%3D0)

### Cato

**[EARLY ATTESTED]** Cato’s *De agricultura*, composed around 160 BCE, gives operational evidence rather than mythology. On arriving at an estate the owner should greet the *Lar Familiaris*. At the Kalends, Nones, Ides, and festivals, the farm manager’s wife must clean and garland the hearth and offer to the household Lar according to available means. The same work restricts religious action by estate staff to the master’s authorization, revealing household hierarchy alongside participation by dependents and enslaved people. [Cato, *De agricultura* 143](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Cato/De_Agricultura/J%2A.html)

### Late Republican evidence

**[EARLY ATTESTED]**

- Cicero distinguishes household religious institutions—hearths, altars, Penates, and familial Lares—as legally and emotionally constitutive of the home.
- Cicero’s *De natura deorum* 2.62 includes Lares among divine beings whose received cult must be respected, while philosophical speakers debate what kinds of beings count as gods.
- Livy 8.9 preserves the *devotio* prayer attributed to Decius Mus in 340 BCE, invoking Mars, Quirinus, Bellona, Lares, Novensiles, Indigetes, and others. The narrative was written centuries after the alleged event, but the formula treats Lares as public powers of battle and community, not merely household spirits. [Livian prayer text](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/livius/livy7.pdf)

### Augustan and early imperial corpus

**[IMPERIAL ATTESTED]**

- **Virgil, *Aeneid*:** uses *Lares* in the broad language of home, settlement, and transferred domestic religion, although translations frequently blur Lares and Penates. The epic supplies no sustained Lares myth.
- **Ovid, *Fasti* 2.571–616:** supplies the only full divine genealogy, making the twin Lares sons of Mercury and the silenced nymph Lara.
- **Ovid, *Fasti* 5.129–146:** explains the Lares Praestites, their dog, paired statues, and Augustus’s neighborhood triad.
- **Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities* 4.14:** attributes Compitalia and the organization of crossroads shrines to Servius Tullius; households contribute honey-cakes and enslaved people conduct service because the Lares supposedly delight in it.
- **Augustan calendars and neighborhood altars:** materially document the calendar, *vici*, and *magistri* more securely than retrospective royal foundation legends.
- **Suetonius, *Augustus* 30:** says Augustus revived Compitalia and ordered the crossroads Lares crowned twice yearly with spring and summer flowers. [Suetonius, *Augustus*](https://files.romanroadsstatic.com/materials/romans/historians/Seutonius_Life_Augustus_1-0.pdf)

### Later imperial and late-antique witnesses

**[LATER SYSTEMATIZATION]**

- **Festus, epitome of Verrius Flaccus:** woolen balls and male/female effigies were hung at crossroads, one set corresponding to enslaved and free members of the household, so the infernal gods “whom they call Lares” would spare the living. The surviving text is epitomized and fragmentary.
- **Plutarch, *Roman Questions* 51:** reports that Lares Praestites wore dog skins and had a dog beside them; he offers competing interpretations—watchfulness or punitive, dog-like pursuit.
- **Apuleius, *De deo Socratis* 15:** systematizes human souls after death: a benevolent soul entrusted with descendants becomes a *Lar Familiaris*; a restless malign soul becomes a *Larva*; collectively the dead are *Manes*. This philosophical taxonomy is second-century CE, not evidence of a uniform archaic belief.
- **Arnobius, *Adversus nationes* 3.41:** a hostile Christian witness, reporting Varro’s alternatives: Lares as children of Mania, “gods of the air,” or spirits of the dead.
- **Servius, commentary on Virgil:** associates household Lares with ancient burial inside houses. This is late-antique antiquarian inference, not archaeological proof of their origin.
- **Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 1.7.34–35:** narrates an alleged change from child sacrifice to heads of garlic and poppy and woolen effigies at Compitalia. The work dates around 430 CE and describes supposed regal practice nearly a millennium earlier. [Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 1](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/l/roman/texts/Macrobius/Saturnalia/1%2A.html)

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains and powers

**[ATTESTED ACROSS PERIODS]** Lares guard bounded human environments and the communities acting within them. Their domains include:

- household continuity, property, food, marriage, and family transitions;
- farm, hearth, cultivated territory, and harvest;
- crossroads, neighborhood identity, and local communal order;
- roads, voyages, armies, city walls, and the Roman state;
- reciprocal prosperity: offerings elicit protection, while neglect may withdraw favor.

They are rarely portrayed as cosmic sovereigns. Their power is local, distributive, and relational. No ancient source assigns them a planet, fixed color, canonical number, or universal sacred plant.

### Domestic and Pompeian iconography

**[IMPERIAL ATTESTED]** Pompeii and Herculaneum, sealed in 79 CE, preserve the largest archaeological sample. Modern archaeologists call these shrines *lararia*, though the ancient structures vary: painted wall panels, niches, shelves, miniature temples, masonry altars, or rooms with multiple cult objects.

A common Pompeian composition contains:

- a central togate Genius holding a cornucopia and pouring a libation;
- two youthful Lares in short belted tunics;
- each Lar stepping or “dancing,” with one arm raised;
- rhyta or drinking horns and *situlae* or buckets;
- one or two large serpents approaching an altar;
- garlands, sacrificial attendants, musicians, pigs, or food;
- optional gods relevant to a household or business, such as Mercury or Bacchus.

The “dancing” pose may represent ritual movement, celebratory service, or wine-pouring. It is not evidence that dance was their original defining function. The imagery became standardized in the late Republican/early imperial period and should not be projected unchanged into archaic Rome. [Hanging Garlands of Pompeii](https://www.mdpi.com/2076-0752/9/2/65)

**[IMPERIAL ATTESTED]** Bronze statuettes show the same youthful male type: curled hair, short tunic, boots, tiptoe stance, *patera* or bowl, and raised rhyton. A Metropolitan Museum example is classified broadly as Roman imperial; many unprovenanced bronzes cannot be connected to a specific household ritual context. [Metropolitan Museum, bronze dancing Lar](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/246311)

### The serpent

**[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** Pompeian serpents have been read as the Genius of the place, fertility, chthonic power, ancestors, or protective beings. Their frequent visual association with Lares does not prove identity with them. Flower interprets the recurring garden serpent as a central expression of localized divine presence; competing scholarship stresses fertility or the Genius loci.

### Lares Praestites

**[LATER CANONICAL POETIC ACCOUNT]** Ovid says paired images with a dog formerly stood by their altar. He derives *Praestites* from their standing before and protecting the city, and compares their vigilance to dogs guarding a home. He could no longer see the images because they had decayed. His description is testimony to Augustan memory of a lost monument, not direct observation of an archaic statue. [Ovid, *Fasti* 5](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidFasti5.html)

### Sacred animals, offerings, and calendar

- **Dog:** securely associated with the Lares Praestites in Ovid and Plutarch; not universal to all Lares.
- **Pig:** frequent sacrificial animal in domestic and Compital contexts.
- **Wine, incense, food, garlands:** literary and visual evidence.
- **Honey-cakes:** Dionysius’s Compitalia account.
- **Kalends, Nones, Ides:** Cato’s domestic schedule.
- **Compitalia:** movable winter festival, announced by magistrates; not assigned a single permanently fixed Republican date.
- **1 May:** calendars and Ovid associate the day with the Lares Praestites.
- **Twice-yearly crowning:** Suetonius’s account of Augustus’s arrangement.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Competing origin schemes

1. **No genealogy.** **[EARLY ATTESTED]** The Arval hymn, Plautus, and Cato give Lares functions but no parents. This is the earliest evidentiary stratum.

2. **Children of Lara and Mercury.** **[AUGUSTAN POETIC ELABORATION]** In Ovid’s *Fasti*, Jupiter desires Juturna. The talkative nymph Lara reveals his plan to Juturna and Juno. Jupiter tears out Lara’s tongue and orders Mercury to conduct her to the underworld. Mercury rapes or coerces the mute captive en route; she bears twins who guard crossroads and the city—the Lares. Ovid also invents or reports the speaking-name *Lala*, from Greek λαλεῖν, “to talk.” This narrative explains Tacita/Muta rites and Lares together; no earlier surviving source gives it. [Ovid, *Fasti* 2](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidFasti2.html)

3. **Children of Mania/Mater Larum.** **[LATER SYSTEMATIZATION]** Varro as reported by Arnobius, Festus, and Macrobius connects the Lares with Mania or *Mater Larum*. Whether Mania was an old independently worshipped goddess or an antiquarian personification of Compital effigies (*maniae*) is disputed.

4. **Deified human souls.** **[IMPERIAL PHILOSOPHICAL SYSTEMATIZATION]** Apuleius makes a good ancestral soul a Lar; Arnobius reports a related classification; Servius derives domestic cult from burials in houses.

5. **Place-guardians later ancestralized.** **[MODERN RECONSTRUCTION]** Wissowa and successors stressed land, boundaries, and inhabited place. The Arval hymn and Compital cult support territorial guardianship, while Plautus’s multi-generational Lar need not be a dead family member.

### The ancestors debate

**Ancestor interpretation:** Apuleius explicitly defines a virtuous dead guardian of descendants as a Lar Familiaris; Festus calls Lares infernal gods; Dionysius renders a domestic Lar as a household *hero*; late authors connect them with Manes and burial. Household continuity across generations strengthens this reading.

**Place-guardian interpretation:** the earliest ritual invocation concerns cultivated land; Compitales belong to fixed crossroads and communities rather than bloodlines; Lares Viales, Permarini, Militares, and Publici cannot naturally be reduced to family ancestors; Plautus’s Lar says he has served three generations but never says he is one of them.

**Finding:** **[UNRESOLVED]** Some Romans certainly understood some Lares as beneficial dead. The surviving evidence does not establish that all Lares were originally ancestors. “Deified ancestors” should therefore be reported as one ancient and modern theory, not the definition.

### Every documented divine interaction or joint episode

- **Mars —** Jointly invoked with the *Lases* and Semones to avert ruin from the fields. *Carmen Arvale*, archaic composition preserved in the acta of 218 CE. **[EARLY CORE PRESERVED IMPERIALLY]**
- **Semones —** Joint invocation in the Arval hymn. Their precise class and relationship to the Lares remain uncertain. **[EARLY CORE PRESERVED IMPERIALLY]**
- **Dea Dia —** The Lares are invoked during her agricultural rites so her domain may remain intact. Arval Acts, 218 CE. **[IMPERIAL RITUAL ATTESTATION]**
- **Mercury —** Fathers the twin Lares by Lara while conducting her to the underworld. Ovid, *Fasti* 2.607–616, circa 8 CE. **[AUGUSTAN POETIC ELABORATION]**
- **Lara/Larunda/Tacita/Muta —** Mother of the twins in Ovid; her identity with other similarly named figures is an antiquarian/modern harmonization rather than a securely early unity. **[AUGUSTAN POETIC ELABORATION]**
- **Jupiter —** His punishment of Lara initiates the sequence culminating in the Lares’ birth. Ovid, *Fasti* 2. **[AUGUSTAN POETIC ELABORATION]**
- **Juturna —** Lara protects her by disclosing Jupiter’s intended assault; she does not meet the resulting twins in the surviving text. Ovid, *Fasti* 2. **[AUGUSTAN POETIC ELABORATION]**
- **Juno —** Receives Lara’s warning; no direct meeting with the Lares occurs. Ovid, *Fasti* 2. **[AUGUSTAN POETIC ELABORATION]**
- **Mania/Mater Larum —** Called their mother in antiquarian traditions reported by Varro/Festus/Macrobius. **[LATER SYSTEMATIZATION]**
- **Genius Augusti —** Joined with the two neighborhood Lares in the Augustan three-figure cult described by Ovid and represented on compital altars. **[AUGUSTAN CULTIC ASSOCIATION]**
- **The household Genius —** Frequently shown between paired Lares in Pompeian painting. This is a joint cult image, not a narrative interaction. **[IMPERIAL ICONOGRAPHIC ASSOCIATION]**
- **Penates —** Repeatedly joined with Lares in Roman descriptions of household religion, but remain conceptually distinguishable. No surviving myth narrates a personal encounter. **[ATTESTED CULTIC ASSOCIATION]**
- **Vesta —** Shares the domestic hearth setting and later theological catalogues with Lares and Penates; no discrete narrative encounter survives. **[ATTESTED CULTIC ASSOCIATION]**
- **Diana —** Ovid compares her hunting pack with the vigilant dog and Lar; this is poetic analogy, not an episode. *Fasti* 5. **[POETIC ASSOCIATION]**
- **Romulus and Remus —** Paired Lares Praestites were later compared or identified with Rome’s founding twins, but no early source securely equates them. **[MODERN/ANTIQUARIAN RECONSTRUCTION]**
- **Dioscuri —** Their paired youthful iconography may have influenced imperial images of paired Lares. No ancient narrative makes them interact. **[MODERN ICONOGRAPHIC RECONSTRUCTION]**
- **Hercules —** Macrobius places Hercules in a broader Italian story about replacing human heads with effigies, relevant to later explanations of Compital practice; he does not provide a clear personal meeting with the Lares. **[LATE ANTIQUE ACCRETION]**
- **Augustus:** Though deified after death, Augustus’s lifetime Genius—not “the god Augustus” in a straightforward sense—was installed with neighborhood Lares. **[AUGUSTAN POLITICAL-CULTIC ASSOCIATION]**

No securely attested combat, marriage, contest, theft, or rescue involving the Lares survives outside Ovid’s genealogy. Their evidence is overwhelmingly ritual and locative rather than mythic.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Lara’s punishment and the birth of the Lares

**[AUGUSTAN POETIC ELABORATION]** Ovid’s version is the fullest surviving Lares narrative. Jupiter attempts to have Juturna detained. Lara exposes the scheme to both Juturna and Juno. Jupiter removes Lara’s power of speech and sends her to the dead with Mercury. Mercury sexually assaults her; the resulting twins become guardians of crossroads and the city.

The account links:

- silence and the ritual of Tacita;
- boundaries between upper world and underworld;
- Mercury as guide of souls;
- twins and crossroads;
- political guardianship of Rome.

There is no evidence that this was a universally believed Roman “creation myth.” It is an Augustan literary aetiology combining names, rites, and divine characters. Later summaries often sanitize Mercury’s act as “love” or “union”; Ovid’s captive, mutilated, voiceless Lara cannot consent.

### Servius Tullius and the Compitalia

**[LATE REPUBLICAN/EARLY IMPERIAL FOUNDATION LEGEND]** Dionysius attributes the division of Rome into neighborhoods and the institution of Compital shrines to King Servius Tullius. Households contributed cakes; enslaved people ministered because the Lares favored their service.

Related traditions make Servius himself the child of a divine phallus appearing at the royal hearth—identified variously as a Lar, Vulcan, or a royal tutelary power—and the enslaved woman Ocresia. These variants associate Lares, hearth, servile status, fertility, and neighborhood incorporation. They cannot securely document sixth-century-BCE institutions; they reveal how late Republican and Augustan writers explained those institutions.

### The Lares Praestites and the dog

**[AUGUSTAN ANTIQUARIAN AETIOLOGY]** Ovid says an old altar and small paired images were established on 1 May. A stone dog stood at their feet because both dog and Lar guard the house, frequent crossroads, pursue thieves, and stay awake. Ovid looked for the statues but found that time had destroyed them. Plutarch later reports dog skins and alternative explanations.

### Mania and substituted sacrifice

**[LATE ANTIQUE ACCRETION WITH EARLIER RITUAL ELEMENTS]** Festus preserves the credible ritual datum that woolen balls and human-shaped effigies were hung at the Compitalia. Macrobius supplies a sensational origin: Tarquinius Superbus, following an oracle demanding “heads for heads,” sacrificed children to Mania; Brutus substituted garlic and poppy heads.

No Republican archaeological or contemporary textual evidence confirms child sacrifice to the Lares. Macrobius wrote around 430 CE. The correct documentary conclusion is:

- woolen apotropaic effigies are independently attested;
- a late source interprets them as replacements for children;
- actual child sacrifice to the Lares is **not established**.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Domestic cult

**[EARLY AND IMPERIAL ATTESTED]** The Lar’s shrine was integrated into household space, but not invariably beside one standardized hearth. Pompeian examples occur in kitchens, atria, peristyles, gardens, shops, taverns, and service zones. The word *lararium* should not imply that only Lares were worshipped there.

Practices included:

- greeting the Lar on arrival;
- cleaning and garlanding hearth or shrine;
- incense, wine, food, cakes, and first portions;
- libations;
- opening or decorating the shrine for family transitions;
- offerings connected with coming of age, marriage, departure, return, and household meals;
- deployment of portable bronze or terracotta statuettes.

Participation was not confined to the *paterfamilias*. Literary prescriptions express elite male authority, while inscriptions, service spaces, and Compital institutions show women, freedpersons, and enslaved people as active ritual agents.

### Compitalia

**[REPUBLICAN AND IMPERIAL ATTESTED]** At each *compitum*, the Lares Compitales protected the adjacent neighborhood. The annual festival was *conceptiva*—its date proclaimed rather than immovably fixed. Dionysius reports household cakes and service by enslaved people. Festus reports woolen balls for enslaved persons and anthropomorphic effigies for free inhabitants.

The shrines were also social and political centers. Their associations could mobilize urban lower-status populations. Senate action against collegia in the late Republic and Clodius’s revival of neighborhood organizations show that suppression or regulation had political as well as theological motives. [“A Note on the Compitalia”](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Journals/CP/27/3/Compitalia%2A.html)

### Augustus’s reform

**[SECURELY ATTESTED AUGUSTAN TRANSFORMATION]** In 7 BCE Augustus divided Rome into fourteen regions and more than 260 *vici*. Four annual *magistri vici*, often freedmen, and subordinate *ministri*, often enslaved, managed local rites and firefighting. Lares Compitales were widely restyled Lares Augusti; the Genius Augusti appeared between them.

This was neither a simple abolition of traditional cult nor merely spontaneous emperor worship. It:

- restored or regularized neighborhood ritual;
- tied local organization to the new regime;
- offered highly visible office to freedmen;
- associated Augustus’s protective capacity with pre-existing guardians;
- preserved considerable local agency in commissions, imagery, and self-representation.

Surviving altars name *magistri*, date their tenure, and combine sacrifice, imperial symbols, neighborhood identity, and the paired Lares. A modern study rightly warns that these monuments should not be reduced to elite “consensus”: freedmen used them to display their own office, status, and benefaction. [Bueno, “Undoing the consensus”](https://revistas.usp.br/marenostrum/en/article/view/179122)

### Public shrines and temples

- **Sacellum Larum:** ancient shrine on or near Rome’s early sacred boundary; its exact topography and relationship to the Lares Praestites sanctuary remain disputed.
- **Lares Praestites altar:** associated with 1 May; Ovid says its statues had vanished by his day.
- **Temple of the Lares Permarini:** vowed in the war against Antiochus, dedicated in 179 BCE near the Porticus Minucia.
- **Crossroads shrines:** hundreds existed in Augustan Rome; archaeology and inscriptions preserve only a fraction.
- **Ostia and Italian cities:** *sacella* and inscriptions show adaptation of Lares Augusti worship outside Rome.
- **Provinces:** dedications occur across the western empire, sometimes attaching indigenous place-guardians to Latin Lar terminology. Such cases are regional translations, not evidence of one uniform cult.

### Pompeii

**[ARCHAEOLOGICALLY SECURE TO 79 CE]** Pompeii preserves several hundred structures classified as domestic or commercial shrines. Famous examples include the House of the Vettii and the tavern of L. Vetutius Placidus. Their paintings commonly show paired Lares, Genius, snakes, altar, and offerings, but considerable variation demonstrates household choice. A shrine could accumulate multiple gods or repeated images; it was not a fixed canonical tableau.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Archaic formation: before the fourth century BCE

**[RECONSTRUCTED FROM LATER EVIDENCE]** The archaic *Lases* were invoked with Mars and Semones in an agricultural rite. Their precise ontology and original image are unknown. Later claims that they were specifically Etruscan, exclusively rural, or exclusively ancestral exceed the evidence.

### Fourth–second centuries BCE

**[EARLY ATTESTED]** Lares emerge as guardians of cultivated territory, household, and family continuity. Plautus and Cato show the *Lar Familiaris* embedded in everyday reciprocal ritual. Public specialization also existed: Livy’s traditions of battle invocation and the temple of the Lares Permarini point beyond the house.

### First century BCE

**[REPUBLICAN TRANSFORMATION]** Crossroads cults became politically important through neighborhood associations, games, and popular participation. Periodic restrictions on collegia affected the Compitalia. Domestic imagery was increasingly Hellenized: youthful paired figures and serpent imagery developed into forms familiar from Pompeii.

### Augustus to the Julio-Claudians

**[IMPERIAL REORGANIZATION]** Augustus revived and reorganized the crossroads cult, coupled Lares Augusti with his Genius, and integrated *vici* into the principate’s administration. Ovid supplied literary aetiologies for the Lares’ genealogy and civic forms. Paired dancing Lares became the dominant surviving visual type.

### First–third centuries CE

**[IMPERIAL DIFFUSION]** Household, military, roadside, maritime, civic, and imperial Lares appear across Italy and the provinces. Pompeian material records extraordinary diversity up to 79 CE. The Arval inscription of 218 CE shows continued performance of an archaic formula even when its language was difficult for contemporary Romans.

### Third–early fourth centuries

**[TRANSFORMATION]** Household worship remained sufficiently visible for Christian writers such as Tertullian, Arnobius, and Lactantius to attack Lares as demons, dead humans falsely worshipped, or manufactured idols. These writers preserve valuable quotations and classifications but are hostile witnesses, selecting material for polemical effect.

### 392 CE and legal suppression

**[DOCUMENTED SUPPRESSION]** Theodosius I’s constitution of 8 November 392, preserved as *Codex Theodosianus* 16.10.12, prohibited private offerings as well as public sacrifice: no one was to honor the Genius with wine, the Lares with fire, or the Penates with incense, light lamps, burn incense, or hang garlands. This is direct legal evidence that domestic practices associated with the Lares were targeted. Enforcement was uneven; repeated laws demonstrate both coercion and persistence. [Translation of *Codex Theodosianus* 16.10.12](https://www.cuhk.edu.hk/crs/tobias/primary_docs_ch05.html)

### Fifth century and after

**[LATE ANTIQUE DECLINE]** Macrobius, Servius, and Christian polemicists preserved learned accounts as active public cult disappeared. There is no evidence for an unbroken, institutionally continuous Lares priesthood into the medieval period.

**[FOLKLORE—UNESTABLISHED CONTINUITY]** European household spirits, fairies, ancestral customs, hearth beings, and saints sometimes perform similar functions. Functional similarity does not demonstrate direct survival from the Lares without an evidentiary chain.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Scholarship, museums, and domesticity

**[MODERN RECEPTION]** Renaissance and early-modern antiquarians recovered Lares chiefly through Latin literature and excavated bronzes. Eighteenth- and nineteenth-century writers made them exemplary “household gods,” frequently treating them as ancestors on the model of Fustel de Coulanges’s patriarchal household religion. Excavation of Pompeii gave the paired dancing youths and serpent tableau enormous influence over the modern image.

The English word “lares and penates” became a literary expression for treasured possessions, domestic gods, or the home itself. This reception often erases distinctions among Lares, Penates, Genius, and Manes.

### Occultism

**[MODERN REINTERPRETATION]** The Lares have never held the central position in Renaissance Hermetism, ceremonial magic, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or standard grimoire traditions that planetary gods, angels, and demons do. Modern occult manuals sometimes recast them as “familiar spirits,” “ancestor spirits,” or household wards. Those are modern syntheses drawing on ancient vocabulary, folklore, and contemporary spirit-work; no continuous ancient initiatory Lares tradition is documented.

### Reconstructionist worship

**[MODERN REVIVAL]** Contemporary *Cultus Deorum Romanorum* practitioners maintain household *lararia*, offer incense, wine, food, and garlands, and celebrate Kalends, Nones, Ides, or Compitalia. Nova Roma publishes daily and monthly lararium rites and constitutionally identifies reconstructed Roman religion as its official religion. These practices consciously reconstruct ancient evidence and adapt it to modern homes; they do not establish continuous transmission from antiquity. [Nova Roma rites](https://www.novaroma.org/religio_romana/rites_and_rituals.html)

Modern prayers bearing phrases such as “Lares Familiares, guardians of my family” are often newly composed Latin. Unless a text cites an ancient source, grammatical Latin does not make it ancient.

### Popular culture and commerce

**[MODERN RECEPTION]** Museums, replica vendors, games, novels, and online pagan communities favor the Pompeian dancing pair. Commercial “Lar” statuettes often reproduce imperial bronzes without provenance or supply generic paired figures marketed as ancestor gods. Modern fantasy may treat “a Lar” as an individualized ghost or magical familiar; that characterization draws selectively on Apuleius and later folklore rather than representing the full Roman evidence.

### Political appropriation

**[MODERN REINVENTION]** Italian Roman-traditionalist movements sometimes present domestic and civic cult as an unbroken national spirituality. Fascist Romanità promoted Roman symbols broadly, but a distinctive mass state revival of Lares worship is not documented. Claims of secret, continuous family cult from antiquity require independent evidence. No significant modern extremist monopoly on the Lares comparable to appropriations of some Germanic symbols is established.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Major approaches

- **Georg Wissowa, *Religion und Kultus der Römer*:** classified cults institutionally and emphasized territorial/agricultural origins. His orderly categories remain influential but can make fluid local practice look like a formal theological system. [Digital edition](https://repositum.tuwien.at/handle/20.500.12708/230656)
- **Georges Dumézil, *La religion romaine archaïque* / *Archaic Roman Religion*:** interpreted Roman religion through inherited Indo-European structures. His attention to ritual systems was transformative, but many specific comparative reconstructions are contested. [Google Books record](https://books.google.com/books/about/Archaic_Roman_Religion.html?id=_aoicpjjhyMC)
- **Kurt Latte, *Römische Religionsgeschichte*:** emphasized historical development and Greek/Italic influence.
- **Mary Beard, John North, Simon Price, *Religions of Rome*:** shifted attention from timeless belief to institutions, practice, political change, and the conditions under which evidence was produced.
- **John Scheid:** stresses ritual precision, civic context, and the danger of translating Roman religion into individualized “faith.” His account notes singular domestic *Lar* before the common era and increasing plural usage under influence of public imperial forms. [Scheid, *An Introduction to Roman Religion*](https://pdfcoffee.com/john-scheid-an-introduction-to-roman-religion-edinburgh-university-press-2003-compressed-pdf-free.html)
- **Harriet Flower:** rejects the assumption that Lares were essentially ancestral ghosts and reconstructs them as localized gods central to neighborhood, household, garden, and imperial practice.

### Central controversies

1. **Ancestors or place-spirits?** The evidence supports both interpretations in different periods; no single origin is demonstrable.
2. **Etruscan origin?** Possible, not proved. Etruscan mirrors and funerary images offer comparanda, not an identified Etruscan cult identical to Roman Lares.
3. **Singular or pair?** Early sources favor a singular domestic Lar; imperial iconography favors pairs. The shift may reflect Compital/Augustan standardization and Greek twin imagery.
4. **Augustan continuity or invention?** Augustus restored a recognizable crossroads cult while transforming administration, title, imagery, and political meaning.
5. **“Imperial cult”?** Lares Augusti participated in ruler-centered religion but were not simply Augustus renamed. The Genius Augusti was added to pre-existing neighborhood guardians.
6. **Human sacrifice?** Only late antiquarian narrative alleges child victims. Effigies are attested; historical child sacrifice is not.
7. **What is a lararium?** Modern archaeological classification sometimes labels any domestic shrine a lararium even when no Lar appears. The term can conceal cultic diversity.
8. **Pompeii as norm?** Pompeii is exceptionally preserved, not statistically representative of the whole empire.

### Evidence and losses

The evidence is structurally uneven:

- no securely identified archaic cult image survives;
- Republican domestic installations are poorly preserved;
- Varro’s major theological works survive largely in quotation;
- Festus survives in an epitome and damaged manuscript;
- Pompeii overrepresents affluent Campanian houses of the first century CE;
- portable bronzes frequently lack excavation context;
- inscriptions are dispersed through the *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum*, regional corpora, museum stores, and databases.

The Arval Acts’ discovery in 1778 established the archaic form *Lases*. Pompeian excavation supplied a material record that overturned reliance on elite texts. Recent scholarship on neighborhood altars and lived religion has further shifted attention toward enslaved people, freedmen, women, commerce, and local agency.

## 10. Chronology

- **Before fourth century BCE — [RECONSTRUCTED]:** probable composition of the archaic Arval invocation *enos Lases iuvate*.
- **Traditional sixth century BCE — [FOUNDATION LEGEND]:** Servius Tullius institutes or organizes Compitalia and neighborhood shrines.
- **509 BCE — [LATE LEGEND]:** Brutus supposedly substitutes garlic, poppies, and effigies for children sacrificed to Mania.
- **Fourth century BCE — [DISPUTED]:** possible dedication styling Aeneas *Lar*; reading and implications contested.
- **340 BCE — [LATER RECORDED FORMULA]:** Livy places Lares in Decius Mus’s *devotio* prayer.
- **Early second century BCE — [SECURE LITERARY ATTESTATION]:** Plautus’s *Aulularia* gives the Lar Familiaris a speaking role.
- **190s–179 BCE — [HISTORICAL CULT]:** temple of the Lares Permarini vowed and then dedicated.
- **Circa 160 BCE — [SECURE PRACTICAL TEXT]:** Cato prescribes regular worship of the household Lar.
- **First century BCE — [DOCUMENTED POLITICAL DEVELOPMENT]:** Compital associations become entangled in urban popular politics and regulation.
- **44–43 BCE — [LITERARY/PHILOSOPHICAL]:** Cicero discusses the religious standing of Lares and the sanctity of household cult.
- **12–7 BCE — [AUGUSTAN TRANSFORMATION]:** neighborhood administration and Compital cult reorganized; Lares Augusti and Genius Augusti become prominent.
- **Circa 8 CE — [POETIC SYSTEMATIZATION]:** Ovid publishes the *Fasti* accounts of Lara’s twins and the Lares Praestites.
- **Early second century CE — [PHILOSOPHICAL SYSTEMATIZATION]:** Apuleius defines the benevolent dead guardian as a Lar Familiaris.
- **79 CE — [ARCHAEOLOGICAL HORIZON]:** Vesuvius preserves Pompeian lararia and cult assemblages.
- **218 CE — [SECURE INSCRIPTION]:** Arval Acts inscribe the archaic hymn invoking the *Lases*.
- **Late third–early fourth century — [CHRISTIAN POLEMIC]:** Arnobius preserves Varro’s competing classifications while attacking pagan cult.
- **392 CE — [LEGAL SUPPRESSION]:** *Codex Theodosianus* 16.10.12 specifically prohibits private offerings to Genius, Lares, and Penates.
- **Circa 400–430 CE — [LATE ANTIQUE ANTIQUARIANISM]:** Servius and Macrobius record burial, Mania, effigy, and alleged human-sacrifice explanations.
- **1778 — [REDISCOVERY]:** the inscribed Arval Acts, including *enos Lases iuvate*, are found in Rome.
- **Nineteenth century — [HISTORIOGRAPHIC SYSTEMATIZATION]:** ancestor-cult and Etruscan-origin theories become standard in European reference works.
- **1890s–1912 — [INSTITUTIONAL HISTORY]:** Wissowa’s framework shapes twentieth-century study.
- **1966/1970 — [COMPARATIVE RECONSTRUCTION]:** Dumézil publishes and translates his major synthesis of archaic Roman religion.
- **1998 — [HISTORIOGRAPHIC TURN]:** Beard, North, and Price consolidate practice-centered and historically contingent approaches.
- **1998 onward — [MODERN REVIVAL]:** Nova Roma and other reconstructionists publish adapted household rites including worship of Lares.
- **2017 — [MAJOR REASSESSMENT]:** Flower’s *The Dancing Lares and the Serpent in the Garden* offers the first book-length synthesis centered on the Lares themselves.
- **Present — [MODERN REVIVAL/RECEPTION]:** Lares receive reconstructed worship, museum display, commercial reproduction, and popular reinterpretation; no continuous institutional lineage from antiquity is established.
