# The Manes: Research Dossier

**Class:** collective divine dead; an open, plural class of deceased persons conceived as gods or empowered ancestral spirits, and sometimes more broadly as the infernal gods or the realm of the dead. The Latin normally has no singular corresponding to this collective sense.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the oldest extant direct use in a securely transmitted contemporary text is Lucretius, *De rerum natura* 1.133–135 and 3.51–52, composed approximately 60–55 BCE; he mentions sacrifices to the *divi Manes* and fear of the Manes while arguing against survival after death. Cicero’s *De legibus* 2.22, probably written in the later 50s BCE, independently prescribes, *Deorum Manium iura sancta sunto*—“Let the rights of the divine Manes be sacred.” Earlier evidence is indirect: Ennius’s lost *Annales* reportedly narrated Decius Mus’s self-devotion to the Manes, but the poem survives only in later quotations; Plautus attests ghosts and dangerous dead without securely supplying the developed collective title. The *Fasti Antiates Maiores* of approximately 84–55 BCE physically attest the Feralia and Lemuria in the Republican calendar, though not necessarily the word *Manes* itself.

**Central evidentiary conclusion:** “the Manes” were not a single anthropomorphic god, a fixed species of benevolent ancestor, or a Roman equivalent of one Greek being. The evidence moves among at least four overlapping senses: the dead collectively as divine recipients of cult; the divinized identity of an individual deceased person; infernal powers invoked with Earth, Dis Pater, and related gods; and, especially in poetry, the dead, their shades, or their underworld abode. Ancient writers themselves disagreed about the relationship of Manes to *Lares*, *Lemures*, and *Larvae*. The familiar chart “good dead = Lares, bad dead = Lemures/Larvae, uncertain dead = Manes” is an Antonine philosophical system in Apuleius, preserved and criticized by Augustine—not a securely attested archaic Roman doctrine.

## Evidentiary labels used below

- **Securely attested early material:** Republican or early imperial evidence directly preserved in a text, inscription, calendar, or archaeological context.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** influential Augustan or imperial literary formulation, not necessarily evidence for archaic practice.
- **Regional variant:** locally limited epigraphic, linguistic, or ritual form.
- **Learned systematization:** an antiquarian, philosophical, grammatical, or theological attempt to organize inconsistent inherited material.
- **Syncretistic identification:** explicit or demonstrable equation with another religious category.
- **Medieval or early-modern accretion:** post-antique reception unsupported as ancient Roman doctrine.
- **Folklore:** vernacular or customary survival whose continuity cannot automatically be proved.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference from incomplete sources.
- **Modern invention:** a claim or practice first demonstrable in modern literature, occultism, popular culture, or revival religion.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

**Securely attested early material.** *Di Manes* or *Dii Manes* means literally “the Manes gods” or “divine Manes.” Translations such as “spirits of the dead,” “departed spirits,” and “shades” communicate part of the sense but suppress the noun *di*, “gods.” Charles W. King therefore prefers “the divine Manes” or “the deified dead.” Cicero’s religious law gives them legally protected *iura*; Lucretius’s polemic assumes that contemporaries sacrifice to them; Livy’s *devotio* makes them recipients of a commander and enemy army; imperial epitaphs dedicate tombs to them.

The category could be collective and individual at once. Latin normally employs the grammatically plural *Manes*, yet imperial inscriptions speak of the *Manes* of one named deceased person. This is not evidence that Romans imagined every dead person as a crowd. It reflects a plural divine title applied to an individual dead recipient, comparable to other Roman plural or collective divine formulations.

### Forms and formulas

- **Latin:** *Manes*; *Di Manes*, *Dii Manes*; dative *Dis Manibus* or *Diis Manibus*.
- **Funerary abbreviations:** **D·M**, **D M**, **D·M·S**, or **DMS**, normally expanded *Dis Manibus* and *Dis Manibus sacrum*, “to the divine Manes” and “sacred to the divine Manes.”
- **Expanded variants:** *Dis Manibus et memoriae* (“to the Manes and the memory…”), *Dis Inferis Manibus*, and locally *memoriae aeternae* or *quieti aeternae*. These extensions became especially common in parts of Gaul and Germany from the second or third centuries CE.
- **Related descriptions:** *divi Manes* (“divine Manes”), *dei/dei inferi* (“gods below”), *di parentes* (“divine ancestors/parents”), *animae silentum* (“souls of the silent”), and poetic *umbrae* (“shades”). These are overlapping expressions, not universally interchangeable technical synonyms.

### Etymology

**Ancient explanation / learned systematization.** Varro, *De lingua Latina* 6.4 and related antiquarian tradition connected *Manes* with an archaic adjective *manus*, “good”: *bonum antiqui dicebant manum*—“the ancients called the good *manum*.” Festus and later grammarians preserve similar reasoning. On this account, *Di Manes* means “the good/kindly gods,” a propitiatory euphemism for powers that might otherwise be dangerous.

**Modern scholarly reconstruction.** The euphemistic derivation from Italic *manus/manis*, “good,” remains the standard cautious proposal and has comparanda in Celtic words such as Old Irish *maith* and Welsh *mad*, “good.” It is nevertheless not demonstrable beyond dispute. Relationships proposed with Latin *mane* (“morning”), *maturus* (“ripe, timely”), *Mania*, *manare* (“flow”), or *mundus* are phonetic or antiquarian associations, not established derivations. Latin *manus*, “hand,” belongs to a different etymological history.

### Classification and semantic range

1. **Divine dead:** Cicero’s *deorum Manium* and the funerary formula explicitly classify them as *di/dei*.
2. **An individual’s posthumous divine identity:** imperial epitaphs address the *Manes* of named women, men, children, enslaved people, freed people, soldiers, and provincials.
3. **Infernal gods collectively:** Livy and Macrobius invoke them with Tellus, Dis Pater, Veiovis, or other underworld powers.
4. **Shades or souls:** Virgil and Ovid frequently use *manes* poetically for the deceased.
5. **The underworld or condition of death:** phrases such as “go to the Manes” or “suffer one’s own Manes” can denote the realm or destiny of the dead.
6. **A morally indeterminate category:** Apuleius’s classification, discussed below, is an explicit second-century learned system, not the meaning of all earlier uses.

### Manes, Lares, Lemures, and Larvae

The sources do not support a single stable taxonomy.

- **Lares:** normally protective local, domestic, crossroads, or civic gods. Some ancient intellectuals understood certain Lares as good human souls, but Lares also possessed independent place-based cults and cannot simply be reduced to ancestors.
- **Lemures:** in Ovid, the “silent souls” driven from the house during the Lemuria. Ovid’s derivation of *Lemuria* from an earlier *Remuria* honoring Remus is poetic etiology and linguistically implausible.
- **Larvae:** frightening apparitions or malevolent dead; *larva* can also mean a mask or a terrifying figure and occurs as an insult in comedy.
- **Manes:** a broad honorific/divine designation, often without a moral distinction.

**Learned systematization.** Apuleius, *De deo Socratis* 15, approximately 150–170 CE, states that a separated human soul is a *lemur*; a benevolent guardian of descendants is a *Lar familiaris*; a restless and harmful spirit is a *larva*; and one whose character is uncertain is a *Manis deus*. Augustine, *City of God* 9.11, quotes or paraphrases this system polemically: good dead become Lares, bad dead Lemures or Larvae, and morally uncertain dead are called Manes. Elsewhere the vocabulary overlaps too greatly for this to be a universal popular doctrine.

### Identifications outside Rome

There is no securely attested interpretatio graeca equating the Manes with one Greek god. Greek-speaking Romans could use broad equivalents such as *daimones*, *heroes*, *psychai*, or *chthonioi*, depending upon context. None reproduces the Roman combination of collective divinity, family cult, funerary dedication, and legal status.

Claims that the Manes are simply Etruscan spirits, or children of the Etruscan underworld goddess Mania and Mantus, exceed the evidence. Etruscan tomb art demonstrates elaborate conceptions of the dead and infernal beings, and Etruria or Campania may have influenced Roman funerary spectacles. No Etruscan inscription or mirror supplies a direct linguistic ancestor of Latin *Di Manes*. The proposed Etruscan genealogy is consequently **modern reconstruction**, not an attested Roman genealogy.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

#### Ennius and Plautus

**Possible earlier literary evidence, incompletely transmitted.** Ennius’s *Annales*, composed in the early second century BCE, included the self-devotion of a Decius Mus to the *Di Manes*. Because the epic survives in fragments quoted by later authors, the episode is evidence for a Republican tradition but not a contemporary manuscript or inscription.

Plautus’s *Mostellaria*, early second century BCE, assumes that a murdered and improperly buried person may haunt a house; *Mercator* 981 uses *larva* abusively. These are early evidence for Roman ghost concepts, not secure proof that Plautus used *Manes* as the formal collective title.

#### Lucretius

**Securely attested early material, approximately 60–55 BCE.** In *De rerum natura* 1.133–135 Lucretius asks what the mind and soul consist of and what produces supposed encounters with dead persons. At 3.51–52 he lists fear of the Manes and Acheron among terrors generated by ignorance. At 3.52 and 6.759–763 the poem refers to black victims and offerings made to the *divi Manes*. Lucretius rejects postmortem survival, but his rejection is strong evidence that such rites and vocabulary were intelligible to his audience.

#### Cicero

**Securely attested early material, later 50s BCE.** *De legibus* 2.22 prescribes: *Deorum Manium iura sancta sunto. Suos leto datos divos habento*—approximately, “Let the rights of the divine Manes be sacred; let people regard their own dead as divine.” This is Cicero’s idealizing religious legislation, modeled on ancestral law. It must not be quoted uncritically as the text of the Twelve Tables.

Elsewhere in *De legibus* 2.54–57 Cicero discusses pontifical burial law, the religious status of graves, and restrictions traditionally attributed to the Twelve Tables. *De natura deorum* (45 BCE) is important for Roman debates over divine ontology but does not give a systematic doctrine of the Manes. Cicero’s philosophical works instead demonstrate that elite Romans could maintain ancestral funerary law while debating or denying personal immortality.

#### Republican calendars

The painted *Fasti Antiates Maiores*, approximately 84–55 BCE and found at Antium, are the earliest substantially preserved pre-Julian Roman calendar. They name the Feralia on 21 February and Lemuria on 9, 11, and 13 May. Their evidence is independent of Ovid and proves that these observances were not his inventions. The calendar does not establish every ritual detail supplied by his poem.

### Major primary corpus

#### Varro

Varro’s *Antiquitates rerum divinarum* is lost and survives chiefly through Augustine and other late authors. His *De lingua Latina* 6, written in the mid-first century BCE, preserves etymological and calendrical material. Macrobius quotes Varro’s statement that when the *mundus* stands open, “the gate of the grim and infernal gods is, as it were, open.” Because the original contexts are lost, Varro must often be reconstructed through hostile or selective intermediaries.

#### Livy

Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 8.9–10, written in the Augustan period, narrates the consul Publius Decius Mus’s *devotio* at the battle near Mount Vesuvius, conventionally dated 340 BCE. Under pontifical direction Decius invokes Janus, Jupiter, Mars Pater, Quirinus, Bellona, the Lares, Novensiles, Indigetes, gods controlling Romans and enemies, and the *Di Manes*. He then devotes himself and the enemy legions to the Manes and Tellus.

Livy presents the formula as archaic ritual, but his written account is approximately three centuries later than the purported event. It securely attests Augustan knowledge of the rite; it does not provide a verbatim fourth-century-BCE document.

#### Virgil

Virgil’s *Aeneid*, composed approximately 29–19 BCE, is the richest poetic corpus for *manes*. The term may mean the shade of a particular dead person, dead ancestors, infernal powers, or the underworld.

Important passages include:

- 3.62–68: Aeneas conducts rites at Polydorus’s grave and calls upon the dead man’s spirit.
- 4.385–387: Dido threatens to pursue Aeneas with her shade after death.
- 4.490 and 4.654–705: funeral magic, Dido’s death, and her passage among the dead.
- Book 5: funeral games and offerings for Anchises on the anniversary of his death.
- 6.264–901: the descent to the underworld, including Palinurus, Dido, Deiphobus, Anchises, and future Roman souls.
- 6.743: *quisque suos patimur Manes*, notoriously difficult—“each of us suffers his own Manes/fate/purification.”
- 10.519–520 and 11.81–84: promised funerary honor and rites for fallen warriors.
- 12.646: Turnus anticipates death and “the Manes.”

This is poetic theology, not a priestly handbook. Virgil’s underworld combines Greek epic, philosophical eschatology, Roman funerary language, and Augustan political teleology.

#### Ovid

Ovid’s *Fasti*, composed and revised approximately 2–17 CE, is indispensable but literary:

- 2.533–570: Parentalia and Feralia; modest offerings of garlands, grain, salt, wine-soaked bread, and violets suffice to placate the ancestral shades.
- 2.547–556: neglect of the dead allegedly causes ancestral spirits to emerge, howl through Rome, and produce pestilence until rites are restored.
- 2.571–616: the Feralia rite of an old woman who binds hostile tongues and invokes *Tacita/Muta*; this is a separate magical-etiological complex.
- 5.419–492: Lemuria; the householder rises at midnight, washes, makes an apotropaic hand gesture, throws black beans behind him nine times, says that the beans redeem himself and his family, clashes bronze, and nine times commands, *Manes exite paterni*, “ancestral Manes, depart.”
- 5.449–492: Remus appears to his foster parents and asks Romulus for commemorative rites; Ovid claims *Remuria* became *Lemuria*. This is **later canonical poetic etiology**, not credible linguistic history.

Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* uses *manes* broadly in narratives of death and descent, including Orpheus and Eurydice, Ceyx and Alcyone, and apotheosis. It supplies poetic usage rather than a discrete “myth of the Manes.”

#### Festus and Verrius Flaccus

Festus’s second-century-CE epitome of Verrius Flaccus’s Augustan lexicon, itself surviving in a damaged medieval manuscript and Paul the Deacon’s eighth-century epitome, preserves crucial material on the *mundus*. Under *mundus*, Festus attributes to Ateius Capito the statement that it was opened three times annually: the day after the Volcanalia, 5 October, and 8 November—normally understood as 24 August, 5 October, and 8 November. Its lower portion was consecrated to the Manes and normally closed. Because divine secrets were exposed when it opened, the days were *religiosi* and public business was avoided.

The textual transmission is damaged, and “mundus” can mean the cosmos, a ritual pit, or ceremonial equipment. The archaeological identification of a particular surviving pit in the Roman Forum as this exact Manes-*mundus* remains disputed.

#### Apuleius

*De deo Socratis* 15, approximately the mid-second century CE, sets Roman dead spirits within Middle Platonic demonology. Its good Lar/bad Larva-or-Lemur/uncertain Manes classification is the clearest ancient taxonomy, precisely because it is philosophical and unusually explicit. It should be labeled **syncretistic learned systematization**.

#### Macrobius

Macrobius’s *Saturnalia*, approximately 400–430 CE, is late-antique antiquarian literature:

- 1.16.16–18 states that war, levies, voyages, and marriage were religiously prohibited while the *mundus* stood open. It identifies the occasion with Dis Pater and Proserpina and quotes Varro on the gate of the infernal gods.
- 3.9.9–13 preserves an *evocatio* and *devotio* allegedly used against Carthage in 146 BCE, invoking Dis Pater, Veiovis, and the Manes to deprive the enemy of upper-world light.

Macrobius probably drew on lost antiquarian collections. His formulas may contain old language, but their purported Republican performance is not independently documented word for word.

#### Servius

Servius’s late-fourth/early-fifth-century commentary on the *Aeneid* glosses Virgilian *manes*, burial practices, and infernal theology. Servius sometimes preserves earlier antiquarian learning unavailable elsewhere, but his explanations regularly harmonize poetic passages into late learned systems.

#### Christian apologists

- **Tertullian,** *De spectaculis* 12, approximately 197–202 CE, says gladiatorial display developed from funeral duty and blood offered to the dead, then condemns it as demonic. He is an early but hostile witness.
- **Arnobius,** *Adversus nationes*, approximately 295–305 CE, attacks the multiplicity and contradictions of Roman divine classifications.
- **Augustine,** *City of God* 7 and 9, begun 413 CE, preserves large extracts or paraphrases from Varro and Apuleius while arguing that Roman gods and dead spirits are demons.
- **Prudentius,** *Contra Symmachum*, approximately 402–403 CE, associates the old cults and spectacles with infernal powers.
- **Isidore of Seville,** *Etymologies* 8.11, early seventh century, repeats late etymological and demonological classifications.

These authors are invaluable because they quote lost material. Their descriptions of “demons,” sacrifices, and moral depravity are Christian polemic rather than neutral ethnography.

### Epigraphic corpus

The formula *Dis Manibus* appears sporadically around the Augustan period and securely at Rome by the middle of the first century CE; one often-cited early Roman occurrence is dated to approximately 59 CE. It becomes common late in the first century and nearly ubiquitous in many regions from Hadrian’s reign onward. Absence of D·M does not automatically date a tomb earlier: regional epigraphic habits differed.

The formula occurs across Italy, Britain, Gaul, Germany, Hispania, North Africa, the Danubian provinces, Greece, and the eastern Mediterranean. It appears for people across status categories. It could mark the tomb as sacred to the divine dead generally, dedicate it to the named deceased’s Manes, or do both simultaneously.

The *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum*, Epigraphic Database Roma, Epigraphic Database Heidelberg, and EDCS preserve tens of thousands of relevant records. The corpus shows no uniform theology: formulaic use ranges from elaborate personal addresses to two conventional initials.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain and powers

The Manes governed no bounded portfolio comparable to Mars’s war or Ceres’s grain. Their “domain” was relational:

- the continuing identity and cultic claims of deceased persons;
- family continuity, memory, inheritance, and tomb ownership;
- the dangerous boundary between living community and dead;
- power from below over health, fertility, survival, military victory, terror, and death;
- the sacred status of burial places;
- poetic access to the underworld.

**Securely attested early material.** Cicero assigns them rights; Livy makes them capable of receiving and destroying devoted armies; Ovid makes neglected ancestors capable of emerging from tombs and harming Rome; epitaphs make individual dead recipients of dedication.

### Powers and limitations

The Manes could receive food, wine, flowers, animal victims, prayers, vows, and a human life ritually devoted in battle. They could be asked to protect descendants, extend life, enforce a curse, receive enemies, or remain peaceably in their proper sphere.

They were not portrayed as omniscient or omnipotent. Access between dead and living was structured by burial, anniversary, festival, dream, necromancy, poetic descent, or a ritually opened boundary. Many Roman philosophical texts denied that the dead retained consciousness at all; ritual practice and philosophical disbelief coexisted.

### Iconography

**Absence of evidence is decisive here.** There is no standard anthropomorphic image of “the Manes,” no canonical sex, face, costume, weapon, sacred animal, color, number, planet, or temple statue.

Funerary monuments bearing D·M may display portraits of the dead, banquet scenes, occupations, mythological scenes, cupids, garlands, dolphins, birds, lions, or abstract ornament. Such images portray or commemorate the deceased and funerary hopes; they are not a fixed iconography of a collective Manes deity.

The paired letters **D M** are their most recognizable visual sign. They function almost as an epigraphic header but remain words, not an image.

Etruscan tomb paintings and engraved mirrors depict banquets, ancestors, demons, and underworld gods. They illuminate Italic funerary imagination but cannot be identified automatically as pictures of the Roman Manes. LIMC entries for particular underworld figures do not establish a collective Manes iconography.

### Associated objects and substances

- tomb, urn, sarcophagus, funerary altar, and grave enclosure;
- garlands and violets;
- grain, salt, wine-soaked bread, milk, wine, oil, eggs, and family meal offerings;
- black beans at Lemuria;
- bronze sounded to expel household ghosts;
- black animals in literary descriptions of chthonic sacrifice;
- blood in disputed accounts of funeral games;
- earth in the *devotio*, where Tellus and the Manes receive the devoted victim;
- the *mundus*, in antiquarian testimony, as a normally sealed ritual opening.

The numbers nine and three recur in the nine-day Parentalia, ninefold Lemuria formulas, three Lemuria dates, and three openings of the *mundus*. They belong to particular rites; “nine is the sacred number of the Manes” would overgeneralize.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

The Manes have no securely attested parentage, birth, spouse, offspring, siblings, or personal genealogy. They are a class created by human death and cultic transformation, not a reproductive divine family.

Late antiquarian references sometimes connect *Mania* or *Mater Larum* with the Lares and dead spirits. Modern reference works occasionally transform this into “Mania, mother of the Manes.” That exact genealogy is not securely supported. Similarity between *Mania* and *Manes* encouraged ancient and modern association; it is not proof of descent.

### Every securely identifiable god–god interaction or joint episode

Because the Manes are a collective class rather than a narrative protagonist, their documented relations are chiefly joint invocation and shared ritual jurisdiction. The following list covers the distinct named interactions in the principal surviving sources; mere co-membership in the Roman pantheon is excluded.

- **Tellus:** joint recipient of Decius Mus’s life and the enemy legions in the *devotio*. Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 8.9.6–8, written approximately 27 BCE–14 CE, narrating 340 BCE. **Secure Augustan attestation of a claimed archaic rite.**
- **Janus:** jointly invoked with the Manes in Decius’s preliminary prayer for Roman victory. Livy 8.9.6. **Secure Augustan attestation; no separate narrative interaction.**
- **Jupiter:** jointly invoked in the same prayer; the general raises his hands when Jupiter is named in later ritual explanation. Livy 8.9; Macrobius 3.9. **Attested ritual conjunction.**
- **Mars Pater:** jointly invoked in Decius’s prayer. Livy 8.9. **Attested ritual conjunction.**
- **Quirinus:** jointly invoked in Decius’s prayer. Livy 8.9. **Attested ritual conjunction.**
- **Bellona:** jointly invoked in Decius’s prayer. Livy 8.9. **Attested ritual conjunction.**
- **Lares:** jointly invoked in Decius’s prayer. Livy 8.9. Apuleius later treats Lares as morally good deceased souls in contrast to other categories. **Early ritual conjunction; later learned classification.**
- **Divi Novensiles:** jointly invoked in Decius’s prayer. Livy 8.9. **Attested ritual conjunction.**
- **Di Indigetes:** jointly invoked in Decius’s prayer. Livy 8.9. **Attested ritual conjunction.**
- **Dis Pater:** invoked with Veiovis and the Manes in Macrobius’s formula for devoting Carthage and its army. Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 3.9.10–11, approximately 400–430 CE, claiming a 146-BCE rite. **Late antiquarian preservation of purported Republican material.**
- **Veiovis:** invoked with Dis Pater and the Manes in the Carthage *devotio*. Macrobius 3.9.10–11. **Late antiquarian preservation.**
- **Proserpina:** the open *mundus* is said to be sacred to Dis Pater and Proserpina, while Varro describes it as the gate of infernal gods. Macrobius 1.16.17–18. **Late-antique learned conjunction.**
- **Ceres:** Festus calls the structure *mundus Cereris*, while its lower portion is consecrated to the Manes. Festus, *De verborum significatu*, second century CE epitomizing Verrius Flaccus. **Antiquarian ritual association; the exact relation is disputed.**
- **Lemures:** Ovid uses *Manes* in the Lemuria expulsion formula and calls Lemures the souls of the silent; Apuleius makes *lemur* a general postmortem soul-category. Ovid, *Fasti* 5.419–492; Apuleius, *De deo Socratis* 15. **Overlapping classification, not a meeting between distinct persons.**
- **Larvae:** Apuleius classifies harmful dead as Larvae and morally uncertain dead as Manes; Augustine repeats the contrast. **Antonine learned systematization and late-antique Christian reception.**
- **Mania/Mater Larum:** later antiquarian traditions associate her with Lares, masks or *maniae*, and the dead. No surviving early source records a direct episode with the Manes. **Disputed learned association; no secure interaction.**
- **Mercury:** Virgilian and later underworld narratives give Mercury psychopompic functions, but no surviving episode makes the collective Manes meet or act jointly with him. **Absence of a specific interaction.**
- **Pluto/Orcus:** poets use these names for the underworld or its ruler, while the Manes may denote its population or powers. Macrobius calls the open *mundus* “Pluto’s jaws.” **Poetic and learned spatial association; not a genealogy.**
- **Hecate, the Furies, and other infernal gods:** appear in the same literary underworld as dead souls, especially in Virgil, but no distinct collective-Manes interaction is narrated. **Shared setting only.**

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

The Manes have no independent epic biography. Their myths are etiologies and narratives about entry into, emergence from, or ritual exchange with the dead.

### The neglected dead return to Rome

**Later canonical elaboration.** Ovid, *Fasti* 2.547–556, says Rome once neglected the Parentalia during war. Ancestral souls left their tombs, howled through city and countryside, and brought disease. When the ancestral rites were restored, the prodigies ceased.

The story explains why even modest offerings are indispensable. No earlier independent source preserves the incident. It should be read as Ovidian festival etiology, probably drawing on older assumptions about offended dead, not as securely dated Roman history.

### Remus and the alleged origin of the Lemuria

Ovid, *Fasti* 5.449–492, relates that Remus’s bloody ghost appeared to his foster parents, Faustulus and Acca, lamented his murder, and asked Romulus to establish a commemorative day. Romulus founded *Remuria*; phonetic change supposedly produced *Lemuria*, after which the silent dead themselves were called Lemures.

This story is **Augustan-era poetic elaboration**. The phonetic derivation R → L is rejected by modern linguistics, and the Republican *Fasti Antiates* already attest LEMURIA. The narrative is valuable evidence for Ovid’s attempt to bind domestic ghost expulsion to Rome’s fratricidal foundation myth.

### Decius Mus and self-devotion

Livy’s Decius receives a battlefield sign that one commander must be offered to the gods below. The pontifex M. Valerius dictates the prayer. Decius veils his head, places one hand beneath his toga to his chin, stands on a spear, names the Roman gods, devotes himself and the enemy to Tellus and the Manes, then rides into the enemy and dies. His death transfers danger to the opposing army and secures victory.

The historical Decius was consul and died in the Latin War, but the ritualized speech is Livian. A related story assigns another *devotio* to his son at Sentinum in 295 BCE. Ennius’s lost epic seems to have treated the family tradition. The cycle is simultaneously family legend, political exemplum, military ritual narrative, and theology of substitution.

This is the clearest ancient case in which a human is deliberately consecrated alive to the Manes. It differs from ordinary homicide: Roman sources explicitly call it *devotio*, treat it as pontifically regulated, and prescribe what to do if the devoted commander survives.

### The devotio of Carthage

Macrobius claims that, after the protective gods of an enemy city were ritually invited out by *evocatio*, a Roman commander could devote the city, land, army, inhabitants, and enemies to Dis Pater, Veiovis, and the Manes. He connects a preserved formula with Scipio Aemilianus’s destruction of Carthage in 146 BCE.

The ritual’s archaizing Latin may preserve Republican elements, but Macrobius wrote more than five centuries later. There is no contemporary inscription or Polybian account confirming that this exact prayer was spoken at Carthage. It is **late antiquarian testimony to an alleged Republican rite**.

### Aeneas, Anchises, and the underworld

Virgil’s *Aeneid* makes ancestral dead central to Rome’s destiny. Aeneas observes anniversary rites and games for Anchises in Book 5; in Book 6 the Sibyl leads him through the underworld to his father. Anchises explains the purification and reincarnation of souls and displays future Romans.

Virgil sometimes calls these souls *Manes*, but the episode integrates Homeric *nekyia*, Orphic/Pythagorean or Platonic eschatology, Roman ancestor cult, and Augustan ideology. It is not evidence that all Romans believed in reincarnation or that all Manes inhabited Virgil’s carefully tiered underworld.

### Dido’s threatened shade

At *Aeneid* 4.384–387 Dido promises that, even absent in body, she will pursue Aeneas with dark fire and that news of his punishment will reach her among the Manes. After death Aeneas encounters her shade in Book 6. This illustrates the dead person’s retained identity and possible hostile agency, but Virgil does not make Dido a generic Larva under a formal taxonomy.

### Orpheus and other poetic descents

Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* and Virgil’s *Georgics* describe Orpheus appealing to underworld rulers and the dead for Eurydice’s return. *Manes* can mean the underworld population or infernal realm. These Greek-derived narratives expanded Latin poetic usage but did not create a separate cult of “the Manes of Orpheus.”

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult sites

There was no known principal Temple of the Manes and no securely attested centralized Manes priesthood. Their cultic geography was dispersed:

- individual tombs and grave precincts;
- family burial grounds and roadside cemeteries;
- columbaria and communal burial associations;
- domestic spaces during Lemuria;
- the disputed *mundus* at Rome;
- battlefields during *devotio*;
- the entire civic calendar during Feralia.

A tomb could become *locus religiosus*, protected by religious and civil law. Violating, alienating, or improperly using it could offend both the dead and the legal rights of survivors.

### Parentalia and Feralia

The *dies parentales* ran from 13 to 21 February. The bulk of the observance was private family commemoration; the concluding Feralia on 21 February was publicly marked in surviving calendars.

Ovid says families carried garlands, scattered grain and salt, wine-soaked bread, and violets to the tombs. The shades required *pietas* more than wealth. Temples were closed, marriages avoided, and magistrates laid aside certain insignia. On 22 February the Caristia brought living relatives together and marked reintegration after attention to the dead.

Late calendars add details. The Calendar of 354 states *Virgo Vestalis parentat*, “a Vestal makes ancestral offering,” on 13 February; Polemius Silvius in 448/449 records the beginning of tomb commemoration. These witnesses may preserve public ritual but are centuries later than the Republican calendar.

### Lemuria

The Lemuria occurred on 9, 11, and 13 May. Ovid’s householder performs the rite barefoot at midnight:

1. rises when household and animals are silent;
2. makes an apotropaic hand sign;
3. washes in spring water;
4. throws black beans backward without looking;
5. repeats nine times that the beans redeem himself and his family;
6. washes again and clangs bronze;
7. repeats *Manes exite paterni* nine times;
8. finally looks back, considering the rite completed.

This is not a loving tomb meal. Its action separates and expels intrusive ancestral dead from domestic space. Nevertheless, Ovid calls the recipients both Lemures and paternal Manes, showing that the terms were not cleanly separated in practice or poetry.

The claim that temples closed and marriages were unlucky in May is attested by Ovid. The modern rhyme or superstition “marry in May, rue the day” may preserve later European seasonality, but direct unbroken descent from Roman Lemuria is not demonstrable.

### The mundus

Festus and Macrobius describe a normally sealed opening connected with the lower gods:

- traditional opening dates: 24 August, 5 October, and 8 November;
- lower part consecrated to the Manes;
- public business, war, military levies, voyages, and marriage restricted;
- Varro’s metaphor: a gate of the infernal gods stood open.

Two or more concepts may have become entangled: the *mundus Cereris*; a foundation pit into which first-fruits and soil were deposited; and a portal of the dead. Ancient antiquarians may already have harmonized them. The common modern picture of a literal trapdoor from which all ghosts emerged three days each year is stronger than the surviving evidence.

No excavation has indisputably identified the ritual *mundus*. The structure conventionally called the *Mundus* or *Umbilicus Urbis* in the Forum cannot simply be equated with Festus’s object.

### Funerary and anniversary rites

The funeral established separation from the living and the deceased’s new ritual status. A nine-day mourning sequence concluded with a meal or sacrifice; birthdays, death anniversaries, and family festivals renewed the bond. Tubes inserted into graves allowed liquid offerings in some regions, while tables, couches, dining rooms, and food remains document cemetery meals.

The evidence does not show that every offering was addressed verbally as “to the Manes.” Material practice, literary terminology, and epigraphic formulas overlap without being identical.

### Sacrifice and the human-sacrifice dispute

#### Animal and food offerings

Literary sources associate infernal rites with dark or black victims. Ovid emphasizes cheap vegetarian offerings at the Parentalia. Both can be true in different contexts: family commemoration did not require the same offering as a state *devotio* or literary necromancy.

#### Devotio

Decius Mus’s act is an explicitly documented ritual self-consecration to Tellus and the Manes. It is therefore a form of human self-sacrifice in ancient terminology, though embedded in battle rather than temple slaughter.

#### Gladiatorial munera

The first recorded Roman gladiatorial *munus* was held in 264 BCE at the funeral of D. Junius Brutus Pera, where three pairs fought. Later authors and Christian critics describe funeral combat as blood offered to the dead or as a substitute for sacrificing captives.

The interpretation remains contested:

- **Sacrificial-origin position:** funeral setting, vocabulary of a duty owed to the dead, later testimony about blood for the Manes, and Italic/Etruscan parallels suggest development from funerary human sacrifice.
- **Cautionary position:** no contemporary source for the 264-BCE event explicitly calls it a sacrifice to the Manes; death was not structurally required in every bout; *munus* could mean an aristocratic duty or gift; Etruscan origin is not proven and Campanian evidence is also important.
- **Secure conclusion:** gladiatorial combat entered Rome as an elite funerary performance and later became public spectacle. Calling every gladiatorial death “a sacrifice to the Manes” is not supported.

#### Buried Gauls and Greeks

Rome buried pairs of Gauls and Greeks alive in the Forum Boarium in crisis years such as 228 and 216 BCE. Ancient writers treated these as extraordinary rites under Sibylline authority. The victims are not securely said to have been offered specifically to the Manes. Their inclusion in Manes dossiers without qualification is therefore misleading.

### Magic and curses

Curse tablets frequently consign targets to underworld gods, tomb spirits, or an untimely dead person. Some name the Manes; others invoke Dis Pater, Proserpina, Hecate, or anonymous *di inferi*. Tombs were used because the dead were believed capable of carrying or enforcing messages. This represents particular magical transactions, not ordinary family cult.

### Priesthood and organization

No *flamen Manium* or collegium devoted solely to the Manes is securely attested. Pontiffs regulated burial law and *devotio*; Vestals appear in late evidence for the opening of the Parentalia; family heads conducted domestic rites; burial collegia managed collective commemoration. The absence of a specialist priesthood is consistent with a distributed class of recipients rather than a temple deity.

### Economics and politics

Funerary monuments converted family wealth, status, affection, and citizenship into public memory. The D·M formula was inclusive: enslaved and freed people, women, children, soldiers, immigrants, and municipal elites all appear beneath it. Burial clubs enabled people of modest means to secure funerals and memorial meals.

Elite funeral games displayed aristocratic resources and ancestry. The Decian *devotio* converted a commander’s death into hereditary civic capital. Virgil’s underworld made the ancestral dead authorize Augustan Rome. The Manes were therefore private kin, legally protected recipients, and instruments of public ideology.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before the surviving direct evidence

Burial and ancestor rites in Latium and Etruria long predate the word *Manes*. Archaeology demonstrates cremation and inhumation, grave goods, cemetery meals, and changing representations of the dead. It cannot tell us that all these rites addressed beings already called *Di Manes*.

Older scholarship often reconstructed an “original” undifferentiated mass of fearful dead that later became benevolent ancestors. The linear evolutionary model is unproven. Fear, affection, divine honor, and expulsion coexist in the earliest full evidence.

### Third–second centuries BCE

Plautine comedy demonstrates lively ghost beliefs. Ennius apparently treated Decius’s self-devotion. Funeral gladiatorial combat entered Rome in 264 BCE. None supplies a contemporary Manes inscription.

### Late Republic, approximately 84–30 BCE

This is the first securely documented phase:

- *Fasti Antiates Maiores* attest Feralia and Lemuria.
- Lucretius attacks fear of the Manes and mentions offerings.
- Cicero gives the Manes sacred rights and treats family dead as divine.
- Varro systematizes etymology, calendars, and infernal rites.
- individual dead increasingly emerge in Latin poetry and elite memorial culture.

### Augustan period

Livy codifies the *devotio*; Virgil makes dead ancestors central to Rome’s destiny; Ovid gives the fullest ritual descriptions and etiologies. These writers shaped virtually all later narratives about the Manes. Their works are literary reconstructions produced amid Augustan religious restoration, not transparent windows onto regal Rome.

### First century CE

The epigraphic formula *Dis Manibus* begins to spread. Early examples are occasional, becoming markedly more frequent in the Flavian period. The shift is not the “invention” of the Manes; it is the rise of a particular monumental habit that individualizes the divine dead.

### Second–third centuries CE

This is the epigraphic peak. D·M and D·M·S appear across the empire. Local expansions—especially references to memory and eternal rest—show provincial adaptation.

Apuleius incorporates Roman categories into Platonic demonology. Tomb art, sarcophagi, funerary associations, anniversary meals, and magical texts show multiple ideas of postmortem power. There was no empire-wide doctrinal authority enforcing a single definition.

### Christianization, fourth–sixth centuries CE

Imperial laws increasingly restricted sacrifice, temple finance, and public pagan rites during the fourth century, especially under Theodosius I and successors. No surviving law uniquely abolishes “the cult of the Manes.” Suppression operated through broader prohibitions and through transformed burial institutions.

Christian apologists reclassified traditional gods and dead spirits as demons. Yet D·M continued on some Christian epitaphs, sometimes beside Christian formulas such as *in pace*, *spiritus sanctus*, or invocations of eternal rest. In these cases the letters could function as inherited funerary convention rather than a confession of pagan theology.

Cemetery meals, anniversary commemoration, offerings at tombs, and relationships with powerful dead continued in Christian settings, including martyr cult. Direct identity between the Manes and saints is too simple: Christian authorities reinterpreted, regulated, and sometimes condemned inherited practices while developing distinct resurrection and intercession doctrines.

### Medieval and early-modern reception

*Manes* survived as a learned Latin poetic word for shades, ancestors, and the underworld. Medieval readers encountered it in Virgil, Ovid, Servius, Augustine, and Isidore. Renaissance antiquarians recovered Roman calendars and inscriptions and used *manes* in neo-Latin epitaphs.

The phrase “appease his Manes” became a literary expression meaning to honor or avenge the dead. This is reception of classical vocabulary, not evidence of a continuous organized cult.

### Modern period

Archaeological excavation, publication of the CIL, and systematic study of Roman calendars transformed the dossier from literary mythology into social and epigraphic history. The Manes now appear in museum displays primarily through funerary monuments bearing D·M.

Modern Roman polytheists and reconstructionists sometimes observe Parentalia, Feralia, or Lemuria and address deceased relatives as Manes. These are genuine modern religious practices, but no census establishes their scale and no institutional lineage connects them continuously to an ancient priesthood. They are **modern reconstruction and revival**, informed by Ovid, calendars, inscriptions, and contemporary religious judgment.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and public imagery

The letters D·M remain among the most visible signs of Roman religion because they occur on museum tombstones throughout the former empire. Popular summaries often mistranslate them simply as “to the memory of.” That may approximate their later commemorative function, but the literal expression invokes divine Manes.

The film *Gladiator* (2000) presents Maximus praying to figurines of his dead wife and son. The scene materially popularized a composite image of Roman domestic ancestor worship. It is plausible in general religious structure but does not reproduce a documented Manes rite or specific archaeological household shrine.

### Occultism

Unlike Hecate, Mercury, Isis, or named demons, the Manes did not acquire a major independent identity in Renaissance ceremonial magic, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or standardized modern grimoires. Early-modern authors continued to use *manes* as learned Latin for spirits of the dead. Modern occult writers may treat the word as a generic class of ancestral or chthonic spirits.

Claims that there is an ancient fixed Manes sigil, planetary seal, color, tarot correspondence, summoning hierarchy, or named king of the Manes are **modern inventions** unless independently sourced. No such system is present in Roman calendars, CIL epitaphs, Ovid, Festus, or Macrobius.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist use

Modern practitioners reconstruct:

- grave offerings during 13–21 February;
- a concluding Feralia;
- family meals at Caristia;
- bean-and-bronze purification on 9, 11, and 13 May;
- prayers addressed to the *Di Manes* of named relatives.

These practices generally derive directly from Ovid and modern handbooks. Adjustments for ethical, household, or ecological reasons are modern ritual development. Assertions of an unbroken secret Roman lineage are not documented.

### Literature and language

“Manes” became a conventional poetic plural in European literature. English, French, Italian, and Spanish writers use “the manes of X” for the dead person’s spirit or posthumous honor. This often erases the explicitly divine force of Roman *Di Manes*.

The zoological name “lemur,” coined by Linnaeus in 1758 for nocturnal primates, alludes to Roman *lemures*, not directly to the Manes. Later popular culture sometimes collapses Manes, Lemures, Lares, and generic ghosts into one species. That collapse reflects modern mythographic simplification.

### Politics and nationalism

Roman funerary formulas have been reused in classicizing memorial architecture and nationalist invocations of ancestral dead. No major modern extremist movement appears to have adopted the Manes as an exclusive emblem comparable to better-known solar, martial, or Germanic symbols. General fascist use of Roman antiquity should not be converted into a specific Manes cult without evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Older ritualist and evolutionary models

Georg Wissowa’s *Religion und Kultus der Römer* organized Roman religion through calendars, priesthoods, and public cult. In this framework the Manes belonged primarily to the cult of the dead and were frequently distinguished schematically from Lares and harmful ghosts.

William Warde Fowler emphasized survival of archaic taboo and ghost fear in Lemuria and the *mundus*. Such accounts remain valuable collections of evidence but often assume an evolution from “primitive fear” toward civilized commemoration.

Franz Cumont stressed Greek philosophy, mystery religions, and eastern influence in changing Roman afterlife expectations. His model of an initially vague Roman afterlife displaced by more consoling imported doctrines has been substantially qualified by epigraphic and social-historical research.

### Dumézil

Georges Dumézil’s *La religion romaine archaïque* (1966; English translation 1970) treated Roman religion as a structured Indo-European system rather than a disorderly survival. His comparative method transformed the field, but trifunctional reconstructions are strongest where linguistic and ritual correspondences converge. The Manes cannot be assigned a detailed Proto-Indo-European theology merely because ancestor cult is widespread.

### Wissowa, Latte, Scheid, and Beard–North–Price

Kurt Latte’s *Römische Religionsgeschichte* (1960) remains fundamental for terminology and cult, including caution about deriving a uniform doctrine from late sources.

John Scheid emphasizes ritual action, civic organization, and the absence of dogmatic creeds. This corrects attempts to force Roman evidence into Christian-style theology, although it does not mean Romans lacked beliefs about the dead.

Mary Beard, John North, and Simon Price’s *Religions of Rome* integrate literary, political, archaeological, and epigraphic evidence and resist treating late antiquarian statements as straightforward archaic survivals.

### Charles W. King and the “belief” debate

Charles W. King’s *The Ancient Roman Afterlife: Di Manes, Belief, and the Cult of the Dead* (2020) is the most sustained recent study. King argues that scholarship has too often translated away *di* and reduced the Manes to metaphor, convention, or vague “spirits.” He treats ordinary dead Romans as genuinely divinized cult recipients capable, in Roman belief, of helping and harming.

The corrective is important: the epigraphic and ritual evidence cannot be explained solely as empty formula. The surviving material nevertheless remains internally contradictory. Some Romans denied survival; some used D·M conventionally; poetic and philosophical models differ. The most defensible conclusion is plural belief, not universal dogma or universal disbelief.

### Disputed questions

#### Were the Manes originally one anonymous mass?

Older scholarship often says yes, with individual Manes developing later. Imperial inscriptions clearly individualize them, but Republican family ritual already concerned “one’s own” dead. The evidence supports a growing epigraphic emphasis on named individuals, not proof that early Romans could not imagine individual dead.

#### Were all dead automatically Manes?

Cicero’s ideal law and imperial formulas point toward broad inclusion; King argues forcefully for an inclusive class. Yet burial, remembrance, ritual transition, premature death, and moral status could affect how particular dead were imagined. “Every human automatically became a fully empowered god at the instant of death” is stronger than the evidence.

#### Were Manes benevolent?

The euphemistic etymology suggests “good/kindly,” and family dead could protect. Ovid’s rites, curses, *devotio*, and the open *mundus* show danger. Benevolent and dangerous are relational conditions, not mutually exclusive species.

#### Are Lares ancestors?

Some ancient theories say good human souls become Lares. Material and civic evidence also makes Lares protective gods of places, neighborhoods, roads, fields, and the state. “Lares are simply ancestors” is an overstatement.

#### Are Lemures and Larvae different?

Apuleius differentiates them imperfectly; other authors use them as overlapping terms. Modern charts present more precision than the sources.

#### Was the mundus a ghost gate?

Festus and Macrobius support an infernal-gate interpretation. Its connection to Ceres, city foundation, first-fruits, and a specific Forum monument remains disputed. The dates are better attested than the popular architectural reconstruction.

#### Were gladiators sacrificed to the Manes?

Funerary origin and later testimony make a connection plausible. Lack of contemporary sacrificial language, nonfatal outcomes, and political development preclude certainty. The careful formula is “funerary combat later interpreted as blood payment to the dead,” not “all gladiators were human sacrifices.”

#### Etruscan origin

Etruscan influence on Roman funerary culture is substantial; direct derivation of the name and entire Manes concept is unproved. Etruscan mirrors do not furnish a named collective equivalent.

### State of the evidence

- Literary evidence survives through medieval manuscripts, often many centuries later than composition.
- Varro, Verrius Flaccus, and Ennius are fragmentary and mediated.
- Festus’s principal manuscript is damaged.
- Epigraphy is abundant but formulaic and often imprecisely dated.
- Archaeology records practices but rarely identifies the supernatural recipient by name.
- No canonical Manes image, myth cycle, temple archive, or priestly liturgy survives.
- CIL, EDR, EDH, and EDCS allow regional and chronological comparison, but database coverage and dating continue to change.
