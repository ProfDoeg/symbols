# Fortuna: Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess (*dea*) in Roman religion; also an increasingly abstract personification of favorable or adverse contingency, civic destiny, imperial success, and the instability of worldly status.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest securely datable written evidence naming **Fortuna Primigenia** is generally taken to be the bronze dedication **CIL XIV 2863 = CIL I² 60**, from Praeneste, dated palaeographically to the first half of the third century BCE. Archaeology shows earlier cult activity at Praeneste and at Rome’s Sant’Omobono sanctuary, but identifying the earliest structural phases specifically with Fortuna depends on later literary testimony and is not epigraphically secure.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early attested]** Republican evidence, especially inscriptions, calendars, archaeology, and early literary fragments.
- **[Later canonical]** Versions fixed by late Republican or imperial authors and subsequently treated as standard.
- **[Regional variant]** A locally specific cult, theology, title, or iconography.
- **[Learned systematization]** Antiquarian, philosophical, or theological ordering imposed on diverse cults.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A historically observable equation or fusion with another deity.
- **[Medieval/early-modern accretion]** Post-antique transformation of Fortuna.
- **[Folklore]** Popular tradition whose institutional or textual transmission is uncertain.
- **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** An inference from incomplete evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A recent claim presented without ancient or medieval support.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identity

Fortuna is not reducible to “the Roman goddess of luck.” The Roman record contains many Fortunae differentiated by community, social constituency, place, occasion, and desired result. At Praeneste she was a civic and oracular goddess, **Fortuna Primigenia**, whose sanctuary delivered written lots. At Rome she appeared as **Fors Fortuna**, **Fortuna Muliebris**, **Fortuna Virilis**, **Fortuna Publica Populi Romani Quiritium**, **Fortuna Huiusce Diei**, **Fortuna Equestris**, **Fortuna Redux**, and under numerous domestic, occupational, military, and imperial titles. By the imperial period her image, heavily influenced by Greek Tyche, commonly combined a rudder, globe, and cornucopia. Late-antique Christian polemic increasingly treated her as the irrational “chance” opposed to providence. Medieval literature retained her as a female power turning a wheel, normally no longer as the recipient of a functioning Roman state cult.

### Name and etymology

**Fortūna** is Latin, written **FORTVNA** in monumental capitals. It is conventionally derived from **fors**, “chance, hap, lot,” itself traditionally connected with *ferre*, “to bring”: fortune is what circumstance “brings.” Lewis and Short record both the common noun—chance, condition, property—and the divine name. The derivation from *fors* is secure at the level of Latin word-family, although the deeper Indo-European analysis is less certain.

**Fors** could itself be personified, and the compound **Fors Fortuna** is not simply redundant in Roman usage: it named a particular cult and festival. Varro, *De lingua Latina* 6.17, connects the rites of Fors Fortuna with 24 June.

A once-influential theory derived Fortuna, or essential features of her cult, from the Etruscan **Nortia**, goddess of Volsinii. Its principal supports were Fortuna’s connection with Servius Tullius—whom Etruscan tradition may have associated with Mastarna—and a general resemblance between fate goddesses. Jacqueline Champeaux objects that *Fortuna* is transparently Latin and that no evidence demonstrates a historical transformation of Nortia into Fortuna. An Etruscan cultural environment may have influenced individual cults without making Fortuna an imported Nortia. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction; disputed]**

### Principal documented titles

- **Fortuna Primigenia** — “First-born,” “Primordial,” or “Original Fortuna.” At Praeneste, inscriptions call her **Diovo filea**, “daughter of Jupiter,” while Cicero describes an image of the infant Jupiter and Juno sitting in Fortuna’s lap and reaching for her breast. These two formulations create a genuine theological tension: Jupiter’s first-born daughter is simultaneously represented as his nurse or maternal superior. *Primigenia* therefore should not be mechanically translated as “firstborn mother.” **[Early attested; regional variant]**
- **Fortuna Praenestina** — Fortuna of Praeneste, emphasizing the city and oracle. **[Regional variant]**
- **Fors Fortuna** — cult celebrated on 24 June at temples beyond the Tiber; Ovid depicts crowds traveling by decorated boats and drinking freely. Tradition attributed at least one foundation to Servius Tullius; Plutarch alternatively associated an early Fortuna foundation with Ancus Marcius. **[Later canonical account of an archaic cult]**
- **Fortuna Muliebris** — “Women’s Fortuna.” Linked to the matrons whose embassy persuaded Coriolanus to withdraw from Rome. Livy 2.40 says a temple was built and dedicated as their memorial; Plutarch and Augustine preserve the prodigy that its statue spoke approvingly to the women. **[Later canonical legend; cult historically attested]**
- **Fortuna Virilis** — literally “Manly Fortuna” or “Fortuna of men.” Ovid, *Fasti* 4.145–150, says women offered incense so that she would conceal bodily imperfections from men. The rites occurred on 1 April beside those of Venus Verticordia; the festival name **Veneralia** is only securely documented much later. The cult’s title does not mean it was restricted to male worshippers. **[Later canonical ritual explanation]**
- **Fortuna Publica Populi Romani Quiritium** — “Public Fortuna of the Roman People, the Quirites.” Calendars locate her Quirinal cult and record observances on 25 May and other dates. **[Republican state cult]**
- **Fortuna Huiusce Diei** — “Fortuna of This Very Day,” vowed by Q. Lutatius Catulus at the Battle of Vercellae in 101 BCE and housed in the monumental temple of Area Sacra di Largo Argentina, conventionally Temple B. The title isolates the decisive fortune of a particular day. **[Historically attested victory cult]**
- **Fortuna Equestris** — “Equestrian Fortuna,” vowed by Q. Fulvius Flaccus during war in Spain in 180 BCE and dedicated in 173. Livy relates that Flaccus stripped marble roof tiles from Juno Lacinia’s temple for it, returned them after senatorial condemnation, and later died amid stories of divine retribution. **[Historically attested temple; moralizing narrative elaboration]**
- **Fortuna Redux** — “Fortuna Who Brings Back,” especially the safe return of emperors. Augustus established an altar after returning from Syria in 19 BCE; 12 October entered the calendar as the **Augustalia**. **[Imperial state cult]**
- **Fortuna Augusta / Augusti / Augustorum** — the favorable fortune of the emperor or imperial house, extremely widespread in inscriptions, military contexts, household shrines, and coinage. **[Imperial development]**
- **Fortuna Populi Romani**, **Fortuna Romana** — Rome’s collective good fortune. Plutarch’s *De Fortuna Romanorum* rhetorically debates whether Fortune or Virtue created Roman greatness. **[Republican/imperial civic theology]**
- **Fortuna Privata** — private or personal fortune, with a Palatine cult reported by Plutarch. **[Local Roman cult]**
- **Fortuna Obsequens** — “Compliant,” “Favorable,” or “Gracious Fortuna,” attributed to Servius Tullius. **[Later antiquarian attribution]**
- **Fortuna Brevis** — “Brief” or “Little Fortuna.” Plutarch asks whether the title recalled Servius’s humble origin or the brevity of fortune. **[Antiquarian evidence]**
- **Fortuna Viscata** — “Bird-liming” or “Sticky Fortuna,” imagined as catching and holding people as birdlime catches birds. **[Antiquarian evidence]**
- **Fortuna Virgo / Virginalis**, **Respiciens**, **Bonae Spei**, **Mala**, **Dubitata**, **Manens**, **Conservatrix**, **Salutaris**, **Balnearis**, **Aeterna**, and numerous occupational or local forms occur in literary or epigraphic evidence. Their proliferation reflects Roman cult practice rather than a single systematic theology.

### Equations

- **Tyche (Τύχη):** The Greek goddess of civic fortune and contingent success became Fortuna’s standard Greek equivalent during sustained Roman-Hellenistic contact. Imperial iconography—mural crown, rudder, globe, cornucopia—often makes the two visually interchangeable. This is a historical convergence, not proof that archaic Fortuna began as Tyche. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Isis-Fortuna:** From the late Republic and especially in the imperial period, Isis could carry Fortuna’s rudder and cornucopia, while Fortuna could acquire Isis’s headdress and dress. Pompeian material makes the fusion especially visible. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Nemesis-Fortuna:** Provincial and imperial dedications sometimes combine the names or attributes, particularly in arenas and military settings, where reversal, retribution, and success overlap. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Panthea:** Dedications to **Fortuna Panthea** present her as “all-divine” or as concentrating multiple divine powers. **[Imperial learned/syncretistic development]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest evidence

The bronze plaque **CIL XIV 2863**, probably first half of the third century BCE, names **Fortuna Primigenia** at Praeneste and calls her Jupiter’s daughter. This is the safest answer to “earliest secure attestation.” It establishes a named goddess, local cult, and genealogy; it does not establish the later monumental sanctuary’s date, the complete oracle procedure, or the Servian foundation legends.

The lower sanctuary at Praeneste contained earlier votive and structural phases, while the great terraced “upper sanctuary” belongs broadly to the later second century BCE. Older scholarship often pushed the entire visible complex into Sulla’s period after 82 BCE; architectural, epigraphic, and archaeological arguments have produced continuing debate over phases before and after the Sullan colony. **[Archaeologically attested; chronology disputed]**

At Rome, Sant’Omobono in the Forum Boarium preserves an archaic sanctuary beginning in the sixth century BCE. Livy 24.47 and 25.7 records temples of Fortuna and Mater Matuta there burning in 213 and being restored in 212 BCE; Ovid later attributes a Fortuna temple in the Forum Boarium to Servius Tullius. Identifying the earliest excavated cellae specifically as Fortuna and Mater Matuta remains an inference from continuity, not a contemporary inscription. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**

### Primary corpus

- **Plautus and Ennius, third–second centuries BCE:** The common noun *fortuna* and its personification already move between circumstance and divine agency. Ennius’s “*fortibus est fortuna viris data*” belongs to the cultural background of Fortuna and *virtus*, though its fragmentary transmission prevents a full cultic interpretation.
- **Fasti and inscriptions:** Republican and imperial calendars are essential evidence for 1 April, 25 May, 11 June, 24 June, and imperial observances. **CIL** volumes I and XIV contain the main Republican and Praenestine material; thousands of imperial dedications document diffusion.
- **Cicero, *De divinatione* 2.85–87, 44 BCE:** The fullest ancient account of the Praenestine lots. Cicero says Numerius Suffustius, warned repeatedly in dreams, split a rock and discovered oak lots bearing ancient characters. A child mixed and drew them. He mentions the image of Fortuna nursing Jupiter and Juno, then dismisses the oracle as chance and notes that educated contemporaries had largely lost confidence in it, while the sanctuary’s antiquity and beauty sustained its reputation. This is both valuable testimony and skeptical elite rhetoric.
- **Cicero, *De natura deorum*, 45 BCE:** Philosophical discussion distinguishes divine providence, fate, accident, and the popular attribution of unexpected outcomes to fortune. It illuminates conceptual Fortuna more than specific ritual.
- **Varro, *De lingua Latina* 6.17, 40s BCE:** Explains the name and festival of Fors Fortuna and records temples across the Tiber. Varro’s lost *Antiquitates rerum divinarum*, excerpted polemically by Augustine, catalogued multiple specialized Fortunae.
- **Livy, *Ab urbe condita*, late first century BCE:** Major notices include Fortuna Muliebris and Coriolanus (2.40), fires and restorations at the Forum Boarium (24.47; 25.7), the vow to Fortuna Primigenia in 204/203 BCE (29.36), Fortuna Equestris (40.40; 42.3), and other temples and prodigies. Livy is indispensable but often writing centuries after the alleged royal or early Republican event.
- **Virgil, *Aeneid* 10.284, 29–19 BCE:** “*Audentis Fortuna iuvat*,” “Fortune helps the daring.” This is poetic personification, not a cult scene.
- **Ovid, *Fasti* 4 and 6, c. 8 CE:** Principal poetic evidence for Fortuna Virilis/Venus on 1 April, the Forum Boarium temple on 11 June, Servius’s veiled statue, and Fors Fortuna on 24 June. Ovid explicitly offers alternative explanations, demonstrating that ritual etiologies were already plural.
- **Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities*, late first century BCE:** Preserves extended Greek versions of early Roman legends, including Coriolanus and the matrons.
- **Valerius Maximus, *Memorable Deeds and Sayings*, c. 30 CE:** Records prodigies associated with Fortuna, including the speaking statue.
- **Pliny the Elder, *Natural History*, 77 CE:** Mentions Fortuna’s extensive worship and remarks on humanity’s tendency to credit Fortune with everything; also preserves information about artworks and sanctuaries.
- **Plutarch, *Roman Questions* and *On the Fortune of the Romans*, late first/early second century CE:** The fullest catalogue of Servius’s Fortuna foundations, the sexual union of Fortuna and Servius, Fortuna Muliebris, and the rhetorical competition between Fortune and Virtue.
- **Suetonius, *Lives of the Caesars*, early second century CE:** Records emperors’ private and dynastic Fortuna cults, including Galba’s Fortuna and Domitian’s consultation of Praeneste.
- **Festus, epitome of Verrius Flaccus, second century CE:** Preserves otherwise lost antiquarian definitions, cult titles, and calendar information, though the text is fragmentary and mediated by later epitomes.
- **Apuleius, *Metamorphoses*, second century CE:** “Blind Fortune” drives the hero’s reversals until Isis provides rescue; the novel literary opposition reflects contemporary Isis-Fortuna associations without simply equating them.
- **Christian apologists:** Tertullian, Arnobius, Lactantius, and Augustine cite the multiplication of Fortuna’s titles, anthropomorphic legends, and speaking statues to attack Roman polytheism. Augustine, *City of God* 3.19, mocks Fortuna Muliebris; books 4 and 7 use Varro’s classifications. They are hostile witnesses but sometimes preserve otherwise lost antiquarian material.
- **Servius, commentary on Virgil, late fourth/early fifth century:** Preserves antiquarian explanations of Roman cults and the Servian royal tradition; individual notes may transmit much earlier sources but cannot automatically be dated to archaic Rome.
- **Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, early fifth century:** A late-antique learned compilation important for calendars, antiquarian theology, and the transition from living cult to literary inheritance.
- **Boethius, *Consolation of Philosophy* 2, c. 524:** Personified Fortune defends her right to withdraw her gifts and turns her wheel. This became the dominant medieval Fortuna.
- **Codex Buranus, Clm 4660, c. 1230:** The poems “Fortune plango vulnera” and “O Fortuna” address Fortune as mutable ruler or “empress of the world”; the manuscript’s wheel miniature depicts ascent, rule, fall, and dispossession.
- **Machiavelli, *Il Principe* 25, written 1513, printed 1532:** *Fortuna* governs perhaps half of human affairs, resembles a destructive river that prudent people channel, and is gendered as a woman whom youthful boldness violently masters. This is political allegory, not revival of ancient cult.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

Fortuna distributes or embodies uncertain outcomes: prosperity and disaster, childbirth, adulthood, sexuality, navigation, victory, civic survival, military promotion, imperial return, private livelihood, and the condition of a particular day. Her cult titles do not prove that Romans believed in one abstract goddess artificially divided into “aspects”; many communities encountered a locally bounded Fortuna with its own temple, ritual, and constituency.

At Praeneste, the essential domains were:

- civic protection;
- fertility, childbirth, and generational continuity;
- sovereignty and Jupiter’s order;
- divination through the *sortes*;
- the unpredictable bestowal of success.

### Iconography

Republican identifications can be difficult because attributes are neither fully standardized nor exclusive. Under Hellenistic and imperial influence the normal image is a mature, draped woman with:

- **cornucopia:** abundance and prosperity;
- **rudder or steering oar:** direction of human or civic affairs;
- **globe:** universal scope, instability, or Roman dominion;
- **patera:** sacrifice;
- **mural crown:** civic Tyche;
- **wings:** speed or instability, less consistently;
- **wheel:** mutability, present in ancient imagery but overwhelmingly developed in medieval art;
- **blindfold:** Fortune’s indiscrimination; especially prominent in later allegory.

A British Museum statue of c. 150–200 CE shows Fortuna with a rudder resting upon a globe and a cornucopia, a standard imperial type. Provincial figurines often require the rudder, rather than the cornucopia alone, for secure identification.

**Isis-Fortuna** may wear an Isiac headdress or knotted garment while carrying Fortuna’s rudder and cornucopia. This is not evidence that every rudder-bearing Fortuna is Isis.

### Praenestine cult image

Cicero describes infant Jupiter and Juno seated in Fortuna’s lap, Jupiter reaching for her breast. The image makes her a divine nurse or mother-like power. Inscriptions calling her Jupiter’s daughter prevent a simple, harmonized genealogy. It may reflect distinct ritual statements, successive theological layers, or a Roman misunderstanding of local imagery. **[Regional variant; interpretation disputed]**

### Wheel

The wheel was not Fortuna’s uniquely defining archaic emblem. Globes and rudders are much more characteristic of Roman imperial imagery. Boethius’s *Consolation* made the turning wheel the great Western image of reversible status: the wheel’s very nature is not to remain still. Medieval manuscripts added the conventional stages **regnabo** (“I shall reign”), **regno** (“I reign”), **regnavi** (“I have reigned”), and **sum sine regno** (“I am without a kingdom”). **[Medieval accretion based on ancient imagery]**

No ancient evidence assigns Fortuna a canonical color, number, crystal, herb, “planetary hour,” or wheel-shaped cake. Modern lists giving her green or gold colors, the number ten, cinnamon, orange, gambling dice, or fixed lunar correspondences are eclectic neopagan or occult systems. **[Modern invention unless explicitly identified as a practitioner’s modern system]**

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogical versions

1. **Daughter of Jupiter:** Praenestine inscriptions call Fortuna **Diovo filea**, “daughter of Jupiter.” **[Early attested; regional]**
2. **Nurse or maternal superior of Jupiter and Juno:** Cicero’s Praenestine image places both infants in her lap, with Jupiter nursing. **[Late Republican testimony to regional iconography]**
3. **No genealogy:** Most Roman cults treat Fortuna functionally, without parents, consort, or offspring. **[Dominant cultic pattern]**
4. **Tyche’s genealogies are not automatically Fortuna’s:** Greek literary traditions call Tyche a daughter of Oceanus and Tethys or of Zeus; those genealogies should not be transferred to Fortuna unless an author explicitly equates them. **[Syncretistic caution]**
5. **Servius Tullius as lover, favored mortal, or quasi-son:** Plutarch says Fortuna visited Servius through a window and “consorted” with him. Other traditions emphasize his fiery birth, Tanaquil’s patronage, or captive maternity instead. Servius is never securely a god in this relationship. **[Later canonical legend]**

### Every securely identifiable named divine interaction

- **Jupiter — daughter and father:** Praenestine inscriptions call Fortuna Jupiter’s daughter; *CIL XIV 2863*, third century BCE. **[Early attested regional theology]**
- **Jupiter — nursing child:** The Praenestine image described by Cicero places the infant Jupiter on Fortuna’s lap reaching for her breast; *De divinatione* 2.85–87, 44 BCE. **[Regional variant reported in a late Republican text]**
- **Juno — fellow nursing child:** Juno sits with infant Jupiter in Fortuna’s lap; Cicero, *De divinatione* 2.85, 44 BCE. **[Regional variant]**
- **Juno Lacinia — cultic conflict through human agents:** Fulvius Flaccus stripped roof tiles from Juno’s temple for Fortuna Equestris; the Senate ordered their return, and later tradition interpreted his downfall as punishment; Livy 42.3, Augustan-era history of events in 173 BCE. **[Historically grounded episode with moralizing elaboration]**
- **Mater Matuta — joint sanctuary and calendar association:** Their temples stood together in the Forum Boarium and burned together in 213 BCE; Livy 24.47 and 25.7. **[Republican cultic association]**
- **Venus Verticordia — joint 1 April observance:** Ovid places rites to Venus and Fortuna Virilis in the same ritual complex; *Fasti* 4.133–160, c. 8 CE. **[Later canonical ritual account]**
- **Tyche — explicit cross-cultural equation:** Greek and Roman literary, epigraphic, and artistic practice translates or assimilates Fortuna as Tyche, increasingly from the Hellenistic and Roman imperial periods. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Isis — fused iconographic and cultic form:** Isis-Fortuna carries cornucopia and rudder; numerous imperial statuettes, paintings, gems, and dedications, especially at Pompeii. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Nemesis — combined name or attributes:** Nemesis-Fortuna appears in imperial provincial and arena-related contexts. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Virtus — rhetorical contest over Rome’s greatness:** Plutarch stages Fortune and Virtue as rival explanations for Roman empire; *De Fortuna Romanorum*, c. 100 CE. This is philosophical personification, not a cult myth. **[Learned systematization]**
- **Spes — adjacent or overlapping functions:** Fortuna Bonae Spei and Fortuna’s association with favorable expectation connect her with Spes, but no surviving narrative describes a personal meeting. **[Cultic relation; no narrative interaction]**
- **Felicitas — functional overlap:** Imperial ideology distinguishes favorable Fortuna from Felicitas, the realized condition of success. No securely attested mythic encounter survives. **[Theological distinction; absence of interaction]**
- **Bonus Eventus — functional overlap:** Both could signify successful outcome; no surviving narrative interaction is known. **[Absence of interaction]**

There is no securely attested Roman narrative in which Fortuna fights, marries, deceives, or gives birth to another deity. Most of her divine relationships are cultic adjacency, genealogy, iconographic fusion, or learned comparison.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Fortuna and Servius Tullius

**Fullest form:** Servius rises from servile or captive origins to kingship through extraordinary favor. Fortuna visits his chamber through the **Porta Fenestella** and becomes his lover or patron. In gratitude he establishes numerous Fortuna shrines, including Primigenia and Obsequens. A statue of Servius in Fortuna’s temple is veiled; Ovid supplies alternative explanations, including Fortuna’s modesty over their liaison and the statue’s refusal to behold Servius’s murderous daughter Tullia.

**Sources:** Ovid, *Fasti* 6; Plutarch, *Roman Questions* 74 and *Fortune of the Romans* 10; later antiquarian notices.

**Development:** No contemporary sixth-century BCE text verifies the story. Archaeology confirms archaic monumental cult at the Forum Boarium, but not the narrative. The legend explains Servius’s improbable rise, Fortuna’s special attachment to socially marginal people, and the density of Roman Fortuna cults. The liaison is therefore **[later canonical royal legend]**, possibly preserving older oral or cult tradition but not independently datable.

### Fortuna Primigenia and the discovery of the lots

Cicero says Numerius Suffustius was repeatedly commanded in dreams to split a rock. The townspeople mocked him, but he obeyed; lots of oak inscribed in ancient writing emerged, and honey flowed from an olive tree from which a container was made. A child mixed the lots before one was drawn.

The story legitimates the oracle by divine dream, miraculous discovery, antiquity, and an incorruptible child. Cicero’s purpose is skeptical: he attributes the procedure to chance and reports Carneades’ joke that nowhere was Fortune more fortunate than at Praeneste. Nevertheless, the description proves that the oracle remained physically visible and culturally famous in 44 BCE. **[Later canonical foundation legend; cult and lots securely attested]**

### Fortuna Muliebris and Coriolanus

When the exiled Coriolanus leads a Volscian army against Rome, male embassies fail. Roman matrons enlist his mother Veturia and wife Volumnia; their appeal makes him withdraw. Rome rewards the women, and a temple of Fortuna Muliebris commemorates their deed. Later accounts say its image speaks twice, declaring that the matrons dedicated it according to sacred law.

Livy 2.40, Dionysius, Valerius Maximus, Plutarch, and Augustine transmit variants. The temple was a real cult site outside the city on the Via Latina; the fifth-century BCE narrative cannot be verified independently. The speaking statue is **[later prodigy tradition]**, not epigraphically attested.

### Fors Fortuna and the river festival

On 24 June Romans traveled to temples across the Tiber, on foot and in flower-decked boats, drinking and celebrating. Ovid emphasizes popular participation and associates the foundations with Servius, himself once lowly. This is not a developed divine adventure but a ritual narrative binding Fortuna to the urban plebs, summer solstice, river crossings, and social mobility. Claims that it preserved a prehistoric sacred-king sacrifice or universally involved sexual license are unsupported. **[Cult securely attested; speculative reconstructions rejected]**

### Fortuna Virilis and the Veneralia

On 1 April women washed and adorned Venus Verticordia; Ovid says they also burned incense to Fortuna Virilis so that men would not see their bodily defects. Some scholarship distinguishes two rites subsequently combined by Ovid; prostitutes’ participation is stated in late sources and often generalized beyond the evidence. The name **Veneralia** is late, and there is no documentation of “temple prostitution.” **[Ritual attested; explanations and constituency disputed]**

### Imperial Fortuna

Imperial coins and inscriptions turn Fortuna into the guardian of ruler and empire: **Fortuna Redux** returns the emperor; **Fortuna Augusta** protects the Augustan order; a ruler’s personal Fortuna becomes public security. Suetonius describes emperors consulting or honoring Fortuna, including Domitian’s annual recourse to Praeneste. These are many historical cult applications, not one mythic cycle. **[Imperial political theology]**

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Praeneste

The terraced sanctuary at modern Palestrina is Fortuna’s most monumental surviving cult center. Its principal visible phase belongs to the late second century BCE, though earlier dedications and cult activity preceded it. The complex ascended the hillside through terraces, ramps, porticoes, and exedrae toward the upper temple. Its axial concrete architecture later became a model in accounts of Roman architectural development.

The oracle’s **sortes** were inscribed wooden lots, reportedly oak. A boy drew them from a container made from the miraculously honey-bearing olive. Consultation could concern political and military affairs, but Cicero says senatorial authority once stopped a Roman consul from using it, implying tension between local divination and Roman state control. Sulla’s capture and colonization of Praeneste in 82 BCE transformed the city; whether he constructed, completed, or merely restored major portions remains debated.

The sanctuary survived into the imperial period. Domitian reportedly consulted it annually. Imperial dedications show continuing civic and dynastic importance. Late antique decline is clear in broad outline, but no source records a single definitive Christian “suppression of Fortuna” at Praeneste. Medieval and early-modern building over the terraces altered the complex; wartime destruction in 1944 exposed remains and enabled systematic excavation.

### Rome

- **Forum Boarium:** Joint sacred area of Fortuna and Mater Matuta; archaic phases, rebuilt after the fire of 213 BCE. Later tradition attributed Fortuna’s temple to Servius.
- **Across the Tiber:** Temples of Fors Fortuna associated with 24 June.
- **Via Latina:** Fortuna Muliebris, beyond the city boundary.
- **Quirinal:** Temples of Fortuna Publica and related titles; calendar evidence records several anniversaries.
- **Capitoline:** A Fortuna Primigenia shrine attributed to Servius in Plutarch.
- **Largo Argentina, Temple B:** Widely identified as Fortuna Huiusce Diei, dedicated after 101 BCE.
- **Fortuna Equestris:** Dedicated in 173 BCE, later disappeared; its precise location remains disputed.
- **Altar of Fortuna Redux:** Established for Augustus’s return in 19 BCE near the Porta Capena.

### Officials and worshippers

Evidence names priests, priestesses, **magistri**, **ministrae**, municipal officials, collegia, soldiers, slaves, freedpeople, merchants, women, and imperial personnel. There was no single universal “High Priestess of Fortuna.” Offices belonged to particular sanctuaries or associations.

Offerings included incense, wine, animal sacrifice, statues, altars, coins, garments, jewelry, lamps, and fulfillment of vows. The Praenestine evidence has often been connected with childbirth and family continuity. Imperial military altars commonly thank Fortuna for preservation or success.

### Contested practices

- **Human sacrifice:** No reliable evidence associates Fortuna’s regular cult with human sacrifice. Theories connecting the wheel, Servius, or Fors Fortuna with a slain sacred king are comparativist reconstructions, not ancient testimony. **[Unestablished]**
- **Temple prostitution:** No reliable evidence documents temple prostitution in any Fortuna sanctuary. Associating Fortuna Virilis with women’s sexuality or prostitutes does not establish institutional sacred prostitution. **[Unestablished]**
- **Cult violence:** The destruction associated with Sulla’s sack of Praeneste was political and military, not a sacrificial rite of Fortuna.
- **Suppression:** Christian emperors’ general restrictions on sacrifice and temple revenues affected Fortuna’s cults, but a Fortuna-specific empire-wide suppression edict is not known.
- **Oracle fraud:** Cicero ridicules the mechanism but does not document priestly fraud. Skepticism is evidence of controversy, not proof of fabrication.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Archaic period, sixth–fifth centuries BCE

Later Roman tradition located Fortuna’s Roman beginnings under Ancus Marcius or, overwhelmingly, Servius Tullius. Sant’Omobono proves archaic cultic activity at the place later identified with Fortuna and Mater Matuta, but the named attribution remains inferential. The Servian connection may encode the goddess’s relationship to kingship, outsiders, social advancement, and the Forum Boarium’s commercial communities.

### Early and middle Republic, fourth–third centuries BCE

Fortuna Muliebris was retrojected to the Coriolanus crisis around 488 BCE. Secure epigraphy appears at Praeneste in the early third century BCE. Roman warfare and political expansion generated victory cults and intensified state interest in Fortuna.

### Later Republic, second–first centuries BCE

This was the period of greatest visible monumentalization and diversification: Fortuna Primigenia’s sanctuary, Fortuna Equestris, Fortuna Huiusce Diei, public Quirinal cults, and innumerable vows. Hellenistic Tyche iconography increasingly shaped her representation. Sulla’s special claims to felicity and his capture of Praeneste tied personal political success to the city’s goddess, although the sanctuary should not simply be labeled “Sulla’s temple.”

Cicero’s simultaneous testimony to the oracle’s fame and elite skepticism shows that cultic prestige did not require philosophical assent.

### Early Empire

Augustus integrated Fortuna into imperial return, stability, and dynastic ideology. Coins circulated rudder-and-cornucopia imagery throughout the empire. Emperors possessed or cultivated personal Fortunae; dedications to Fortuna Augusta and Redux multiplied. Provincial soldiers and civilians localized her under new titles.

### High Empire

Fortuna became one of the empire’s most portable divine images. Her worship spread from Britain to North Africa and the Danube. Tyche, Isis, Nemesis, and local civic protectresses could fuse with her. This was not the disappearance of cult into allegory: altars, statuettes, vows, and coin types show active worship.

### Late Antiquity

Christian apologists converted Fortuna into evidence against polytheism: if she is blind chance, she cannot be a provident deity; if she is a goddess, why distinguish her from Felicitas or multiply her by sex and social group? Meanwhile, pagan antiquarians preserved titles and traditions. Imperial laws against sacrifice, confiscation or closure of temples, and Christianization weakened her institutions between the fourth and sixth centuries.

No continuous institutional line connects an ancient Fortuna priesthood with medieval or modern worship.

### Medieval transformation

Fortuna survived chiefly as literary allegory. Boethius subordinated her apparent randomness to divine providence: her wheel strips away false worldly goods and can teach detachment. Manuscript art made her a crowned woman turning rulers upward and downward. Christian authors could use her without accepting an independent pagan goddess.

### Renaissance and early modernity

Humanists recovered classical Fortuna beside Boethian Fortune. Emblem books paired her with sails, rudders, globes, and unstable footing. Machiavelli made her a limit on political control: impetuous *virtù* may master circumstances, but never abolish contingency.

### Modernity

Fortuna persists in art, commercial language, gambling imagery, political theory, opera and concert music, tarot, and revived Roman polytheism. Modern worship is discontinuous revival based on texts, archaeology, and contemporary religious creativity.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Carmina Burana

The **Codex Buranus**, produced around 1230, contains over two hundred Latin and Middle High German pieces. The Fortune poems and wheel image belong to medieval moral and satirical culture, not a survival of Roman ritual. The manuscript was found at Benediktbeuern in 1803 and first published under the title *Carmina Burana* in 1847.

Carl Orff’s cantata *Carmina Burana*, premiered in 1937, opens and closes with “O Fortuna.” Orff’s massive orchestral and choral setting, not the medieval melody, created the modern cinematic image of Fortuna as overwhelming doom. Using Orff’s piece as evidence for ancient Roman belief is therefore an anachronism. **[Modern artistic reinvention]**

### Tarot and occultism

The tarot trump “Wheel of Fortune” descends from medieval Fortune imagery. Fifteenth-century cards already show figures rising and falling on a wheel. Nineteenth-century occultists, especially Éliphas Lévi and the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, added astrological, Hebrew, alchemical, Egyptian, and Qabalistic correspondences. The Golden Dawn assigned the card to **Kaph** and Jupiter. Pamela Colman Smith’s image for Arthur Edward Waite’s 1909 deck added a sphinx, serpent, Anubis-like figure, Hebrew letters, and alchemical symbols.

These correspondences are not ancient Roman teachings about Fortuna. Claims that Roman priests used tarot, assigned Fortuna to the tenth Major Arcana, or invoked her through Golden Dawn Hebrew formulae are **[modern inventions]**.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist worship

Contemporary Roman polytheists celebrate 1 April or 24 June, make offerings, and reconstruct prayers from Roman ritual formulas. The revival is documented but decentralized, with no reliable global membership count. Where worshippers use ancient dates and epithets, those components are reconstructed; personalized correspondences, tarot-based identification, “deity reaching out” narratives, and crystal lists belong to modern devotional or occult practice.

### Politics and psychology

Machiavellian *fortuna* remains central to political theory as the realm of contingency opposed or answered by *virtù*. Modern psychological readings treat Fortune’s wheel as an archetype of change, inflation, loss, or individuation. These are twentieth-century interpretive systems, not ancient cult doctrine.

There is no substantial documented modern extremist movement centered specifically upon Fortuna. Roman imagery can appear in nationalist classicism, but generalized use of Roman symbols should not be mislabeled as Fortuna worship.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Major scholarship

- **Georg Wissowa, *Religion und Kultus der Römer*:** Classic institutional reconstruction of Roman cult and calendars; foundational but inclined to impose systematic categories.
- **Hendrik Wagenvoort and Kurt Latte:** Developed functional and historical interpretations of Roman religion.
- **Georges Dumézil, *La religion romaine archaïque* / *Archaic Roman Religion*:** Connected Fortuna to Indo-European structures, sovereignty, and archaic Roman institutions. Influential, but structural reconstructions often exceed direct evidence.
- **Jacqueline Champeaux, *Fortuna: Le culte de la Fortune à Rome et dans le monde romain*:** The fullest modern monograph; emphasizes Fortuna’s Latin character, cult diversity, and historical development while rejecting easy derivation from Etruscan Nortia.
- **John Scheid:** Reframes Roman gods through ritual, civic institutions, and historically situated practice rather than timeless mythology.
- **Mary Beard, John North, Simon Price, *Religions of Rome*:** Integrates literary, epigraphic, political, and archaeological evidence and cautions against treating Roman religion as a fixed theological system.
- **LIMC:** Principal iconographic catalogue for Fortuna/Tyche types.
- **Brill’s New Pauly:** Concise philological, cultic, and topographical synthesis.
- **Daniele Miano, *Fortuna: Deity and Concept in Archaic and Republican Italy*:** Reassesses the relationship between goddess, concept, political agency, and competing local traditions.

### Central disputes

1. **Latin or Etruscan origin?** The Latin etymology and diverse Latial cults favor indigenous Latin development; Etruscan influence remains possible but Nortia-to-Fortuna descent is unproved.
2. **Meaning of Primigenia:** “First-born” fits inscriptions calling her Jupiter’s daughter; “primordial” better accommodates her nursing Jupiter. No consensus dissolves the contradiction.
3. **Age of Praeneste:** The cult predates the monumental terraces. Dating individual architectural phases—pre-Sullan, Sullan, or restored after Sulla—remains contested.
4. **Mother goddess or chance goddess?** Fertility, childhood, sovereignty, oracle, and contingency all have evidence. Reducing Fortuna to either an original mother goddess or pure abstract chance suppresses historical plurality.
5. **Servius as history or cult legend?** His connection with Fortuna is ancient literary tradition but not contemporary documentation.
6. **Plebeian goddess?** Fors Fortuna’s popular festival and Servius’s low origin support special associations with common people, yet elite magistrates, generals, emperors, and the Senate also cultivated Fortuna. “Plebeian goddess” is therefore incomplete.
7. **Wheel’s antiquity:** Wheels and globes occur in ancient imagery, but the canonical *rota Fortunae* is chiefly a Boethian and medieval elaboration.
8. **Christian “destruction”:** Institutional cult declined under Christian rule, but the evidence does not support a single coordinated destruction of every Fortuna sanctuary or a precise last pagan ceremony.
9. **Human sacrifice and sacred kingship:** These theories lack direct evidence for Fortuna and should be labeled speculative or discredited.
10. **Continuity into modern witchcraft:** No documentary institutional continuity exists; present practice is revival and reinvention.

## 11. Sources

### Primary sources and corpora

The indispensable primary record comprises **CIL I and XIV**, Roman fasti, Cicero’s *De divinatione* and *De natura deorum*, Varro’s *De lingua Latina*, Livy, Ovid’s *Fasti*, Dionysius of Halicarnassus, Valerius Maximus, Pliny, Plutarch, Suetonius, Festus, Apuleius, Augustine, Arnobius, Servius, and Macrobius. For the medieval and early-modern transformations, the core texts are Boethius’s *Consolation*, Codex Buranus Clm 4660, and Machiavelli’s *Prince* 25.

### Evidentiary conclusions

- Fortuna is securely a named Latin goddess by the early third century BCE.
- Her cult almost certainly has earlier roots, but claims about its sixth-century form depend on archaeological inference and much later texts.
- There was never one completely unified Fortuna theology.
- Praenestine Fortuna’s genealogy is internally contradictory in the surviving evidence and must remain so.
- The oracle, lots, state temples, festivals, imperial cult, and wide provincial diffusion are securely documented.
- Human sacrifice and temple prostitution are not documented features of her cult.
- The canonical wheel is predominantly medieval.
- Tarot, Golden Dawn correspondences, crystal lists, and most contemporary “ancient Fortuna rituals” are modern constructions.
- Modern worship represents revival, not demonstrable continuity.
