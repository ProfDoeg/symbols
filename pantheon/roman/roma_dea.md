# Dea Roma: Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess (*dea*, Greek *thea*), civic tutelary deity, and personification of the city, people, state, and eventually empire of Rome.

**Earliest secure attestation:** A temple to the personified city of Rome at Smyrna in **195 BCE**, dated by Tacitus, *Annals* 4.56, to the consulship of Marcus Porcius Cato and Lucius Valerius Flaccus. Tacitus wrote about three centuries later, but his precise consular date and Smyrna’s appeal to an institutional precedent make this the earliest securely dated cult. Republican coins offer earlier possible images, but their identification as the goddess Roma is disputed.

**Evidentiary synopsis:** Roma was not an ordinary mythological personality with a birth, genealogy, and narrative cycle. She emerged from the visual and verbal personification of Rome. Her earliest securely documented worship arose not in Rome but in the Greek East, where cities already possessed a developed language of divine city-personifications, ruler cult, festivals, and interstate honor. Under Augustus she became the regular divine partner of the emperor in provincial imperial cult. Hadrian brought her first monumental state temple in Rome itself, pairing **Roma Aeterna** with **Venus Felix**. Coins, reliefs, inscriptions, temples, festivals, priesthoods, panegyric, and political theology—not narrative myth—constitute her principal record.

## Evidentiary labels

- **Securely attested early material:** contemporary or closely datable Republican evidence, or an ancient report containing a precise institutional date.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** imperial forms that became standard throughout the empire.
- **Regional variant:** a locally configured cult, title, image, or ritual.
- **Learned systematization:** antiquarian, poetic, theological, or apologetic construction rather than direct cult evidence.
- **Syncretistic identification:** an explicit or functional combination with another divine figure.
- **Medieval or early-modern accretion:** post-antique reinterpretation without evidence of continuous ancient cult.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an interpretation produced from incomplete evidence.
- **Modern invention:** a recent claim or practice represented as ancient without adequate ancient documentation.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

**Securely attested early material:** Roma is the divine personification of *Roma*, first the city and Roman people and increasingly the Roman state and its dominion. Greek communities treated her as **Θεὰ Ῥώμη, Thea Rhōmē**, “the goddess Rome.” Her gender followed the grammatical gender of Latin *Roma* and Greek *Rhōmē*, both feminine, while her martial appearance drew upon Athena, Amazon, and armed city-*Tyche* iconography.

Two conceptions must be distinguished:

1. **Roma as the visibly named city-goddess**, securely attested in the Greek East from 195 BCE.
2. **The secret tutelary deity or secret name of Rome**, mentioned by late antiquarians and Christian polemicists.

The second was not securely identified with Roma. Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 3.9, and Servius on *Aeneid* 2.351 preserve traditions that Rome concealed the name of its protecting deity so enemies could not ritually “call it out” by *evocatio*. Pliny, *Natural History* 28.18, reports that divulging Rome’s secret name was forbidden. Late sources variously associate this mystery with Angerona, Ops Consivia, or other powers. **Learned systematization:** treating the publicly named Dea Roma as that deliberately unnamed tutelary deity collapses two different bodies of evidence.

### Names and forms

- **Latin:** *Roma*; *dea Roma*, “the goddess Roma”; dative dedication *deae Romae*, “to the goddess Roma.”
- **Greek:** Ῥώμη (*Rhṓmē*); Θεὰ Ῥώμη (*Theà Rhṓmē*).
- **Roma Aeterna / Romae Aeternae:** “Eternal Roma” or “to Eternal Roma.” This became especially important under Hadrian and in later imperial coinage.
- **Roma Victrix:** “Victorious Roma,” primarily an ideological or numismatic formulation rather than evidence for a wholly separate goddess.
- **Urbs Roma:** “the city Rome,” a late-imperial coin legend paired with the personified city.
- **Roma Renascens / Renovatio Urbis Romae:** formulations of Rome renewed or reborn; these express political programs and should not automatically be treated as independent cult titles.
- **Communis Asiae Roma et Augustus / Θεὰ Ῥώμη καὶ Θεὸς Σεβαστός:** “Roma and Augustus of the commonwealth of Asia” and “to the goddess Roma and the divine Augustus,” attested in provincial coinage and imperial-cult contexts.

### Etymology

The goddess’s name is the city’s name; she has no independent divine etymology.

The etymology of *Roma* itself remains disputed:

- Ancient Greek and Roman writers proposed derivations from Greek ῥώμη, “strength,” or from legendary persons named Rhōmē.
- Other ancient stories derived the name from Romulus or made a Trojan woman Roma burn the refugees’ ships, forcing settlement.
- Modern proposals have included an Etruscan or Italic place-name, association with the *Rumon* or Tiber, and derivation from an Etruscan clan name such as *Ruma*. None commands universal acceptance.

Melinno’s surviving Greek hymn exploits the resonance between **Ῥώμη**, Rome, and **ῥώμη**, strength. Stobaeus may even have preserved the hymn under a heading concerning courage because he read the opening word as “strength.” This is wordplay, not proof that the city’s name originated in Greek.

### Classification shifts

- **Third–second centuries BCE:** possible numismatic personification; secure goddess in Greek civic cult by 195 BCE.
- **Late Republic:** divine recipient of eastern festivals and honors; poetic embodiment of Roman power.
- **Augustan and high imperial periods:** goddess and official partner in provincial ruler cult.
- **Hadrianic Rome:** state goddess in Rome itself, specifically **Roma Aeterna**.
- **Late antiquity:** goddess, imperial emblem, literary speaker, and increasingly secularizable personification.
- **Medieval to modern periods:** civic allegory and national symbol; no demonstrated continuous institutional pagan cult.
- **Modern reconstructionism:** again addressed as a goddess, but this is a revival based on ancient evidence, not an unbroken succession.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Smyrna, 195 BCE

Tacitus, *Annals* 4.55–56, recounts the competition in 26 CE among Asian cities for permission to build a provincial temple to Tiberius, Livia, and the Senate. Smyrna’s delegates argued that their city had been **the first to build a temple to the city of Rome**, “in the consulship of Marcus Porcius,” while Carthage still stood and Asian kings remained powerful. The consular synchronism yields **195 BCE**.

What this establishes:

- Smyrna maintained a cult and temple directed to Rome as a divine civic entity.
- The foundation belonged to the political setting of Rome’s eastern wars and alliances.
- By 26 CE it was a prestigious and remembered institutional precedent.

What it does not establish:

- The temple’s plan, cult statue, priestly rules, or complete ritual calendar.
- That Romans in Rome already worshipped an anthropomorphic goddess Roma.
- That 195 BCE was the first artistic personification of Rome anywhere.

### Earlier coins: probable image, uncertain deity

**Modern scholarly reconstruction:** Helmeted female heads occur on Romano-Campanian silver and Republican coinage from the late fourth or third century BCE onward. Some coins bear the legend **ROMA**, but a legend can name the issuing state rather than the portrait. Proposed identifications include Roma, Minerva, Bellona, an Amazon, or an anonymous martial genius of the Roman people.

The evidence becomes progressively stronger as the helmeted woman acquires a stable repertoire—Attic helmet, spear, shield, trophy, Victory, globe—and appears in contexts explicitly naming Roma. Accordingly, “Roma appears on Roman coins before 195 BCE” is plausible; “a cult image of the goddess Roma is securely demonstrated before 195 BCE” is not.

### Melinno’s *Hymn to Rome*

A five-stanza Greek ode attributed to **Melinno** survives only in the fifth-century CE anthology of Stobaeus. It addresses Rome as “daughter of Ares,” enthroned on Olympus, clothed in martial splendor, and granted unbroken sovereignty by Fate.

Its date is contested:

- C. M. Bowra and many later scholars placed it in the first half of the second century BCE, near the beginnings of Roma’s eastern cult.
- Hugh Lloyd-Jones argued that its language and literary manner could fit the second century CE.

**Status:** The text is unquestionably an ancient hymn to personified Rome, but it cannot safely provide second-century-BCE evidence unless the early dating is expressly qualified. Its “daughter of Ares” formula is poetic genealogy, not evidence for a widely accepted cultic family tree.

### Augustan and imperial texts

- **Virgil, *Aeneid* (29–19 BCE):** Rome is the destined imperial community and power, particularly in Jupiter’s prophecy in Book 1 and Anchises’ pageant in Book 6. Roma rarely behaves as a separately worshipped character; the poem supplies the theological environment in which city, people, empire, and divine destiny converge.
- **Horace, *Odes* and *Carmen Saeculare* (late first century BCE):** celebrates Rome’s divinely favored permanence without providing a narrative mythology of Dea Roma.
- **Ovid, *Fasti* (c. 8 CE) and *Metamorphoses* (c. 8 CE):** supplies Roman foundation, apotheosis, and festival myths, but no developed independent cycle for Roma. Later readers frequently convert Ovid’s Rome into the goddess more readily than the text warrants.
- **Livy, *Ab Urbe Condita* (late first century BCE):** foundational history and divine protection of the city; not a primary dossier of Roma’s cult. His narrative of *evocatio* at Veii concerns Juno Regina, demonstrating the ritual idea behind later speculation about Rome’s secret protector.
- **Cicero, *De natura deorum* (45 BCE):** important for Roman theories of divinity and the deification of abstractions and benefits, but it does not furnish a sustained mythology of Roma.
- **Varro, *Antiquitates rerum divinarum* (first century BCE), surviving mainly through Augustine:** crucial for Roman theological classification but offers no securely recoverable early cult narrative for Dea Roma.
- **Festus, Macrobius, and Servius:** preserve antiquarian material about Rome’s names, guardian deity, *evocatio*, and ritual secrecy. These are late and learned witnesses, not evidence that Roma possessed an archaic genealogy.
- **Cassius Dio, *Roman History* 51.20:** reports that in 29 BCE Octavian permitted temples in Asia and Bithynia to **Roma and himself** for Greeks, while Roman citizens were to honor Roma and Divus Julius. This is central evidence for the political regulation of the imperial cult.
- **Suetonius, *Augustus* 52:** says Augustus would not accept temples in the provinces unless his name was joined with Roma’s. The rule kept living-emperor worship within a civic and state framework.
- **Tacitus, *Annals* 4.37–38, 4.55–56:** records Tiberius’ restrictive posture toward divine honors and the Asian temple competition, including Smyrna’s claim.
- **Cassius Dio 69.4 and the *Historia Augusta*, *Hadrian* 19:** principal literary witnesses for Hadrian’s building project and the relocation of Nero’s Colossus. Dio’s anecdote that Apollodorus mocked the seated cult statues because they could not stand without striking the ceiling is vivid but belongs to hostile biographical tradition, not an architectural report.
- **Symmachus, *Relatio* 3 (384 CE):** makes aged Rome speak in defense of ancestral rites during the controversy over the Altar of Victory.
- **Ambrose, *Epistle* 18 (384 CE):** answers Symmachus by appropriating Rome’s voice for Christianity.
- **Prudentius, *Contra Symmachum* (c. 402–403):** converts Roma into a Christianized literary speaker who renounces the old gods.
- **Claudian (late fourth–early fifth century):** repeatedly presents Roma as *dea*, *diva*, or *numen*, armed and participating in the divine council. This is literary panegyric produced for a substantially Christian imperial court; whether an audience perceived cult, allegory, or both cannot be settled uniformly.

### Christian apocalyptic reception

A widespread scholarly interpretation identifies the woman seated on seven hills in **Revelation 17–18**—“Babylon the Great”—as a polemical inversion of Rome and imperial ideology. Provincial viewers in Asia knew Roma as a crowned or enthroned female city-deity. Revelation never names her “Dea Roma,” however. The equation is a strong historical interpretation, not an explicit identification in the text.

### Sources named in the brief but yielding negative results

Arnobius, Augustine, and other apologists are useful hostile witnesses to Roman personification, emperor worship, and theological abstraction. They do not preserve an independent narrative corpus for Roma comparable to those of Venus, Mars, or Cybele. The same is true of the requested Etruscan mirrors: they illuminate Italic adoption of Greek divine iconography, but no securely identified Etruscan prototype of Dea Roma has been established.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

Roma governs or embodies:

- the physical city of Rome;
- the Roman people and state;
- Roman sovereignty and military victory;
- the unity and authority of the empire;
- the endurance or eternity of Rome;
- in provincial cult, loyalty between a community and imperial government.

These are not static. The earliest eastern cult honored Rome as a foreign hegemonic community. Augustan cult joined that power to an emperor. Hadrianic cult relocated Roma’s eternity to the capital itself. Late-antique authors could detach her from sacrifice and retain her as a political or literary emblem.

### Standard visual type

Roma is usually a mature, idealized, martial woman:

- crested Attic or Corinthian helmet;
- long chiton or military dress, sometimes with one breast exposed in an Amazonian convention;
- spear or scepter;
- round shield;
- sword or parazonium;
- trophy or captured arms;
- globe, expressing world rule;
- small Victory, often held in her hand;
- Palladium, asserting Trojan continuity and possession of Rome’s sacred guarantee;
- seated enthroned, or standing and crowning an emperor.

No single attribute proves identity. Minerva, Bellona, Virtus, and armed city-*Tychai* share elements. A legend, architectural context, or associated figure is often necessary.

### Republican coinage

**Disputed early material:** Helmeted female heads paired with **ROMA** appear widely on Republican issues. The legend may label the minting authority, but repeated usage encouraged the later reading of the head as Roma. On denarii she can occupy the obverse while the reverse narrates military victory, binding the issuing republic to an armed female face.

### Ara Pacis

A seated armed woman on the **Ara Pacis Augustae**, dedicated in 9 BCE, is commonly identified as Roma, resting upon captured weapons. The identification is strong because of the arms and Augustan program, but the monument does not label her. Some scholarship consequently retains “Roma” with a question mark.

### Gemma Augustea

The **Gemma Augustea**, normally dated about 9–12 CE and now in Vienna’s Kunsthistorisches Museum, provides one of Roma’s most influential images. In the upper register, helmeted Roma sits beside the enthroned Augustus, while surrounding cosmic and terrestrial figures frame Roman victory; below, Roman soldiers raise a trophy over captives.

**Later canonical elaboration:** Roma’s physical equality beside Augustus presents emperor and imperial city as mutually validating powers. The museum describes Augustus as enthroned beside, and effectively equal to, Roma. Identification of Roma’s facial features specifically with Livia is possible but disputed; it should not be reported as fact.

### Hadrianic cult statue and Roma Aeterna

Coins associated with Hadrian’s temple show Roma enthroned with spear, shield, Palladium, globe, or celestial emblems. A rare Hadrianic **ROMAE AETERNAE** type presents her with solar and lunar imagery: Rome’s duration is made coextensive with cosmic time.

Under Antoninus Pius, coins depict helmeted Roma seated with the Palladium and spear. Later emperors reused her image during anniversaries, civil wars, restorations, and military crises.

### Late-antique transformation

Fourth-century coinage paired **VRBS ROMA** with **CONSTANTINOPOLIS**. Roma retained a helmeted martial identity; Constantinopolis appeared as the Tyche of the new capital, often with prow and scepter. They are counterparts, not evidence that one goddess literally gave birth to or transformed into the other.

### Unattested “sacred correspondences”

No ancient evidence securely assigns Roma a fixed sacred animal, plant, color, weekday, planet, or mystical number. Wolves, eagles, horses, and woodpeckers belong primarily to Roman foundation mythology or other gods; purple, red, and gold express imperial status but are not documented as Roma’s exclusive sacred colors. Modern correspondence tables giving her crystals, zodiacal rulers, or magical seals are **modern inventions** unless individually sourced to ancient evidence.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

Roma has no stable cult genealogy.

- **Melinno, date disputed:** calls her “daughter of Ares.” This is the only prominent ancient parental formula and is poetic.
- **Roman visual theology:** commonly associates her with Mars without calling him her father.
- **No ancient source securely names a mother, consort, siblings, or offspring.**
- **Modern claim that Minerva is Roma’s mother or that Roma is simply Minerva under another name:** unsupported.
- **Modern claim that Romulus fathered Roma:** reverses the normal relation between founder and personified city and is unattested.

### Syncretistic and functional relations

Roma borrows iconography from Athena/Minerva, Amazons, Virtus, and city-*Tychai*, but iconographic borrowing is not necessarily theological identity. Her pairing with Venus under Hadrian was architectural and ideological, not fusion into a single goddess.

### Documented god–god or god–divinized-person interactions

Because Roma lacks narrative mythology, the list is short and primarily visual or cultic.

- **Ares — poetic paternity:** Roma is called Ares’ daughter. *Melinno, Hymn to Rome*, date disputed between the second century BCE and second century CE. **Tier: disputed ancient poetic elaboration.**
- **Augustus — joint cult:** Augustus authorizes temples in Asia and Bithynia that join his worship to Roma; Suetonius says he refused provincial temples unless paired with her. Cassius Dio 51.20; Suetonius, *Augustus* 52, describing arrangements beginning in 29 BCE. **Tier: securely attested imperial institution.**
- **Divus Julius — parallel authorized cult:** Roman citizens in eastern provinces were permitted to honor Roma and the deified Julius rather than the living Octavian. Cassius Dio 51.20, concerning 29 BCE. **Tier: securely attested imperial institution.**
- **Augustus — enthroned companionship:** Roma sits beside the Jupiter-like Augustus on the *Gemma Augustea*, c. 9–12 CE. **Tier: securely attested imperial image.**
- **Augustus — coronation:** provincial temple coin types show Roma crowning Augustus while holding a cornucopia. Claudian, Flavian, Nerva, and Trajanic cistophori preserve the composition; RPC I 2221, RPC II 859/875, RPC III 1303/1320/1717. **Tier: later canonical provincial iconography.**
- **Victory/Victoria — attribute and subordinate figure:** Roma holds or receives a small Victory on imperial coins and reliefs. **Tier: canonical imperial iconography; not a surviving narrative episode.**
- **Venus Felix — paired residence:** the goddesses occupy opposed back-to-back cellae in Hadrian’s Temple of Venus and Roma. Construction began in the 120s CE; dedication probably occurred on 21 April 121 or 135/136, with chronology disputed. **Tier: securely attested Hadrianic state cult.**
- **Pallas/Minerva — possession of the Palladium:** Roma holds the Palladium in Antonine and later images, asserting Roman custody of the Trojan talisman associated with Pallas. No surviving myth tells how Roma personally acquired it. **Tier: later canonical iconographic theology.**
- **Sol and Luna — cosmic duration:** Hadrianic Roma Aeterna imagery associates Roma with solar and lunar symbols. **Tier: Hadrianic learned/cosmic elaboration.**
- **Romulus — co-representation:** Severan and later coins place Roma and Romulus within programs of foundation and renewal. No source records a conversation or family relation. **Tier: late imperial commemorative iconography.**
- **Constantinopolis — paired capital-personifications:** fourth-century coins and art juxtapose Roma and Constantinopolis as old and new imperial capitals. **Tier: late-antique political personification.**
- **Jupiter — shared council or authority:** Claudian brings Roma into the literary divine world ruled by Jupiter; the *Gemma Augustea* gives Augustus Jupiter’s pose beside Roma. **Tier: late literary elaboration and Augustan visual analogy, not an early cult myth.**

**Absence of evidence:** No securely attested combat, seduction, marriage, theft, punishment, or rescue narrative joins Roma to another deity.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Roma has no major narrative cycle comparable to the *Aeneid*’s stories of Aeneas or Ovid’s transformations. Her “myths” are political-historical narratives expressed through personification.

### Roma as daughter of Ares and sovereign of the world

Melinno’s hymn pictures Roma as an Olympian martial goddess to whom Fate granted lasting, unshaken rule. She controls nations with strong reins and remains undefeated.

- **Earliest source:** Melinno, preserved by Stobaeus.
- **Dating:** disputed, second century BCE to second century CE.
- **Function:** encomium and perhaps cult hymn; it translates Roman hegemony into Greek divine and poetic language.
- **Development:** later imperial images similarly combine martial force, world rule, and eternity, but this does not prove direct dependence.

### Rome’s Trojan and divine destiny

Virgil’s *Aeneid* makes Rome the future terminus of Trojan suffering and divine providence. Jupiter promises an empire without limit; Anchises reveals Roman rulers and Rome’s governing mission.

Roma is not consistently a speaking goddess in the epic. The “myth of Roma” lies in later reception’s conversion of the destined city into a female divine body. Images of Roma holding the Palladium visually compress Aeneas, Troy, Vesta, and Roman eternity into one figure.

### Roma Aeterna

“Eternal Rome” is a political-theological myth rather than an episode. Its components include:

- claims that Roman rule has no temporal boundary;
- cosmic symbols of sun, moon, stars, globe, and phoenix;
- renewed foundation imagery—Romulus, wolf and twins, Palladium;
- imperial restoration after crisis.

Hadrian formalized the divine title in connection with the temple on the Velia. Antonine and third-century emperors reused it. Philip I’s millennial celebrations in 248 CE, marking Rome’s traditional thousandth year, made the city’s continued life a state spectacle. Gallienus, Aurelian, Maxentius, and Constantine employed foundation or renewal imagery in different political circumstances.

### The aged Roma who changes religion

In 384 CE, Symmachus personified Rome as an aged woman asking that ancestral rites be preserved: her longevity, she argues, validates her religious customs. Ambrose replied by giving Rome a contrary lesson—no age is too late to learn—and Prudentius subsequently made her embrace Christianity.

This is a genuine late-antique contest over Roma’s identity:

- **Symmachus:** Rome’s ancient success supports traditional cult.
- **Ambrose and Prudentius:** Rome’s historical destiny culminates in Christian conversion.
- **Claudian:** Roma remains a classical divine speaker within Christian imperial culture.

It is improper to harmonize these into a single conversion myth. They are rival rhetorical appropriations.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Smyrna

The temple founded in 195 BCE is the earliest secure cult center. Its establishment probably expressed alliance with Rome during conflict involving Antiochus III, but Tacitus does not state the immediate occasion. No complete architectural reconstruction or ritual manual survives.

### Greek festivals and civic worship

Greek cities celebrated **Rhomaia**, festivals for Roma that could include sacrifices, processions, musical and athletic contests, and dating by eponymous priests. Their exact content varied by city. Epigraphic evidence for priests and contests is more secure than generalized claims about a uniform “cult of Roma.”

The cult operated through Greek civic institutions: councils, assemblies, leagues, benefactors, priests, and agonistic festivals. It was not simply a Roman state religion exported intact. Communities used familiar Hellenistic mechanisms for honoring a powerful external benefactor.

### Pergamum and Nicomedia

Cassius Dio reports that Octavian authorized:

- a temple at Pergamum for the province of Asia;
- a temple at Nicomedia for Bithynia;
- Greek worship of Roma and Augustus;
- Roman-citizen worship of Roma and Divus Julius.

These foundations connected provincial assemblies to the emerging imperial cult. Priesthood of Roma and Augustus became a prestigious office through which provincial elites competed for status.

### Athens

The small circular Temple of Roma and Augustus stood east of the Parthenon on the Acropolis and was probably erected after 27 BCE. Its position beside Athena’s great sanctuary visually inserted Roman power into Athens’ sacred topography. It is provincial honor expressed in a Greek architectural and religious setting, not evidence that Athena and Roma were formally fused.

### Ancyra

The Temple of Roma and Augustus at Ancyra was built after Galatia became a province, conventionally around 25–20 BCE. It later received the best-preserved copy of Augustus’ *Res Gestae*, in Latin and Greek. The building and inscription show how the cult furnished a physical archive of imperial self-presentation.

### Lugdunum

The federal altar at Lugdunum, inaugurated in 12 BCE, was dedicated to **Roma and Augustus** and served the three Gallic provinces. Provincial delegates met there; its priesthood was a pinnacle of elite Gallic advancement. The sanctuary illustrates how loyalty, competition, sacrifice, and administration interlocked.

### Temple of Venus and Roma, Rome

Hadrian constructed the largest known temple in Rome on the Velia, between the Forum and Colosseum. Two back-to-back cellae housed:

- **Venus Felix**, facing the Colosseum;
- **Roma Aeterna**, facing the Forum.

The arrangement joined Rome’s divine ancestry through Venus to the eternity of the imperial capital. Its Greek-looking peripteral exterior and paired cellae also advertised Hadrian’s philhellenism.

Chronology is disputed:

- A traditional reconstruction places inauguration or consecration on **21 April 121 CE**, Rome’s birthday.
- Other scholarship favors a dedication in **135 or 136 CE**, with completion under Antoninus Pius.
- Coins of Severus Alexander in 227–228 have sometimes been read as centennial issues supporting 128, but Mary Boatwright argues that their meaning need not be anniversary commemoration.

Cassius Dio’s story about Apollodorus criticizing the statues’ scale is a late narrative colored by the theme of Hadrian’s jealousy. It confirms ancient interest in the project but cannot independently prove every architectural detail.

The temple was restored after a fire under Maxentius in 307 CE. Its vaulted apses largely reflect that reconstruction. By late antiquity cult ceased; an oratory dedicated to Peter and Paul occupied Roma’s cella by the eighth century, later developing into Santa Maria Nova and then Santa Francesca Romana.

### Offerings and priesthood

Attested worship included:

- animal sacrifice and incense in public civic ritual;
- votive dedications;
- festivals and games;
- provincial and municipal priesthoods;
- imperial anniversaries and processions;
- coins and statues functioning as public theological media.

There is **no evidence specific to Roma** for temple prostitution, mystery initiation, ecstatic possession, or an oracle. No documented human sacrifice was offered to her. Roman executions, arena killings, warfare, and suppression conducted under imperial authority should not be redescribed as her sacrificial rite without evidence.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 195 BCE: formation of the image

Female personifications of cities were established in Greek art, and Rome’s coinage used helmeted female heads. Whether these early heads already constituted Dea Roma remains disputed.

### 195–30 BCE: Greek invention of a Roman goddess

The secure cult emerges in Smyrna. Other Greek communities adopted Roma as the divine recipient of temples, priesthoods, and *Rhomaia*. “Greek invention” means that Greek cities first made the cult institutionally visible; it does not mean the image lacked Italian antecedents.

### 29 BCE–14 CE: Augustan partnership

Augustus regulated worship by pairing himself with Roma. This arrangement simultaneously accepted eastern ruler-cult language and avoided straightforward official worship of a living ruler by Romans. Temples, provincial leagues, coins, and the *Gemma Augustea* turned Roma into the emperor’s divine partner.

### First–early second centuries CE: imperial normalization

Roma and the reigning or deified emperor became recurring institutional and numismatic partners. RPC coin types show her crowning Augustus inside a temple, with legends naming Roma, Augustus, and the Asian *koinon*. This was a durable provincial language of hierarchy and reciprocity.

### Hadrian to the Antonines: Roma Aeterna at Rome

Hadrian gave Roma her first great temple in the capital. Her pairing with Venus and title *Aeterna* translated an eastern personification-cult into Roman monumental state religion. Antonine coinage consolidated the enthroned, armed Roma bearing Palladium, spear, globe, or Victory.

### Third century: crisis and eternity

Imperial issuers repeatedly invoked Roma during military instability and anniversaries. Philip I’s games in 248 celebrated Rome’s millennium. Emperors used **ROMAE AETERNAE**, **RESTITVTOR VRBIS**, and related imagery to claim restoration. Such issues are political theology, but their repetition also demonstrates the goddess’s continuing intelligibility.

### Fourth century: Roma and Constantinopolis

Constantine’s new capital generated a new city-personification. Coinage from 330 onward paired **VRBS ROMA** and **CONSTANTINOPOLIS**. Roma did not disappear with imperial Christianity; her image could remain as an emblem of the state while its sacrificial setting eroded.

### 380s–fifth century: suppression and Christian appropriation

Anti-sacrifice legislation culminated under Theodosius I in the 390s. The Temple of Venus and Roma presumably ceased functioning as a sacrificial institution in this setting, although no surviving decree names its closure individually.

The debate of Symmachus, Ambrose, and Prudentius shows transformation rather than instant oblivion. Christian authors retained Roma as a speaking embodiment of history while denying the efficacy of her traditional gods. Claudian could still employ her as a divine literary figure.

### Medieval period

There is no evidence for continuous organized worship of Roma after antiquity. The city remained personified in poetry, seals, manuscript art, and political thought. Medieval **Roma** might be queen, widow, mother, or fallen imperial city, but these are allegorical continuations, not proof of a hidden pagan priesthood.

### Renaissance to nineteenth century

Humanists revived classical personification and the phrase **Roma aeterna**. Rome appeared in civic, papal, antiquarian, republican, and national imagery. These appropriations often combined pagan visual forms with Christian or civic meanings.

### Modern worship

Roman reconstructionist movements emerging especially in the late twentieth century sometimes honor Dea Roma. Nova Roma, founded in 1998, and various Italian or international *cultus deorum* groups reconstruct rites from ancient sources. The existence of revival practice is documented; its scale is small and no centralized orthodoxy exists. Claims of uninterrupted transmission from an ancient priesthood are unsupported.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### The Vittoriano

Angelo Zanelli’s monumental **Dea Roma** at the Vittoriano/Altar of the Fatherland in Rome was developed in the early twentieth century as the focal image behind the Tomb of the Unknown Soldier. The official monument history documents prolonged debate over whether she should stand or sit and over the material used.

This Roma is a modern national civic deity-image: she expresses Rome as the destined capital of unified Italy. She is not a reproduction of one identified ancient cult statue.

### Fascist appropriation

Italian Fascism systematically mobilized *romanità*: imperial architecture, the fasces, Augustan anniversaries, military imagery, and the myth of a “Third Rome.” Roma’s broader visual world contributed to this program, although not every modern statue of Roma was created by Fascists and not every use of **SPQR** or *Roma aeterna* is fascist.

The 1937–1938 *Mostra Augustea della Romanità* reframed Augustus and empire as predecessors of Mussolini’s state. This was **modern political reinvention**, not continuation of ancient cult. Contemporary far-right and white-nationalist groups sometimes reuse Roman symbols, especially SPQR, eagles, legionary imagery, and “eternal Rome.” Specific appropriations must be documented individually; classical imagery by itself does not establish extremist intent.

### Occult and esoteric traditions

The dedicated contested-material search found no strong evidence that Roma acquired a stable role in Renaissance ceremonial magic, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or the major modern grimoire traditions. She is conspicuously absent from the standard planetary and angelic hierarchies used by those systems.

Recent websites may assign her crystals, planetary correspondences, Tarot cards, invocations, or “ancient” magical seals. Unless tied to an identifiable ancient inscription or ritual text, these are **modern inventions**.

### Literature and visual culture

Roma’s most consequential post-antique forms are political allegory rather than character-centered fiction:

- the mourning or triumphant Roma of Renaissance and Baroque art;
- the female city on medals and monuments;
- national Roma in Risorgimento art;
- imperial Roma in Fascist spectacle;
- the popular slogan **Roma aeterna**;
- game, comic, and fantasy depictions that turn her into a conventional warrior goddess.

No modern film, novel, or game has established a single globally dominant character-version comparable to modern portrayals of Thor or Hades.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Major scholarship

- **Georg Wissowa, *Religion und Kultus der Römer*:** placed Roma within the historical development of Roman state religion.
- **Kurt Latte, *Römische Religionsgeschichte*:** treated the expansion and political transformation of Roman cult.
- **Georges Dumézil, *Archaic Roman Religion*:** fundamental for archaic structures, but Roma’s late secure attestation limits attempts to assign her an Indo-European archaic office.
- **Ronald Mellor, *Thea Rhome: The Worship of the Goddess Roma in the Greek World* (1975):** the foundational modern study of Roma’s Greek cult.
- **Carla Fayer, *Il culto della dea Roma*:** emphasized origins, diffusion, and especially numismatic evidence.
- **C. C. Vermeule, *The Goddess Roma in the Art of the Roman Empire*:** major iconographic collection.
- **Mary T. Boatwright, *Hadrian and the City of Rome*:** central for the Temple of Venus and Roma and its debated chronology.
- **Beard, North, and Price, *Religions of Rome*; John Scheid’s studies:** situate Roma and imperial cult within civic ritual rather than treating them as mere propaganda or private belief.
- **LIMC, “Roma”:** essential catalogue of securely and tentatively identified images.
- **Brill’s New Pauly:** synthetic reference treatment with bibliography.

### Principal controversies

1. **Did Roma begin in Italy or the Greek East?**  
   Italian coins supplied imagery; Greek cities provide the earliest secure cult. The best formulation is that a Roman political identity and Italian visual type were transformed into a worshipped goddess within Greek civic religion.

2. **Are early helmeted coin heads Roma?**  
   Sometimes probably; not always demonstrably. The legend ROMA can denote issuer rather than portrait.

3. **Was Roma Rome’s secret tutelary deity?**  
   Ancient texts attest secrecy but do not securely identify the hidden deity as Roma. The identification is conjectural.

4. **Was Melinno Republican or imperial?**  
   The poem’s lack of an emperor supports an early date; literary style has supported a later one. The surviving fifth-century quotation cannot resolve the matter.

5. **Was Roma “really believed in” or merely propaganda?**  
   This is a false binary. Temples, sacrifices, priesthoods, vows, festivals, and divine titles constitute cult; their political function does not negate religious status. Individual internal belief is generally unrecoverable.

6. **When was Hadrian’s temple dedicated?**  
   Proposals include 121, 128, and 135/136 CE. The construction, inauguration, completion, and installation of cult statues may have occurred at different times.

7. **Did Roma become Constantinopolis?**  
   No. Late-antique art deliberately paired distinct city-personifications while allowing iconographic exchange.

8. **Does Revelation depict Dea Roma?**  
   The seven-hilled woman is very probably a polemical Rome/Babylon, and Roma’s provincial iconography belongs to its historical context. The text does not name Dea Roma, so a direct portrait claim remains interpretive.

### Discredited or unsupported claims

- Roma was an archaic Indo-European goddess worshipped from Rome’s foundation: **not established**.
- Roma was originally an Etruscan goddess identifiable on named mirrors: **not established**.
- Roma possessed a canonical marriage, children, or extensive myth cycle: **false on present evidence**.
- Her cult practiced human sacrifice or temple prostitution: **no evidence**.
- A continuous secret cult survived from antiquity to present reconstructionism: **no evidence**.
- Modern planetary, crystal, Tarot, or astrological correspondences are ancient: **generally modern invention**.

## 10. Chronology

- **Late fourth–third centuries BCE:** Helmeted female heads and ROMA legends appear on Roman coinage; identification as Dea Roma remains disputed.
- **195 BCE:** Smyrna erects the earliest securely attested temple to Rome, according to Tacitus, *Annals* 4.56.
- **Second–first centuries BCE:** *Rhomaia*, priesthoods, and honors to Roma spread among Greek communities.
- **Second century BCE or second century CE:** Melinno’s *Hymn to Rome* composed; date disputed.
- **29 BCE:** Octavian authorizes provincial temples joining himself to Roma for Greek worshippers; Roman citizens honor Roma and Divus Julius.
- **27 BCE onward:** Temple of Roma and Augustus built on the Athenian Acropolis.
- **c. 25–20 BCE:** Temple of Roma and Augustus established at Ancyra.
- **12 BCE:** Federal altar of Roma and Augustus inaugurated at Lugdunum.
- **9 BCE:** Ara Pacis dedicated; armed seated figure commonly identified as Roma.
- **c. 9–12 CE:** *Gemma Augustea* represents Roma enthroned beside Augustus.
- **14 CE onward:** The *Res Gestae* is inscribed at the Ancyra temple in Latin and Greek.
- **26 CE:** Smyrna invokes its 195 BCE temple when competing for the provincial temple authorized under Tiberius.
- **First–second centuries CE:** Provincial coinage repeatedly represents Roma crowning Augustus in the temple of Roma and Augustus.
- **121–136 CE:** Hadrian’s Temple of Venus Felix and Roma Aeterna inaugurated, dedicated, or completed; exact sequence disputed.
- **145–161 CE:** Antoninus Pius issues coins with Roma enthroned, holding Palladium and spear.
- **c. 191 CE:** Fire damages the Temple of Venus and Roma; later restoration follows.
- **227–228 CE:** Severus Alexander issues Roma and temple types, interpreted either as anniversary issues or traditionalist restoration imagery.
- **248 CE:** Philip I celebrates Rome’s millennium; *Roma Aeterna* ideology gains renewed force.
- **307 CE:** Maxentius restores the Temple of Venus and Roma after fire.
- **330 CE:** Foundation coinage for Constantinople pairs the new capital’s personification with *Urbs Roma* types.
- **354 CE:** The *Chronograph of 354* demonstrates the continuing prestige of Rome’s civic and calendrical imagery in a Christianizing empire.
- **384 CE:** Symmachus and Ambrose deploy opposed speaking personifications of Rome in the Altar of Victory controversy.
- **391–392 CE:** Theodosian prohibitions on sacrifice effectively terminate public sacrificial cult; no surviving order individually records the closing of Roma’s temple.
- **c. 402–403 CE:** Prudentius’ *Contra Symmachum* represents Roma’s Christian conversion.
- **Early fifth century CE:** Claudian continues to portray Roma as an armed divine participant in imperial panegyric.
- **Eighth century:** An oratory of Peter and Paul occupies the former Roma cella of Hadrian’s temple.
- **Fifteenth century:** The associated church becomes Santa Francesca Romana.
- **Nineteenth–early twentieth centuries:** Roma becomes a central allegory of Italian unification and national identity.
- **1911–1925:** Angelo Zanelli’s Dea Roma becomes the focal image of the Vittoriano’s Altar of the Fatherland.
- **1922–1943:** Fascist Italy appropriates Roman imperial imagery and *romanità* for nationalist and colonial ideology.
- **1974–1975:** Vermeule’s iconographic study and Mellor’s *Thea Rhome* establish the modern scholarly framework.
- **1998:** Nova Roma is founded, representing a visible phase of modern Roman religious reconstructionism.
- **Present:** Roma survives as archaeological subject, civic allegory, political symbol, popular-culture figure, and occasionally revived goddess; continuity from the ancient cult is not demonstrated.

### Primary evidence and corpora

The principal primary witnesses are Tacitus, *Annals* 4.37–38 and 4.55–56; Cassius Dio 51.20 and 69.4; Suetonius, *Augustus* 52; the *Res Gestae Divi Augusti* and Monumentum Ancyranum; Melinno’s hymn as preserved by Stobaeus; Republican and imperial coinage catalogued in RRC, RIC, BMC, OCRE, and RPC; provincial and Latin inscriptions in *Inscriptiones Graecae*, *Inschriften griechischer Städte aus Kleinasien*, and *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum*; the *Gemma Augustea*; the Ara Pacis; the temples at Smyrna, Athens, Ancyra, Lugdunum, and Rome; Symmachus, *Relatio* 3; Ambrose, *Epistle* 18; Prudentius, *Contra Symmachum*; and Claudian’s panegyrics.

The corpus is unusually dependent on coins, inscriptions, and monuments. Literary silence about Roma’s biography is itself significant: she was an institutionally powerful goddess without a developed personal mythology.
