# Juventas: Research Dossier

**Class:** Roman goddess; more precisely, a divine personification of youthful maturity, especially the transition of male citizens into the adult, military, and civic body. In later literary and artistic interpretation she is identified with Greek Hebe.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest datable episode is the state lectisternium and supplication ordered in **218 BCE**, recorded by Livy, *Ab Urbe Condita* 21.62.9, when Juventas received a lectisternium during the religious crisis at the beginning of the Second Punic War. Livy wrote approximately two centuries later, so this is secure evidence for a Republican cult event as preserved by an Augustan historian, not a contemporary inscription. Claims that Juventas already possessed a Capitoline shrine under the kings belong to later Roman aetiological tradition; they may preserve an old cultic arrangement, but cannot independently date it to the regal period.

---

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Name

The standard Latin form is **Iuventas**—in modern typography, *Juventas*. The spelling with initial *I* is ancient; *J* is a post-classical graphic convention. Related or variant forms include:

- **Iuventas**, genitive *Iuventatis*: the normal divine name.
- **Iuventus**, “youth” or “the body of young people,” occasionally used as a divine name and increasingly common in imperial political language.
- **Iuventa**: a rare, generally later form, notably in imperial coin legends such as *IUVENTA IMPERII*.
- Greek **Ἥβη**, Hebe: the regular Greek equivalent.
- Greek **Νεότης**, Neotēs, “Youth”: used to translate the Roman goddess’s name or temple, including Cassius Dio’s designation of her sanctuary as *to tēs Neotētos megaron*.
- Greek **ναὸς Νεότητος**, “temple of Youth,” in the Greek version of Augustus’ *Res Gestae*.

The Latin noun *iuventas* denotes youth, youthful age, or the state of being young and belongs with *iuvenis*, “young person.” Its immediate Latin etymology is not seriously disputed. More ambitious Indo-European reconstructions connect *iuvenis* with the inherited Indo-European word for “young,” conventionally reconstructed as \**h₂yu-h₁en-* or a related formation. Such linguistic ancestry does **not** demonstrate that Juventas was inherited as a fully formed Proto-Indo-European goddess.

### Nature and domain

**Secure Republican and early-imperial evidence:** Juventas governs the threshold at which a Roman boy ceases to wear the purple-bordered *toga praetexta* and assumes the plain adult *toga virilis* or *toga pura*. This transition made him publicly available for adult civic and military obligations. The goddess therefore represents not childhood but **youth admitted into the citizen body**.

**Roman learned interpretation:** Roman antiquarians connected her cult with the census and military manpower. Dionysius of Halicarnassus, citing the annalist Lucius Calpurnius Piso, says that Servius Tullius required those reaching manhood to pay a coin into Juventas’s treasury, enabling the king to count military-age citizens (*Roman Antiquities* 4.15.5).

**Political elaboration:** Her refusal to yield her place on the Capitol was interpreted as an omen that Rome’s *iuventus*—its military-capable youth—would never yield. Augustine preserves this patriotic interpretation while attacking it as disproved by Roman defeats (*City of God* 4.23).

**Hellenizing identification:** By the late Republic and Augustan period Juventas could be equated with **Hebe**, daughter of Zeus/Jupiter and Hera/Juno and wife of Heracles/Hercules. Cicero uses Juventas in discussions of Greek divine genealogy, while Ovid makes the identification narratively explicit in *Fasti* 6.65–88.

### Epithets and titles

Juventas has fewer individualized cult epithets than major Roman gods. Documented extensions include:

- **Iuventas / Iuventus Augusta**: “August Juventas/Youth,” an imperial-era dedication transferring youthful vitality to the emperor or imperial order. Provincial inscriptions include *Iuventuti Aug(ustae)*.
- **Iuventa Imperii**: “the Youth/Renewal of the Empire,” found on coins of Caracalla.
- **Iuventus Augusti**: “the youthfulness of the Augustus,” particularly in third-century coinage.
- **Iuventus Imperii**: the rejuvenation, continuity, or renewed military capacity of imperial government.
- **Princeps iuventutis** is not an epithet of the goddess but an imperial title, “leader of the youth,” bestowed on young imperial heirs. It belongs to the same political vocabulary but must not be treated as a priesthood of Juventas.

There is no secure ancient evidence for fixed sacred colors, numbers, gemstones, planets, or weekdays belonging specifically to Juventas. Lists supplying such correspondences are modern constructions.

### Etruscan and Italic origin claims

No securely identified Etruscan goddess can presently be demonstrated as the direct source of Juventas. Etruscan mirrors depict Greek Hebe under Greek-derived or locally adapted mythological conventions, but the presence of Hebe in Etruscan art establishes reception of Greek myth, not an Etruscan origin for the Capitoline Juventas. Older scholarship’s readiness to derive Roman institutions wholesale from Etruria exceeds the evidence in this case.

**Finding:** Juventas is best documented as a Roman abstraction-deity whose cult was subsequently Hellenized through Hebe. A deeper Italic background is possible but not textually recoverable.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The regal-period traditions

#### Capitoline refusal legend

Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities* 3.69, written around 7 BCE, relates that when Tarquinius wished to clear existing sacred places for the temple of Jupiter, the auguries permitted removal of all except those of **Terminus** and **Juventas**. The older cult places were consequently incorporated into the new Capitoline complex. Livy 5.54.7 and Augustine, *City of God* 4.23, know the associated claim that Roman boundaries and youth would never yield.

This is **later aetiological tradition**, not contemporary evidence for a sixth-century-BCE sanctuary. Livy’s account of the original clearing in 1.55 emphasizes Terminus; the fuller appearance of Juventas in later versions led Wissowa and other scholars to suspect that she was secondarily added to an older Terminus legend. Against that, the existence of a Juventas shrine within or adjoining Minerva’s cella requires some historical explanation and may preserve an early cultic fact.

#### Servius Tullius and the treasury

Dionysius 4.15.5, citing Piso, says Servius Tullius instituted three age-linked payments:

- at birth, to Juno Lucina;
- at entry into manhood, to Juventas;
- at death, to Libitina.

The payments permitted population accounting. The report is **Republican antiquarian reconstruction preserved in Augustan Greek historiography**. It is evidence that Romans of Piso’s period associated Juventas with the civic registration of adult males; it does not securely prove a sixth-century-BCE institution.

### Republican state evidence

#### 218 BCE: lectisternium

Livy 21.62.9 reports that, following prodigies at the beginning of the Second Punic War, the decemviral priests consulted the Sibylline books. A lectisternium was ordered for Juventas, while a supplication took place at the temple of Hercules and then at all shrines. This is the earliest firmly datable event involving the goddess.

The rite was conducted in a Sibylline and conventionally “Greek” ritual framework. That does not prove that Juventas herself was newly imported from Greece; Roman gods could be honored *ritu Graeco*.

#### 207–191 BCE: vow and temple

Livy 36.36.5–6 says Marcus Livius Salinator vowed a temple to Juventas during the Battle of the Metaurus in 207 BCE, began construction while censor in 204, and that Gaius Licinius Lucullus, acting as duumvir, dedicated it during the consulship narrated under 191 BCE.

Some reference works give **193 BCE**, but Livy’s placement and the consular chronology support **191 BCE**; the discrepancy should not be silently harmonized. The item-specific “temple of 191 BCE” is therefore defensible.

The sanctuary stood **in or beside the Circus Maximus**, probably toward its western, Aventine side and near the shrine of Summanus. Its exact archaeological footprint has not been conclusively identified.

### Late-Republican and Augustan texts

#### Cicero, *De Natura Deorum*

Cicero, writing in 45–44 BCE, discusses the proliferation and genealogy of gods through competing Academic, Stoic, and Epicurean voices. Juventas appears within the Roman translation of Greek divine genealogy, effectively as Hebe. Cicero is evidence for elite late-Republican theological and philosophical classification, not for an independent Juventas narrative cycle.

#### Virgil

Virgil does not give Juventas an independent mythic episode comparable to Ovid’s. Uses of *iuventas* and *iuventus* in Virgil ordinarily mean human youth or youthful strength. Later commentaries may allegorize or supply Roman religious information, but capitalizing every poetic occurrence into a goddess would be erroneous.

#### Ovid, *Fasti* 6.65–88

Ovid, writing under Augustus and revising under Tiberius, stages Juventas as a speaking goddess contesting the derivation of June. She identifies herself through Greek genealogy:

- her mother shares the Capitoline temple with Jupiter;
- she is the wife of Hercules;
- Hercules’ Roman deeds give Rome reason to honor her;
- June may derive from *iuvenes*, the young men whom Romulus distinguished from the *seniores*.

This is **Augustan literary elaboration** combining Roman political age classes with the Greek Hebe-Heracles marriage. Concord then offers a rival etymology from the “joining” of Romans and Sabines, and Ovid refuses to choose.

#### Ovid, *Metamorphoses*

In *Metamorphoses* 9, Hebe appears in the Heracles apotheosis complex and possesses power to renew youth. Ovid’s Latin poem uses Greek mythic material; translating Hebe automatically as Juventas can obscure that cultural layering.

### Imperial and late-antique sources

- **Pliny the Elder, *Natural History* 29.57; 35.108**: preserves topographical and ritual information concerning the Circus area and Capitoline sacred arrangement.
- **Cassius Dio 54.19.7**: records the burning of the Temple of Youth in 16 BCE, calling it the hall or sanctuary of Neotēs.
- **Augustus, *Res Gestae* 19**: lists the temple of Juventas among buildings restored or constructed under Augustus. The surviving Latin and Greek copies were inscribed after his death; the best-preserved is at Ankara.
- **Festus**: the epitomized antiquarian lexicon preserves Roman explanations of *iuvenes*, age divisions, and rites, but its severely abridged transmission requires caution.
- **Servius and Servius Auctus**: fourth- to early-medieval Virgilian commentary preserves earlier antiquarian material, but the two recensions must be distinguished.
- **Macrobius, *Saturnalia***: early fifth-century learned synthesis of Republican and imperial antiquarianism. It is valuable as a transmitter, not an unmediated source for archaic practice.
- **Arnobius, *Adversus Nationes*, and Augustine, *City of God***: hostile Christian witnesses. Their ridicule of Juventas as one of many narrowly functional Roman gods demonstrates that her name and conventional function remained available to late-antique polemicists; it does not show a flourishing contemporary cult.
- Augustine 4.11 reports the theological reduction of functional gods to aspects of Jupiter: Jupiter himself is called Juventas when presiding over the beginning of youth after the *praetexta*. This is **late-antique learned systematization**, quoted polemically.

### Calendars and inscriptions

The ordinary Republican fasti do not reveal a major annual festival exclusively for Juventas. That silence is important: her cult was institutional, but not supported by evidence for a large public festival comparable to the Saturnalia or Matronalia.

The **Feriale Cumanum** (*CIL* X 8375 = *ILS* 108), an early-imperial calendar from Cumae, prescribes a supplication to **Spes and Iuventus** on 18 October, commemorating Augustus’ assumption of the *toga virilis*. This explicitly joins imperial biography, coming-of-age ritual, Hope, and Youth.

Provincial dedications include forms such as *Iuventuti Aug(ustae)*. Maria Jaczynowska’s collection emphasizes how few inscriptions securely connect Juventas with municipal youth associations. A natural-looking association between *iuvenes* and the goddess should therefore not be assumed without epigraphic proof.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Core functions

1. **Passage into adult male status:** The best-attested personal function.
2. **Registration of military-age citizens:** An antiquarian explanation of the monetary offering.
3. **Collective vitality of Rome:** Developed through the Capitoline refusal omen.
4. **Dynastic and imperial renewal:** Prominent in imperial coinage.
5. **Perpetual youth:** Especially after assimilation to Hebe.
6. **Rejuvenation:** Greek literary material attributes renewal of youth to Hebe; it is not clearly an early Roman Juventas function.

### Ritual attributes

The characteristic offering was a **coin or monetary contribution** made when a youth assumed the *toga virilis*. Later sources sometimes describe an animal sacrifice, but the monetary rite is the more specific tradition. It marked a legal and civic transition rather than a birthday.

No exclusive priesthood of Juventas is securely documented. Administration of her public cult probably fell within Rome’s ordinary magistratial and pontifical structures.

### Iconography

No securely identified archaic cult image survives.

Under Antoninus Pius and Marcus Aurelius, coin types associated with the young prince and his assumption of the adult toga show a standing female personification, commonly identified as Iuventas:

- she holds a patera or pours incense over an altar;
- she may stand beside a flaming altar;
- her image emphasizes sacrifice and civic initiation rather than Hebe’s role as cupbearer.

Later imperial issues broaden the imagery:

- **Caracalla:** *IUVENTA IMPERII*, sometimes represented through the youthful emperor holding Victory and a spear.
- **Gallienus:** *IUVENTUS AUG(USTI)*, stressing the emperor’s vigor.
- **Claudius II:** Hercules accompanied by a *IUVENTUS* legend, projecting robust or renewed strength rather than depicting the goddess herself.

Consequently, a coin legend naming *Iuventus* does not guarantee that the pictured figure is Juventas. Imperial numismatics often turns the abstraction into a quality embodied by the emperor, Hercules, or military vigor.

### Unsupported correspondences

No ancient source securely assigns Juventas:

- a planet;
- a zodiacal sign;
- a magic number;
- a canonical flower or tree;
- a distinctive animal;
- a fixed color;
- an occult sigil.

Modern web pages that attach such correspondences generally combine Hebe, generic “maiden” symbolism, and modern magical systems.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Roman core versus Greek genealogy

Early Roman cult evidence gives Juventas **no securely attested parents, siblings, or children**. As an abstraction, she need not originally have possessed a narrative family.

Through identification with Hebe she acquires:

- **father:** Zeus, rendered Jupiter;
- **mother:** Hera, rendered Juno;
- **siblings:** principally Ares/Mars and Eileithyia/Lucina in Greek genealogy, with many half-siblings through Zeus;
- **husband:** Heracles/Hercules;
- **children:** later Greek sources sometimes name Alexiares and Anicetus as sons of Heracles and Hebe, but this is not a developed Roman Juventas tradition.

The dossier must therefore distinguish “Juventas interacts with Hercules in Ovid” from “every relation of Greek Hebe automatically belonged to the native Roman goddess.” The latter is learned equivalence, not proof of archaic Roman belief.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### The unyielding Capitoline shrine

**Narrative:** Tarquinius clears sacred sites on the Capitol for Jupiter’s great temple. The gods are consulted through augury about relocation. All consent except Terminus and Juventas. Their sacred places remain incorporated within the new temple. Romans interpret this as promising immovable boundaries and undiminished youthful power.

**Earliest surviving versions:** Livy and Dionysius in the late first century BCE.

**Development:** Livy 1.55 makes Terminus central when narrating the temple’s construction; the later appearance of Juventas alongside him may be patriotic expansion. Livy 5.54.7, in a speech against abandoning Rome after the Gallic sack, invokes both Juventas and Terminus as guarantees of permanence. Augustine later attacks the omen by listing Roman military defeats and territorial contractions.

**Function:** The myth explains an architectural anomaly and turns it into an ideology of Roman continuity.

### Servius Tullius’ registers

**Narrative:** Servius creates monetary offerings marking birth, adulthood, and death. Contributions to Juventas count those reaching military age.

**Source:** Piso, fragment 14, through Dionysius 4.15.5.

**Function:** An aetiology for census classification and the goddess’s treasury.

**Historical status:** It may preserve a genuine ritual, but its attribution to Servius participates in the Roman habit of crediting that king with census and constitutional institutions.

### Juventas claims June

**Narrative:** In Ovid’s *Fasti*, three explanations compete for June’s name. Juno claims it; Juventas/Hebe argues from *iuvenes*, explaining that Romulus divided Romans into elder counselors and young warriors; Concord derives June from *iungere*, “to join,” recalling Roman-Sabine union. Ovid declines judgment.

**Source:** *Fasti* 6.1–100.

**Status:** Augustan poetic and antiquarian elaboration, not a cult myth known from Republican ritual.

**Importance:** This is the fullest surviving scene in which Juventas speaks as a character. It fuses Greek genealogy, Roman age organization, Hercules’ Italian mythology, and calendar speculation.

### Hebe and the apotheosis of Hercules

Greek tradition makes Hebe cupbearer of the gods and wife of Heracles after his apotheosis. Latin writers transfer some of this material to Juventas. The marriage is early in Greek literature—already reflected in Homeric tradition—but its Roman application to Juventas is a **syncretistic reception**, not independently attested Roman narrative.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Capitoline shrine

The shrine or altar of Juventas stood within the Capitoline complex, traditionally in the **cella of Minerva**. Its precise architectural form is uncertain; ancient vocabulary ranges between altar, shrine, and small chapel.

A youth assuming the *toga virilis* offered money to the goddess. This made the Capitol the ritual terminus of an individual’s admission to adult citizenship. The association with Minerva may be architectural rather than theological; no ancient source explains a special doctrinal bond between them.

### Temple at the Circus Maximus

- **Vowed:** 207 BCE by Marcus Livius Salinator during the Metaurus campaign.
- **Construction begun:** 204 BCE, when Salinator was censor.
- **Dedicated:** probably 191 BCE by Gaius Licinius Lucullus.
- **Location:** *in Circo Maximo*; likely on the Aventine side, near Summanus.
- **Fire:** burned in 16 BCE.
- **Restoration:** Augustus states in *Res Gestae* 19 that he restored or built the Temple of Juventas.

The surviving sources do not establish whether the coming-of-age offering permanently moved from the Capitoline shrine to this temple. Platner and Ashby treat a transfer as possible, not certain.

### Rituals

Documented practices include:

- payment or dedication upon assuming the *toga virilis*;
- public lectisternium in 218 BCE;
- votive temple-building after a military crisis;
- imperial-calendar supplication with Spes;
- incense or libation imagery on coins;
- provincial votive dedications, including Augustan forms.

There is no evidence of:

- initiation mysteries;
- temple prostitution;
- sexual rites;
- human sacrifice;
- an oracle of Juventas;
- a distinctive magical cult;
- a hereditary priesthood;
- a large pilgrimage network.

### Cult economics and politics

The treasury story places individual payments at the intersection of religion and state accounting. Even if Piso’s Servian attribution is legendary, it captures the political meaning of the rite: new adults were counted as potential soldiers and citizens.

The Metaurus vow tied Juventas to the preservation of Rome’s manpower during the Hannibalic war. Under the empire, *iuventus* became a dynastic resource: youthful heirs represented succession, military vigor, and renewal.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Possible archaic phase

The Capitoline shrine may be older than the Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus, but that conclusion rests on late aetiology and the shrine’s anomalous location. There is no contemporary regal inscription.

### Middle Republic

The first securely datable cult event is the 218 BCE lectisternium. The Metaurus vow of 207 and temple completed by 191 demonstrate formal state investment during and after the Second Punic War. Juventas’s domain suited a state concerned with military recruitment, demographic survival, and generational replacement.

### Late Republic

Antiquarians reconstructed the cult through Servius Tullius, the census, and Capitoline topography. Greek education encouraged identification with Hebe. The goddess thereby gained a genealogy and husband absent from the recoverable Roman core.

### Augustan period

Ovid incorporated Juventas into Augustan calendar poetry; Augustus commemorated restoration of her temple. The *Feriale Cumanum* linked Iuventus and Spes with the prince’s own coming of age. The figure now operated simultaneously as:

- an old Roman cult power;
- Hebe under a Latin name;
- the collective young citizen body;
- a symbol of dynastic futurity.

### High Empire

Coinage increasingly detached *Iuventus* from a discrete female deity. She could still appear as a sacrificing personification, but legends also described the emperor’s quality or the empire’s renewal. Municipal youth organizations sometimes used related religious language, though direct dedication to Juventas is rarer than older scholarship assumed.

### Third century

*Iuventa Imperii* and *Iuventus Augusti* became flexible claims of vigor amid military and dynastic instability. Hercules could carry a *IUVENTUS* legend, showing that the term now functioned as an imperial virtue as much as a goddess’s name.

### Christianization and disappearance

Christian apologists catalogued Juventas among specialized Roman gods to argue that pagan theology multiplied divine beings unnecessarily. Augustine preserves valuable traditions but deploys them to demonstrate the impotence or incoherence of the old gods.

No edict or act of temple destruction specifically targeting Juventas is known. Her institutional cult disappeared through the general withdrawal of state funding, Christianization of public office, temple closure, and the loss or reuse of sanctuaries from the fourth century onward. Claims of a dramatic, individually documented “suppression of Juventas” cannot be established.

### Medieval to early modern reception

Juventas largely survived as:

- a Latin common noun;
- an antiquarian name in mythographical dictionaries;
- the Roman equivalent of Hebe;
- an allegorical personification of youth.

Renaissance and neoclassical artists strongly favored Hebe’s Greek cupbearer imagery. Works retrospectively labeled “Juventas” generally derive from Hebe rather than the Capitol or the *toga virilis* rite.

### Present-day revival

Some Roman polytheist and eclectic neopagan practitioners honor Juventas, especially in rites for adolescence, birthdays, education, or assuming adult responsibility. These observances are **modern revivals**. No continuous cultic lineage from antiquity is documented, and there are no reliable population figures for exclusive or primary devotees of Juventas.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

Juventas has not developed the extensive independent occult afterlife found around Hecate, Isis, or Hermes.

### Esotericism and occultism

No major Renaissance magical text, Golden Dawn ritual, Theosophical system, or classical grimoire assigns Juventas a major office. Modern correspondence tables occasionally give her planetary, floral, elemental, or magical attributes, but these are **modern inventions or extrapolations from Hebe and generic youth symbolism**.

The absence is itself significant: western occultism usually absorbed her beneath Hebe, the “maiden” archetype, or generalized personifications of youth.

### Neopaganism

Modern Roman reconstructionists may reasonably reconstruct offerings from literary evidence—wine, incense, cakes, or a coin—but the exact liturgy is necessarily modern. Gender-neutral use of Juventas as protector of all young people is ethically and socially intelligible today, but it expands an ancient evidentiary core focused chiefly on male citizen transition.

### Literature and visual culture

The name survives in:

- mythological dictionaries identifying Juventas with Hebe;
- personifications of youth in public art;
- educational, athletic, and youth organizations adopting variants of *Juventus*;
- commercial language promising rejuvenation.

The Italian football club **Juventus** takes its name from the Latin word “youth”; this is not evidence of devotion to the goddess. Similar commercial uses capitalize on the lexical meaning, not the ancient cult.

### Political appropriation

Roman imperial ideology used *iuventus* for dynastic succession and military vitality. Modern political youth movements have also used Latin youth vocabulary, but no substantial extremist or nationalist tradition centered specifically on the goddess Juventas was found. Assertions that she was a patron deity of a particular modern extremist movement require evidence and should not be inferred merely from Romanizing aesthetics.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Juventas archaic?

**Wissowa’s caution:** Because Livy’s foundation account foregrounds Terminus, the inclusion of Juventas in the refusal legend may be secondary patriotic elaboration.

**Counterargument:** The awkward placement of her shrine within Minerva’s Capitoline cella is easier to explain if an older sacred site really had been preserved.

**Assessment:** The shrine is historically credible; its regal date and the exact form of the refusal episode are not independently demonstrable.

### Native goddess or Hebe?

Older handbooks often write “Juventas (Hebe)” as though the names were interchangeable from the beginning. Modern historical analysis separates:

1. Roman Juventas as a functional civic abstraction;
2. Greek Hebe as a genealogical Olympian and cupbearer;
3. late-Republican and Augustan identification of the two;
4. imperial allegory of youthful power.

### Goddess or personification?

The distinction is partly modern. Roman religion could worship an abstraction without treating it as merely literary. A shrine, offerings, a lectisternium, a vowed temple, and dedications make Juventas a cult recipient. Yet her limited narrative personality before Ovid and the later political use of *iuventus* show that “goddess” and “personified quality” remained overlapping categories.

### The temple’s dedication date

Livy places the dedication in the year narrated in Book 36, conventionally **191 BCE**. Platner and Ashby give **193 BCE**, followed by some derivative references. The safest procedure is to cite Livy’s consular context and acknowledge the secondary chronological discrepancy.

### Juventas and youth associations

Earlier scholarship inferred that organizations of *iuvenes* naturally worshipped Juventas. Jaczynowska’s epigraphic review stresses that direct evidence was long absent and remains sparse. Linguistic similarity is not enough to establish institutional patronage.

### State of the material evidence

No securely identified cult statue or surviving temple structure supplies an uncontested visual portrait. The evidence is distributed across:

- Livy, Dionysius, Cicero, Ovid, Pliny, Dio, and late commentators;
- the *Res Gestae* copies, especially Ankara;
- the *Feriale Cumanum*;
- scattered provincial inscriptions;
- Roman imperial coinage;
- antiquarian topography.

The greatest loss is the absence of Republican temple archives, priestly books, and a contemporary inscription from the Circus temple.

### Discredited or unsupported claims

The following should not be presented as ancient fact:

- that Juventas had a fully developed independent mythology before contact with Greek Hebe;
- that Etruscan mirrors prove an Etruscan origin for her Roman cult;
- that she had fixed astrological or magical correspondences;
- that she presided over rejuvenation potions or a mystery initiation;
- that her cult practiced human sacrifice or temple prostitution;
- that a continuous secret cult survived Christianization;
- that all ancient uses of *iuventus* name the goddess.

| **c. 27–9 BCE** | Livy writes the surviving accounts of the 218 lectisternium, Capitoline omen, and temple history. |
| **16 BCE** | Cassius Dio records the burning of the Temple of Youth. |
| **Late first century BCE–early first century CE** | Augustus restores the temple; the act is recorded in *Res Gestae* 19. |
| **c. 7 BCE** | Dionysius publishes the surviving Greek accounts of the Capitoline shrine and Servian treasury. |
| **Early first century CE** | *Feriale Cumanum* orders a supplication to Spes and Iuventus on the anniversary of Augustus’ *toga virilis*. |
| **c. 8–17 CE** | Ovid’s *Fasti* gives Juventas her fullest literary speech and explicitly merges her with Hebe. |
| **c. 77 CE** | Pliny preserves topographical and ritual notices relevant to her shrines. |
| **Second century CE** | Antonine coinage portrays Iuventas as a female sacrificant in connection with princely coming of age. |
| **Early third century CE** | Caracallan coinage uses *Iuventa Imperii* for imperial renewal. |
| **Mid-third century CE** | Gallienus and Claudius II issues use *Iuventus* as imperial or Herculean vigor. |
| **c. 200–230 CE** | Cassius Dio’s history preserves the fire of 16 BCE under the Greek designation Neotēs. |
| **Late fourth–early fifth century CE** | Servius, Macrobius, Arnobius, and Augustine transmit, systematize, or attack Roman functional theology involving Juventas. |
| **Fourth–fifth centuries CE** | Public cult disappears during the broader Christianization and defunding of Roman civic religion; no Juventas-specific suppression event survives. |
| **Renaissance–nineteenth century** | Mythography and art generally subsume Juventas under Hebe or allegorical Youth. |
| **1897** | Founding of Juventus Football Club illustrates modern secular use of the Latin noun, not restoration of the cult. |
| **Twentieth century** | Wissowa, Latte, Dumézil, topographical handbooks, and studies of Roman civic religion debate the archaic shrine and functional deity model. |
| **1979** | Maria Jaczynowska re-examines inscriptions connecting Juventas with Roman youth associations, emphasizing the earlier lack of direct proof. |
| **Late twentieth–twenty-first century** | Roman reconstructionist and eclectic pagan observances revive Juventas; rites are reconstructed or newly composed, with no demonstrated institutional continuity. |
