# Consus: Research Dossier

**Class:** Male Roman god (*deus*), associated in surviving Roman ritual with the Consualia, an earth-covered altar in the Circus Maximus, horse and mule races, and—by modern reconstruction—the storage of harvested grain. Ancient antiquarians also classified him as a god of hidden counsel and identified or equated him with Neptune/Poseidon in equestrian or earth-shaking form.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest presently secure physical evidence is the **Fasti Antiates Maiores**, a painted pre-Julian calendar from Antium, normally dated approximately 84–55 BCE. It marks **CONS(UALIA)** on 21 August and 15 December. This proves that the two Consualia were established Roman calendar observances by the late Republic; it does not independently establish their original meaning, prove their institution by Romulus, or identify Consus’s earliest domain. The earliest surviving literary witness naming Consus is Varro’s *De lingua Latina* 6.20, written in the 40s BCE.

## Evidentiary labels

- **Securely attested:** Directly stated by an ancient text, calendar, inscription, or material remain whose date and character can be assessed.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** A version found in the major Augustan or Imperial literary retellings rather than in contemporary evidence for early Rome.
- **Regional variant:** A geographically localized practice or equation.
- **Learned systematization:** An antiquarian, philosophical, grammatical, or priestly explanation imposed upon inherited ritual.
- **Syncretistic identification:** A historically situated equation of Consus with another god.
- **Late accretion:** Late-antique, medieval, or early-modern interpretation not demonstrably present in the earlier cult.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** An inference from names, calendar position, ritual, or comparative evidence.
- **Modern invention:** A claim for which no ancient evidence has been located and whose documented appearance is modern.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

Consus was a minor but publicly recognized Roman god whose individuality is clearest in ritual rather than mythology. His defining evidence is:

1. two annual festivals called the **Consualia**, on 21 August and 15 December;
2. an altar at or near the first turning-posts of the Circus Maximus, normally hidden beneath earth and exposed for rites;
3. horse, chariot, mule, or ass racing and the garlanding and rest of draft animals;
4. a traditional association between the first Consualia, Romulus, and the seizure of the Sabine women;
5. several ancient explanations of his name and nature—stored produce, hidden counsel, Neptune Equester, or Poseidon the Earth-shaker—which cannot all be treated as original simply because later writers juxtapose them.

“God of stored grain” is the prevailing modern interpretation and is plausible, but it is a **modern scholarly reconstruction**, not a surviving ancient definition in precisely those words. The concise current formulation in the *Oxford Classical Dictionary* calls him “a Roman god of the granary” and derives his name from *condere*, “to store”; its confidence should be distinguished from the more cautious position of linguistic studies that regard the derivation as possible rather than certain. [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548723305)

### Name and forms

- **Latin:** *Consus*, genitive *Consī*; inscriptions normally use capitals, **CONSVS**.
- **Festival:** *Consualia*, neuter plural; also *ludi Consuales*, “Consual games.”
- **Temple:** *aedes Consi*, “temple of Consus.”
- **Altar:** *ara Consi*.
- **Greek rendering:** Κῶνσος or Κώνσος in Greek treatments of Roman religion. Dionysius calls the god *Konsos* and attempts Greek equations rather than preserving an independent Greek cult.
- **Italian:** *Conso*.
- **French:** *Consus*.
- **German:** *Consus*.
- **Spanish:** *Consus* or *Conso*.

No securely ancient Sabine or Etruscan spelling of the god’s name is known. Assertions that Consus is “Sabine” or “Etruscan” are hypotheses about origin, not epigraphically demonstrated identifications. No Etruscan mirror securely labels a figure as Consus. Thulin’s work on Etruscan divine groupings and mirrors is relevant to broader Roman–Etruscan comparison, especially the *Di Consentes*, but it does not supply a demonstrable Etruscan Consus.

### Etymologies

#### 1. *Condere*: to put away, store, conceal

**Modern scholarly reconstruction.** This is the dominant modern proposal. Latin *condere* can mean to put together, lay away, store, bury, conceal, or establish. On this reading, *Consus* is an old action- or agent-name connected with the putting away of harvested grain. The earth-covered altar and the calendar position after harvest strengthen the interpretation.

The derivation is attractive but not mechanically certain: phonological and morphological steps have to be reconstructed. Ernout and Meillet reportedly marked it *peut-être*, “perhaps”; more recent criticism notes that scholarship has sometimes treated the agricultural interpretation as proven when it rests partly on circular reasoning—Consus is a storage god because his name derives from storage, and the derivation is accepted because he is assumed to be a storage god. Dan-el Padilla Peralta’s study of Ops emphasizes this methodological weakness. [Study of Ops and agrarian interpretations](https://eprints.whiterose.ac.uk/id/eprint/114534/1/ops%20revised.pdf)

#### 2. *Consilium*, *consulere*: counsel, deliberation

**Ancient learned etymology / learned systematization.** Plutarch, Dionysius, Tertullian, Festus’s tradition, Servius, and Christian apologists preserve or exploit the interpretation of Consus as a god of counsel, especially secret counsel.

Tertullian reports an inscription at the Circus altar:

> *CONSUS CONSILIO, MARS DUELLO, LARES COILLO POTENTES.*

Approximately: “Consus powerful in counsel, Mars in war, the Lares in assembly/intercourse.”

The last term is textually and semantically difficult: *coillo* may represent an archaic form related to *coetus*, “coming together,” rather than the domesticated translations sometimes offered online. Tertullian’s report is literary testimony to an inscription; the stone itself is not extant. The alliteration and archaizing forms may preserve old ritual language, but neither the date nor original wording can be independently verified. [Tertullian, *De spectaculis* 5](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0303.htm)

Servius explicitly says:

> *Consus autem deus est consiliorum* — “Consus is the god of counsels.”

He explains the underground shrine allegorically: counsel ought to be hidden. This is a **late-antique learned systematization**, written roughly eight centuries after Rome’s conventional foundation date. [Servius on *Aeneid* 8.636](https://www.monumenta.ch/latein/anregung.php?domain=&lang=1&s=56339&tabelle=Servius)

Modern historical linguistics generally does not accept similarity to *consilium* as sufficient proof of common derivation. The counsel interpretation is nevertheless genuinely ancient and became part of Consus’s received identity.

#### 3. *Conserere*: to sow

**Ancient or early-modern agrarian etymology, now disputed.** This connects Consus with sowing and with the epithet *Consiva/Consivia* applied to Ops. It suits the December festival near the end of sowing but less obviously explains the August observance after harvest. Modern scholarship generally treats it less favorably than *condere*.

#### 4. “The hidden one”

**Learned interpretation.** Because *condere* can mean conceal or bury, and because the altar was covered, Consus has been interpreted as “the hidden god” or “the buried one.” This may preserve something about ritual representation, but it is not an attested ancient translation of his name.

### Titles and identifications

- **Neptunus Equester**, “Equestrian Neptune”: Livy calls the god honored at Romulus’s games *Neptunus Equester* rather than Consus. This is the most consequential ancient equation.
- **Poseidon Hippios**, “Horse Poseidon”: the Greek interpretive counterpart of Neptune Equester; associated with horse racing.
- **Poseidon Seisichthon**, “Earth-shaking Poseidon”: reported by Dionysius as one proposed interpretation of Consus’s subterranean altar.
- **God of counsels:** Servius and related antiquarian tradition.
- **God of the granary/stored harvest:** dominant modern reconstruction.
- **Chthonic god:** modern classification inferred from the altar’s position; ancient evidence does not show Consus ruling the dead.
- **Agricultural god:** broadly defensible as a functional classification, but not a complete ancient definition.

There is no secure ancient epithet “Consus Granarius,” no surviving canonical genealogy, and no evidence that Consus was a demon, hero, deified human, celestial body, or ruler of an underworld population.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Fasti Antiates Maiores

**Securely attested physical evidence, approximately 84–55 BCE.**

The painted wall calendar from Antium is the oldest surviving Roman calendar from before Caesar’s reform. It gives:

- 21 August: **C[o]ns(ualia)**
- 15 December: **Cons(ualia)**

This establishes two annual observances by the late Republic. Because only the festival abbreviation survives, the calendar says nothing about the altar, Romulus, Neptune, grain storage, animals, or the Sabine women. [Fasti Antiates transcription](https://fr.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fasti_Antiates_maiores)

### Varro, *De lingua Latina* 6.20

**Securely attested late-Republican literary evidence, composed in the 40s BCE.**

Varro states:

> *Consualia dicta a Conso, quod tum feriae publicae ei deo et in circo ad aram eius ab sacerdotibus ludi illi, quibus virgines Sabinae raptae.*

“The Consualia are named from Consus, because then there are public holidays for that god and, in the Circus at his altar, the games conducted by priests at which the Sabine maidens were seized.”

Varro therefore attests:

- Consus as a god;
- public holidays in his honor;
- an altar in the Circus;
- priestly games;
- the tradition connecting those games with the seizure of the Sabine women.

Varro does not in this passage call him a granary god, derive his name from *condere*, say that the altar is underground, or equate him with Neptune. [Latin text](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/varro.ll6.html) [English rendering](https://readancienttexts.com/varro/de_lingva_latina/2)

### Cicero, *De natura deorum*

**Contextual, not substantive evidence, 45 BCE.**

Cicero’s dialogue is central to late-Republican Roman theology and the philosophical rationalization of divine names, but Consus is not a major speaking or narrative figure in it. It should not be cited as evidence for a detailed Consus theology without a precise passage. Its relevance is methodological: it illustrates the same age’s tendency to explain inherited divine names through natural philosophy and etymology. [Perseus catalog and editions](https://catalog.perseus.tufts.edu/catalog/urn%3Acts%3AlatinLit%3Aphi0474.phi050)

### Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 1.9

**Later canonical elaboration, written approximately 27–9 BCE.**

Livy relates that, after neighboring communities refused intermarriage, Romulus concealed his anger and prepared solemn games for **Neptune Equester**, calling them *Consualia*. He invited the Caeninenses, Crustumini, Antemnates, and Sabines. At a signal, Roman men seized unmarried women from the spectators.

Livy’s wording is important: he does not simply say “Consus, also called Neptune.” He names Neptune Equester as recipient while retaining *Consualia* as the games’ name. This can indicate an Augustan-era equation, an alternate annalistic tradition, or an attempt to explain equestrian rites through a more familiar deity.

The narrative is not contemporary evidence for eighth-century BCE events. It is evidence for how Romans of the late first century BCE narrated their origins, marriage system, incorporation of neighbors, and festival. [Early Rome sourcebook containing Livy 1.9](https://warwick.ac.uk/fac/arts/classics/warwickclassicsnetwork/stoa/anchist/gcse/foundations/resources/early_rome_sourcebook_and_key_learning.pdf)

### Virgil, *Aeneid* 8.635–641

**Later canonical elaboration, approximately 20s BCE.**

On Aeneas’s shield, Virgil depicts the Sabine women seized from the crowded seating during the great Circensian games. Consus is not named in the poem’s surviving wording. The passage monumentalizes the episode as Roman foundation history but should not be used independently to establish Consus’s identity. Servius’s much later commentary identifies the games as Consualia and supplies the counsel theology. [Virgil passage and commentary](https://dcc.dickinson.edu/vergil-aeneid/vergil-aeneid-viii-630-670)

### Ovid, *Fasti* 3.199–228

**Augustan literary elaboration, composed circa 2–8 CE.**

Ovid’s surviving *Fasti* covers only January–June. In the March narrative, Mars explains how Romulus obtained wives; Ovid postpones fuller treatment:

> *Consus tibi cetera dicet* — “Consus will tell you the rest.”

This is a forward reference to the August Consualia in the unwritten or lost second half of the poem. Ovid makes **Mars**, not Consus, advise Romulus to arrange a festival and seize women. Thus the popular claim that “Ovid says Consus advised the abduction” is imprecise: Consus is the festival’s future narrator or marker, while Mars supplies the plan in the surviving text. [Ovid, *Fasti* 3](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidFasti3.html)

### Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14

**Augustan literary reception.**

Ovid includes the Sabine conflict within his account of Romulus but does not offer a substantial independent theology of Consus. The work is evidence for the canonical circulation of the foundation narrative, not for Consus’s archaic function.

### Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities* 2.30–31

**Later canonical elaboration plus antiquarian variants, published beginning in 7 BCE.**

Dionysius reports that the Romans of his own day continued the Consualia. He gives unusually valuable ritual observations:

- a subterranean altar near the Circus Maximus was uncovered by removing earth;
- sacrifices, burnt offerings, and first-fruits were offered;
- races were run by chariot teams and single horses.

He then records incompatible explanations:

1. Some equated Consus with Poseidon Seisichthon, the Earth-shaker, and said the underground altar reflected Poseidon’s holding of the earth.
2. Another tradition distinguished the equestrian festival of Neptune from the underground altar, which belonged to an unnamed deity guarding hidden counsels.
3. Dionysius declines to decide: “it is hard to say what the truth is.”

Dionysius is therefore evidence that the god’s interpretation was already disputed in the Augustan period. [Dionysius 2.31](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/e/roman/texts/dionysius_of_halicarnassus/2b%2A.html)

### Fasti Praenestini and other Imperial calendars

**Securely attested calendar evidence, principally Augustan–Tiberian and later.**

The Fasti Praenestini, compiled by Verrius Flaccus and publicly inscribed at Praeneste, preserve *Consualia: feriae Conso*—“Consualia: a festival for Consus.” Other calendars, including the Vallenses and Amiternini, document Consus’s temple anniversary but disagree on its date. [Fasti Praenestini](https://www.attalus.org/docs/cil/add_8.html) [Calendar corpus guide](https://www.attalus.org/docs/cil/calendars.html)

### Plutarch, *Life of Romulus* 14

**Imperial Greek elaboration, late first or early second century CE.**

Plutarch says Romulus announced that he had discovered an underground altar of Consus. He presents several explanations:

- Consus was the god of counsel, connected with *consilium* and *consules*;
- Consus was Neptune in equestrian form;
- the altar was hidden because counsel should be secret.

These are alternatives, not one coherent archaic doctrine. Plutarch also embeds the altar’s “discovery” in Romulus’s stratagem to gather neighboring peoples. [Plutarch, *Romulus*](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Lives/Romulus%2A.html)

### Plutarch, *Roman Questions* 48

**Imperial antiquarian testimony.**

Plutarch asks why horses and asses are garlanded and rested during the Consualia. He proposes:

- honor to Poseidon Hippios;
- rest for agricultural animals after labor;
- a particular association of the ass with the festival.

His question confirms the observed rite more securely than any one of his speculative answers. [Plutarch, *Roman Questions*](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Roman/Texts/Plutarch/Moralia/Roman_Questions%2A/home.html)

### Tacitus, *Annals* 12.24

**Secure Imperial topographical testimony, approximately 115–120 CE.**

In describing Claudius’s inquiry into Rome’s ancient *pomerium*, Tacitus makes the altar of Consus one boundary point associated with the Palatine circuit. This attests the altar’s topographical and antiquarian importance but not the Romulean date claimed for it.

### Tertullian, *De spectaculis* 5

**Hostile Christian witness, approximately 197–202 CE.**

Tertullian attacks spectacles as idolatrous. He says:

- the Consualia were originally games for Neptune because Neptune was also called Consus;
- Consus advised Romulus’s act;
- an altar to Consus lay underground at the first turning-post;
- it carried the *Consus consilio* inscription;
- public priests sacrificed there on 7 July;
- the priest associated with Romulus and the Vestals sacrificed on 21 August.

His polemical purpose is explicit: he wants to stain the games’ origin with sexual violence, hatred, and idolatry. Yet his cultic details are valuable because they concern visible institutions and ritual offices. Claims about motives and moral genealogy require separation from those observations. [Tertullian, English](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/0303.htm) [Latin text](https://www.tertullian.org/latin/de_spectaculis.htm)

### Festus and Paul the Deacon

**Antiquarian lexicographical evidence; Festus second century CE, preserved through an incomplete manuscript and Paul’s eighth-century epitome.**

Festus preserves information about:

- the Consualia;
- the Aventine temple;
- paintings of triumphators in the temples of Consus and Vertumnus;
- etymological or ritual explanations associated with the cult.

Because Festus’s text is damaged and Paul abbreviates it, individual reconstructions must be checked against critical editions such as Lindsay’s *Sexti Pompei Festi De verborum significatu*.

### Servius, Commentary on *Aeneid* 8.636

**Late-antique learned systematization, fourth–fifth century CE.**

Servius calls Consus a god of counsels, treats the covered shrine as an image of secrecy, says the Sabines were seized at rites dedicated to him so that the plan might remain concealed, and identifies him with Equestrian Neptune. His report is indispensable for late-antique reception but too late to establish Consus’s original function by itself. [Servius passage](https://www.monumenta.ch/latein/anregung.php?domain=&lang=1&s=56339&tabelle=Servius)

### Arnobius and Augustine

**Hostile Christian reception, early fourth and early fifth centuries CE.**

Arnobius and Augustine use Roman divine specialization and antiquarian etymology polemically. Their importance lies less in independent evidence for Consus’s early cult than in preserving fragments, categories, and arguments from works such as Varro’s lost *Antiquitates rerum divinarum*. Their testimony must be treated as doubly mediated: pagan antiquarian doctrine selected by Christian opponents for refutation.

Arnobius’s *Adversus nationes* survives in a single medieval manuscript tradition; its catalogues of Roman gods frequently derive from earlier antiquarian material. [Arnobius Latin text](https://www.ancienttexts.org/library/latinlibrary/arnobius7.html)

Augustine’s *City of God* Books 4, 6, and 7 preserves extensive Varronian theology while attacking both its multiplicity and its naturalistic interpretation. Augustine is therefore sometimes the only witness to Varro, but never a neutral copyist. [Augustine, *City of God*](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120108.htm)

### Macrobius

**Late-antique antiquarian context, early fifth century CE.**

Macrobius’s *Saturnalia* is important for the December festival complex and for late-antique attempts to systematize Roman ritual. It does not provide an early independent biography or mythology of Consus. Any claim attributed to it should identify the exact book and section rather than using “Macrobius” as generalized authority. [Macrobius, *Saturnalia*](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Macrobius/Saturnalia/1%2A.html)

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Stored grain and the granary

**Modern scholarly reconstruction, strongly but indirectly supported.**

The case for Consus as guardian or embodiment of stored grain rests on cumulative evidence:

- proposed derivation from *condere*, “store”;
- the hidden or earth-covered altar, comparable to underground grain storage;
- 21 August, near completion of harvest and storage;
- 15 December, near the completion of sowing and the safeguarding of remaining grain;
- festivals of Ops following the Consualia by several days in both August and December;
- agricultural working animals being rested and garlanded.

No surviving archaic hymn says, “Consus, guard our grain.” The reconstruction is persuasive but must not be mistaken for a direct quotation.

### Counsel and secrecy

**Ancient learned systematization, securely attested as an interpretation by the Imperial period.**

The underground altar, the supposed inscription, the resemblance of *Consus* to *consilium*, and the concealed plot against the Sabines produced a coherent theology of hidden counsel. This theology is ancient even if its etymology is incorrect. Consus genuinely became, in Roman literary reception, a divine patron of secret deliberation.

### Horses and draft animals

**Securely attested ritual association.**

Dionysius reports horse and chariot races; Plutarch reports garlanded, resting horses and asses; later sources include mules. The equestrian association is therefore not merely a deduction from Livy’s *Neptunus Equester*. What remains disputed is its explanation:

- Consus was intrinsically equestrian;
- agricultural animals were honored after harvest;
- games transferred Neptune/Poseidon’s horse associations to Consus;
- Consus and Neptune were alternate names for one god;
- the races and underground altar originally belonged to different ritual complexes.

### Chthonic character

**Modern classification with ancient interpretive precedent.**

The buried altar prompted Dionysius’s equation with Earth-shaking Poseidon. Calling Consus “chthonic” is reasonable in the literal sense of earth-related ritual placement. It does not prove that he was a god of death, ghosts, or the underworld. Older reference works sometimes expanded “subterranean” into “infernal” without adequate evidence.

### Form and anthropomorphic image

No securely identified ancient statue, relief, coin type, or vase image of Consus is known. There is consequently no established ancient iconographic type: no securely attested face, clothing, weapon, plant, or animal companion belongs uniquely to him.

The Aventine temple reportedly contained a painted triumphal image of Papirius Cursor, but that is not an image of Consus. Modern illustrations showing a mature man holding grain, standing beside horses, or emerging from the earth are **modern inventions** assembled from inferred domains.

Claims that Consus “was represented by a grain seed” are not securely supported by an identifiable ancient object or primary textual passage.

### Symbols and associations

- **Earth-covered altar:** securely attested in Imperial literary sources.
- **First turning-posts of the Circus Maximus:** securely attested.
- **Horse and chariot racing:** securely attested.
- **Garlanded horses, mules, or asses:** securely attested in Imperial antiquarian tradition.
- **First-fruits:** stated by Dionysius.
- **Grain/storehouse:** modern reconstruction.
- **Colors, numbers, planets, zodiacal signs, gemstones, magical seals:** no ancient evidence found.
- **Sacred day:** 21 August and 15 December; 7 July was a separate sacrifice at the altar according to Tertullian, not a third Consualia securely marked in early calendars.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

There is no surviving ancient account of Consus’s birth, parents, siblings, offspring, or death. He is not incorporated into a Greek-style genealogical family in the extant evidence.

### Consort: Ops?

The pairing of Consus with **Ops Consiva** is a strong modern reconstruction based on:

- paired calendar sequences: Consualia on 21 August followed by Opiconsivia on 25 August; Consualia on 15 December followed by Opalia on 19 December;
- complementary interpretations of stored produce and abundance;
- the epithet *Consiva/Consivia*;
- later scholarly organization of Roman agricultural divinities into functional pairs.

No surviving early narrative explicitly calls Ops “the wife of Consus.” “Consort” should therefore be labeled **modern scholarly reconstruction**, not securely attested genealogy.

### Every documented divine interaction or equation

Because Consus has almost no narrative mythology, the list is short.

- **Neptune / Neptunus Equester — syncretistic identification:** Livy says Romulus’s games were dedicated to Equestrian Neptune and called Consualia. Tertullian says Neptune was also called Consus. Source: Livy 1.9, late first century BCE; Tertullian, *De spectaculis* 5, circa 200 CE.
- **Poseidon Hippios — syncretistic identification:** Plutarch presents Equestrian Neptune/Horse Poseidon as one explanation of Consus and of the animal rites. Source: *Romulus* 14 and *Roman Questions* 48, late first–early second century CE.
- **Poseidon Seisichthon — regional/learned Greek identification:** Dionysius reports that some interpreted Consus as Earth-shaking Poseidon because the god “holds” the earth and received a subterranean altar. Source: *Roman Antiquities* 2.31, 7 BCE.
- **Mars — joint inscription and alternate instigator:** Tertullian’s reported altar inscription juxtaposes “Consus in counsel” with “Mars in war.” In Ovid’s *Fasti*, Mars gives Romulus the plan that later tradition often assigns to Consus. This is not a mythic meeting between Mars and Consus. Sources: Tertullian, circa 200 CE; Ovid, circa 2–8 CE.
- **Lares — joint inscription:** The Lares appear beside Consus and Mars in Tertullian’s reported inscription. No narrative interaction occurs. Source: *De spectaculis* 5.
- **Ops Consiva — calendrical-functional pairing:** festivals of Ops follow each Consualia; modern scholars infer complementary divine functions. No ancient narrative interaction or marriage is securely attested. Evidence: Roman calendars; scholarly synthesis.
- **Quirinus — ritual relation through priesthood:** the Flamen Quirinalis reportedly sacrificed at Consus’s altar on 21 August. This is an institutional interaction between cults, not a narrated meeting of gods. Source: Tertullian, *De spectaculis* 5.
- **Romulus as Quirinus — indirect relation:** later Roman tradition deified Romulus as Quirinus; Consus’s festival was attributed to Romulus, and Quirinus’s flamen later officiated. The sources do not explicitly stage Consus meeting the deified Quirinus.
- **Jupiter Feretrius — sequential mention only:** Tertullian moves from Consus’s games to games instituted for Jupiter Feretrius. No interaction between the gods is recorded.
- **Ceres — modern functional comparison:** both concern grain, but no specific ancient Consus–Ceres encounter is attested.
- **Saturn — modern or late systematizing comparison:** both can be associated with sowing, abundance, or hiddenness, but no securely attested interaction is known.
- **Pales — modern comparative association:** similarities involving animals and summer ritual have been proposed; no direct interaction is attested.

**Finding:** No surviving text records Consus fighting, marrying, seducing, judging, deceiving, rescuing, or being rescued by another named god. His divine relations consist almost entirely of equations, calendrical proximity, priestly connections, and the joint altar inscription.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

### Romulus, the Consualia, and the seizure of the Sabine women

#### Earliest extant form

Varro, in the 40s BCE, gives the earliest surviving explicit Consus connection: priestly games at Consus’s altar were those at which the Sabine maidens were seized.

#### Livy’s version

Rome lacked women and hence a future population. Embassies requesting intermarriage were rejected. Romulus concealed his resentment, announced solemn games for Equestrian Neptune called the Consualia, and invited surrounding peoples. At an agreed signal during the spectacle, Roman men seized unmarried women. Livy differentiates the individual women’s treatment from the collective violence by having Romulus promise lawful marriage and civic status; these speeches are literary constructions, not documentary testimony.

The abduction leads to wars with neighboring communities and finally the Sabine intervention. The women place themselves between their Roman husbands and Sabine fathers and produce political union. The story functions as an origin narrative for Rome’s mixed population and the incorporation of outsiders.

#### Dionysius’s version

Dionysius supplies a more elaborate diplomatic and chronological account. He reports disagreement over whether the seizure occurred in Romulus’s first or fourth year and favors the fourth. He gives the number of seized women as 683 and describes marriage rituals involving fire and water. These figures and details belong to late annalistic reconstruction, not contemporary record.

Dionysius connects the festival to the underground altar but preserves incompatible theological explanations. His hesitation is valuable evidence that no stable explanation commanded universal assent in his day.

#### Plutarch’s version

Romulus announces the discovery of a buried altar, establishes sacrifices and games, and uses the gathering to execute the seizure. Plutarch connects the name Consus with counsel and secrecy while also acknowledging Equestrian Neptune. The concealed altar thus becomes both cult object and symbol of the concealed plan.

#### Ovid’s version

Mars tells Romulus to arrange a spectacle and seize the women. Consus is invoked as the deity or festival through which the rest will later be told. This shifts agency away from Consus and toward Rome’s martial father-god.

#### Virgil’s version

The seizure appears on Aeneas’s shield as one scene in Rome’s providential history. Consus is absent by name. The effect is political and teleological: conflict produces Roman union.

### Historical assessment of the story

The seizure cannot be treated as a documented eighth-century BCE event. The surviving accounts are late-Republican and Augustan or later. They reveal how Romans explained:

- their composite population;
- marriage practices;
- incorporation through conflict;
- the festival’s antiquity;
- the Circus as a foundation-era gathering place.

The story may preserve memories of marriage by capture, intercommunity festival exchange, or political incorporation, but these are hypotheses. It is equally possible that the existing Consualia furnished annalists with a plausible setting for an independently circulating foundation legend.

The modern phrase “rape of the Sabine women” uses “rape” in its older sense of abduction, from Latin *rapere*, but the story entails coercive seizure and forced marriage. Ancient authors sometimes mitigate the episode through speeches and later reconciliation; those literary mitigations do not remove the violence described.

### Discovery of the buried altar

Plutarch says Romulus claimed to have found Consus’s altar underground. The story explains both the altar’s location and the institution of the festival. It is an **Imperial etiological elaboration**, not an archaeologically verified discovery account.

### Consus advises Romulus

Tertullian and Servius state or imply that Consus supplied or governed the secret plan. Plutarch makes the god’s name and hidden altar symbolize counsel. Ovid instead has Mars advise Romulus. Thus:

- **Consus as adviser:** Imperial and late-antique learned tradition.
- **Mars as adviser:** Augustan poetic version.
- **Romulus as strategist without divine adviser:** Livy’s effective narrative presentation.
- **Neptune as festival recipient:** Livy.

No single version should be made canonical.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### The Circus Maximus altar

The *ara Consi* stood near the **primae metae**, normally understood as the first turning-posts at the southeastern end of the Circus Maximus’s central barrier. Ancient and modern topographies differ in reconstructing the exact orientation.

The evidence describes it either as subterranean or as normally covered with earth and exposed by removing the covering. Those descriptions need not mean a deep underground chamber. “The underground altar” is convenient shorthand, but a covered sunken altar is also compatible with the texts.

The altar is mentioned by Varro, Dionysius, Plutarch, Tacitus, Tertullian, and Servius. No excavated structure has been universally accepted as its remains. A brick receptacle found in 1825 was proposed as its enclosure, but the identification remains speculative. [Platner and Ashby, *Ara Consi*](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Places/Europe/Italy/Lazio/Roma/Rome/_Texts/PLATOP%2A/Ara_Consi.html)

### Ritual schedule

#### 21 August: Consualia

**Securely attested.**

- Public festival for Consus.
- Altar uncovered.
- Sacrifice by the Flamen Quirinalis with Vestal participation, according to Tertullian.
- Horse, individual-horse, mule, ass, or chariot races, depending on source and period.
- Agricultural animals rested and garlanded.
- First-fruit and burnt offerings according to Dionysius.
- Traditional date of the seizure of the Sabine women.

#### 15 December: Consualia

**Securely attested in calendars; less fully described in narrative sources.**

The December observance likely belonged to the sowing/storage cycle, but details are poorly preserved. It should not automatically be assumed that every August rite was duplicated identically in December.

#### 7 July: sacrifice at the altar

Tertullian says *sacerdotes publici*, public priests, sacrificed at the altar on the Nones of July. This is a separate rite, not securely called a Consualia in the earliest calendars. Older topographical works list July 7 alongside the two principal exposures, but the evidence comes principally from Tertullian.

### Priests

- **Flamen Quirinalis:** officiated at the August rite according to Tertullian.
- **Vestal Virgins:** assisted or participated.
- **Sacerdotes publici:** officiated on 7 July.
- **Pontiffs:** some later summaries make them presidents of the games, but exact ancient sourcing should be checked in each instance.
- **No flamen Consualis is attested.**

The involvement of the Flamen Quirinalis and Vestals marks the cult as public and embedded in Rome’s central priestly system. It does not prove Consus was subordinate to Quirinus or identical with Romulus.

### Animals

The animals were primarily working and racing animals:

- horses;
- mules;
- asses;
- possibly other draft animals by generalization.

They received garlands and rest. Sources emphasize races rather than animal sacrifice. No evidence was found for human sacrifice, gladiatorial killing offered specifically to Consus, or the killing of the garlanded work animals.

### Offerings

Dionysius names sacrifices, burnt offerings, and first-fruits. The exact victim species and liturgical formula are not preserved. Claims that a specific grain, wine, incense, or animal was mandatory should therefore be marked unestablished.

### The Aventine temple

An **aedes Consi** stood on the Aventine. It was probably vowed or built by a Papirius Cursor following a triumph, often identified with Lucius Papirius Cursor in 272 BCE. Festus’s fragment reports that Papirius Cursor was painted in triumphal dress in Consus’s temple, parallel to Fulvius Flaccus in Vertumnus’s temple.

The temple’s dedication anniversary is inconsistent in surviving calendars:

- Fasti Vallenses: 21 August;
- Fasti Amiternini: 12 December.

Platner and Ashby suggested an Augustan restoration could explain the discrepancy, but this remains a hypothesis. [Platner and Ashby, *Aedes Consi*](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Places/Europe/Italy/Lazio/Roma/Rome/_Texts/PLATOP%2A/Aedes_Consi.html)

The Aventine temple and Circus altar were distinct cult sites. Older scholarship sometimes blurred them.

### Votive and epigraphic evidence

Consus has a remarkably slight independent votive record. The strongest epigraphic evidence consists of calendar entries, not a large body of private dedications. Tertullian’s reported altar inscription cannot substitute for the lost original.

No significant corpus of theophoric personal names derived securely from Consus is known. This is negative evidence: his public festival was durable, but private naming and dedicatory practices do not demonstrate mass personal devotion.

### Cult economics and politics

No temple treasury, landholding archive, priestly privilege list, or offering tariff for Consus survives. Politically, however, the festival was integrated into Rome’s foundation story: a public spectacle attracts neighbors, enables appropriation of women, produces war, and ends in incorporation. Augustan literature used this story to situate conflict and mixture at the beginning of Roman identity.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Hypothetical archaic phase

The two calendar dates, unusual covered altar, involvement of ancient priesthoods, and association with agricultural animals strongly suggest an old cult. Calling it “Romulean” is traditional, not demonstrable. No eighth- or seventh-century BCE inscription names Consus.

Possible original functions include:

- storage of grain;
- completion of harvest and sowing;
- protection or ritual release of work animals;
- a localized earth power;
- equestrian games.

The evidence cannot securely select only one.

### Middle Republic

A temple on the Aventine was probably established in connection with Papirius Cursor’s triumph in 272 BCE. If correct, this brought Consus from a distinctive Circus altar into conventional monumental temple cult. The triumphal painting also connected the sanctuary with elite commemoration.

### Late Republic

The Fasti Antiates provide the first surviving physical attestation. Varro documents the public festival, altar, priestly games, and Sabine legend. By this stage the cult was old enough to require antiquarian explanation.

### Augustan age

This is the richest phase of surviving literary reception:

- Livy associates the Consualia with Neptune Equester and the Sabine seizure.
- Virgil places the seizure on Aeneas’s shield.
- Ovid makes Mars advise Romulus and promises later treatment under Consus.
- Dionysius describes contemporary rites and competing interpretations.
- Verrius Flaccus monumentalizes the festival in the Fasti Praenestini.

The evidence for a “peak” is therefore a peak of documentation and ideological reuse, not proof that Consus received more worshippers than before.

### Early and High Empire

Plutarch continues antiquarian debate. Tacitus uses the altar in reconstructing sacred topography. The Circus remained monumentalized, and equestrian spectacles could sustain the Neptune equation even if the original agrarian meaning had become obscure.

### Christian polemical phase

Tertullian, Arnobius, and Augustine treat Roman cult through Christian apologetic goals. Tertullian’s attack on spectacles preserves especially valuable ritual information while condemning the Consualia’s connection to abduction and idolatry.

Consus’s cult was not singled out in any surviving suppression decree. Rather, it would have been affected by the broader fourth-century restrictions on sacrifice, temple funding, and public non-Christian rites. No evidence identifies a precise final Consualia or a documented destruction of the altar by Christians.

### Late antiquity

Servius preserves an allegorical theology of secrecy and the Neptune identification. Macrobius and related antiquarian literature place the festival in learned reconstructions of the Roman calendar. The cult as public performance disappears; the god survives in books.

### Medieval and Renaissance transmission

Consus persisted chiefly through manuscripts of Livy, Virgil and Servius, Plutarch, Tertullian, Augustine, Festus/Paul, and Ovid. Medieval readers were more likely to encounter him as an exemplum of pagan error, secret counsel, or Roman history than as an object of worship.

Renaissance antiquarians revived interest in Roman topography, calendars, and the Circus. The association between Consus, political counsel, and secrecy became especially usable in emblem literature. Juan de Solórzano Pereira’s seventeenth-century political emblems employ Consus as a figure for prudent, concealed counsel—an **early-modern allegorical reinvention** grounded in Servius and Plutarch, not revived ancient cult. [Study of Solórzano’s Consus emblem](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/309311890_El_dios_romano_Conso_en_el_emblema_XLVII_de_Juan_de_Solorzano)

### Modern period

Classical handbooks increasingly defined Consus as an agrarian or storage god and treated the counsel theology as secondary wordplay. Wissowa systematized the calendar and cult; Fowler interpreted the festival through agricultural seasons; Dumézil integrated Consus into a structural account of archaic Roman religion; later scholars have questioned how readily transparent agrarian functions may be projected backward.

Modern Roman reconstructionists may observe the Consualia, but no reliable demographic evidence isolates the number of Consus worshippers. There is no documented continuous community preserving his rites from antiquity to the present.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Scholarship and popular mythology

Nineteenth- and early twentieth-century handbooks consolidated a standard portrait: Consus was an ancient Italian agricultural god, specifically guardian of stored grain, whose subterranean altar symbolized seed or grain underground and whose horse races caused later identification with Neptune.

This portrait remains plausible, but several components are reconstructions:

- “granary god” depends substantially on etymology and calendar;
- the altar’s agricultural symbolism is inferred;
- the Neptune identification may not be merely a late misunderstanding;
- Ops as wife or consort is not explicitly narrated;
- chthonic does not mean underworld ruler.

### Occult and esoteric reception

The dedicated contested-material search found no substantial Renaissance magical, Hermetic, Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or grimoire tradition centered on Consus. He has no standard planetary seal, angelic correspondence, conjuration, or role in the major Solomonic grimoires.

Modern websites sometimes extend “hidden counsel” into claims that Consus governs intuition, covert planning, secret knowledge, or subconscious insight. Unless explicitly presented as new devotional theology, these are **modern extrapolations**, not recovered ancient doctrine.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist practice

Modern practitioners of *religio Romana* sometimes reconstruct the Consualia from calendars and literary evidence. Plausible reconstructed acts include:

- uncovering a temporary altar;
- offerings of grain or first-fruits;
- resting or honoring working animals;
- prayers for prudent storage or counsel.

Exact ancient prayers, sacrificial formulae, colors, incense recipes, and domestic altar arrangements have not survived. Any modern rite supplying them is necessarily reconstructive or innovative.

### Literature, games, and commercial uses

Consus has not acquired a major independent modern literary, film, comics, or game persona comparable to Mars, Neptune, or Venus. His principal popular afterlife remains as a supporting entry in mythological dictionaries and as the divine name attached to the Sabine episode.

The name has been used for unrelated modern technical projects, including the distributed transaction protocol “Consus.” Such usage is nominal borrowing, not reception of ancient cult theology.

### Political appropriation

The Sabine-women story has repeatedly been used in art and political discourse about state formation, marriage, conquest, and the absorption of defeated peoples. Consus himself rarely functions as the focus of nationalist appropriation.

No documented Consus-centered extremist cult or significant fascist, racialist, or ethnonationalist appropriation was found. General attempts to appropriate Roman religion politically should not be attributed to Consus without specific evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Consus originally a storage god?

**Majority reconstruction:** Yes. *Condere*, the calendar, earth covering, and Ops sequence form a coherent pattern.

**Critical position:** The argument risks circularity and rests on an uncertain etymology. The surviving Romans themselves did not preserve a universally agreed original meaning. The storage interpretation should be described as the strongest current reconstruction, not an attested archaic creed.

### Was he a god of counsel?

**Ancient reception:** Certainly by the Imperial period. The counsel theology is attested by Plutarch, Tertullian, and Servius and may draw upon older inscriptional language.

**Original function:** Uncertain. Linguistic resemblance to *consilium* is insufficient. The Sabine stratagem and hidden altar may have generated or reinforced the interpretation.

### Was Consus Neptune Equester?

Three positions occur:

1. **Identity:** Livy and Tertullian can be read as treating Consus as another name or form of Neptune.
2. **Interpretatio:** Greek and Roman writers equated a local god with Poseidon/Neptune because of horse racing.
3. **Ritual layering:** Neptune received the equestrian spectacle while the hidden altar belonged to Consus or an unnamed guardian of secrets.

Dionysius explicitly records uncertainty. A responsible dossier must preserve that uncertainty.

### Did the altar symbolize underground granaries?

This is plausible and widely repeated but nowhere securely explained in those terms by an early source. Alternative ancient explanations include secrecy and Poseidon’s subterranean earth power. The physical form of the altar may precede all surviving explanations.

### Did the Consualia commemorate the Sabine seizure?

Ancient tradition linked them. Whether the festival originated to commemorate the event is another matter. The more economical historical inference is that an existing festival supplied a setting for the foundation story; proof is impossible.

### The Aventine temple’s foundation and anniversary

Its connection with Papirius Cursor and 272 BCE is reconstructed from Festus’s triumphal painting. Calendar disagreement between 21 August and 12 December may reflect restoration, rededication, copying error, or multiple commemorations. No solution is certain.

### The July rite

Tertullian’s July 7 sacrifice is often described as a third annual uncovering. It is safer to say that Tertullian attests a separate sacrifice at the same altar. The two Consualia remain the securely calendrical festivals.

### Archaeological status

The Circus altar has not been conclusively identified. Modern maps place it from literary topography. Proposed discoveries are hypotheses. The Aventine temple’s exact archaeological footprint is likewise uncertain.

### State of primary evidence

- **Calendars:** preserved in fragments and edited in *CIL* I² and *Inscriptiones Italiae* 13.2.
- **Tertullian’s inscription:** known only through his literary quotation.
- **Varro’s divine antiquities:** lost; fragments survive in Augustine and other authors.
- **Festus:** damaged eleventh-century Farnese manuscript plus Paul the Deacon’s epitome.
- **Servius:** complicated manuscript tradition, including expanded material conventionally called Servius Danielis.
- **Material image corpus:** no securely identified Consus iconography suitable for a substantive *LIMC*-style iconographic sequence.

### Major modern approaches

- **Georg Wissowa:** organized Consus within Roman state cult and agricultural ritual; foundational but sometimes more confident about original functions than the evidence allows.
- **W. Warde Fowler:** emphasized seasonal agricultural interpretation and the sequence of Roman festivals. [Fowler, *Roman Festivals*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/59007/59007-h/59007-h.htm)
- **Kurt Latte:** treated the cult within the institutional and historical development of Roman religion.
- **Georges Dumézil:** integrated Consus, Ops, Quirinus, and the agricultural cycle into a structural account of archaic Roman religion.
- **Mary Beard, John North, and Simon Price:** shifted emphasis from fixed divine “personalities” to ritual, institutions, historical change, and the politics of Roman religious knowledge.
- **John Scheid:** stresses performance, civic institutions, and the need to avoid translating Roman ritual into modern belief-system categories.
- **Michael Lipka:** treats Roman gods as conceptually differentiated through place, function, ritual, and relations rather than through biographies.
- **Recent critical work:** questions automatic agrarian etymologies and the retrospective construction of tidy divine couples.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Consus had a securely known Etruscan name and image: **not established**.
- An ancient statue portrayed him as a man holding grain: **not established**.
- He was literally a grain seed: **not securely attested**.
- Ops was explicitly his wife in ancient myth: **not established**.
- He ruled the Roman underworld: **unsupported expansion of “chthonic.”**
- Ovid says Consus personally ordered the abduction: **incorrect; Mars gives the advice in the surviving *Fasti*.**
- His altar has been definitively excavated: **not established**.
- The altar was uncovered only twice annually: **incomplete if Tertullian’s July rite is accepted, though only two observances were called Consualia.**
- He had an ancient occult correspondence system: **modern invention**.
- Human sacrifice, temple prostitution, or a Consus-specific violent priesthood: **no evidence found**.
