# Pax: Research Dossier

**Class:** Roman goddess or divinized abstraction/personification of negotiated peace, civic order, and the prosperity produced by victorious settlement; under the Principate especially an imperial *dea/numen* expressed as **Pax Augusta**.  
**Earliest secure attestation:** a quinarius of the moneyer L. Aemilius Buca, struck at Rome in **44 BCE**, bearing a female head explicitly labelled **PAXS** (an archaizing spelling of *Pax*), RRC 480/24. Earlier Republican coins use symbols later associated with peace, but none securely names or unmistakably personifies Pax. The earliest securely documented monumental state cult is the **Ara Pacis Augustae**, constituted by senatorial decree on 4 July 13 BCE and dedicated on 30 January 9 BCE.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early attested]** Direct Republican or Augustan textual, numismatic, epigraphic, calendrical, or archaeological evidence.
- **[Imperial canonical]** Widely disseminated imperial-period cult, literature, coins, or official ideology.
- **[Regional variant]** Provincial or locally modified expression.
- **[Learned systematization]** Antiquarian, philosophical, grammatical, or mythographic classification rather than proof of an old cult.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** Historical equation with another figure, especially Greek Eirene.
- **[Late accretion]** Post-classical development not securely present in ancient cult.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** Scholarly inference from incomplete evidence.
- **[Modern invention]** A recent attribution presented without ancient documentation.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### The figure

Pax is the feminine embodiment of Latin *pax*: not originally “pacifism,” universal nonviolence, or merely an inward state, but a binding settlement or ordered relationship. In Roman political usage peace normally followed negotiation, submission, or victory. Imperial Pax therefore could carry two inseparable messages: cessation of civil or foreign war and the coercive Roman power that made that cessation possible. The olive branch and cornucopia visualize the benign result; trophies, captive weapons, or Victory sometimes disclose its military premise.

Unlike Jupiter, Mars, or Ceres, Pax has almost no independent mythic personality. Her record is principally numismatic, monumental, ritual, poetic, and political. Her “life” is the transformation of a common noun into a labelled divine image and state cult, followed by its deployment as an imperial claim.

### Names and forms

| Form | Language/date | Meaning and status |
|---|---|---|
| **pax, pacis** | Latin, archaic onward | “Compact, settlement, peace.” Feminine noun. Its semantic family includes *paciscor*, “make an agreement,” *pactum*, “compact,” and *pacare*, “bring to peace/subdue.” |
| **PAXS** | Latin coin legend, 44 BCE | Archaizing spelling on L. Aemilius Buca’s quinarius; the first securely labelled Roman image of the goddess. **[Early attested]** |
| **Pax** | Latin literary and cult name | The personified or divine Peace. Ovid directly addresses her as a goddess in *Fasti* 1.709–724. |
| **Pax Augusta** | Latin, 13 BCE onward | “Augustan/August Peace.” *Augusta* can mean venerable, consecrated, and associated with the emperor and his power; it need not mean simply “the peace belonging personally to Augustus.” **[Early attested; imperial canonical]** |
| **Pax Augusti** | Imperial Latin, especially coins | “The Peace of Augustus/the emperor.” The possessive formulation more explicitly ties peace to the reigning emperor. |
| **Pax Aeterna** | Imperial coinage and inscriptions | “Eternal Peace,” an imperial promise rather than evidence of a separate goddess. |
| **Pax Perpetua** | Later imperial usage | “Perpetual Peace”; again an epithet or political claim, not a separately documented mythic individual. |
| **Pax Publica / Pax Populi Romani** | Roman political-religious language | Public or Roman civic peace; attestations vary and must not automatically be treated as formal cult titles. |
| **Εἰρήνη, Eirēnē** | Greek | Greek Peace, an older goddess and one of the Horai in Hesiod. Greek authors could render Roman Pax as Eirene, but the Roman cult is not simply proof that every Greek story about Eirene was transferred to Pax. **[Syncretistic identification]** |
| **Ara Pacis Augustae** | Official Latin | “Altar of Augustan Peace.” The genitive identifies the divine recipient or consecrated abstraction. |
| **Templum Pacis / τέμενος Εἰρήνης** | Latin/Greek, Flavian era onward | Vespasian’s Temple or precinct of Peace, later commonly called the Forum of Peace. |

The etymology connects *pax* with the Indo-European root conventionally reconstructed as \**pak-*/*\**pag-*—“fasten,” hence “bind by agreement”—also represented by Latin *pangere* and *pactum*. The exact prehistoric morphology is reconstructed, not directly attested. The older Roman sense was therefore closer to an enforceable compact than to the modern ethical ideal of total nonviolence.

### Eirene and the supposed genealogy of Pax

Hesiod’s *Theogony* 901–906, composed approximately 700 BCE, calls Greek Eirene a daughter of Zeus and Themis and sister of Dike and Eunomia. This is securely ancient evidence for **Eirene**, not for an indigenous Roman genealogy of Pax. Roman art and Augustan ideology drew on Greek Eirene imagery, making the equation historically important. Nevertheless:

- “Pax is the daughter of Jupiter and Justitia” is best classified as **[modern harmonization/invention]** when stated as native Roman scripture.
- It is a Romanized translation of the Hesiodic parentage of Eirene—Zeus → Jupiter, Themis or Dike → Justitia—and sometimes incorrectly substitutes Dike/Justitia for Eirene’s mother Themis.
- No surviving Roman cult text gives Pax an independent Roman birth, spouse, or offspring.

### Etruscan and Italic origin claims

No securely identified Etruscan goddess can presently be demonstrated as Pax’s direct prototype. Etruscan mirrors provide valuable evidence for Greek and Italic divine iconography, but no labelled mirror establishes a native Etruscan Pax genealogy. Claims that Pax descends from an unnamed Etruscan peace-goddess are **[modern speculation]**.

The absence matters: the oldest explicit Roman Pax is late Republican, while the artistic vocabulary of Greek Eirene was already centuries old.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The first explicit evidence: L. Aemilius Buca’s coin

In 44 BCE the moneyer L. Aemilius Buca issued a quinarius whose obverse shows a female head with **PAXS**; associated types use clasped hands and the caduceus. The imagery presents reconciliation and agreement during the terminal crisis of Julius Caesar’s dictatorship and assassination. It proves that Pax could be imaged and named as a female personification by 44 BCE. It does **not** prove an archaic temple, priesthood, mythology, or continuous cult extending into early Rome. Modern numismatic research treats it as the first explicit labelled coin image of Pax.

### Republican conceptual background

Before 44 BCE, *pax* was a major legal, diplomatic, and religious concept:

- *Pax deorum/deum* meant a right or harmonious relationship between Rome and the gods, maintained through correct ritual.
- Treaties and settlements made *pax* a product of authorized agreement.
- *Pacare* could mean both “pacify” and “subdue.”

Livy’s references to seeking or restoring the *pax deum* concern relations with the collective gods, not necessarily worship of a goddess Pax. Likewise, the absence of Pax from many Republican catalogues of established gods cautions against retrojecting the Augustan cult into the regal or early Republican period.

### Cicero

Cicero’s *De natura deorum* was written in 45 BCE, almost exactly at the moment the named coin image emerged. In Book 2 Cicero discusses gods formed from beneficial powers and abstractions; his examples include such figures as Fides, Mens, Virtus, and Concordia. Pax is not developed as an established mythological personality. The treatise supplies the intellectual environment in which useful qualities might receive divine names and cult, but it is not evidence for a detailed Pax mythology. **[Late Republican learned systematization]**

### Varro through Augustine

Varro’s *Antiquitates rerum divinarum*, composed in the first century BCE, is lost except through fragments and later citations, particularly Augustine’s *De civitate Dei*. Augustine mocks the Roman multiplication of functional deities and divine abstractions, while preserving otherwise lost Varronian classifications. Because this transmission is hostile and selective, it establishes that Roman antiquarians systematized numerous divine functions, but it cannot safely furnish a pre-Augustan biography or genealogy for Pax unless a precise fragment names her. **Finding:** no surviving Varronian fragment establishes an archaic independent cult of Pax.

### Augustan documentary core

#### *Res Gestae Divi Augusti* 12

Augustus records that, after his return from Spain and Gaul in the consulship of Tiberius Nero and Publius Quintilius Varus, the Senate decreed an altar of **Pax Augusta** in the Campus Martius and ordered magistrates, priests, and Vestal Virgins to perform an annual sacrifice there.

The text was composed late in Augustus’s reign and publicly copied after his death; the best-preserved version is the bilingual Monumentum Ancyranum at Ankara. It is first-person imperial testimony and therefore interested testimony, but it is confirmed by calendars and archaeology.

#### The *Fasti* calendars

The *Fasti Praenestini*, compiled under Verrius Flaccus in the early imperial period and preserved on marble, record 30 January as a public holiday because the Ara Pacis Augustae was dedicated in the Campus Martius during the consulship of Drusus and Crispinus—9 BCE. Other calendar traditions, including the Caeretan and Verulan fasti, corroborate the dedication. Calendar evidence also preserves the **constitutio** on 4 July 13 BCE.

These are direct epigraphic witnesses to the cult’s position in the civic year.

#### Ovid, *Fasti* 1.709–724

Written in the early first century CE, Ovid’s calendar poem reaches the altar on the second day before February and invokes Pax directly:

> *Pax, ades et toto mitis in orbe mane* — “Peace, be present and remain gentle throughout the whole world.”

She is crowned with Actian foliage; Ovid says peace nourishes Ceres and the farmer, asks that soldiers bear arms only to restrain arms, and imagines Rome loved by lands that once feared it. The poem is simultaneously hymn, calendrical notice, and Augustan political poetry. It is the closest surviving thing to a literary hymn to Roman Pax.

### Virgil

Virgil does not provide a narrative myth of Pax as a goddess. His *Eclogues*, *Georgics*, and *Aeneid* make peace, restored agriculture, closed gates of war, and a golden age central themes. *Aeneid* 1.291–296 predicts harsh ages softened, Faith and Vesta honored, Quirinus giving law, and the Gates of War closed; *Aeneid* 6 and 8 place Roman rule and imposed order within providential history. These passages are crucial ideological context but should not be rewritten as episodes in which a named goddess Pax acts. **[Augustan literary ideology, not Pax mythology]**

### Ovid, *Metamorphoses*

The *Metamorphoses* uses *pax* as peace, reconciliation, or absence of conflict, but supplies no sustained mythic cycle for Pax comparable to those of Venus, Diana, or Minerva. Assertions that the work contains a full “myth of Pax” are unsupported.

### Later antiquarian and commentary traditions

Festus, Servius, and Macrobius preserve Roman ritual vocabulary, calendar lore, interpretations of Virgil, and associations between peace, Janus, agriculture, and imperial order. They are valuable for reception and learned systematization but preserve no independent birth-to-adventure myth of Pax.

Arnobius, Augustine, and other Christian apologists are hostile witnesses to Roman divine personifications. Their polemic confirms that Romans could treat qualities and social goods as gods; it must not be accepted uncritically as a neutral inventory of what every Roman believed.

### Flavian and later literary evidence

Josephus, *Jewish War* 7.158–162, describes Vespasian’s magnificent Temple of Peace and the deposition there of spoils taken from the Jerusalem Temple, except for the Law and the purple hangings kept in the palace. Pliny the Elder lists famous artworks housed in the complex; Suetonius dates Vespasian’s dedication; Cassius Dio and Herodian describe its later history and catastrophic fire. Collectively these sources show that the precinct was temple, victory monument, museum, garden, archive, and dynastic statement.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

Pax governs or embodies:

- treaty and settled relations;
- cessation of civil conflict;
- order secured after victory;
- agricultural recovery;
- commercial movement and abundance;
- imperial stability;
- the harmonious relationship expressed in *pax deorum*, although that phrase does not necessarily personify her.

Her key historical transformation is from a negotiated condition to a divine imperial benefit. Under Augustus, Pax becomes something the ruler restores; under later emperors, coin legends make it something the emperor possesses, gives, renews, or promises.

### Standard iconography

On coins and reliefs Pax normally appears as a mature, draped woman, standing or seated. Her principal attributes are:

- **Olive branch:** the most persistent identifier of peaceful settlement.
- **Cornucopia:** abundance made possible by order.
- **Grain ears:** agriculture and food security; also an overlap with Ceres.
- **Caduceus:** negotiation, concord, and commerce; shared with Mercury and Felicitas, so not sufficient alone for identification.
- **Sceptre:** sovereign or divinely sanctioned order.
- **Torch applied to arms or a trophy:** peace demonstrated by destroying or disabling the instruments of war.
- **Victoriola or Victory:** peace as the product of military success.
- **Laurel or Actian foliage:** Augustan victory, particularly Actium.
- **Globe:** universal scope.
- **Children and fruits:** prosperity and generational renewal, chiefly where Pax overlaps with Eirene, Tellus, Ceres, Venus, or Italia.

There is no securely exclusive sacred animal, color, number, planet, or weekday for Pax. Modern tables assigning her a fixed color, crystal, zodiacal sign, incense, or planet are **[modern occult or devotional invention]** unless explicitly presented as contemporary practice.

### The Ara Pacis image problem

The famous southeast relief of the Ara Pacis shows a seated, veiled mother with two children, fruits in her lap, livestock, luxuriant vegetation, and two female figures riding a bird and sea creature. Identifications proposed include:

- Tellus/Terra Mater;
- Italia;
- Venus Genetrix;
- Ceres;
- Pax Augusta;
- a deliberately polyvalent fusion of several.

Nancy de Grummond argued for Pax Augusta, noting grain, poppies, fruit, children, and parallels with Eirene. Barbette Spaeth emphasized Ceres. Other scholars retain Tellus or favor purposeful polysemy. The museum itself presents Tellus, Venus, and Pax as competing readings.

Therefore “this is a surviving portrait of Pax” is **[contested modern identification]**, not an inscriptionally secured fact. Its imagery unquestionably communicates the fecundity and abundance of peace, but the central figure is unlabelled.

The attendants have likewise been called Aurae, Nymphs, or Horae. Identifying them as the Horae would strengthen the Eirene/Pax reading, but remains disputed.

### Securely identified coin images

A safer iconographic corpus is provided by labelled coins. The 44 BCE **PAXS** quinarius names her. Octavianic and imperial issues then combine her with olive, cornucopia, caduceus, sceptre, Victory, or burned arms. A Vespasianic aureus in the Metropolitan Museum, for example, shows seated Pax holding an olive branch and winged caduceus. Domitianic provincial issues explicitly label Pax while showing her with olive and cornucopia.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

Roman sources preserve no independent genealogy for Pax.

| Version | Evidence | Assessment |
|---|---|---|
| No stated parents, consort, or children | Roman cult, calendars, coins, Ovid | The secure Roman position. |
| Daughter of Zeus and Themis; sister of Dike and Eunomia | Hesiod, *Theogony* 901–906, concerning Eirene | **[Greek early attested]**, transferred to Pax only through later identification. |
| Daughter of Jupiter and Justitia | Modern popular summaries | **[Modern harmonization/invention]**; it mistranslates or compresses the Greek genealogy. |
| One of the Horae | Greek Eirene in Hesiod and later art | **[Syncretistic identification]**, not securely a native Roman classification of Pax. |

### Every documented divine interaction or joint appearance

Because Pax has almost no narrative mythology, the list is short. Mere membership in Roman religion is not an interaction.

**Negative finding:** no securely ancient text records Pax marrying, bearing children, seducing another deity, fighting a monster, stealing an object, judging a divine dispute, or undertaking an adventure.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Pax has no surviving autonomous mythic cycle. Her major “narratives” are ritual-political constructions.

### A. The advent of Pax after Actium

**[Early attested/imperial canonical]**

The Augustan story runs from civil war through victory to closure and fertility. Octavian defeats Antony and Cleopatra at Actium in 31 BCE; civil war ends after Alexandria in 30 BCE; the Gates of Janus are closed; agriculture, religion, law, and family succession revive. Poets render the settlement as the return of a golden age.

The fullest direct invocation is Ovid’s *Fasti* 1.709–724: Pax arrives crowned with Actian leaves, remains gentle throughout the world, feeds Ceres, and becomes a glory greater than triumph. The paradox is deliberate: the goddess who ends triumphs wears the foliage of victory.

This is not a myth in which Pax personally fights at Actium. It is a state narrative turning military victory and monarchical stabilization into divine peace.

### B. The Ara Pacis foundation narrative

**[Early attested]**

Augustus returned from reorganizing Spain and Gaul in 13 BCE. The Senate decreed an altar of Pax Augusta in the Campus Martius, where magistrates, priests, and Vestals would sacrifice annually. The *constitutio* was commemorated on 4 July; the completed altar was dedicated on 30 January 9 BCE.

Later claims that 30 January was deliberately selected solely because it was Livia’s birthday are plausible but not proved by the dedication formula itself. The calendrical coincidence is real; the motive is an inference.

The monument’s processional friezes integrate Augustus’s household, priestly colleges, civic magistrates, Roman foundation legends, conquered arms, and natural fecundity. Its operative narrative is that dynastic continuity, correct ritual, Roman victory, and abundant nature converge in Pax Augusta.

### C. Pax and the closed Gates of Janus

**[Augustan canonical]**

Augustus states in *Res Gestae* 13 that the Gates of Janus Quirinus—closed when peace had been secured by victories throughout Roman dominions—were closed three times during his principate, compared with only twice in all preceding history according to ancestral memory.

The gate-closing is a rite and political claim, not a personal encounter between Janus and Pax. Later summaries often turn it into a simple myth that “Pax arrived when Janus shut his doors.” The evidence supports association, not such a literal scene.

### D. Flavian peace after the Jewish War

**[Imperial canonical; politically contested]**

Vespasian’s dynasty represented its victory in the Jewish War and the end of the civil wars of 68–69 CE as restored peace. Between 71 and 75 CE Vespasian built and dedicated the Templum Pacis. Josephus reports that spoils from the Jerusalem Temple were displayed there. Thus Roman Pax was materially constituted through another people’s defeat, the destruction of Jerusalem, mass death and enslavement, and the transfer of sacred objects to Rome.

The monument’s “peace” was not neutral cessation; it was victorious Flavian order. That political violence is directly documented and should not be hidden behind the benign personification.

### E. Pax Romana

The expression *Romana pax* is ancient—Seneca uses it—but the familiar period label “Pax Romana,” commonly applied to the roughly two centuries from Augustus to Marcus Aurelius, is principally a later historiographical convention. Ancient imperial peace did not mean an absence of war: frontier campaigning, annexation, punitive expeditions, revolt suppression, enslavement, and civil violence continued.

Accordingly, Pax Romana is best understood as relative internal stability and an imperial ideology of ordered rule, not a literal empire-wide absence of armed conflict.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Ara Pacis Augustae

- **Location:** northern Campus Martius, beside the Via Flaminia and near the legal-religious boundary at which returning commanders shifted from military to civic space.
- **Constitutio:** 4 July 13 BCE.
- **Dedication:** 30 January 9 BCE.
- **Authority:** the Roman Senate, not a private foundation by Augustus.
- **Personnel:** magistrates, priests, and Vestal Virgins.
- **Rite:** annual sacrifice, explicitly required by the senatorial decree as reported in *Res Gestae* 12.
- **Victims:** the inner altar frieze includes sacrificial procession and animals, but reconstruction of the exact annual sacrificial prescription is incomplete.
- **Evidence:** Augustus’s inscription, Ovid, inscribed calendars, the marble architecture and reliefs.

The altar was an open-air sacrificial installation within a carved marble precinct. It had no documented autonomous priesthood of Pax. Ordinary private votive practice at this altar is poorly attested compared with the public state ceremony.

### Calendrical festivals

Two anniversaries belong to the monument:

- **4 July:** establishment or *constitutio* of the altar in 13 BCE.
- **30 January:** dedication in 9 BCE; the *Fasti Praenestini* mark it as a public holiday by senatorial decree.

Modern lists sometimes call both dates “the feast of Pax.” More precisely, they commemorate two legal-ritual stages in one state foundation.

### Templum Pacis

Vespasian began the complex after the capture of Jerusalem and dedicated it in 75 CE. It stood southeast of the Republican Forum and later formed one of the Imperial Fora, although ancient sources initially call it *templum* or *temenos* rather than *forum*.

Its components included:

- a vast porticoed enclosure;
- landscaped beds, pools, or fountains—the interpretation of features shown on the Severan Marble Plan remains disputed;
- a cult hall with a statue of Pax;
- celebrated Greek artworks transferred from Nero’s Domus Aurea;
- spoils of the Jerusalem Temple;
- libraries or archival functions;
- eventually the wall carrying the early third-century marble plan of Rome, the *Forma Urbis*.

The juxtaposition is fundamental: a goddess carrying olive and abundance presided over a complex financed and legitimated by conquest and filled with sacred war booty.

A fire in 192 CE devastated the complex. Septimius Severus restored it, and the *Forma Urbis Romae* was mounted there under the Severans, approximately 203–211. Parts were subsequently altered, quarried, buried, or incorporated into later urban fabric.

### Provincial and numismatic cult

Pax Augusta spread more readily through altars, imperial dedications, civic honorific language, and coins than through a network of large independent temples. Provincial mints adapted her attributes to local visual conventions. Coins reached soldiers, taxpayers, traders, and provincial communities on a scale no single sanctuary could match.

The principal evidence for popularity is therefore official circulation rather than a mass of surviving personal prayers.

### Priesthood and oracle

No dedicated ancient priestly college of Pax is securely documented. Her Roman rites were performed by existing civic officials, priests, and Vestals. No securely attested oracle, mystery initiation, sacred scripture, temple-prostitution institution, or specialized divinatory system belongs to Pax.

### Sacrifice, sexual rites, and human sacrifice

- Animal sacrifice at the Ara Pacis is both textually prescribed in general terms and represented monumentally.
- **No evidence establishes human sacrifice to Pax.**
- **No evidence establishes temple prostitution in a sanctuary of Pax.**
- Claims connecting either practice to Pax are **[modern invention or unsupported generalization]** from unrelated ancient religions.
- The violent foundation of Flavian peace—the Jewish War, destruction of Jerusalem, killings, enslavement, and seizure of temple treasure—is historically documented political and military violence, but it is not evidence of ritual human sacrifice to the goddess.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before 44 BCE: concept without a secure personal cult

*Pax* existed as a diplomatic, legal, and religious condition. Peace with gods and humans was made, kept, broken, or imposed. There is no secure archaic temple, priesthood, genealogy, or image of a goddess Pax.

### 44–31 BCE: named personification amid civil war

The **PAXS** coin of 44 BCE shows the concept acquiring an explicit female image. Subsequent symbols—clasped hands, caduceus, olive—made peace a partisan promise during continuing civil wars. The historical irony is important: Pax becomes visually prominent precisely when Roman political peace had collapsed.

### 31–13 BCE: Octavianic victory becomes peace

After Actium and Alexandria, Octavian represented military supremacy as restoration. Closure of Janus, agricultural revival, temple restoration, and golden-age poetry made peace a central benefit of the new regime. Pax was not yet merely the opposite of conquest; she was conquest successfully completed.

### 13 BCE–14 CE: institutionalization as Pax Augusta

The Ara Pacis created a permanent state cult with calendrical anniversaries and annual sacrifice. Ovid’s *Fasti* gave it literary voice. The cult connected the Senate, priesthoods, Vestals, Augustus’s return, imperial family, and prosperity.

Cornwell’s formulation of a “new cult of Pax Augusta” is useful because the monumental evidence is genuinely new. Whether this was wholly invented by the Augustan regime or developed from an already emerging late-Republican personification remains debated. The 44 BCE coin favors development rather than creation from nothing.

### Julio-Claudian era

Imperial coinage continued the imagery. The seated female on Tiberian issues traditionally called Livia as Pax is not always labelled; identifications can range among Pax, Justitia, and other imperial virtues. Such portraits illustrate the deliberate interchangeability of empress, virtue, and goddess.

### 69–96 CE: Flavian re-foundation

After the civil war of 68–69 and victory in Judaea, Vespasian made Peace a dynastic centerpiece. The Templum Pacis, dedicated in 75, exceeded the Ara Pacis in scale and joined cult with art display, garden, archive, and triumphal booty. Pax coins proliferated under Vespasian, Titus, and Domitian.

### Second and third centuries

Pax remained a standard imperial coin type. Legends such as **PAX AVG**, **PAX AVGVSTI**, **PAX AETERNA**, or **PAX PVBLICA** announced stability during both tranquil and deeply unstable reigns. Their appearance during civil wars or usurpations is not evidence that peace existed; it is evidence that rulers claimed the capacity to restore it.

The fire of 192 destroyed much of the Templum Pacis. Severan restoration and the installation of the *Forma Urbis* gave the complex a new administrative and representational life.

### Late antiquity

As emperors adopted Christianity, public sacrificial cults lost funding and legal standing. Fourth-century legislation progressively restricted sacrifice and traditional temple practice. There is, however, no securely documented dramatic episode in which Christians specifically stormed a functioning Temple of Pax and destroyed its cult statue. Pax’s institutional cult disappears through the wider transformation of Roman civic religion, the earlier fire and rebuilding history of the precinct, urban reuse, and legal suppression of sacrifice.

Christian Latin retained *pax* with enormous force—*pax Christi*, liturgical greetings, episcopal and imperial concord—but this is semantic and theological succession, not demonstrated continuous worship of the goddess under a Christian disguise.

### Medieval and early-modern survival

Pax survived as:

- a Latin word in law, diplomacy, liturgy, and political theory;
- an allegorical woman in manuscripts and civic art;
- a companion or opponent of Concord, Justice, Abundance, Minerva, and Mars;
- an emblem of treaties and princely government.

This was allegorical reception, not evidence of an unbroken priesthood or secret cult.

### Present-day worship

Contemporary Roman-polytheist and reconstructionist groups sometimes honor Pax, including modern projects placing Pax with Concordia and Salus Publica. These are documented modern revivals based on ancient sources. There is no evidence for uninterrupted transmission from an ancient congregation.

The scale of Pax-specific devotion cannot presently be established. Most modern Roman polytheists appear to include her within a reconstructed pantheon rather than belong to a large exclusive cult.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Renaissance and Baroque allegory

Humanist emblem books and court art revived Peace as a classical woman with olive, cornucopia, burned arms, or children. Rubens’s *Minerva Protects Pax from Mars* (1629–1630) portrays Peace nourishing children while Minerva drives away War. Jan Lievens’s *Allegory of Peace* (1652) similarly uses white clothing, olive, and cornucopias. These are creative early-modern syntheses, not recovered ancient myths.

### Rediscovery of the Ara Pacis

Fragments had resurfaced by the early sixteenth century; an engraving made before 1536 records an acanthus-and-swan slab. Cardinal Giovanni Ricci acquired nine carved blocks in 1566. Further remains appeared beneath Palazzo Fiano in 1859. Friedrich von Duhn identified the complex as the Ara Pacis; excavations in 1903 recovered fifty-three fragments before structural danger halted work.

### Fascist appropriation

In 1937 Mussolini’s government ordered complete excavation and reassembly for the bimillennium of Augustus’s birth. Advanced freezing techniques helped excavate beneath the palace. Giuseppe Moretti supervised reconstruction; Vittorio Ballio Morpurgo designed the hurried pavilion near Augustus’s Mausoleum. Mussolini inaugurated it on 23 September 1938.

The project placed Augustan “peace” inside Fascist *romanità* after the conquest of Ethiopia and proclamation of empire. It was therefore not politically neutral conservation: Augustus’s claimed world order became an analogue for Fascist imperial power. The reconstructed monument also contains extensive modern restoration and was rotated relative to its ancient orientation.

### Modern museum

The inadequate 1938 pavilion exposed the marble to pollution, humidity, and temperature changes. Later conservation culminated in Richard Meier’s museum, opened in 2006. The altar is now at Rome’s Museo dell’Ara Pacis, while fragments and casts elsewhere complicate the modern physical history of the monument.

### Occultism and esotericism

No major Renaissance grimoire, Theosophical system, or Golden Dawn grade gives the Roman goddess Pax a securely continuous specialist cult. The word *pax* occurs in Christian-Latin formulas and in occult titles. Aleister Crowley’s *Konx Om Pax* (1907), however, does not document worship of the Roman goddess; the title belongs to a separate esoteric wordplay and Golden Dawn milieu.

Modern correspondences assigning Pax crystals, chakras, tarot cards, planets, or magical hours are **[modern invention]** unless their contemporary author is explicitly cited. They should not be described as ancient Roman lore.

### Literature, political language, and commerce

“Pax” became a productive political prefix: *Pax Britannica*, *Pax Americana*, and similar formulations imitate *Pax Romana* to describe order maintained by hegemonic power. The usage preserves the Roman ambiguity between peace and dominance.

Pax also survives as a personal name, institutional name, brand, and international-peace emblem. These uses rarely entail belief in the Roman deity.

### Nationalist and extremist appropriation

The clearest documented political appropriation is Italian Fascism’s staging of the Ara Pacis and Augustan bimillennium. Classical imperial imagery is also used more diffusely by modern nationalist movements, but a distinct, well-documented extremist cult specifically centered on Pax has not been established. Claims of one require case-specific evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Pax an old Roman goddess?

Older handbooks sometimes list Pax as though she belonged timelessly to a fixed Roman pantheon. The evidence instead favors a late development:

1. the word and religious-political concept are old;
2. the first explicit image is in 44 BCE;
3. the first securely documented monumental state cult is Augustan;
4. Greek Eirene supplied an established artistic and genealogical vocabulary.

Wissowa, Latte, Dumézil, and later historians of Roman religion differ in how strongly they distinguish genuine cult from literary personification. Current source-critical treatment generally resists assuming antiquity merely because a Latin abstraction has divine grammar.

### Roman creation or Greek borrowing?

One extreme makes Pax only a translation of Eirene; the other makes her wholly indigenous. The material suggests convergence. Rome possessed its own legal-religious concept of *pax*, while Greek Eirene offered female form, Hora genealogy, children, and abundance imagery. The Augustan result was neither simply imported nor culturally isolated.

No evidence requires an Etruscan intermediary, though Etruscan and Italic artistic channels may have participated in transmitting Greek forms.

### What does *Augusta* mean?

Scholars debate whether *Pax Augusta* means:

- Peace personified and made august;
- the sacred peace of the Augustan settlement;
- Peace associated with Augustus’s charisma and authority;
- a step toward ruler cult.

These are not mutually exclusive. The Senate’s formula attaches the cult to Augustus’s return while grammatically dedicating the altar to Pax Augusta.

### Who is the Ara Pacis mother?

This remains the central iconographic controversy. Tellus, Italia, Venus, Ceres, and Pax each explain part of the imagery. De Grummond identifies Pax and the attendants as Horae; Spaeth argues for Ceres; other scholars emphasize Tellus or deliberate polysemy.

The strongest conclusion is limited: the relief is a programmatic image of fertility under peace. Naming the woman “Pax” without qualification exceeds the inscriptional evidence.

### Is the procession a literal historical snapshot?

The monument’s north and south friezes are often called the dedication procession. Scholars have also proposed the *constitutio*, an idealized state sacrifice, or a composite ceremonial representation. The presence of family members and priests need not reproduce one photographed instant. Any precise event-identification is a reconstruction.

### Peace or propaganda?

“Propaganda” captures deliberate imperial messaging but can oversimplify Roman religion by treating ritual as mere cynical advertising. The annual sacrifice was a real cult act; the political claims were likewise real. Roman civic religion did not sharply separate worship, constitutional honor, art, and imperial legitimation.

Conversely, devotional language must not hide domination. The Ara Pacis followed western campaigning; the Flavian temple monumentalized victory over Judaea. Pax was both worshipped benefit and language of power.

### State of the evidence

Principal surviving evidence includes:

- RRC 480/24 and later Roman coins in international collections;
- the Monumentum Ancyranum and other copies of the *Res Gestae*;
- fragments of inscribed Roman calendars in CIL;
- the reconstructed Ara Pacis and associated fragments;
- literary manuscripts of Ovid, Augustus, Josephus, Pliny, Suetonius, Dio, and Herodian;
- fragments of the Severan *Forma Urbis*;
- archaeological remains of the Templum Pacis.

Losses include Varro’s complete theological works, much ritual documentation, most cult images, the original bronze pillars of the *Res Gestae* at Rome, and much of the Temple of Peace’s library and archives in the fire of 192.

### Essential modern scholarship

- Georg Wissowa, *Religion und Kultus der Römer*: foundational institutional treatment, now used critically.
- Kurt Latte, *Römische Religionsgeschichte*: major synthesis attentive to historical development.
- Georges Dumézil, *La religion romaine archaïque / Archaic Roman Religion*: influential structural interpretation, but not direct evidence for Pax’s antiquity.
- Mary Beard, John North, and Simon Price, *Religions of Rome*: ritual, institutions, and political context.
- John Scheid’s studies of Roman ritual and civic religion.
- Carlos Noreña on imperial virtues and the Templum Pacis.
- Hannah Cornwell, *Pax and the Politics of Peace: Republic to Principate*: the most focused modern account of the transformation of Roman peace.
- Nancy Thomson de Grummond on Pax and the Horae at the Ara Pacis.
- Barbette Stanley Spaeth on the Ceres interpretation.
- LIMC and *Brill’s New Pauly* for iconographic and historical reference, to be checked against primary corpora.

### Discredited or unsupported popular claims

- Pax had a fully developed archaic Roman mythology: unsupported.
- Pax was certainly the daughter of Jupiter and Justitia: a mistaken Romanization of Eirene’s Greek genealogy.
- The Ara Pacis mother is unquestionably Pax: disputed.
- The Ara Pacis celebrates universal pacifism: anachronistic.
- Pax had temple prostitutes or received human victims: no evidence.
- Pax has ancient crystal, chakra, tarot, zodiacal, or planetary correspondences: modern invention.
- Roman worship of Pax survived continuously and secretly into modern reconstructionism: undocumented.

## 10. Chronology

| Date | Attestation or historical event |
|---|---|
| ca. 700 BCE | Hesiod’s *Theogony* 901–906 names Greek Eirene daughter of Zeus and Themis, later important to Pax’s genealogy and imagery. |
| Republican period | *Pax* is established as a word for compact, peace, and right relations; *pax deorum* belongs to Roman religious discourse. No secure goddess-cult yet. |
| 45 BCE | Cicero writes *De natura deorum*, discussing divinized abstractions but giving Pax no independent mythology. |
| **44 BCE** | L. Aemilius Buca’s quinarius labels a female head **PAXS**, the earliest secure explicit Roman attestation of the goddess/personification. |
| 31 BCE | Actium becomes the foundational victory behind Augustan peace imagery. |
| 30 BCE | Fall of Alexandria ends the major civil-war struggle; Octavianic imagery increasingly links victory, concord, and peace. |
| 29 BCE onward | Augustus closes the Gates of Janus among his claims of restored peace; *Res Gestae* 13 eventually says he did so three times. |
| **4 July 13 BCE** | Senate constitutes the Ara Pacis Augustae for Augustus’s return from Spain and Gaul. |
| **30 January 9 BCE** | Ara Pacis dedicated in the Campus Martius; calendrical fasti record a public holiday. |
| ca. 2–8 CE | Ovid composes *Fasti* 1.709–724, directly invoking Pax at her altar. |
| 14 CE and after | The *Res Gestae* is publicly inscribed in Rome and provinces; the Ankara copy preserves the altar decree. |
| First century CE | Pax becomes a recurring imperial coin type with olive, caduceus, sceptre, cornucopia, grain, Victory, or burned arms. |
| 54–68 CE | Neronian coinage deploys Pax amid claims of imperial settlement; attribution of particular unlabelled female figures remains type-specific. |
| 68–69 CE | Civil war makes restored Pax a central claim of competing and succeeding rulers. |
| 70 CE | Roman forces destroy the Jerusalem Temple; its spoils later become central exhibits in Vespasian’s peace complex. |
| 71–75 CE | Vespasian constructs the Templum Pacis. |
| **75 CE** | Temple of Peace dedicated; Josephus, Pliny, Suetonius, and later authors document its contents and fame. |
| 81–96 CE | Domitianic issues continue Pax iconography in Rome and provincial mints. |
| Second century CE | Pax becomes a routine imperial virtue on coins across the empire; the later historiographical “Pax Romana” is conventionally extended through this era. |
| **192 CE** | Fire devastates the Temple of Peace and adjoining stores and archives. |
| ca. 193–211 CE | Septimius Severus restores the complex. |
| ca. 203–211 CE | The marble *Forma Urbis Romae* is mounted on a hall wall in the Temple of Peace complex. |
| Fourth century CE | Imperial Christianization and anti-sacrifice legislation erode the institutional basis of Pax’s public cult; no specific destruction narrative for her cult statue is secure. |
| Fifth–sixth centuries CE | The precinct loses its ancient cultic role amid urban transformation and reuse. |
| Before 1536 | An engraving shows an Ara Pacis floral-and-swan fragment already known. |
| 1566 | Cardinal Giovanni Ricci acquires nine sculpted blocks from the altar. |
| 1859 | Further remains found beneath Palazzo Fiano. |
| 1903 | Excavations recover fifty-three fragments but stop because of danger to the palace. |
| 1937 | Fascist government orders full recovery for the Augustan bimillennium. |
| **23 September 1938** | Mussolini inaugurates the reassembled Ara Pacis in Morpurgo’s pavilion; the monument becomes a major artifact of Fascist *romanità*. |
| 1970s | Conservation work attempts to mitigate the pavilion’s environmental inadequacies. |
| 1990 | De Grummond publishes the influential identification of the altar’s fecundity goddess as Pax Augusta with the Horae. |
| 2006 | Richard Meier’s new Museo dell’Ara Pacis opens. |
| Twenty-first century | Pax remains an academic subject, civic allegory, political metaphor, museum figure, and occasional deity in reconstructed Roman polytheism; no continuous ancient cult is demonstrated. |
