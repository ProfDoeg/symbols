# Fides: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Roman goddess and divine personification of reciprocal trust, pledged word, reliability, protection, and political loyalty. Ancient authors treat *fides* both as a human/social quality and as a cult-receiving female deity; the two senses cannot always be separated.
>
> **Earliest secure attestation:** the earliest securely recoverable institutional event is the dedication of a Capitoline temple by Aulus Atilius Calatinus, conventionally dated to **254 BCE**, with **250 BCE** as an alternative reconstruction. Cicero records the dedication in *De natura deorum* 2.61; Republican calendars preserve its anniversary on 1 October. No contemporary dedication inscription survives. Ennius’s fragment invoking “nurturing, winged Fides” was probably composed during the later third or early second century BCE and is the earliest surviving literary invocation, although it is preserved only through Cicero. The attribution of Fides’s cult and ritual to King Numa belongs to Roman foundation legend recorded centuries later, not securely datable evidence from the regal period.
>
> **Evidence labels:** **[Early attested]** securely recoverable Republican evidence; **[Foundation legend]** retrospective claims about regal or heroic Rome; **[Later canonical]** influential Augustan or imperial formulation; **[Regional variant]** locally restricted form; **[Learned systematization]** antiquarian, philosophical, or theological classification; **[Syncretistic identification]** historical equation with another divine figure; **[Late accretion]** later antique or medieval elaboration; **[Modern reconstruction]** scholarly inference; **[Modern invention/revival]** post-antique representation or worship not continuously inherited from the Roman cult.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Basic identity

**[Early attested]** Latin *Fidēs* is grammatically feminine and names both a relational quality—trust, credit, reliability, fulfillment of an undertaking—and the goddess who guarantees it. Cicero defines it as *dictorum conventorumque constantia et veritas*, “constancy and truth in words and agreements,” and calls it the foundation of justice (*De officiis* 1.23). Elsewhere he gives the Stoic-style folk etymology *quia fiat quod dictum est*—Fides is so called “because what has been said is done.” This is valuable evidence for Roman interpretation, not correct historical linguistics.

Fides was not simply “honesty” in the modern private-moral sense. The semantic field included:

- fidelity to promises and oaths;
- the trustworthiness that makes credit and contracts possible;
- confidence placed in another;
- the protection owed by the stronger party to someone who has entrusted or surrendered himself;
- patronal and diplomatic protection;
- the credibility of testimony;
- the pledged loyalty of soldiers, subjects, officials, spouses, and political communities.

The word is consequently “two-sided”: it can denote the reliability one offers and the confidence one places in another. Gérard Freyburger’s semantic study stresses this active/passive reciprocity and the unity of the *fid- / foed-* family.

### Name and linguistic history

- **Latin:** *Fidēs*, genitive *Fideī*; the long final vowel distinguishes its first-declension-like nominative shape from ordinary first-declension nouns. In inscriptions it appears as **FIDES**, **FIDEI**, and in titles such as **FIDEI PVBLICAE**.
- **Probable Indo-European derivation:** **[Modern linguistic reconstruction]** from the root conventionally written *\*bheidh-* or *\*bʰeydʰ-*, “trust, confide, persuade,” also represented in Latin *fidere* (“to trust”), *fidus* (“trustworthy”), *fiducia*, and probably *foedus* (“treaty”). Greek *pístis* belongs to a comparable semantic field but is not a simple sound-law cognate formed identically in Latin.
- **Ciceronian etymology:** **[Ancient learned systematization]** *fiat quod dictum est*, “let what was said be done” (*De officiis* 1.23). Cicero signals that he is imitating Stoic etymologizing; it is wordplay, not modern etymology.
- **Greek rendering:** *Πίστις* (*Pístis*, “Trust/Faith”). Greek-writing authors, notably Dionysius of Halicarnassus and Plutarch, use Pistis when describing the Roman cult. This is a translation-equivalence, not proof that Rome imported a Greek goddess.
- **Umbrian comparison:** **[Modern reconstruction grounded in ritual and language]** *Fisus Sancius/Fisovius Sancius*, attested in the Iguvine Tables, belongs to the same Italic sphere of oath-sanction and employs the *mandraculum*, a hand-covering comparable with the Roman rite of Fides. Similarity supports a shared Italic religious vocabulary; it does not establish that Fides was merely another name for the Umbrian god.
- **Semo Sancus Dius Fidius:** **[Functional comparison, disputed identity]** this Roman/Sabine-associated male oath-god overlaps strongly with Fides and Jupiter as guarantor of sworn obligations. Wissowa and later scholars debated whether these were differentiated developments of an earlier sovereign oath-function. Ancient evidence does not make Fides the wife, daughter, or female form of Sancus.
- **Etruscan roots:** **[Unestablished]** no securely identified Etruscan goddess named Fides is demonstrated by the inscribed mirrors. Etruscan mirrors are essential evidence for many divine identifications, but they do not supply a secure Fides prototype. Claims that Fides is “an Etruscan goddess” exceed the evidence.

### Cult titles and literary epithets

- **Fides Publica** — “Public/State Fides.” **[Early attested/Republican development]** The form under which the goddess guaranteed Rome’s public undertakings, treaties, diplomatic relations, and the credibility of the *populus Romanus*.
- **Fides Publica Populi Romani / Fides Populi Romani** — “Public Fides of the Roman People.” **[Epigraphically attested imperial title with Republican roots]** Military diplomas describe their official master copies as posted at or behind the *aedes Fidei populi Romani*.
- **Fides Romana** — “Roman good faith.” Usually a political and historiographical expression rather than a separate cult-personality.
- **Bona Fides** — “good faith.” Common legal and ethical phrase; evidence for the concept, not by itself an ancient cult title securely attached to her Capitoline temple.
- **Alma Fides** — “nourishing/kindly Fides.” **[Early literary]** Ennius, preserved by Cicero, *De officiis* 3.104.
- **Apta pinnis** — “fitted with wings.” **[Early literary]** Ennius’s Fides is winged. This isolated poetic attribute did not become her standard visual type.
- **Cana Fides** — “white-haired/hoary/venerable Fides.” **[Later canonical]** Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.292; Servius interprets the whiteness through her antiquity and white ritual covering.
- **Rara Fides** — “rare Fides.” **[Augustan poetic]** Horace, *Odes* 1.35.21–22, playing on the scarcity of trust while describing her veiled in white.
- **Casta, sancta, sacra, antiqua, incorrupta** — “chaste, holy, sacred, ancient, incorruptible.” **[Literary, not necessarily formal cult titles]** These belong chiefly to moralizing and poetic discourse.
- **Fides Augusta / Augusti / Augustorum** — “the emperor’s/the emperors’ Fides.” **[Imperial political theology]** The emperor’s reliability or loyalty owed to him; the grammatical ambiguity could serve both claims.
- **Fides Exercitus, Fides Militum, Fides Legionum, Fides Praetorianorum, Fides Equitum** — loyalty of the army, soldiers, legions, praetorians, or cavalry. **[Imperial numismatic development]** Especially common from the civil wars of 68–69 CE and in the politically unstable third century.
- **Fides Victor / Fides Victoriae** — victorious Fides or loyalty associated with victory. **[Rare imperial numismatic form]**
- **Fides Coniugalis** — conjugal fidelity. **[Private/imperial development]** Clasped hands and marriage concord could evoke Fides, but this was not the primary domain of the Republican state cult.

### Greek and other equations

**[Syncretistic identification]** Ancient and modern reference works frequently call Greek Pistis the equivalent of Fides. The equation is legitimate as translation but historically asymmetrical: Greek literature personified *Pistis*, yet there is no comparably documented Greek civic cult with a temple and annual state ritual equivalent to Rome’s. Greek authors translated Roman Fides as Pistis because the words overlapped.

Equations with Themis, Dike, Homonoia, Eusebeia, or Zeus Pistios/Horkios are functional comparisons. They are not evidence that Fides was permanently fused with any of them. Her closest Roman functional partners were Jupiter, Dius Fidius/Sancus, and the fetial institutions that sanctioned oaths and treaties.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The evidentiary sequence

#### The Numa tradition

**[Foundation legend]** Livy (*Ab urbe condita* 1.21.4, written in the late first century BCE) says Numa instituted an annual rite to Fides, transported the flamines to her shrine in a covered carriage, and ordered sacrifice with the right hand covered to the fingers. Dionysius of Halicarnassus (*Roman Antiquities* 2.75.2–3, likewise late first century BCE) credits Numa with establishing a shrine to Pistis and cultivating a trustworthy civic character. Plutarch (*Numa* 16, late first/early second century CE) says Numa was the first to establish a sanctuary of Faith and taught Romans their most solemn oath.

These witnesses establish that late Republican and early imperial Romans regarded Fides as Numan and primordial. They do not provide contemporary evidence for an eighth- or seventh-century BCE cult. “Numa founded it” often functioned in Roman religious memory as an attribution of venerable, peaceful, law-giving antiquity.

#### Aulus Atilius Calatinus and the historical temple

**[Earliest secure institutional attestation]** Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.61 (45 BCE), includes the temple of Fides among Roman temples dedicated by named magistrates and attributes it to Aulus Atilius Calatinus. The standard date is his consulship in **254 BCE**; some older reconstructions preferred **250 BCE**, another year in which he held high command. Calendrical entries for 1 October corroborate the temple’s continuing public anniversary, though not the exact construction year. Modern topographical scholarship therefore treats 254 BCE as the most likely date while retaining the uncertainty.

The evidence establishes a state temple by the mid-third century BCE. It does not establish what building, altar, or open-air *sacrarium*, if any, preceded it.

#### Ennius

**[Early literary attestation]** Quintus Ennius (239–169 BCE) wrote:

> *O Fides alma apta pinnis et ius iurandum Iovis!*  
> “O nurturing Fides, fitted with wings, and oath of Jupiter!”

The fragment survives in Cicero, *De officiis* 3.104. Its original work and dramatic context are lost. It proves that Fides could be invoked as a goddess, associated with Jupiter’s oath, and imagined as winged by the later third or early second century BCE. It cannot establish a standard cult statue with wings.

### Principal ancient corpus

- **Ennius, fragment, later third/early second century BCE; preserved in Cicero, 44 BCE.** Fides is *alma*, winged, and coupled with Jupiter’s oath.
- **Plautus, comedies, c. 205–184 BCE.** Numerous uses of *fides* illuminate everyday trust, credit, promises, and patronage. Most are common-noun uses rather than explicit appearances of the goddess.
- **Cato the Elder, fragment preserved by Cicero, *De officiis* 3.104.** Cato locates an oath episode *in Capitolio, ad Fidem*, “on the Capitol, by Fides,” and thus supplies indirect Republican evidence for the sanctuary’s recognized location near Jupiter.
- **Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.61, 45 BCE.** In the Academic-Stoic theological discussion, the speaker accepts the deification of beneficial powers such as Fides and records Calatinus’s dedication and Scaurus’s restoration.
- **Cicero, *De natura deorum* 3.61–62, 45 BCE.** The skeptical speaker argues that Mens, Fides, Spes, Virtus, and similar qualities are powers or states in people, not gods; their images are consecrated, but their divine agency remains unproved. This is critical evidence for disagreement inside elite Roman theology.
- **Cicero, *De officiis* 1.23 and 3.104, 44 BCE.** Defines *fides*, offers the Stoic folk etymology, preserves Ennius, and associates Fides with Jupiter and the Capitol.
- **Catullus 30.11, c. 55 BCE.** *At di meminerunt, meminit Fides*—“but the gods remember; Fides remembers.” Fides is an avenging witness to betrayed friendship. Whether this is full cultic invocation or vivid personification cannot be decided.
- **Varro, *Antiquitates rerum divinarum*, 40s BCE, lost; fragments chiefly in Augustine.** Varro classified Roman gods and cult institutions. Because the work is lost, claims attributed to him must be kept distinct from Augustine’s Christian argument and paraphrase.
- **Horace, *Odes* 1.35.21–24, published 23 BCE.** Spes and “rare Fides,” veiled in white cloth, accompany Fortuna even when she abandons powerful houses. This is the clearest poetic echo of Fides’s ritual hand-covering.
- **Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.291–293, c. 29–19 BCE.** Jupiter foretells that in Rome’s peaceful future *cana Fides et Vesta*, with Remus and Quirinus, will give laws. Fides represents restored civic order after civil war.
- **Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 1.21.4, composed from the late 20s BCE onward.** Fullest account of Numa’s ritual: covered carriage, flamines, covered right hands, and an explicit symbolic explanation.
- **Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities* 2.75, 7 BCE.** Greek-language Numa tradition, calling the deity Pistis.
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 1.127–150, completed c. 8 CE.** In the Iron Age, *pudor, verum,* and *fides* depart; the passage participates in the myth of moral powers abandoning corrupt humanity. It is poetic cosmology, not a cult narrative. Ovid’s surviving *Fasti* is incomplete after June, so it cannot describe the 1 October feast; claims that it supplies the festival narrative are erroneous.
- **Valerius Maximus, *Memorable Deeds and Sayings*, c. 30 CE.** Calls the sanctuary the temple of Fides Publica and invokes Fides as a venerable protective figure. His anecdotes show its moral and political symbolism.
- **Pliny the Elder, *Natural History* 35.100, c. 77 CE.** Records that an Apelles painting of an old man teaching a boy to play the lyre stood in the Temple of Fides. He also criticizes the multiplication of abstract divinities, a philosophical objection later useful to Christian polemicists.
- **Silius Italicus, *Punica* 2.475–525, c. 80–101 CE.** Supplies Fides’s only substantial surviving mythic episode: Hercules petitions her to save Saguntum; she cannot overturn fate but grants heroic endurance and posthumous fame.
- **Statius, *Thebaid* 11, c. 80–92 CE.** Fides and Pietas try unsuccessfully to restrain the fratricidal duel of Eteocles and Polynices. This is Flavian literary allegory rather than Roman cult legend.
- **Plutarch, *Numa* 16, c. 90–120 CE.** Elaborates Numa’s institution of Faith and oath practice.
- **Festus, *De verborum significatu*, second century CE, epitomizing Verrius Flaccus.** Preserves antiquarian notices, including Agathocles’ otherwise unattested story of a Palatine temple founded by Rhome, daughter or descendant of Aeneas.
- **Apuleius, *Metamorphoses* 4.21, c. 160–180 CE.** Jokes that Fides has fled human life to dwell among the dead because of human perfidy—literary evidence for the “departed virtue” motif.
- **Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, early fifth century CE, and Servius, commentary on Virgil, late fourth/early fifth century.** Preserve antiquarian interpretations of ritual, epithets, and literary passages. Servius specifies a white cloth and connects *cana Fides* with antiquity. These are late witnesses, potentially drawing on lost earlier scholarship.
- **Lactantius, *Divine Institutes* 1.20–21, c. 303–311 CE.** A hostile Christian witness. He lists Faith, Hope, Concord, Peace, Chastity, and Piety as qualities Romans wrongly placed inside temples, while conceding that these are more suitable objects of reverence than harmful powers. His testimony confirms that the cultic personification remained intelligible.
- **Arnobius, *Adversus nationes*, c. 300 CE.** A hostile but antiquarianly valuable attack on Roman polytheism. Its details must be checked because Arnobius writes rhetorically and sometimes relies on second-hand antiquarian material.
- **Augustine, *City of God* 4 and 6–7, c. 413–426 CE.** Uses Varro’s lost theology to attack the multiplication of Roman gods from human functions and virtues. Augustine is indispensable for Varro’s fragments but cannot be treated as a neutral transcript.

### Material and manuscript qualification

Almost all literary witnesses survive in manuscripts many centuries later than composition, often Carolingian or medieval copies. Their dates above are dates of composition, not of the physical manuscripts consulted today. The calendrical inscriptions, coins, military diplomas, and architectural fragments therefore provide indispensable independent material evidence.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**[Early attested]** Fides governed the reliable performance of spoken undertakings. Her proper sphere was not belief in propositions but the relationship created when one party entrusted itself to another and the second became answerable for protection or performance.

Her public form covered:

- treaties and diplomatic commitments;
- the integrity of Rome’s pledged word;
- surrender *in fidem*, placing a community under Roman protection;
- oaths and sworn testimony;
- safe conduct and protection of suppliants;
- public documents whose effectiveness rested upon Roman authority.

This domain had an unequal side. A defeated people who surrendered *in fidem populi Romani* trusted Rome’s discretion; Roman authors represented this as protection, but it did not constitute a treaty between equals and did not prevent punitive treatment. *Fides Romana* was both a moral ideal and an ideological language of empire.

**[Imperial transformation]** On coins, Fides increasingly became loyalty toward the emperor—especially the fidelity of soldiers. *Fides Militum* could advertise actual solidarity, solicit it, or compensate rhetorically for its fragility. Its frequency during contested reigns warns against reading every coin legend as a factual report that loyalty existed.

### Form and identifying attributes

No securely identified surviving cult statue is known. Visual identification rests overwhelmingly on coins:

- a mature, fully draped woman;
- often veiled;
- standing or seated;
- one or two military standards;
- ears of grain;
- a patera or dish of fruit;
- cornucopia;
- poppy;
- globe;
- small Victory;
- occasionally a rudder;
- clasped right hands (*dextrarum iunctio*) in place of the goddess.

The clasped hands are especially important but not uniquely hers. They could signify agreement, concord, marriage, military loyalty, or political union; the legend determines whether a coin invokes Fides specifically.

**White cloth:** Horace’s *albo … velata panno* and Servius’s commentary connect Fides with white ritual covering. Livy says the sacrificing hands were covered as far as the fingers. The primary symbolism was the protected sanctity of the pledged right hand; interpretations involving secrecy are later scholarly development.

**Right hand:** The *dextera* was Fides’s sacred “seat.” Hand-clasping enacted agreement. This explains both the wrapped sacrificial hand and the clasped-hand coin type without requiring a lost myth.

**Wings:** Ennius calls Fides “fitted with wings.” No continuous winged iconographic type is securely established. It may assimilate her to mobile Greek personifications or evoke the swiftness with which pledged reputation travels.

**Agricultural attributes:** Grain, poppy, fruit, and cornucopia on imperial coins connect public trust with peace and abundance. They are not evidence that Fides began as a crop goddess.

**Military standards:** These become dominant in imperial *Fides Militum* types. They mark a historical shift from treaty-guaranteeing civic trust to loyalty of the armed forces.

### Unattested modern correspondences

No ancient source securely assigns Fides a planet, zodiacal sign, weekday, sacred color beyond the ritual white cloth, canonical number, sacred animal, or plant. Modern lists assigning candles, crystals, incense, dogs, or astrological correspondences are **[Modern invention]** unless explicitly presented as contemporary devotional choices.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

Fides has no securely attested parents, spouse, children, birth narrative, or permanent household. This absence is structural: she is a divine quality rather than a genealogically embedded member of the Greco-Roman Olympian family.

Silius calls her “older than Jupiter” and “sister of Justice.” These are poetic claims in a Flavian epic, not a stable Roman genealogy. “Older than Jupiter” expresses primordial moral authority; “sister of Justice” expresses conceptual kinship.

She had no documented mount, servant order, or personal priesthood. The three major flamines served her annual public rite but were priests of Jupiter, Mars, and Quirinus respectively.

### Every documented divine interaction or joint episode

- **Jupiter — oath association.** Ennius calls Fides “the oath of Jupiter”; Cicero preserves the line in *De officiis* 3.104 (44 BCE). **[Early literary attestation]**
- **Jupiter — prophecy about Fides.** In Virgil, Jupiter tells Venus that venerable Fides, Vesta, Remus, and Quirinus will give laws in Rome’s age of peace (*Aeneid* 1.291–293, c. 29–19 BCE). Fides does not answer him. **[Later canonical]**
- **Jupiter — spatial and functional partnership.** Fides’s historical temple stood beside Jupiter Optimus Maximus’s Capitoline temple; Jupiter was independently a guardian of oaths. Cicero, *De officiis* 3.104, and temple topography. **[Early attested cult relation; no narrative encounter]**
- **Vesta — joint lawgiving.** Fides and Vesta appear together as restored moral and civic order in Jupiter’s prophecy, Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.292. **[Later canonical]**
- **Quirinus — joint lawgiving.** Fides, Vesta, Quirinus, and Remus give laws, Virgil, *Aeneid* 1.292–293. **[Later canonical poetic scene]**
- **Remus — joint lawgiving.** Same Virgilian prophecy. Remus is reconciled with his deified brother, reversing the fratricidal foundation story for an image of Augustan peace. **[Later canonical poetic scene]**
- **Fortuna — attendance.** Horace depicts Spes and veiled Fides accompanying Fortuna as she changes her favor among powerful houses, *Odes* 1.35.21–24 (23 BCE). **[Augustan poetic interaction]**
- **Spes — companionship.** Spes and Fides jointly accompany Fortuna in Horace, *Odes* 1.35. **[Augustan poetic interaction]**
- **Hercules — petition for Saguntum.** Hercules travels secretly to Fides and begs her aid for the besieged city; she replies and intervenes within the limits of fate, Silius Italicus, *Punica* 2.475–525 (late first century CE). **[Late literary myth]**
- **Justice/Iustitia — sisterhood.** Fides calls or is called the sister of Justice in Silius’s Saguntum episode. **[Late literary genealogy]**
- **Juno — indirect constraint.** Hercules fears openly opposing Juno’s decrees and therefore approaches Fides secretly, Silius, *Punica* 2.475–492. Fides and Juno do not meet. **[Late literary relation, indirect]**
- **Pietas — joint resistance to fratricide.** Fides and Pietas resist the Furies’ effort to bring Eteocles and Polynices into final combat but are overcome, Statius, *Thebaid* 11 (c. 80–92 CE). **[Late literary allegory]**
- **The Furies, especially Tisiphone and Megaera — opposition.** Their fratricidal project overcomes the resistance of Fides and Pietas in Statius. **[Late literary allegory]**
- **Concordia — iconographic association.** Imperial rings and coins can combine clasped hands with *FIDES CONCORDIA*. This is a joint symbolic field rather than a surviving narrative meeting. **[Imperial material association]**
- **Victoria — iconographic association.** Fides sometimes carries a small Victory, while coin types associate loyalty with victory. **[Imperial political iconography; no mythic interaction]**
- **Mars and Quirinus through their flamines — ritual relation.** Their priests, together with Jupiter’s flamen, officiated for Fides. This is an institutional relation, not an episode involving the gods personally. **[Cultically attested]**
- **Dius Fidius/Semo Sancus — functional parallel.** Both govern oaths and reliability, but no text records a meeting, genealogy, or fusion with Fides. **[Ancient functional overlap; modern reconstruction]**
- **Pistis — translation-equivalence.** Greek authors call the Roman goddess Pistis. There is no ancient narrative in which distinct goddesses Fides and Pistis meet. **[Syncretistic identification]**

No documented interaction has been found with Minerva, Juno Moneta, Venus, Mercury, Saturn, Ceres, Apollo, Diana, Neptune, or the Lares merely by virtue of membership in Roman religion. Their omission is evidentiary, not an assertion that Romans could never invoke them together.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Fides has no large inherited myth-cycle comparable with those of Jupiter, Mars, Venus, or Hercules. Her history lies chiefly in ritual, political language, and personifying literature. Three narrative complexes nevertheless emerged.

### Numa establishes Fides

**[Foundation legend]** In Livy’s fullest version, Numa turns Romans away from violence by saturating civic life with religious obligation until *fides* and the oath govern more effectively than fear of law and punishment. He establishes an annual sacrifice to Fides. The flamines ride in a covered, arched carriage and sacrifice with hands covered to the fingers, demonstrating that Fides must be protected and that her seat in the right hand is sacred.

Dionysius emphasizes Numa’s deliberate creation of a trustworthy civic character and sanctuary to Pistis. Plutarch treats Faith as guardian of Rome’s greatest oath.

**Development:** The versions were written six or more centuries after Numa’s supposed reign. They use Numa as the ideal peaceful lawgiver and project historically observable Republican institutions into primordial Rome. The ritual itself may be old; its creation by Numa cannot be independently verified.

**Function:** The legend explains why Fides was foundational to Roman civil order, why her rite was performed by the highest-ranking flamines, and why the hand and covered objects mattered.

### The departure of Fides from a corrupt world

**[Poetic myth, Augustan and imperial]** Roman poets adapted the widespread Greek story that moral powers abandon humanity as ages deteriorate. Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* 1.129 says that in the Iron Age *pudor, verum,* and *fides* disappear. Horace’s “rare Fides” likewise suggests scarcity. Apuleius later jokes that she has moved among the dead because she can no longer tolerate human perfidy.

This is not a single canonical cult myth. It is a literary complex used to diagnose present corruption by contrasting it with an imagined golden past.

### Fides and the siege of Saguntum

**[Late literary myth]** Silius Italicus supplies the only extended surviving narrative in which Fides acts and speaks.

During Hannibal’s siege of Saguntum, Hercules—the city’s legendary founder—sees its suffering. Because Jupiter’s or fate’s order and Juno’s hostility prevent direct rescue, he secretly approaches Fides in her remote celestial dwelling. He calls her older than Jupiter, glory of gods and mortals, sister of Justice, and the condition of peace on land and sea. The Saguntines, he says, die because they remain faithful to Rome.

Fides answers that she has not ignored the broken treaty. Human wickedness drove her from earth: tyranny, greed, violence, and collective complicity made terrestrial life unfit for her. Fate will not permit her to save Saguntum, but she will strengthen the inhabitants so that their resistance becomes immortal and will escort their noble spirits to the underworld. She descends, heartens them, and makes their doomed loyalty exemplary.

The episode serves Flavian Roman ideology. Saguntum’s fidelity becomes the moral opposite of “Punic faithlessness,” while Rome inherits the position of trustworthy avenger. Historically, the diplomatic circumstances of Saguntum and Rome’s obligations in 219 BCE were more complicated than Silius’s moral epic suggests.

### Fides and Pietas resist the Theban brothers

**[Late literary allegory]** In Statius’s *Thebaid*, the Furies prepare the fratricidal combat of Eteocles and Polynices. Fides and Pietas resist, and Polynices briefly responds to their pressure, but infernal violence prevails. The episode is not a Roman cult legend about Fides; it uses Roman divine qualities to diagnose civil war. Its contemporary resonance after the civil war of 68–69 CE is difficult to miss, though particular political allegories remain debated.

### The Palatine temple founded by Rhome

**[Antiquarian regional/learned variant; doubtful]** Festus preserves Agathocles’ claim that Rhome, connected genealogically with Aeneas, founded a temple of Fides on the Palatine. No independent text, inscription, or archaeological identification confirms it. Modern topographers generally reject the temple as an antiquarian or flattering construction.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### The Capitoline temple

The historical sanctuary was an *aedes Fidei* on the Capitol, close to the Temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus.

- **Foundation/dedication:** Aulus Atilius Calatinus, probably 254 BCE; possibly 250 BCE. **[Early attested through later literary and calendar evidence]**
- **Restoration/rededication:** Marcus Aemilius Scaurus, usually dated to **115 BCE**; some building chronologies give a broader 115–109 BCE range. Cicero records Scaurus’s work.
- **Festival/dedication anniversary:** **1 October**, recorded in the Fasti Arvales, Amiternini, and related calendars. The day was marked *Fidei in Capitolio*.
- **Location:** in the Capitoline precinct, near Jupiter’s temple and probably toward the southeastern edge. Exact foundations remain disputed.
- **Architecture:** no certain complete plan survives. Architectural blocks, a podium, columns, a large female head, treaty fragments, and bilingual Greek-Latin dedications recovered below the Capitol have been connected with the temple, but material slid or was redeposited downhill. Attribution is probable rather than certain.
- **Contents:** Pliny records an Apelles painting showing an old man teaching a youth to play the lyre.
- **Political use:** meetings of the Senate could occur there. The building or adjacent walls displayed bronze diplomatic documents, treaties, and later master copies of military grants.
- **Disaster omen:** in 43 BCE a storm tore down tablets from the temple area; Cassius Dio and Julius Obsequens treat this as a portent during civil crisis.
- **Later history:** the date of closure, destruction, or conversion is not recorded. No evidence securely identifies a church built directly from or over the shrine.

The location of diplomatic texts was functional theology: Rome placed its written promises in the care of the divine quality that made those promises credible.

### Documentary archive and military diplomas

**[Epigraphically secure]** Imperial military diplomas repeatedly state that their text was copied and authenticated from a bronze tablet posted:

> *Romae in Capitolio aedis Fidei populi Romani parte dexteriore*  
> “at Rome on the Capitol, on the right side of the Temple of Fides of the Roman People.”

Other diplomas locate the master copies on the left side, behind the temple, or near the trophies of Germanicus. CIL XVI 1, from the reign of Claudius in 52 CE, is an early explicit example. These texts transform the sanctuary from a vaguely remembered temple into a physically operating archive whose placement authenticated grants of citizenship and marriage rights.

### Annual rite and priestly personnel

Livy says *flamines* officiated. Modern interpretation, supported by the exceptional dignity of the rite, identifies them as the three *flamines maiores*:

- *Flamen Dialis* of Jupiter;
- *Flamen Martialis* of Mars;
- *Flamen Quirinalis* of Quirinus.

They travelled in a covered *currus arcuatus*, apparently drawn by two animals, and sacrificed with the right hand wrapped up to the fingers. Servius specifies white cloth; the precise form—bandage, mitten-like wrapping, or loose cloth—is unknown.

The rite did not establish a separate *flamen Fidei*. No independent priesthood of Fides is documented. Participation by the three major flamines instead embedded her in the oldest-ranking public priestly order and linked her to the sovereign and civic gods.

A later antiquarian report gives three suckling pigs and grain as offerings, but the evidence is much less secure than Livy’s description. It should not be presented as the universally established sacrifice.

### Worship beyond Rome

The epigraphic record is limited compared with that of Jupiter, Mars, Silvanus, or the Matres. Relevant material includes:

- a wall or dedication at Capua jointly involving Spes, Fides, and Fortuna, dated around 110/109 BCE;
- dedications to *Fides Publica* or *Fides Augusta* in imperial contexts;
- a Picene cippus invoking *Fides Publica Populi Romani*;
- a rare private dedication apparently made by spouses to their own fidelity;
- ship names and military-unit language using *Fides*, which show prestige but are not automatically cult dedications.

The relative scarcity of private votives suggests that her principal cult was institutional and public. It does not prove that individuals never prayed to her.

### Treaty, oath, and fetial practice

The fetial priests’ treaty rituals belonged to the same religious field, but no primary source calls them a formal priesthood of Fides. They invoked Jupiter, pronounced conditional curses, and killed a pig with a flint while ratifying a treaty. Fides was the binding quality and divine sanction of such acts; Jupiter remained the explicit oath-enforcer in many formulas.

The famous phrase *deditio in fidem* belongs to Roman diplomacy. It could promise protection but also left terms to Roman discretion. Accordingly, historians debate whether Roman *fides* was primarily reciprocal good faith, paternal protection, an ideology legitimating domination, or all three in different situations.

### Negative and contested practices

- **Human sacrifice:** no source associates human sacrifice with Fides’s cult. The fetial killing of a pig is not human sacrifice.
- **Temple prostitution:** no evidence associates it with Fides.
- **Sexual ritual:** none documented.
- **Cult violence:** no violence is attributed to her annual rite. Violence committed under rhetoric of treaty enforcement belongs to Roman policy, not a documented sacrificial practice of her temple.
- **Oracle or divination:** no Fides oracle is known.
- **Mystery initiation:** none known.
- **Magic:** Fides does not have a securely defined ancient magical-papyrus cult. Uses of *fides* in spells may mean trust, guarantee, or authentication rather than the goddess.
- **Sacred animals:** none securely attested.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Before the mid-third century BCE

**[Foundation legend versus evidence]** Romans of the late Republic believed the cult went back to Numa. The hand-covering and participation of the major flamines may preserve genuinely old ritual structures. Nevertheless, there is no contemporary regal inscription, building, image, or text for Fides.

Comparisons with Umbrian Fisus Sancius and the *mandraculum* support an old Italic complex connecting trust, oath, sovereign authority, and the covered right hand. Whether a distinct goddess Fides existed in Rome before the Republic remains unprovable.

### Mid-Republic: institutionalization

The First Punic War era gives the first firm institutional horizon. Calatinus’s temple made Fides publicly monumental just as Rome’s alliances, surrenders, and overseas commitments expanded. It is tempting to connect foundation and imperial expansion directly, but no surviving source says why Calatinus built it.

Ennius’s invocation shows that by the later third or early second century BCE she possessed literary divinity as well as a temple.

### Late Republic: archive, diplomacy, and moral crisis

The shrine’s restoration by Scaurus and its use for treaties and Senate meetings reinforced Fides as guardian of the state’s word. Cicero made *fides* fundamental to justice but also preserved philosophical skepticism about turning qualities into gods.

Catullus made her remember private betrayal. Political rhetoric increasingly accused opponents and foreign peoples of *perfidia*. “Roman Fides” could serve as both ethical demand and claim of Roman superiority.

### Augustan age: antiquity restored

Horace and Virgil place Fides in programs of moral restoration after civil war:

- Horace’s Fides is rare, veiled, and follows unstable Fortuna.
- Virgil’s hoary Fides returns as a lawgiver with Vesta, Remus, and Quirinus.
- Ovid’s Iron Age loses Fides with truth and shame.

These are not reports of new ritual. They make the old goddess a measure of whether Augustan peace has repaired Republican betrayal.

### Early Empire: Fides Publica and administrative authority

The temple remained an archive for official documents, demonstrated by military diplomas. Imperial coinage began to make Fides widely visible. Under the Flavians, *Fides Publica* appears as a draped woman with patera, fruit, grain, or cornucopia; a Domitianic bronze of 88–89 CE carries **FIDEI PVBLICAE S C** and shows her holding grain and a dish of fruit.

Flavian poetry simultaneously expanded her narrative role. Silius made her guardian of Saguntum; Statius dramatized her defeat by civil-war frenzy.

### Military Fides

From Vitellius and especially during the second and third centuries, *Fides Exercitus/Militum/Legionum* became common coin language. The goddess holds standards or stands among them. Examples span emperors and rival regimes; OCRE catalogs hundreds of types.

This was a substantial change in emphasis. Republican Fides guaranteed the Roman people’s public undertakings; imperial Fides often proclaimed armed loyalty to the ruler. The older civic meaning was not erased, but the emperor and army increasingly occupied the relationship.

### Philosophical and Christian criticism

Pliny and Cicero’s skeptical Academic speaker regarded Fides as a useful or consecrated quality without necessarily accepting an independent divine being. Christian apologists intensified this critique.

Lactantius argued that Romans wrongly placed Faith, Hope, Concord, Peace, Chastity, and Piety within temple walls because these properly belong in human minds. Augustine used Varro to expose what he saw as incoherent fragmentation of divine power. Their polemics are hostile but confirm the continuing comprehensibility of Fides as a pagan goddess.

### Suppression and disappearance

The cult’s precise end is undocumented. General imperial measures restricting sacrifice and closing temples in the late fourth and early fifth centuries provide the context, but no surviving edict or narrative names a suppression of Fides’s temple. There is likewise no securely documented martyr story, forced conversion episode, or destruction event particular to it.

The temple vanished as an institution; the word did not. Latin Christianity made *fides* central by using it to translate Greek *pistis*.

### Christian *fides*: continuity and discontinuity

**[Semantic transformation, not straightforward survival of a goddess]** In Latin Christian texts, *fides* came to denote trust in God and Christ, fidelity within the divine-human relationship, and increasingly assent to a body of doctrine. Teresa Morgan emphasizes that early Christian *pistis/fides* remained relational and practiced, not merely intellectual “belief.” Augustine later distinguished the faith by which one believes from what is believed.

The pagan goddess did not simply “become” the Christian theological virtue. The same Latin noun carried inherited meanings of reliability, loyalty, and trust into Christian discourse, while Christian scripture and theology reconfigured its object and content. Medieval personifications of Faith are therefore descendants of the word and allegorical method, not evidence for continuous worship of the Capitoline goddess.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Christian and post-classical art

In medieval and Renaissance art, **Fides/Faith** became one of the three theological virtues with Spes/Hope and Caritas/Charity, following 1 Corinthians 13:13. She may carry a cross, chalice, host, or creed-book. This figure is Christian Faith, not a preserved depiction of the Roman goddess, even though Latin naming and the personification of an abstract feminine noun created visual continuity.

Early-modern emblem books and allegorical programs sometimes borrowed Roman dress, coin poses, or clasped hands. Such images can fuse classical and Christian vocabularies, but they should be identified object by object.

### Political and commercial descendants

Clasped hands continued in medals, seals, marriage imagery, revolutionary iconography, fraternal organizations, and commercial symbols. The English phrase “good faith” and legal *bona fides* descend from the Latin concept without implying religious devotion.

“Fidelity,” “fiduciary,” “confide,” and “federal” belong to related Latin histories. The common dog name “Fido” exploits *fidus*, “faithful”; the dog is not therefore Fides’s ancient sacred animal.

### Occultism

**[Negative finding]** Fides has no substantial, securely documented role in Renaissance ceremonial magic, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or standard grimoire demonologies comparable with planetary gods, Egyptian deities, or named angels. Modern websites that assign her crystals, candle colors, planetary hours, or spell functions are constructing new correspondences.

The Christian theological virtue Faith does appear in esoteric allegory, but that is not evidence that occultists maintained or recovered the ancient state cult.

### Modern Roman polytheism

**[Modern revival]** Roman reconstructionist and broader neopagan practitioners sometimes honor Fides on 1 October or invoke her in rites concerning promises, civic trust, contracts, or reconciliation. Some reproduce Roman ritual forms: covered head, handwashing, incense and wine, invocation of Janus and Jupiter, and a concluding offering to Vesta.

This is a modern reconstruction based on Livy, calendars, and general Roman ritual evidence. No continuous initiatory lineage connects it with the Capitoline temple. The size of specifically Fides-centered worship cannot be established; she appears to be a secondary figure within small Roman-polytheist networks rather than the focus of a large independent movement.

The modern ADF calendar’s October rite is evidence for contemporary reception, not for details of ancient sacrifice beyond those separately documented.

### Literature and popular culture

Fides has not acquired the strongly individualized modern character given to Venus, Mars, or Minerva. When she appears in reference works, games, fantasy lists, or online deity guides, she is normally compressed into “goddess of loyalty.” Common modern distortions include:

- treating “Bona Fides” as her securely ancient personal name;
- giving her a dog as sacred animal because of “Fido”;
- treating her as identical with Pistis in every historical respect;
- calling her the daughter of Saturn, Jupiter, or Justice without a source;
- assigning fixed crystals, chakras, planets, colors, or tarot cards;
- presenting October 1 rituals devised today as continuously transmitted antiquity.

These are **[Modern invention]** unless explicitly marked as new devotional symbolism.

### Political appropriation

Roman *fides* has long been praised in nationalist accounts as a uniquely Roman virtue. Ancient Roman authors themselves contrasted Roman reliability with alleged foreign *perfidia*, notably “Punic faith.” Modern repetition can reproduce imperial propaganda as ethnographic fact.

No significant modern extremist organization has been found to use Fides herself as a central emblem. General appropriations of Roman religion, military standards, and civic virtue by fascist or ethnonationalist movements should not be transferred automatically to this goddess without specific evidence.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Was Fides an archaic goddess or a mid-Republican construction?

- **Traditional position:** Livy, Dionysius, and Plutarch preserve an authentic Numan institution; Fides belongs to the oldest Roman religion.
- **Critical position:** the first secure temple belongs to 254 BCE, while Numa is an etiological attribution written centuries later.
- **Balanced conclusion:** the specific Numan foundation is legendary; the ritual’s archaic features and Italic parallels may preserve earlier practice, but its date cannot be fixed.

Wissowa emphasized institutional cult and treated abstract divinities within Roman religious development. Dumézil interpreted Fides through Indo-European sovereignty, Jupiter, oaths, and the sacred right hand. Later scholars such as Freyburger, John Scheid, Mary Beard, John North, Simon Price, Anna Clark, and Teresa Morgan have paid greater attention to social relationships, ritual practice, political deployment, and the danger of separating “abstract concept” from “real deity.”

### Personification or goddess?

Older scholarship often asked whether Fides was “really” worshipped or merely personified. The dichotomy is misleading. A named power could receive temple, sacrifice, calendar date, image, and prayer precisely because Romans treated the quality as divinely effective. At the same time Cicero’s skeptical speaker shows that educated Romans could dispute the ontology.

“Abstract goddess” is therefore a modern analytical classification, not evidence that ancient worshippers regarded her as less real than an anthropomorphic Olympian.

### Temple date and location

Cicero’s attribution to Calatinus is accepted, but whether the dedication fell in 254 or 250 BCE depends on reconstructing his career. Modern works usually prefer 254.

The temple’s exact Capitoline position remains debated. Finds below the hill—architectural fragments, treaty inscriptions, bilingual dedications, and a probable statue base—fit the sanctuary’s archival function, but the deposits are displaced. Claims that a particular podium or female head is certainly Fides should be qualified.

A Palatine temple founded by Rhome is rejected for lack of corroboration. Likewise, a supposed altar of *Fides Candida* in the Forum rests on insecure interpretation.

### The hand-covering

Dumézil treated the covered right hand as a ritual expression of sovereign Fides and compared it with Umbrian Fisus Sancius’s *mandraculum*. Other interpretations emphasize:

- protection and preservation of the pledged hand;
- purity through white covering;
- concealment or secrecy;
- visual enactment of bonds and reciprocal obligation.

Livy himself says Fides must be protected and her seat in the right hand is sacred. “Secrecy” is an interpretation, not his explicit wording.

### Fides and force

Roman sources celebrated surrender *in fidem* as entry into protective trust. Modern historians note that the same formula could authorize nearly unrestricted Roman discretion. Fides could restrain power but also legitimate hierarchy. The record does not support either an entirely cynical or entirely benevolent reading in all periods.

### Fides and Christianity

Older narratives sometimes claim Christianity simply spiritualized or appropriated the goddess. Current semantic work is more careful. Pagan, Jewish, and Christian *pistis/fides* shared a world of trust and relationship, while Christian communities changed its theological center and eventually developed doctrinal “faith.” The Christian word is neither unrelated to Roman usage nor a disguised continuation of the cult.

### State of the evidence

Surviving evidence is unusually distributed:

- no certain cult statue;
- no intact temple;
- no mythological cycle from archaic Rome;
- strong later literary memory;
- calendrical confirmation;
- displaced architectural and treaty fragments;
- numerous military diplomas referring to the temple;
- a vast imperial coin corpus;
- late antique hostile testimony preserving lost antiquarian material.

The principal modern corpora and reference tools are CIL, especially volumes I and XVI; Republican and imperial coin catalogs including RRC and RIC/OCRE; LIMC for iconography; *Realencyclopädie*, *Daremberg-Saglio*, *Brill’s New Pauly*, and modern Roman topographies. Reusser’s study of the Capitoline temple and Ziolkowski’s work on mid-Republican temples materially altered discussion of its location and chronology.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Numa’s foundation as established archaeological fact.
- A continuous shrine securely dated from Numa to late antiquity.
- A certain Etruscan goddess directly ancestral to Fides.
- A standard winged cult statue inferred solely from Ennius.
- A dog as her sacred animal.
- Greek Pistis and Roman Fides as institutionally identical cults.
- Fides as a daughter or consort in a fixed divine genealogy.
- a Golden Dawn, Theosophical, or ancient witchcraft cult of Fides.
- human sacrifice or temple prostitution in her worship.
- the Christian theological virtue as uninterrupted survival of pagan worship.
