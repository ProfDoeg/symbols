# Concordia: Research Dossier

**Class:** Roman goddess; more precisely, a deified civic and relational quality (*dea/numen Concordia*), represented as a matron and treated in cult as a divine person rather than merely a literary allegory.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest independently datable physical evidence presently identified is the veiled, diademed head labeled **CONCORDIA** on denarii of L. Aemilius Lepidus Paullus, minted at Rome in **62 BCE** (RRC 415/1 and 417/1). The cult itself is older: Livy, writing chiefly in the late first century BCE, reports that Cn. Flavius dedicated an *aedes Concordiae* in the Vulcanal in **304 BCE** (*Ab urbe condita* 9.46.6). That event is the earliest reasonably recoverable cult notice, but its surviving witness is nearly three centuries later. A still earlier temple supposedly vowed by Camillus in **367 BCE** is known only from Augustan and imperial authors and is therefore a foundation tradition, not contemporary evidence.

**Evidentiary labels used below:** **[Early/secure]**, **[Later canonical]**, **[Regional]**, **[Learned systematization]**, **[Syncretistic identification]**, **[Late accretion]**, **[Hostile witness]**, **[Modern reconstruction]**, and **[Modern invention]**.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

Concordia is the divine form of Latin *concordia*: agreement, unanimity, civic cohesion, and—by extension—harmony between magistrates and people, political orders, soldiers and emperor, imperial colleagues, and spouses. Her temples repeatedly monumentalized a victor’s definition of “reconciliation”: Camillus’s alleged settlement of patrician–plebeian conflict; Cn. Flavius’s challenge to aristocratic monopoly; Opimius’s destruction of Gaius Gracchus and his adherents; and Tiberius’s presentation of the Julio-Claudian house as guarantor of public and dynastic unity.

She is therefore neither a narrative goddess with a stable birth, family, and cycle of adventures nor an empty metaphor. Romans gave her temples, altars, cult personnel, calendar observances, images, sacrifices, inscriptions, and coins. Cicero distinguishes such divine qualities from traditional anthropomorphic gods while acknowledging their cult: qualities “in us”—*mens, fides, virtus, concordia*—had consecrated images (*De natura deorum* 3.61, written 45 BCE). This is valuable contemporary Republican evidence for how reflective Romans could classify her.

### Name and etymology

- **Concordia**, feminine nominative singular; genitive **Concordiae**.
- Ordinary Latin *concordia* means agreement or harmony. It is traditionally analyzed from *con-* “together” and *cor, cordis* “heart”: literally, a condition of “hearts together.” This is a transparent Latin formation, although “joined hearts” is an etymological gloss, not an ancient secret name or proof of a prehistoric cult.
- The semantic opposite is **discordia**, “disagreement/strife.” Discordia became a vivid literary personification, especially in epic, but there is no comparable secure Roman state cult of Discordia.
- Greek translations and equations include:
  - **Ὁμόνοια (*Homonoia*)**, “oneness of mind/unanimity,” the closest political and civic equivalent.
  - **Ἁρμονία (*Harmonia*)**, “joining/harmony,” a Greek goddess with a separate Theban mythological genealogy.
  
  **[Syncretistic identification]** Roman and modern reference works often call Harmonia “the Greek Concordia,” but Homonoia is generally the closer functional equivalent. Harmonia’s genealogy as daughter of Ares and Aphrodite and wife of Cadmus must not be transferred to Concordia. No Roman source gives Concordia that ancestry.

### Documented titles

- **Concordia** — the unqualified civic goddess.
- **Concordia Augusta** — “Augustan/August Concord.” Attested in imperial calendars, inscriptions, architecture, and coinage. It can mean concord connected with Augustus or the imperial household rather than necessarily “Concordia renamed by Augustus.”
- **Concordia Augustorum** — concord of two or more Augusti, common in imperial coin legends; often abbreviated **CONCORDIA AVGG**.
- **Concordia Augusti** — concord associated with a single emperor.
- **Concordia exercituum / exercitus** — concord of the armies.
- **Concordia militum** — concord of the soldiers, especially prominent in third-century military coinage.
- **Concordia legionum** — concord of the legions.
- **Concordia praetorianorum** — concord of the Praetorians.
- **Concordia provinciarum** — concord of the provinces.
- **Concordia ordinum** — harmony of political orders. This is particularly a political formula, not always the formal title of a cult image.
- **Concordia populi Romani**, **senatus**, **decurionum**, and municipal or collegial forms — agreement of the Roman people, senate, town councils, or corporate bodies.
- **Concordia perpetua / aeterna** — perpetual or eternal concord, especially in later imperial ideology.
- **Concordia Panthea Augusta** — “August Concordia-All-Goddess,” attested at Gigthis in Roman Africa during the later second century CE. The dedication represents a provincial and pantheistic expansion of the figure, not her Republican identity. The temple inscription is CIL VIII 22693; a fragmentary companion inscription is CIL VIII 22692. **[Regional; learned/syncretistic expansion]** [ARYS study](https://e-revistas.uc3m.es/index.php/ARYS/en/article/view/8486).
- **Concordia maritalis** is a useful descriptive expression for matrimonial concord, but claims that it was a universally established ancient cult title require inscriptional proof. Coins and imperial ceremonies certainly apply *concordia* to marriage.

### The disputed title of Tiberius’s Roman temple

The conventional account calls Tiberius’s Forum building the **Aedes Concordiae Augustae**, dedicated on 16 January 10 CE. A recent reassessment by J. Bert Lott argues that the decisive *Augusta* wording rests on a local addition to the *Fasti Praenestini* and does not prove that Tiberius officially renamed the Roman goddess or temple. The secure claims are that Tiberius rebuilt the temple, associated it with himself and his deceased brother Drusus, and dedicated it with Livia; the formal imperial imposition of *Augusta* is disputed. **[Contested]** [Lott’s abstract and argument](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/full/10.1086/722631).

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### The evidence ladder

1. **367 BCE: Camillus foundation tradition.**  
   Ovid, *Fasti* 1.637–650, and Plutarch, *Camillus* 42.4, say that Camillus vowed or caused a temple of Concord to be built after patricians and plebeians were reconciled. Both texts are centuries later; Livy does not include this foundation where it might be expected. **[Later canonical foundation legend]** [Plutarch](https://lexundria.com/plut_cam/1.42.4/prr); [Ovid](https://bcs.fltr.ucl.ac.be/fastam/F1-637-724.html).

2. **304 BCE: Cn. Flavius’s dedication.**  
   Livy 9.46.6 says the curule aedile Cn. Flavius dedicated an *aedes Concordiae in area Vulcani*. The patricians objected, and the pontifex maximus Cornelius Barbatus yielded only under popular pressure, while maintaining that custom permitted only a consul or imperator to dedicate a temple. The senate then promoted legislation restricting future dedications. This is the earliest relatively concrete cult episode, but Livy’s text is Augustan. **[Early cult tradition; later literary witness]** [Latin text](https://lateinlex.de/?call=Puc&permalink=Liv_9_46).

3. **216 BCE: temple on the Arx.**  
   Livy 22.33.7–8 reports contracting for a temple of Concordia on the Capitoline Arx during the Hannibalic crisis; the *Fasti Praenestini* record the anniversary on 5 February: *Concordiae in arce feriae ex senatus consulto*. **[Early annalistic/calendar attestation]** [Calendar transcription](https://www.theatrum.de/2491.html).

4. **121 BCE: Opimius’s Forum temple.**  
   The consul L. Opimius erected or rebuilt a temple after the senatorial suppression and killing of Gaius Gracchus and his followers. Appian, Plutarch, and Augustine preserve the political memory; archaeological remains securely establish a late Republican temple beneath the Tiberian building. The slogan reported by Plutarch—effectively “a work of discord built a temple of Concord”—shows that the dedication was contested at the time or remembered as such. **[Secure late-Republican monument; narrative preserved later]**

5. **62 BCE: first securely datable labeled image.**  
   Denarii explicitly label the veiled and diademed head **CONCORDIA**. **[Secure physical evidence]** [CRRO RRC 417/1](https://numismatics.org/crro/id/rrc-417.1); [Berlin Münzkabinett example](https://ikmk.smb.museum/object?id=18256591&lang=en&view=vs).

### Primary literary corpus

- **Livy, *Ab urbe condita***, Latin, composed approximately 27–9 BCE. Books 9 and 22 supply the 304 and 216 BCE temple traditions. The surviving text comes through medieval manuscripts; it is not a contemporary Republican record.
- **Cicero, *De natura deorum***, Latin, 45 BCE. At 2.61 and 3.47/61, divine qualities including Concordia are evidence in philosophical arguments over what properly counts as a god. Cicero is contemporary evidence for late-Republican classification, not an archaic cult manual. [Book 2 text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Cic.+N.D.+2.60).
- **Cicero, speeches and letters**, 50s–40s BCE. The Temple of Concord served as a senatorial meeting place; Cicero’s *Philippic* 2.19 invokes a meeting there. These passages attest the temple as a political space more than the goddess’s ritual.
- **Sallust, *Bellum Catilinae* 46**, Latin, 40s BCE. The senate meets in the Temple of Concord during the Catilinarian crisis.
- **Varro, *De lingua Latina* 5.73**, Latin, 40s BCE, refers to the topography and divine nomenclature of Rome. Much of Varro’s theological writing is lost and survives only in Christian quotation.
- **Virgil, *Aeneid***, Latin, 29–19 BCE. Virgil elaborates *concordia/discordia* as cosmic and civic opposites but does not give Concordia a substantial independent mythic biography.
- **Ovid, *Fasti***, Latin, composed under Augustus and revised in exile under Tiberius, surviving in medieval manuscripts:
  - 1.637–650: Camillus’s vow and Tiberius’s restoration.
  - 6.89–100: Concordia intervenes in the rivalry among Juno and Juventas over the name of June.
  - 6.637–648: Livia’s shrine or temple dedication on 11 June.  
  [Book 6 text and translation](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidFasti6.html).
- **Plutarch, *Life of Camillus* 42** and *Life of Gaius Gracchus* 17, Greek, late first/early second century CE. These preserve the Camillan foundation and popular criticism of Opimius.
- **Suetonius, *Tiberius* 20**, Latin, early second century CE. Tiberius dedicates temples of Concord and Castor and Pollux in his and Drusus’s names, paid for from war spoils. [Text](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.02.0132%3Alife%3Dtib.%3Achapter%3D20).
- **Cassius Dio, *Roman History* 55.8.2**, Greek, early third century CE. Tiberius undertakes the temple’s repair so that his and Drusus’s names may be inscribed upon it. [Text](https://lexundria.com/dio/55.8/cy).
- **Pliny the Elder, *Natural History***, Latin, 77–79 CE. Books 34–37 inventory Greek sculptures, paintings, gems, and precious works displayed in the temple. The temple had become something like a public art treasury.
- **Festus**, epitome of Verrius Flaccus, second century CE preserving Augustan lexicography, contributes calendar and antiquarian material but no independent Concordia biography.
- **Macrobius, *Saturnalia***, Latin, early fifth century CE, preserves antiquarian material about the Roman calendar and earlier authors. It is late and learned evidence.
- **Servius, commentary on Virgil**, Latin, fourth/fifth century CE, is likewise a late repository of Republican and Augustan antiquarian traditions.
- **Augustine, *De civitate Dei* 3.25–26**, Latin, early fifth century CE, is a hostile but historically valuable witness. He mocks the Opimian temple as a monument erected after massacre and asks why Rome did not also build a temple to Discordia. His polemic preserves the temple’s political memory but must not be mistaken for neutral ritual description. [Book 3, chapter 25](https://www.biblehub.com/library/augustine/city_of_god/chapter_25_of_the_temple_of.htm).
- **Arnobius, Lactantius, and other Christian apologists**, third–fourth centuries CE, attack Roman divine personifications as evidence of arbitrary polytheism. They are witnesses to late-antique intellectual classification and polemic, not reliable guides to early ritual.

### Epigraphic, calendrical, and numismatic corpus

- *Fasti Praenestini*: 5 February observance for Concordia on the Arx; a disputed *Augusta* entry connected with 16 January.
- CIL VI 89: late restoration inscription from the Forum temple, perhaps after the fire of 283/284.
- CIL VI 90–94, 30856–30857: dedications recovered from or near the Forum sanctuary.
- CIL VI 2204, 2205, 8703: temple *aeditui*, custodians or caretakers.
- CIL X 810–811: Eumachia’s Pompeian complex dedicated to **Concordia Augusta and Pietas**, probably in the early first century CE. [Inscription record](https://heurist.huma-num.fr/heurist/judaism_and_rome/tpl/public-record/294).
- CIL VIII 22692–22693: **Concordia Panthea Augusta** at Gigthis.
- African municipal dedication: *Concordiae Augustae sacrum pagus et civitas Thuggensis pecunia publica*—“Sacred to Concordia Augusta; the district and community of Thugga [dedicated it] with public money.” **[Regional/secure inscription]** [SIRAR record](https://humanidadesdigitales.uc3m.es/s/nuevo-sirar/item/47678).
- Hispania: a dedication jointly to Augustus, Pax Perpetua, and Concordia Augusta, paid for by Q. Vibius Felicio and Vibia Felicula. **[Regional; imperial cult network]** [DEpHis record](https://humanidadesdigitales.uc3m.es/s/dephis/item/56798).
- Imperial coinage from the first through late third centuries repeatedly uses Concordia types. These are not mere illustrations: they are officially issued, mass-circulated statements connecting political unity with divine sanction.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domains

- **Civic harmony:** her oldest reconstructable function.
- **Reconciliation of political orders:** patrician and plebeian, senate and people.
- **Legitimization after civil conflict:** particularly visible in the Opimian dedication.
- **Dynastic harmony:** under Augustus and Tiberius, unity of the imperial household.
- **Marital concord:** increasingly emphasized on coins of empresses and imperial couples.
- **Military loyalty:** *Concordia militum, exercituum,* and *legionum*, especially during succession crises.
- **Municipal and collegial solidarity:** provincial cities and occupational associations adapted her to their corporate needs.
- **Prosperity produced by order:** conveyed iconographically through the cornucopia.

There is no secure evidence that Concordia governed music, astronomical harmony, a color, a sacred number, or a particular celestial body. Such lists on modern occult websites are **[Modern invention]** unless tied to a cited ancient object or text.

### Standard image

The normal mature type is a dignified Roman matron:

- veiled or diademed;
- standing or seated on a throne;
- holding a **patera**, marking sacrifice;
- holding one or two **cornucopiae**, marking abundance;
- sometimes holding a **scepter**, indicating authority;
- occasionally associated with a **caduceus**, joined hands, an olive branch, or a small figure of Spes.

A Roman lead token of the first–fourth centuries shows her seated with patera and cornucopia. [British Museum B.8137](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_B-8137).

The earliest securely labeled portrait identified here is the veiled and diademed head on the denarius of 62 BCE. This means the mature matronal iconography was established by the late Republic, but it does not demonstrate that the same image existed in 367 or 304 BCE.

### Joined hands

The **dextrarum iunctio**, clasped right hands, became the most economical symbol of concord. It may represent:

- agreement between political or military parties;
- marriage;
- joint rule;
- the bond between emperor and army.

A 42 BCE denarius of L. Mussidius Longus combines Concordia’s head with hands clasped around a caduceus. [British Museum object](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_2002-0102-4757).

### The Tiberian temple on coins

A sestertius of Tiberius, issued around 35–36 CE, depicts the Forum temple:

- Concordia seated inside;
- a patera and scepter or cornucopia;
- Hercules and Mercury at the approach;
- a Capitoline triad-like group of Jupiter, Juno, and Minerva above;
- Victories as acroteria.

The coin is evidence for the temple’s imperial presentation, although numismatic architecture is selective and schematic rather than a measured elevation.

### Ara Pacis: association, not identification

The Ara Pacis Augustae was decreed in 13 BCE and dedicated in 9 BCE to commemorate Augustus’s return from Spain and Gaul. Its program joins peace, fertility, civic order, priesthood, family succession, and imperial consensus. These ideas strongly overlap the Augustan development of Concordia.

Nevertheless:

- no surviving inscription calls an Ara Pacis figure Concordia;
- the celebrated seated female on the east side has been identified variously as Pax, Tellus, Italia, Ceres, or a composite;
- Nancy de Grummond argued specifically for **Pax Augusta**, not Concordia. [AJA article](https://www.journals.uchicago.edu/doi/pdfplus/10.2307/505125).

Thus a “Concordia–Ara Pacis link” is best stated as **shared Augustan ideology and perhaps workshop/iconographic environment**, not as a secure image of Concordia. **[Modern scholarly interpretation; contested]**

### False or unsupported attributes

No ancient evidence located supports the popular assignment to Concordia of:

- a unique sacred gemstone;
- a fixed magical seal;
- Libra as her zodiacal sign;
- Friday or Venus as her special day;
- a rainbow color scheme;
- a standardized modern prayer or “ancient invocation.”

These are **[Modern invention]** unless separately documented.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

No Latin cult, inscription, Roman historian, or Roman calendar gives Concordia parents, siblings, spouse, or offspring. This absence is structurally significant: Roman divine qualities were often defined by their sphere and civic installation rather than by a Greek-style family tree.

The following genealogies do **not** belong to Concordia:

- Harmonia as daughter of Ares and Aphrodite;
- Homonoia as daughter of Soter and Praxidike in the late lexicographical tradition;
- any claim that Discordia/Eris is literally Concordia’s sister.

Those are analogical transfers. **[Modern harmonization unless explicitly qualified]**

### Pantheonic position

Concordia had no fixed rank in a genealogical hierarchy. Her prestige followed political use. She could be:

- a civic goddess with a temple used by the senate;
- an Augustan divine quality attached to the ruling house;
- a military virtue on coinage;
- a provincial *Panthea* absorbing universalizing language.

This is functional promotion, not a mythic enthronement.

### Every documented interaction or material conjunction with named divine beings

Because Concordia has almost no narrative mythology, this list distinguishes actual narrative contact from mere equation or co-display.

- **Juno — rivalry interrupted:** In Ovid, *Fasti* 6.1–100, Juno and Juventas advance competing etymologies for June; Concordia arrives before their competition becomes a quarrel and offers a third derivation from the joining of Romans and Sabines. Date: early first century CE. **[Later canonical literary scene]**
- **Juventas/Hebe — rivalry interrupted:** Same episode and date. Concordia enters the goddess’s dispute and leaves Ovid unable to adjudicate among the three. **[Later canonical literary scene]**
- **Quirinus/Romulus — narrated exemplar, not face-to-face interaction:** Concordia recounts how Quirinus and Titus Tatius joined their kingdoms and peoples. She does not meet him within the narrated action. Ovid, *Fasti* 6.91–96. **[Later canonical etiological speech]**
- **Apollo — emblematic association:** Ovid describes Concordia entering with her hair bound by “Apollo’s laurel” (*Apollinea lauro*). No conversation or joint action occurs. *Fasti* 6.91. **[Literary attribute, not interaction]**
- **Jupiter — architectural co-presence:** Jupiter, Juno, and Minerva appear in the superstructure of the Tiberian temple as represented on imperial coins. This is a monumental hierarchy, not a mythic episode. **[Secure numismatic conjunction]**
- **Juno — architectural co-presence:** Same evidence and limitation.
- **Minerva — architectural co-presence:** Same evidence and limitation.
- **Hercules — architectural co-presence:** Statues of Hercules and Mercury flank the temple approach on the Tiberian architectural coin type. No myth of Hercules interacting with Concordia is attested. **[Secure numismatic conjunction]**
- **Mercury — architectural co-presence:** Same evidence and limitation.
- **Castor and Pollux — parallel dedication:** Suetonius says Tiberius dedicated Concord’s temple and that of Castor and Pollux from German spoils in his and Drusus’s names. This joins the cult buildings within one dynastic program but does not narrate a meeting among the gods. *Tiberius* 20. **[Secure literary conjunction]**
- **Pietas — joint dedication:** Eumachia dedicated her Pompeian building to Concordia Augusta and Pietas. CIL X 810–811, early first century CE. **[Secure regional cult conjunction]**
- **Pax — joint dedication:** An inscription from Hispania jointly honors Augustus, Pax Perpetua, and Concordia Augusta. **[Secure regional cult conjunction]**
- **Salus — iconographic pairing:** Concordia, Pax, and Salus occur within the same broad imperial virtue-system, but no surviving canonical myth records a personal encounter. **[Imperial iconographic association]**
- **Spes — held image:** Some coin types show Concordia resting on or holding a small figure of Spes. This is a visual relation of concord and hope, not narrative mythology. **[Secure numismatic interaction]**
- **Annona — held image:** An imperial type shows Annona holding a statuette of Concordia, symbolically linking grain supply and social stability. **[Secure numismatic interaction]**
- **Homonoia — functional equation:** Greek civic Homonoia is the closest translation and was used in bilingual imperial environments. This is cultural translation, not a meeting between two goddesses. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Harmonia — loose interpretive equation:** Ancient and modern comparison rests on “harmony,” but their mythic identities differ. **[Syncretistic/modern systematization]**
- **Discordia — conceptual opposition:** Augustine treats Concordia and Discordia as rival divine abstractions and imagines Discordia’s anger, but this is Christian polemical parody drawing on the Greek Eris story. No Roman cult narrative records their combat. *City of God* 3.25–26. **[Hostile late-antique literary construction]**

No securely attested encounter with Venus, Mars, Ceres, Vesta, or Fortuna has been located merely because those gods share imperial ideological space with Concordia. Co-membership in a pantheon is not interaction.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Concordia possesses three substantial narrative complexes. All are political aetiologies, not biographies.

### A. Camillus and the reconciliation of the orders

**Full story:** After prolonged conflict over plebeian access to the consulship, Camillus helps reconcile patricians and plebeians. He vows a temple to Concordia. Plutarch says the assembly voted for it to face the Forum and place of assembly, permanently memorializing the settlement.

**Earliest surviving sources:** Ovid, *Fasti* 1.637–644, written around the turn of the era; Plutarch, *Camillus* 42, approximately a century later.

**Problem:** Livy, whose account of the Licinian-Sextian crisis is fuller and earlier than Plutarch’s, does not record Camillus’s Concordia temple. Archaeology has not securely demonstrated a distinct 367 BCE building beneath the later Forum temple. The story may preserve genuine cult memory, but it may also be late-Republican/Augustan retrojection designed to give political concord a fourth-century origin.

**Classification:** **[Later canonical foundation legend; historicity unresolved]**.

### B. Cn. Flavius and plebeian access to sacred authority

Livy’s account is less mythic but more securely integrated into Republican institutional history. Cn. Flavius, son of a freedman and associated with the publication of legal and calendrical knowledge, dedicates Concordia’s shrine in the Vulcanal. Aristocrats resist; the pontifex maximus is compelled by popular consensus to dictate the formula.

The episode makes the shrine itself an enactment of social conflict:

- Concordia is dedicated amid discord;
- a socially marginal magistrate challenges patrician ritual privilege;
- subsequent legislation restricts dedications.

**Classification:** **[Early cult tradition preserved by an Augustan historian]**. It should not be conflated automatically with Camillus’s supposed building.

### C. Opimius after Gaius Gracchus

In 121 BCE the senate authorized violence against Gaius Gracchus and his supporters. Opimius’s subsequent temple cast senatorial victory as restored concord. Popular memory resisted that framing. Plutarch reports graffiti condemning the building as a work of discord.

Augustine later turns the contradiction into Christian polemic: if Concord had truly been present, the massacre would not have occurred; if divine abstractions deserve cult, Rome should have built for Discordia too.

This is the central political myth of Concordia: not neutral reconciliation but the power to define the outcome of civil violence as consensus.

**Classification:** Monument **[secure]**; slogan and detailed narrative **[later literary preservation of a Republican controversy]**.

### D. Concordia and the naming of June

In Ovid’s *Fasti* 6, Juno and Juventas offer competing derivations of *Iunius*. Their disagreement threatens to become a quarrel. Concordia enters, crowned with Apollonian laurel, and proposes that June derives from *iungere*, “to join,” commemorating the union of Romulus/Quirinus and Titus Tatius after the Sabine conflict. Ovid refuses to select a winner.

This is an Augustan literary creation or elaboration, not an archaic cosmogony. It connects:

- calendar;
- union of Romans and Sabines;
- Augustus/Tiberius as pacifying rulers;
- Concordia’s intervention in divine disagreement.

**Classification:** **[Later canonical Augustan elaboration]**.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Forum temple

The Temple of Concord stood against the Capitoline slope at the western end of the Forum, near the Vulcanal, Tabularium, and later Temple of Vespasian.

Successive phases:

- alleged Camillan foundation, 367 BCE;
- Cn. Flavius’s dedication in the Vulcanal, 304 BCE, possibly a separate shrine;
- Opimian temple, 121 BCE;
- Tiberian rebuilding begun in 7 BCE;
- dedication on 16 January 10 CE;
- late imperial restoration, perhaps after the fire of 283/284.

The Tiberian site was constrained between the Capitoline slope and Forum. Its cella was unusually broad relative to its façade. The surviving podium and architectural fragments belong chiefly to this phase. [Topographical summary and ancient citations](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Places/Europe/Italy/Lazio/Roma/Rome/_Texts/PLATOP%2A/Concordia.html).

### Temple as political chamber

The senate met there on several crucial occasions. This was not incidental reuse: the building’s divine title made it a charged setting for debates on conspiracy, civil conflict, and political legitimacy. Cicero and Sallust attest meetings there.

### Art treasury

Pliny records works by major Greek artists in the temple, including:

- a bronze group of Leto with Apollo and Artemis attributed to Euphranor;
- a Marsyas by Zeuxis;
- a Cassandra by Theoros;
- other sculptures, paintings, gems, and precious objects.

The MANTO aggregation identifies the relevant Plinian passages, including *Natural History* 34.77, 35.66, and 35.144. [MANTO temple record](https://manto.unh.edu/viewer.p/60/2616/object/6580-11329122).

### Arx temple

The temple contracted during the Second Punic War was dedicated on the Arx and had a 5 February anniversary. Its exact archaeological location has not been securely identified. It should be kept distinct from the better-known Forum building.

### Livia’s shrine

Ovid, *Fasti* 6.637–648, assigns 11 June to a dedication by Livia. The structure is normally placed in or near the Porticus Liviae on the Esquiline. It extends Concordia from corporate reconciliation to the moral and dynastic presentation of the imperial household. **[Secure literary attestation; archaeological identification uncertain]**

### Pompeii: Eumachia’s building

Eumachia, a public priestess and elite benefactor, built and dedicated a *chalcidicum*, crypta, and porticoes in her own and her son’s name to **Concordia Augusta and Pietas**. The inscription is secure; the building’s exact economic function remains debated. A traditional identification as a fullers’ guild hall is not established merely because the fullers dedicated a statue to Eumachia.

The complex demonstrates:

- provincial reception of Augustan divine qualities;
- female elite patronage;
- dynastic and familial messaging;
- Concordia joined materially with Pietas.  
  [Pompeii archaeological description](https://www.pompeionline.net/en/buildings-and-places/regio-vii/portico-of-concordia-augusta-building-of-eumachia-vii-9-1-and-67).

### Priests and personnel

No distinct, empire-wide priestly college devoted solely to Concordia is securely attested. The Roman temple had *aeditui*, documented in CIL VI. Provincial dedications could be handled by:

- municipal magistrates;
- public priestesses;
- Augustales or seviri;
- local councils;
- corporate associations;
- private benefactors.

Claims of a specialized secret “Order of Concordia” are **[Modern invention]**.

### Offerings and sacrifice

The patera signifies ordinary Roman sacrificial practice: libation and animal offering within civic cult. Calendar entries establish festival or dedication anniversaries, but no surviving ritual book gives a uniquely Concordian sacrificial victim, hymn, or mystery initiation.

There is **no evidence** connecting Concordia’s cult specifically with:

- human sacrifice;
- temple prostitution;
- sexual initiation;
- ecstatic possession;
- mutilation;
- oracular consultation;
- a mystery cult.

Absence here is a positive finding from the dedicated contested-material search.

### Politics and cult economics

Temples were funded through public decisions, war spoils, imperial patronage, and elite benefaction. The political uses are unusually explicit:

- Flavius’s shrine challenged patrician authority.
- Opimius’s temple claimed senatorial violence had restored order.
- Tiberius used German spoils and inscribed his and Drusus’s names.
- Eumachia used private wealth for public religious benefaction.
- Provincial communities used public money to dedicate to Concordia Augusta.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Before 304 BCE: hypothetical prehistory

Scholars including Wissowa and Latte treated Concordia among Roman “abstract” or functional divinities; Dumézil placed such cults within Roman civic theology. No securely identified Etruscan deity, mirror inscription, or Italic cult can presently be shown to be Concordia’s direct precursor.

The Etruscan-mirror route requested in the brief produced no secure named prototype. Greek Harmonia scenes on Etruscan mirrors do not establish an indigenous Etruscan Concordia cult. **[Absence of evidence; proposed roots unproven]**

### 304–121 BCE: Republican institutionalization

The Flavius and Arx temples associate Concordia with civic order during institutional and military crisis. Evidence is literary and calendrical, preserved later.

### 121–44 BCE: contested late-Republican concord

The Opimian temple makes Concordia an overtly ideological deity. The Roman senate repeatedly met within her temple. Coin portraits from 62 BCE provide the first secure labeled image. During civil war, *concordia* could be invoked by competing factions without ending conflict.

### 44–27 BCE: triumviral appropriation

Joined hands, caduceus, and Concordia’s head circulated on coinage in the aftermath of Caesar’s assassination. “Concord” became a promise made by unstable alliances. The visual language was portable precisely because it did not require a long myth.

### Augustus and Tiberius

The Augustan regime shifted Concordia toward:

- unity of the restored state;
- harmony of the domus Augusta;
- agreement between imperial family members;
- an ordered world linked with Pax and Pietas.

Tiberius’s reconstruction culminated in the dedication of 10 CE. The temple also functioned as an art treasury and dynastic monument. Whether its official name included *Augusta* remains disputed.

### First–second centuries CE: imperial and provincial expansion

Concordia appears broadly on coins and inscriptions. Her referent varies:

- emperor and senate;
- emperor and heir;
- husband and wife;
- co-emperors;
- army and ruler;
- city and imperial house.

At Pompeii she joins Pietas; in Africa and Hispania she enters local imperial-cult systems. At Gigthis she becomes *Panthea Augusta*.

### Third century: military and collegial concord

During rapid succession and civil war, **CONCORDIA MILITVM**, **EXERCITVVM**, **LEGIONVM**, and **AVGG** become especially prominent. Their repetition does not prove actual unity; often it advertises the desired loyalty most urgently when it was insecure.

### Fourth century: legal contraction of public cult

Imperial Christian legislation progressively restricted sacrifice, temple endowments, and public pagan rites. A recent study emphasizes that fourth-century temple policy was uneven and that Rome and Alexandria could receive exemptions or distinctive treatment; closure was a process, not one universal event. [JRS study](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-roman-studies/article/legal-exemption-for-the-temple-cults-of-rome-and-alexandria-in-the-fourth-century/8FCF6B2789837BED8D95874E77605DC5).

No surviving decree specifically names the Temple of Concordia as closed, destroyed, or converted into a church. Claims of a single dramatic Christian destruction are therefore **unestablished**.

### Fifth century onward

Augustine still expected readers to recognize the temple and its political meaning. Thereafter the cult disappears as a functioning institution. The Forum building survived in ruin, was recorded by medieval visitors, and was progressively quarried. Its podium and scattered fragments remain.

There is no evidence of continuous folk worship of Concordia under another name. The Latin word, however, survived powerfully in political, ecclesiastical, legal, and educational vocabulary.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Allegory and political vocabulary

*Concordia* became a durable Latin emblem of agreement:

- confessional documents such as the Lutheran *Formula/Book of Concord*;
- civic seals and monuments;
- universities and cities named Concordia;
- allegorical figures representing national or municipal unity.

These uses often invoke the word rather than worship of the ancient goddess.

### Numismatic revival

Laura Cretara’s design for Italy’s 1970 commemorative 1,000-lire coin marking Rome as capital drew on the Republican Concordia type. This is a deliberate modern national reception of Roman imagery, not cult revival.

### Scholarship and museums

Excavation and reconstruction of the Forum, publication of the CIL, systematic coin catalogues, and the LIMC transformed Concordia from a literary entry into an archaeological and iconographic subject. Digital corpora such as CRRO, OCRE, EDCS, and SIRAR now permit cross-regional comparison.

### Modern paganism

Roman reconstructionist religion emerged within the broader reconstructionist turn of the late twentieth century, with particular growth from the 1990s. Individual practitioners may honor Concordia in civic, household, or peace-making rites. No reliable demographic evidence isolates a Concordia-specific movement, clergy, temple network, or continuous lineage.

Any contemporary ritual is therefore:

- **[Modern reconstruction]** when explicitly composed from Roman prayer and offering conventions;
- **[Modern invention]** when marketed as an unchanged ancient prayer, spell, planetary correspondence, or initiation without primary evidence.

### Occult and esoteric traditions

The dedicated search found no substantial role for Concordia in the canonical systems of Renaissance ceremonial magic, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Thelema, or major modern grimoires. She may appear in modern deity dictionaries or eclectic correspondence lists, but these do not demonstrate an inherited esoteric tradition.

Claims that Concordia has an ancient magical sigil, tarot card, chakra, crystal, or planetary hour are **[Modern invention]** unless the author openly presents them as new symbolism.

### Literature, film, games, and commerce

Unlike Venus, Mars, or Discord/Eris, Concordia has not developed a major independent modern narrative franchise. Modern appearances usually reduce her to:

- a generic goddess of harmony;
- a civic allegory;
- a visual coin type;
- a name for institutions, ships, towns, products, or fictional organizations.

The commercial name “Concordia” ordinarily derives from the Latin virtue rather than from a specific claim of worship.

### Extremist or nationalist appropriation

Roman imperial imagery has been appropriated by multiple modern political movements, but no well-documented Concordia-specific extremist cult or symbol was identified. General claims that her clasped hands or cornucopia are extremist symbols would be misleading. Her imagery has more commonly served state unity and consensual rhetoric, which can itself mask coercive politics—as the Opimius episode already demonstrates.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### From “abstract deity” to divine quality

Older scholarship—especially Georg Wissowa, Kurt Latte, and classical encyclopedias—often classified Concordia among personified abstractions, implicitly contrasting her with supposedly more “authentic” mythic gods. Georges Dumézil treated Roman religion structurally and institutionally, drawing attention to functions rather than demanding Greek-style myth.

More recent scholarship, especially Anna Clark’s *Divine Qualities: Cult and Community in Republican Rome* (2007), argues that terms such as Concordia, Victoria, Salus, and Honos became divine through situated acts of naming, dedication, ritual, and political community. “Personification” must not be allowed to imply unreality to ancient worshippers.

### Was Camillus’s temple historical?

**For authenticity:**

- Ovid and Plutarch preserve a coherent foundation linked to the Licinian-Sextian settlement.
- The story matches Concordia’s later civic function.
- Later rebuilding might have erased early architectural evidence.

**Against or cautious:**

- Livy’s silence is conspicuous.
- the evidence is several centuries late;
- the foundation fits late-Republican and Augustan political retrospection;
- Cn. Flavius’s 304 shrine is the earliest concrete dedication in Livy.

Current responsible formulation: the Camillan temple is a Roman tradition, not an archaeologically established fact.

### One temple or several?

Ancient evidence records:

- a supposed Camillan Forum-facing temple;
- Flavius’s shrine in the Vulcanal;
- a temple on the Arx;
- Opimius’s Forum temple;
- Livia’s Esquiline shrine.

Topographical proximity encouraged ancient and modern conflation. The safest reconstruction treats them as distinct unless architectural or textual evidence explicitly identifies a rebuild on the same footprint.

### Did Opimius restore an old temple or build anew?

The archaeological and literary record permits either a replacement of an earlier sanctuary or a politically presented new foundation. What is secure is the monumental late-Republican phase and its connection with the aftermath of Gaius Gracchus.

### Concordia Augusta: official renaming?

The traditional view treats Tiberius’s dedication as the *Aedes Concordiae Augustae*. Lott’s 2023 article argues that *Augusta* in the Praenestine calendar reflects local editorial initiative and cannot prove an official renaming at Rome. This is now a live textual-historical dispute, not a settled title.

### Homonoia or Harmonia?

- **Homonoia** is the close civic-political analogue.
- **Harmonia** supplies a familiar mythological “Greek equivalent” but has a distinct genealogy and Theban cycle.
- Direct derivation of Roman Concordia from either Greek cult has not been demonstrated.
- Similarity, translation, and later equation must be separated from origin.

### Etruscan and Italic roots

No secure Etruscan name, inscription, or cult image has been demonstrated as Concordia’s ancestor. References to Etruscan mirrors are relevant mainly because they transmit Greek Harmonia imagery. Moving from iconographic transmission to cultic descent would exceed the evidence.

### Ara Pacis identification

Concordia belongs to the Ara Pacis’s ideological field, but identifying the east-panel female as Concordia is unsupported by an inscription and competes with Pax, Tellus, Italia, and Ceres. Pax remains a major scholarly identification. Any categorical “this is Concordia” caption is **[Modern overstatement]**.

### Discredited or unsupported popular claims

- Concordia is the daughter of Venus and Mars — transfer from Harmonia.
- Concordia and Harmonia are simply the same goddess — excessive harmonization.
- Camillus’s temple is archaeologically certain — false.
- Tiberius unquestionably renamed the Roman temple Concordia Augusta — disputed.
- Concordia had mystery initiations or sacred prostitution — no evidence.
- Her cult practiced human sacrifice — no evidence.
- She possessed a surviving ancient magical sigil or tarot correspondence — modern invention.
- The Ara Pacis definitively depicts her — unproven.

### State of the evidence

The record is unusually uneven:

- no archaic hymn;
- no surviving Republican cult statue;
- no complete ritual book;
- rich late-Republican and imperial coinage;
- substantial architectural remains;
- scattered calendar and epigraphic testimony;
- literary accounts written after the earliest supposed foundations.

The principal materials are divided among the Roman Forum, Naples and Pompeii collections, major coin cabinets including London and Berlin, CIL/EDCS inscriptions, and modern digital numismatic corpora.
