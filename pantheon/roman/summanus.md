# Summanus: Research Dossier

**Class:** Male god (*deus*) of Roman public religion; principally the divine sender or controller of nocturnal lightning and thunder. Late-antique writers sometimes reclassified him as Pluto/Dis Pater, ruler of the dead.

**Earliest secure surviving attestation:** Plautus, *Curculio* 413–416, probably performed around 193 BCE, uses *Summanus* as a recognizable divine name in a comic pun. The earliest recorded cultic event is earlier—the dedication of his temple during the Pyrrhic War, conventionally dated 278 BCE—but that date is preserved only in later literary and calendrical evidence. Varro’s claim that Titus Tatius established an altar to Summanus belongs to Roman antiquarian reconstruction, not securely datable regal-period evidence.

**Evidentiary key:** **[Early attestation]**, **[Later Roman evidence]**, **[Regional variant]**, **[Learned systematization]**, **[Syncretistic identification]**, **[Late accretion]**, **[Modern reconstruction]**, **[Modern invention]**.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Core identity

**[Early attestation / later corroboration]** Summanus is the Roman divine power to whom nocturnal lightning was assigned, complementary to Jupiter’s control of daytime lightning. Festus’ epitome of Verrius Flaccus defines the *fulgur nocturnum* as belonging to Summanus; Pliny the Elder, *Natural History* 2.138, likewise says that Romans attributed daytime bolts to Jupiter and nighttime bolts to Summanus. This is the best-attested functional definition of the god. [Pliny’s text](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/holland/pliny2.html) and John Scheid’s modern synthesis in the [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/550566361) support the distinction.

The evidence does not establish that Summanus was merely an “epithet of Jupiter.” Roman sources can coordinate the two powers, and some modern reconstructions speak of a nocturnal Jupiter, but Summanus possessed his own temple, festival, offerings, name in calendars, and independent place in ritual formulas.

### Name and forms

- **Latin:** *Summānus*, genitive *Summānī*. The vowel quantity is generally reconstructed as long in the second syllable.
- **Cult title:** *Summanus Pater*, “Father Summanus,” attested in the Arval records for 224 CE.
- **Temple expression:** *aedes Summani* or *templum Summani*, “temple of Summanus.”
- **Festival food:** *summanalia* or, in some manuscript traditions, *summonalia*: flour cakes shaped *in modum rotae*, “in the form of a wheel.”
- **Lightning formula:** *fulgur Summanum* or *fulgur Summanium*, a bolt classified as belonging to Summanus; inscriptions record the ritual burial or enclosure of such lightning.
- **Plautine wordplay:** *Summanus* is jokingly associated with *summānāre/manāre*, “to soak, drip, or wet,” in *Curculio* 413–416. The joke depends on the name already being recognizable.

### Etymologies

No etymology is certain.

1. **\*sub-māne, “before morning” or “toward dawn.”**  
   **[Ancient/modern linguistic interpretation]** This connects the name with *māne*, “morning,” and explains Summanus as the god of the night immediately preceding dawn. Assimilation of *bm* to *mm* would yield *Summanus*. Wissowa, Fowler, and Dumézil accepted versions of this explanation. Fowler rendered him the god who sends lightning “before dawn.” The phonology and nocturnal function make it plausible, but it remains an etymology, not an ancient cult statement. [Fowler’s discussion](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/59007/59007-h/59007-h.htm)

2. **\*summus Manium, “highest/greatest of the Manes.”**  
   **[Late learned systematization]** Martianus Capella, *De nuptiis Philologiae et Mercurii* 2.161, explains Summanus as Pluto, *quasi summus Manium*, “as though the highest of the Manes.” This late-antique folk etymology helped transform the lightning god into an underworld sovereign. It is linguistically strained and is normally rejected as the original derivation, but it is historically important because it altered the god’s literary identity. [Martianus Capella 2.161](https://lacabalesta.it/biblioteca/MarzianoCapella/Eng/MC_Eng_02_Marriage.html)

3. **Connection with *summus*, “highest.”**  
   **[Disputed modern reconstruction]** This has been invoked to make Summanus a god of the upper sky, but it poorly explains the consistent nocturnal association.

4. **Prosdocimi’s solar proposal.**  
   **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]** A. L. Prosdocimi proposed an association with *summum solis*, the sun’s highest position, relating it to the festival near the summer solstice. The theory attempts to explain both the date and wheel imagery but has not displaced the *sub-māne* interpretation.

5. **Sabine or Etruscan name.**  
   **[Ancient antiquarian claim / disputed reconstruction]** Varro associated the cult with Titus Tatius and a group of allegedly Sabine altars; Pliny placed the Roman two-god lightning classification against an Etruscan system of nine lightning gods. Neither passage proves that the name itself is Sabine or Etruscan. No securely identified Etruscan inscription or labeled mirror supplies an Etruscan god “Summamus.” Claims that such a being is represented on Etruscan mirrors are unsupported or arise from older speculative comparisons.

### Identifications

- **Jupiter:** **[Functional association, not secure identity]** Jupiter sends daytime lightning; Summanus sends nighttime lightning. The two are complementary recipients where the time of a strike was uncertain. The expression “Jupiter Summanus,” although used in some modern accounts, is not adequate evidence that Summanus was originally only a Jovian epithet.
- **Pluto/Dis Pater:** **[Late syncretistic identification]** Arnobius and Martianus Capella treat Summanus as the underworld abducter or as another name for Pluto. This is securely attested as a late-antique identification, but it is not securely the god’s early Roman identity.
- **Varuna:** **[Modern comparative reconstruction]** Dumézil compared Summanus’ dark, alarming sovereignty with Vedic Varuṇa and contrasted him with Dius Fidius as a Roman analogue to the Varuṇa–Mitra opposition. This is a structural comparison, not evidence for Roman worshippers equating the gods.
- **Etruscan lightning gods:** **[Learned comparison]** Pliny’s juxtaposition of Summanus with the Etruscan system encouraged an Etruscan-origin theory. Pliny does not name Summanus as one of the nine Etruscan gods; he says that Romans recognized two lightning powers where Etruscan doctrine recognized nine.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest recoverable evidence

#### Temple foundation, traditionally 278 BCE

**[Historically plausible but retrospectively attested]** Ovid, *Fasti* 6.729–732, says:

> *Reddita, quisquis is est, Summano templa feruntur,  
> tum cum Romanis, Pyrrhe, timendus eras.*

“The temple is said to have been dedicated to Summanus—whoever he may be—when you, Pyrrhus, were a terror to the Romans.”

The wording places the dedication during the Pyrrhic War. Modern chronologies generally select 278 BCE, though Scheid notes that Latte preferred approximately 276 BCE. Ovid wrote around 2–8 CE, almost three centuries after the presumed dedication. His statement therefore establishes an Augustan tradition about the foundation, not a contemporary foundation document. [Ovid, *Fasti* 6](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidFasti6.html)

The Fasti Esquilini, Venusini, and Amiternini record a observance on 20 June, *Summano ad Circum Maximum*, “for Summanus at the Circus Maximus.” These epigraphic calendars independently establish the festival and temple location in late Republican or early Imperial public timekeeping.

#### Plautus, *Curculio* 413–416, approximately 193 BCE

**[Earliest secure surviving literary attestation]** Curculio, using the false name Summanus, explains it by saying that when he falls asleep drunk he “soaks” his clothes. The passage is comic, but its wordplay implies an audience familiar with the divine name. It does not define the god’s function. The surviving manuscripts are medieval; the date applies to the play’s composition/performance.

#### Livy’s notice for 197 BCE

**[Roman annalistic evidence]** Livy 32.29.1 records among the prodigies of 197 BCE that the temple of Summanus was struck by lightning. Livy wrote the relevant book under Augustus, about two centuries afterward, using annalistic records. The entry demonstrates that Romans remembered the temple as a site requiring prodigy reporting and expiation. It does not prove that Summanus hurled the bolt at his own temple.

### Principal corpus

- **Plautus, *Curculio* 413–416 and possibly *Bacchides* 895:** Latin comedy, early second century BCE. The name supports bawdy or verbal play; it is evidence of popular recognizability, not theology.
- **Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 32.29.1:** composed late first century BCE; prodigy notice for 197 BCE.
- **Livy, *Periocha* 14:** late epitome of a lost Livian book. It reports a Capitoline statue struck by lightning and its head found through haruspical direction. The epitome calls it Jupiter’s image, creating a direct variant against Cicero.
- **Cicero, *De divinatione* 1.16 and 2.42:** 44 BCE. Book 1 cites the successful recovery of Summanus’ severed statue-head from the Tiber as proof of haruspical knowledge; Book 2 accepts that the head was found but denies that this proves supernatural prediction. [Cicero, *De divinatione* 2](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/e/roman/texts/cicero/de_divinatione/2%2A.html)
- **Varro, *De lingua Latina* 5.74:** written in the mid-first century BCE. Varro says annals reported that Titus Tatius vowed altars to a group including Summanus. This is evidence for late-Republican antiquarian belief about Sabine origins, not a regal-period document. [Latin text](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/varro.ll5.html)
- **Ovid, *Fasti* 6.729–732:** early first century CE. Records the temple dedication and displays contemporary uncertainty: *quisquis is est*, “whoever he is.”
- **Pliny the Elder, *Natural History* 2.138:** completed 77 CE. Contrasts Etruscan classification of nine lightning gods and eleven types of bolt with the Roman division between Jupiter by day and Summanus by night.
- **Festus, epitomizing Verrius Flaccus:** Verrius wrote around the Augustan/Tiberian period; Festus’ abridgement dates to approximately the second century CE and survives incompletely, with Paul the Deacon’s eighth-century epitome supplying additional readings. The entries define nocturnal lightning and the wheel-shaped *summanalia*.
- **Arval Acts, 224 CE, CIL VI 2107a:** epigraphic ritual record. *Summanus Pater* received two black victims as part of expiation following nocturnal lightning. This is the strongest direct evidence for an operating Imperial cult rather than antiquarian memory.
- **Arnobius, *Adversus nationes* 5.37:** approximately 295–305 CE. A hostile Christian witness quoting or paraphrasing a pagan allegorical narrative in which Summanus emerges from the earth in a four-horse chariot and abducts Proserpina. [Arnobius 5.37](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Ante-Nicene_Fathers/Volume_VI/Arnobius/Adversus_Gentes/Book_V/Chapter_XXXVII)
- **Augustine, *City of God* 4.23:** early fifth century CE. Says that ancient Romans assigned nocturnal lightning to Summanus and daytime lightning to Jupiter, and alleges that Summanus had once been more honored until Jupiter’s grander temple drew public attention. Augustine is polemical, but probably depends here on Varro’s lost *Antiquitates rerum divinarum*. [Augustine 4.23](https://www.newadvent.org/fathers/120104.htm)
- **Martianus Capella, *De nuptiis* 2.161:** fifth century CE. Identifies Pluto with Summanus and derives the latter from *summus Manium*.
- **Servius and Macrobius:** late-antique repositories of Roman religious learning, but no major independent Summanus narrative survives in their extant texts. They are relevant chiefly to the intellectual environment in which old cult names were allegorized and coordinated.
- **Virgil and Ovid’s *Metamorphoses*:** no secure direct episode starring Summanus. References sometimes assigned to them concern Jupiter, Pluto, thunder, or underworld myth more generally. They must not be counted as Summanus narratives without an explicit name or ancient commentator’s identification.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**[Securely attested]** Summanus governs or sends nocturnal lightning and thunder. The primary distinction is temporal rather than meteorological: Jupiter receives daylight bolts, Summanus nighttime bolts. Festus’ ritual rule reportedly required sacrifice to both when it was uncertain whether a strike occurred by night or day.

**[Late development]** Underworld sovereignty appears only clearly in late sources. Black victims and nocturnal ritual are compatible with chthonic worship but do not by themselves prove that early Summanus was a god of the dead.

### Powers and limitations

- He sends or owns nocturnal thunderbolts.
- His bolts create polluted or divinely claimed ground requiring enclosure, burial, and expiation.
- Haruspices interpret events associated with him.
- No primary source gives him universal control of night, dreams, rain, or storms generally.
- No ancient source establishes him as a patron of thieves. Plautine jokes exploit darkness, drunkenness, and similar-sounding verbs; later descriptions turned the joke into a domain.

### Iconography

The only securely reported image is the terracotta figure on the roof or pediment of the Capitoline temple of Jupiter Optimus Maximus.

**[Competing ancient versions]**

- Cicero, *De divinatione* 1.16: the figure was Summanus; lightning knocked off its head, which haruspices correctly located in the Tiber.
- Livy, *Periocha* 14: the struck figure was Jupiter.

The disagreement cannot be resolved. It may reflect variant annalistic traditions, confusion arising from Summanus’ placement on Jupiter’s temple, or later reinterpretation of an archaic roof figure.

No securely identified surviving statue, relief, coin portrait, or Etruscan mirror can be assigned to Summanus. Consequently, modern depictions of a bearded thunder-god, a dark Jupiter, or a charioteer are reconstructions.

### Symbols and associated objects

- **Thunderbolt:** secure by function.
- **Wheel-shaped cake:** secure ritual object, but its symbolism is disputed.
- **Four-horse chariot:** attested only in Arnobius’ late narrative.
- **Black victims:** securely documented in the Arval ritual of 224 CE.
- **Night/darkness:** secure temporal association.
- **Summer solstice:** the festival fell close to it, but ancient sources do not explain the date by solar theology.
- **Sacred animals, plants, colors, numbers, or celestial body:** not securely attested. Black sacrificial animals are victims, not necessarily “sacred animals.”

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

No ancient source supplies parents, siblings, spouse, children, or a birth narrative for Summanus. Calling Jupiter his father, brother, twin, or “daytime half” is **[Modern invention]** unless explicitly presented as metaphor.

There is likewise no securely attested fixed position for Summanus in a Roman genealogical hierarchy. His cult was organized through function, place, calendar, and ritual rather than a divine family tree.

### Every documented god–god interaction or relation

- **Jupiter — division of lightning jurisdiction:** Jupiter receives daytime lightning, Summanus nighttime lightning. Pliny, *Natural History* 2.138; Festus’ entries from Verrius Flaccus. **[Secure Roman systematization]**
- **Jupiter — joint ritual under uncertainty:** when officials could not determine whether a bolt fell by day or night, sacrifice was reportedly made to both. Festus, *provorsum fulgur*. **[Roman ritual rule]**
- **Jupiter — shared Capitoline setting:** Summanus’ reported terracotta image stood on the roof or pediment of Jupiter Optimus Maximus’ temple. Cicero, *De divinatione* 1.16. **[Late-Republican testimony concerning an earlier monument]**
- **Jupiter — cultic supersession:** Augustine says Summanus was formerly more honored, but Jupiter attracted the multitude after receiving a more magnificent temple. Augustine, *City of God* 4.23, probably using Varro. **[Late hostile testimony / antiquarian tradition]**
- **Pluto/Dis Pater — identity, not interaction:** Martianus Capella calls Pluto “also Summanus,” interpreting the name as “highest of the Manes.” *De nuptiis* 2.161. **[Late syncretistic identification]**
- **Proserpina — abduction:** Summanus rises from the earth in a quadriga, seizes Proserpina, and carries her underground. Arnobius, *Adversus nationes* 5.37. **[Late variant replacing Pluto with Summanus]**
- **Dius Fidius — structural opposition:** Dumézil juxtaposed bright, juridical Dius Fidius with dark Summanus. No surviving Roman narrative stages an interaction between them. **[Modern scholarly reconstruction]**
- **Varuṇa — comparative equation:** Dumézil compared Summanus with Vedic Varuṇa. No ancient Roman or Indian source makes the equation. **[Modern comparative reconstruction]**

No documented interaction with Juno, Minerva, Vulcan, Mars, Saturn, Vejovis, or an identifiable Etruscan god has been found. Merely appearing with them in an antiquarian list is not an interaction.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Summanus has no surviving native narrative cycle comparable to the myths of Jupiter, Mars, or Hercules. Three short narrative complexes constitute almost the whole record.

### The struck Capitoline image

**[Republican religious legend, attested first in Cicero]**

A lightning bolt struck the terracotta image of Summanus on the roof of Jupiter’s Capitoline temple. Its head could not be found. Haruspices declared that it had been driven into the Tiber and specified the location; searchers found it there.

Cicero’s brother Quintus cites this as evidence for divination in *De divinatione* 1.16. In Book 2 Cicero’s skeptical speaker replies that professional expertise might explain the recovery without proving divine prophecy.

**Variant:** Livy’s epitome calls the struck object a statue of Jupiter. The Summanus version is particularly appropriate to a nocturnal strike, but the surviving passage does not expressly say it occurred at night.

**Development:** Modern writers often state that this prodigy directly caused the construction of Summanus’ separate temple. That is a plausible synthesis of Cicero, Livy, and Ovid, accepted by several scholars, but no extant ancient sentence explicitly connects omen and foundation.

### The temple’s dedication during the Pyrrhic War

**[Historical foundation tradition]**

Ovid says the temple was dedicated while Pyrrhus threatened Rome. The calendars locate it by the Circus Maximus and preserve 20 June as its anniversary. The conventional date is 278 BCE.

This is not a myth with divine action in the surviving source. Later retellings turn it into a story in which Summanus demanded his own sanctuary by striking the Capitoline image. That causal plot is **[Modern reconstruction from ancient fragments]**.

### Summanus abducts Proserpina

**[Late syncretistic variant]**

Arnobius quotes an allegorical account in which Summanus emerges from the earth in a four-horse chariot and abducts Proserpina. His pagan opponent then interprets the abduction as seed buried in soil. Arnobius attacks the inconsistency of first narrating a maiden’s rape and then converting her into an agricultural symbol.

This is the only developed surviving action-story naming Summanus. It is almost certainly a late replacement or identification of Summanus with Pluto/Dis, not evidence for an archaic Roman Summanus–Proserpina cycle. Nevertheless, it is a genuine ancient variant and should not be discarded.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Temple at the Circus Maximus

**[Secure location; approximate chronology]** Epigraphic calendars give *Summano ad Circum Maximum*. The temple probably stood near the western or Aventine side of the Circus Maximus, but no archaeological remains are securely identified.

- **Dedication:** 20 June, conventionally 278 BCE; perhaps a few years later according to alternative chronologies.
- **Foundation context:** the Pyrrhic War.
- **197 BCE:** struck by lightning, according to Livy.
- **Later history:** unrecorded. No secure destruction, conversion into a church, or excavated final phase is known.

Statements that the temple stood precisely beside a particular gate, shrine, or modern street exceed the evidence.

### Festival and calendar

The anniversary occurred on 20 June: *XII Kalendas Iulias* in pre-Julian inclusive reckoning as transmitted in some fasti, although modern discussions often render it simply June 20. It lay close to the summer solstice.

Ovid’s couplet and the Fasti Esquilini, Venusini, and Amiternini independently confirm the observance. No evidence establishes public games, a named priesthood, processions, mysteries, or initiation.

### Summanalia

Festus describes *summanalia* as flour cakes *in modum rotae ficta*, “fashioned in the form of a wheel.” This is secure evidence for their shape and ritual association.

The frequently repeated recipe “flour, milk, and honey” is not supplied by the surviving Festus definition and should not be treated as certain. Flour is explicit; the other ingredients appear in later reconstruction by analogy with Roman cakes.

Interpretations include:

- solar wheel appropriate to the solstice;
- representation of a thunder-god’s chariot wheel;
- segmented cake designed for division;
- astral or Indo-European wheel symbolism.

All are **[Modern scholarly interpretations]**. Glinister cautions that “wheel-shaped” could mean a round cake marked with spokes rather than a literal wheel. [Glinister on Festus and ritual food](https://www.eruditio-antiqua.mom.fr/vol6/EA6k.Glinister.pdf)

### Animal sacrifice

**[Secure Imperial epigraphic evidence]** The Arval Brothers’ proceedings for 224 CE record two black victims offered to *Summanus Pater* in expiation of nocturnal lightning. Modern summaries differ over whether the damaged technical term denotes wethers or oxen; “two black animals” is safest without inspection of the critical text.

Black victims suit Roman rituals directed toward nocturnal or underworld powers, but this analogy does not prove that every historical sacrifice to Summanus was chthonic.

### Lightning burial and expiation

Roman lightning strikes could be treated as divine property. Material hit by lightning was collected and buried or enclosed in a *bidental* or *puteal*. Inscriptions from Rome, including CIL VI 206, 2107, 30879, and 30880, distinguish lightning attributed to Summanus. Hans-Friedrich Mueller emphasizes that these inscriptions document an active and powerful cultic category, despite the god’s sparse literary mythology. [Mueller’s survey](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1002/9781444338386.wbeah17429)

### Priests and administrators

No exclusive priesthood of Summanus is attested. His rites could be managed by:

- haruspices interpreting lightning;
- pontifical or senatorial authorities overseeing prodigy expiation;
- the Arval Brothers when lightning affected their sacred property;
- ordinary dedicants responsible for inscribed lightning burials.

### Human sacrifice, sexual rites, and cult violence

The dedicated contested-material search found no evidence for human sacrifice, temple prostitution, ritual sexual service, or organized cult violence associated with Summanus. Arnobius condemns the mythic rape of Proserpina, not a historical sexual rite. Claims otherwise would be modern invention unless new primary evidence is produced.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Putative regal background

Varro says annals attributed altars for Summanus and several other gods to the Sabine king Titus Tatius. **[Roman antiquarian tradition]** This reflects late-Republican attempts to distribute Roman cults among Latin, Sabine, Greek, and Etruscan founders. It cannot securely date Summanus’ cult to the regal period.

### Mid-Republican institutionalization

The temple near the Circus was founded during the Pyrrhic War, conventionally 278 BCE. This is the first reasonably datable public institution dedicated solely to Summanus. The traditional Capitoline prodigy may preserve its occasion, but the causal relationship is reconstructed.

### Second and first centuries BCE

Plautus’ joke demonstrates name recognition around 193 BCE. Livy’s prodigy record for 197 BCE shows the temple integrated into the state’s system of reporting and expiating prodigies.

By the late Republic, Varro was cataloguing Summanus as an allegedly Sabine god. Cicero used the decapitated statue as a famous case in debate over divination. These sources show both ritual persistence and increasing antiquarian framing.

### Augustan and early Imperial period

The calendars preserve the temple anniversary. Ovid’s “whoever he may be” shows that an educated Roman poet could know the festival but profess uncertainty about the god’s identity. Pliny still knew the technical day/night division in fulgural doctrine.

This combination—secure ritual memory but thin mythological identity—is characteristic of many Roman functional deities.

### High Empire

Lightning-burial inscriptions and the Arval record of 224 CE demonstrate that Summanus remained ritually operative. The cult had not simply vanished after Ovid. His individual mythology may have faded, but the classification of lightning retained practical force.

### Late Antiquity

Two developments appear:

1. **Antiquarian and allegorical survival:** Arnobius knew a version in which Summanus abducted Proserpina.
2. **Underworld assimilation:** Martianus Capella made Summanus another name for Pluto, explained as *summus Manium*.

Augustine, using the god polemically, says hardly anyone in his day even remembered reading the name. This is evidence of cultural marginalization, though his claim is rhetorical and coexists with learned authors who did remember it.

### Christian suppression and disappearance

There is no preserved decree specifically closing Summanus’ temple or forbidding his rites. His public cult disappeared in the broader fourth- and fifth-century disestablishment of Roman sacrifice and temples. A dossier should therefore distinguish:

- **documented general suppression of public pagan sacrifice**, from
- **an unattested specific suppression of Summanus**.

There is no secure evidence for continuous clandestine worship through the Middle Ages.

### Mount Summano

The mountain near Vicenza has been connected in local antiquarian and folkloric writing with Summanus, Pluto, or Jupiter Summanus. Archaeology indicates ancient cult activity on or around the mountain, but the name alone does not prove that Summanus was worshipped there. No decisive dedication naming him has been established in the evidence reviewed. The association is best labeled **[Regional antiquarian tradition / unproven identification]**.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Renaissance and early-modern literature

The late identification of Summanus with Pluto enabled literary survival.

- **Luís de Camões:** employs “Sumano” for the dark underworld realm or its ruler. **[Early-modern literary reception]**
- **John Milton, *In Quintum Novembris* (1626), lines 23–24:**  
  *Talibus infestat populos Summanus et urbes / cinctus caeruleae fumanti turbine flammae*—“Thus Summanus assails peoples and cities, encircled by a smoking whirlwind of blue flame.” Milton’s Summanus is effectively a Pluto-Satan figure involved in the Gunpowder Plot, not a reconstruction of Roman public cult. [Latin text](https://www.thelatinlibrary.com/milton.quintnov.html); [critical-edition introduction](https://philological.cal.bham.ac.uk/milton/intro.html)

### Scholarship and comparative mythology

Nineteenth- and twentieth-century histories tended to classify Summanus as:

- an archaic nocturnal Jupiter;
- an Etruscan or Sabine import;
- a chthonic thunder-god;
- the dark sovereign opposed to Dius Fidius.

Dumézil’s comparison with Varuṇa gave Summanus a disproportionately prominent role in Indo-European structural theory. The comparison is intellectually influential but does not enlarge the Roman evidence.

### Contemporary paganism

Individual Roman-polytheist and eclectic pagan practitioners now commemorate Summanus on 20 June, make wheel-shaped cakes, or maintain private shrines. Online testimony demonstrates actual contemporary devotion, but no reliable demographic evidence establishes its scale or institutional continuity.

Practices that specify modern recipes, personal revelations, elaborate correspondences, or a fraternal relationship with Jupiter are **[Modern religious creation]**, even where consciously inspired by Festus and the calendars. They are not frauds merely because modern, but they must not be backdated.

### Occult and magical reception

No substantial Summanus tradition appears in the major Renaissance magical systems, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or standard Solomonic grimoires. Modern websites sometimes assign him planetary, elemental, color, crystal, tarot, or astrological correspondences. These are **[Modern inventions]** unless a datable source is cited.

### Astronomy

The asteroid **11885 Summanus**, discovered in 1990, bears the god’s name. This commemorates the ancient figure but transmits no ancient astronomical association. Summanus was not historically identified with an asteroid or planet.

### Popular media

Summanus occasionally appears in mythology websites, games, fantasy writing, and online deity catalogs, normally as a “forgotten god of nighttime thunder.” The modern portrait often combines Jupiter’s iconography, Pluto’s underworld character, and imagined storm magic. No single mass-media work has established a canonical modern appearance comparable to the reception of Jupiter or Mars.

### Political and extremist appropriation

The contested-material search found no documented major nationalist, fascist, or extremist movement centered on Summanus. General Roman symbolism has been appropriated politically, but attributing such use specifically to Summanus would be unsupported.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Origin: Roman, Sabine, or Etruscan?

- **Sabine case:** Varro places Summanus among gods whose altars Titus Tatius allegedly vowed and says those altars “smell of the Sabine language.” This is explicit ancient testimony but comes from a late antiquarian reconstruction.
- **Etruscan case:** Roman lightning divination was deeply indebted to Etruscan *disciplina*, and Pliny discusses Summanus immediately after Etruscan lightning gods. Older scholars, including Müller and Thulin, therefore treated him as Etruscan or Etruscanized.
- **Latin case:** The name admits a Latin derivation from *sub māne*, and no Etruscan-language form is securely attested.

The most economical conclusion is that Summanus is securely Roman in the surviving record, possibly interpreted through Etruscan fulgural doctrine; his ethnic “origin” remains unproved.

### Was he originally chthonic?

Evidence for a chthonic aspect includes black victims, nocturnal operation, late identity with Pluto, and Arnobius’ underworld abduction. Against this, the earliest functional evidence defines a celestial event, and the Pluto equation depends on a late folk etymology. Scholars divide between an originally nocturnal sky-god later chthonicized and a god whose atmospheric and underworld aspects were always connected. The evidence cannot decide.

### Jupiter’s double or independent god?

His own temple, anniversary, food offering, and name support independence. His Capitoline image and complementary lightning function bind him closely to Jupiter. “Nocturnal Jupiter” is a useful functional description, not a securely attested original title.

### Date and cause of the temple

Ovid provides only the Pyrrhic-War setting. Modern works commonly specify 278 BCE and connect the foundation to the struck statue. Latte suggested approximately 276 BCE; archaeological confirmation is absent. The traditional 278 date is probable, not epigraphically exact.

### Wheel symbolism

Fowler, Pettazzoni, Dumézil, Champeaux, and later comparativists have interpreted the cake as solar, seasonal, chariot-related, or Indo-European. Festus establishes only its flour composition and wheel form. Solar and solstitial interpretations remain hypotheses.

### Augustine’s “greater than Jupiter”

Augustine says Summanus was once more honored, then lost attention to Jupiter’s grand temple. This may preserve Varro’s discussion of forgotten Roman gods, but it is not evidence that Summanus was historically supreme over Jupiter. Augustine’s argument ridicules religious popularity as dependent on architecture. Treating the passage as a literal account of a pre-Jovian Roman high god overreads it.

### State of the evidence

The corpus is unusually fragmented:

- no contemporary foundation inscription;
- no securely identified temple remains;
- no surviving cult statue;
- no native hymn;
- no complete ritual handbook;
- no genealogy;
- no sustained Republican myth.

What survives is nevertheless diverse: comedy, annalistic prodigy lists, philosophical dialogue, calendars, lexicography, inscriptions, ritual records, Christian polemic, and late encyclopedic allegory.

Key modern treatments include Wissowa’s *Religion und Kultus der Römer*; Latte’s *Römische Religionsgeschichte*; Dumézil’s *La religion romaine archaïque*; Thulin’s *Die etruskische Disciplin*; Fowler’s *Roman Festivals*; Ziolkowski’s *Temples of Mid-Republican Rome*; Beard, North, and Price’s *Religions of Rome*; Scheid’s work on Roman sacrifice; Champeaux on Summanus at the solstice; and Mueller’s concise evidentiary survey.

## 10. Dedicated Contested-Material Findings

- **Etymology:** disputed; *sub māne* remains plausible, *summus Manium* is securely late, and no theory is demonstrable.
- **Origin:** Sabine and Etruscan origins are ancient or modern hypotheses, not established facts.
- **Syncretism:** equation with Pluto is genuinely ancient but late; equation with Varuṇa is modern comparative scholarship.
- **Human sacrifice:** no evidence.
- **Temple prostitution:** no evidence.
- **Cult violence:** no evidence.
- **Suppression:** disappearance occurred within the general suppression of Roman public sacrifice; no Summanus-specific edict is known.
- **Occult tradition:** no demonstrable continuous grimoire or initiatory tradition.
- **Neopaganism:** small-scale contemporary devotion is documented informally, but it is revival rather than continuous transmission.
- **“Twin brother of Jupiter”:** modern invention.
- **Fixed wheel/sun emblem carried by the god:** inference from the cakes, not ancient iconographic evidence.
- **Flour-milk-honey recipe:** only flour and wheel form are secure from Festus.
- **Etruscan mirror depictions:** none securely identifiable.
- **Mount Summano as his sanctuary:** possible regional association, not proved by the mountain’s name alone.

## 12. Sources and Assessment

The most reliable reconstruction rests on four mutually reinforcing bodies of evidence:

1. **Calendar and epigraphic evidence:** establishes the June 20 observance, Circus Maximus location, lightning expiation, title *Pater*, and black victims.
2. **Late-Republican and early-Imperial texts:** Plautus, Varro, Cicero, Ovid, Livy, Pliny, and Verrius/Festus define the name’s recognizability, public temple, prodigy traditions, and day/night lightning distinction.
3. **Late-antique testimony:** Arnobius, Augustine, and Martianus document reinterpretation, polemical memory, and fusion with Pluto.
4. **Modern scholarship:** reconstructs chronology, etymology, topography, and comparative significance, but often goes beyond what the sparse ancient record can prove.

The result is not a lost biography waiting to be restored. Summanus’ documented history is the history of a precise Roman ritual jurisdiction—nocturnal lightning—that retained institutional reality long after his narrative identity had become obscure. Ovid’s *quisquis is est* is therefore not merely a joke at a forgotten god’s expense. It accurately marks the central historical paradox: Rome could continue to keep Summanus’ day, classify his lightning, bury what his bolts struck, and sacrifice to him even when educated writers no longer knew how to tell readers who he was.
