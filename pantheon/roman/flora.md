# Flora: Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess (*dea*, frequently *Flora Mater*) in Roman and central-Italic religion; more precisely a divine power governing the flowering of crops and other plants. Ovid later recasts her as a Greek nymph, Chloris, transformed into a goddess after her union with Zephyrus.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The Oscan bronze tablet from the sanctuary near Agnone in Samnium, conventionally dated to the third century BCE, names *Fluusaí Kerríiaí*—“to Cerial Flora,” Flora associated with Ceres. Its exact date and original find-context remain debated, but it is the earliest surviving physical text generally accepted as naming the goddess. At Rome, her public temple and games are securely documented for approximately 241–238 BCE, although the *flamen Floralis*, the old Quirinal sanctuary, and Italic linguistic evidence imply an older cult. [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548938643); [Agnone tablet overview](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/G_1873-0820-149); [Oscan text and discussion](https://www.ic.unicamp.br/~stolfi/EXPORT/00-EXPORT/Notes/100/work/projects/wikipedia/geography/00-DOCS/SANNITI_LA_TAVOLA_OSCA_DI_AGNONE.html)

## Evidentiary key

- **Securely attested early material:** Archaic or Republican institution independently supported by inscriptions, calendars, offices, or early literature.
- **Later canonical elaboration:** A narrative that became authoritative or influential but is later than the cult’s formation.
- **Regional variant:** An Italic, provincial, or local form not safely transferable to Rome as a whole.
- **Learned systematization:** Antiquarian, philosophical, or poetic ordering imposed upon older cult data.
- **Syncretistic identification:** A historically identifiable equation with another divine figure.
- **Medieval or early-modern accretion:** Post-classical development before modern scholarship.
- **Modern scholarly reconstruction:** An inference from incomplete evidence, not an ancient assertion.
- **Modern invention or reception:** A recent devotional, artistic, commercial, or popular claim not evidenced in antiquity.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

**Securely attested early material.** Flora’s oldest demonstrable domain is not “flowers” merely as decoration, nor an undifferentiated “nature” or “spring,” but *floratio*: the decisive flowering of cereal plants and, by extension, vines, olives, fruit trees, and other vegetation. The Augustan *Fasti Praenestini* define her with unusual precision: *Florae, quae rebus florescendis praeest*—“Flora, who presides over things coming into flower.” Augustine, reproducing Varro’s learned catalogue of agricultural gods, similarly says that standing grain, when in flower, was assigned to Flora. [Fasti Praenestini](https://www.attalus.org/docs/cil/add_8.html); [Augustine, *City of God* 4.8](https://www.clerus.org/bibliaclerusonline/en%20/cb5.htm)

Her power was consequently ambivalent in practical terms. Flowering was indispensable, but a blossom could be destroyed by wind, rain, hail, or frost before fruit or grain formed. Ovid’s account of the Floralia explains the institution’s annual observance through precisely such failures: winds damaged flowering olives, hail grain, and rain vines. This is poetic aetiology, but it agrees with the agricultural definition preserved in the calendar.

“Goddess of spring,” “fertility goddess,” and “nature goddess” are defensible summaries only if qualified:

- **Spring:** largely an inference from her late-April and early-May festivals.
- **Fertility:** valid specifically as vegetative fecundity; the ancient record does not make her a general patron of pregnancy, marriage, or human sexuality.
- **Nature:** an expansive modern category, not an ancient cult title.

### Names and forms

- **Latin:** *Flōra*, genitive *Flōrae*. The vowel is long: Flōra.
- **Oscan:** *Fluusa*; the Agnone tablet has dative *Fluusaí*, including *Fluusaí Kerríiaí*, “to Cerial Flora.”
- **Italic calendrical forms:** *mense Flusare* and *mesene flusore*, traditionally connected with Flora and interpreted as a “Floral” month or season in Vestinian/Sabine territory.
- **Greek literary equivalent:** Χλωρίς, *Chlōrís*, Chloris—but the equation is explicitly made by Ovid and should not be projected backward as an original Greek-Roman identity.
- **English and Romance forms:** Flora; Italian *Flora*; French *Flore*; Spanish *Flora*.

### Etymology

**Secure linguistic relationship.** *Flōra* belongs to the Latin family of *flōs*, genitive *flōris*, “flower, blossom,” *flōreō*, “to bloom/flourish,” and *floridus*, “flowery, flourishing.” The underlying Indo-European root is conventionally reconstructed as \**bʰleh₃-* or a related formation, “blossom, thrive.” The goddess’s name is thus transparently functional in Latin and need not be borrowed from Greek. [Lewis and Short, *Flōra*](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn%3Acite2%3Ascaife-viewer%3Adictionary-entries.atlas_v1%3Alat.ls.perseus-eng2-n18383/); [Lewis, *flōs*](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0060%3Aentry%3Dflos)

**Ovidian wordplay, not historical etymology.** In *Fasti* 5.193–194 Flora says: *Chloris eram quae Flora vocor; corrupta Latino nominis est nostri littera Graeca sono*—“I was Chloris, who am now called Flora; a Greek letter of my name was corrupted in Latin speech.” This is learned poetic rapprochement between Greek χλωρός, “greenish, fresh,” and Latin *flos/Flora*, not sound historical linguistics. Latin *fl-* does not regularly derive from Greek *chl-* by the casual “corruption” Ovid imagines.

### Titles and epithets

- **Flora Mater / “Mother Flora.”** Attested in Cicero, Lucretius, Ovid, and later literature. *Mater* marks generative divine power and cultic dignity, not an attested genealogy.
- **Flora genetrix.** Arnobius sarcastically calls her *Flora illa genetrix et sancta*, “that generative and holy Flora.” The expression reflects her productive function, although the passage is a hostile Christian polemic.
- **Fluusa Kerríia / Flora Cerialis.** The Oscan Agnone form links her with *Kerres/Ceres* and cereal production. It is regional Italic evidence, not proof of a Roman epithet used everywhere.
- **Flora Rustica.** Appears in later antiquarian descriptions and topographical reconstruction. It is not as securely documented as *Flora Mater*.
- **Flora meretrix.** Not an ancient cult title. It belongs to the late-antique Christian story that Flora had been a wealthy prostitute whose bequest funded the Floralia.
- **Chloris.** A poetic syncretistic identity formulated most fully by Ovid around AD 8, not an early cult name.

No securely attested ancient titles make Flora queen of fairies, patroness of witches, ruler of a “flower moon,” zodiacal goddess, or tutelary deity of a particular modern birth month. Those are modern inventions or eclectic correspondences.

### Sabine, Oscan, or Roman?

Varro, in *De lingua Latina* 5.74, placed Flora among divine powers whose altars the Sabine king Titus Tatius supposedly vowed or established at Rome. Her old sanctuary stood on the Quirinal, which Roman antiquarians regarded as Sabine. These facts generated the enduring designation “Sabine goddess.” [Pauly-Wissowa, “Flora”](https://de.wikisource.org/wiki/RE%3AFlora_1); [Digital Augustan Rome, Quirinal sanctuary](https://www.digitalaugustanrome.org/records/flora-aedes-quirinalis/)

The evidence requires more care:

1. **Attested:** Flora or closely cognate *Fluusa* was worshipped among Oscan-speaking central-Italic communities.
2. **Roman antiquarian tradition:** Varro connected her Roman introduction with Titus Tatius.
3. **Archaeological/topographical support:** an old Roman sanctuary lay in the allegedly Sabine Quirinal zone.
4. **Not established:** that a historically recoverable Sabine king imported a foreign goddess at a specific date.
5. **Modern scholarly judgment:** Flora is best called an Italic deity with Roman and Osco-Samnite attestations. John Scheid notes that there is no decisive reason to classify her as foreign to Rome. [Oxford Classical Dictionary](https://academic.oup.com/edited-volume/61673/chapter-abstract/548938643)

### Identifications

- **Flora = Chloris:** Augustan poetic syncretism, explicit in Ovid, *Fasti* 5.193–214.
- **Flora and Greek Anthousa (“the blooming one”):** occasional learned comparison, not a documented Roman cult equation.
- **Flora and Aphrodite/Venus:** overlapping floral and generative iconography encouraged artistic confusion, but there is no general ancient identity. The “Farnese Flora” was in fact created after a late-fifth-century BCE Aphrodite sculptural type and renamed Flora after Renaissance restoration.
- **Flora and Primavera/Spring:** common in Renaissance and later art; usually personification or iconographic reception rather than evidence of ancient theology.
- **Flora and Pomona:** complementary but distinct. Flora governs flowering; Pomona fruit, orchards, and the ripened product. No ancient source makes them one goddess.
- **Flora and Ceres:** associated through cereal growth and the Oscan epithet *Kerríiaí*, but not identical.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The Agnone tablet

**Securely attested early/regional material.** The small bronze tablet found in 1848 at Fonte del Romito near Agnone, Molise, is written on both sides in the Oscan alphabet. It is commonly dated broadly to the third century BCE, although estimates ranging from the later fourth to second centuries appear in scholarship. It belonged to a sanctuary or sacred grove organized around *Kerres* (Ceres) and lists altars, recipients, rites, and admission or contribution rules.

The relevant form is:

> *fluusaí kerríiaí statíf*

Approximately: “an altar stands for Cerial Flora” or “to Flora of Ceres.”

The tablet proves that an Italic cognate of Flora existed outside Rome and belonged to a cereal-centered cult complex. It does not prove the Ovidian Chloris story, a marriage to Zephyrus, theatrical nudity, or a universal goddess of all flowers. The tablet is now British Museum 1873,0820.149. Its reported findspot has been questioned, with Pietrabbondante proposed as a possible original context, but the object and its Oscan text are authentic. [British Museum collection record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/G_1873-0820-149); [discussion of the sanctuary context](https://ebrary.net/269575/sociology/place)

### Roman institutional evidence

The chronology of the Roman temple is inconsistent among ancient authorities:

- **Velleius Paterculus 1.14.8:** places the temple or Floralia around 241/240 BCE, in the period of the foundation of Spoletium.
- **Pliny, *Natural History* 18.286:** gives Rome’s year 516, normally converted to 238 BCE, saying the Floralia were instituted from the Sibylline Books so that crops might flower successfully.
- **Fasti Praenestini, 28 April:** record games for Flora and explain that a temple was dedicated to the goddess who governs flowering, as a remedy for crop barrenness.
- **Ovid, *Fasti* 5.277–294:** attributes the games to plebeian aediles, the Publicii, using fines collected from those who grazed animals unlawfully on public land.
- **Festus:** similarly connects the Publicii and fines on graziers, although the transmitted designation of the aediles differs from Ovid’s.
- **Tacitus, *Annals* 2.49:** reports the rededication under Tiberius in AD 17 of temples restored by Augustus, including Flora’s.

The safest formulation is that public games and a temple near the Circus Maximus were instituted in the 240s–238 BCE, probably in related but not necessarily identical acts. Exact reconciliation is impossible.

### Principal textual corpus

#### Varro, *De lingua Latina* and lost *Antiquitates*

Marcus Terentius Varro wrote in the first century BCE.

- *Lingua Latina* 5.74 includes Flora among the gods associated with Titus Tatius.
- 5.158 locates a slope or street relative to the Quirinal Flora sanctuary.
- 7.45 names the *flamen Floralis*.
- *De re rustica* 1.1.6 invokes agricultural gods including Flora.
- Material from Varro’s lost *Antiquitates rerum divinarum* survives in Augustine, particularly the functional division of crop development among specialized deities.

Varro is indispensable but also a learned late-Republican systematizer. His Sabine histories and functional divine catalogues are not transparent records from the regal period.

#### Cicero

In *In Verrem* 2.5.36 Cicero refers to his responsibility as aedile for the games of Mother Flora. This securely establishes the Floralia as a regular public responsibility by his aedileship in 69 BCE. *De natura deorum* contributes to the broader philosophical setting in which Roman divine names were interpreted as natural functions, but supplies no independent biography of Flora.

#### Lucretius

*De rerum natura* 5.737–740 places “Mother Flora” in a poetic procession of the seasons, scattering colors along spring’s path. This is a late-Republican literary personification consistent with, but broader and more aesthetic than, the cereal-flowering cult.

#### Virgil

Virgil uses floral and seasonal imagery and participates in the Augustan poetic language surrounding agricultural divinity, but does not preserve a major independent Flora myth. Claims that Virgil supplies a full biography, genealogy, or distinctive ritual for Flora are overstated.

#### Ovid, *Fasti*

Composed around AD 8 and revised incompletely during exile, *Fasti* is the fullest surviving Flora narrative. Book 4.943–948 announces the games beginning at April’s end; Book 5.183–378 gives Flora a first-person speech covering:

- her equation with Chloris;
- Zephyrus’s seizure of her and subsequent marriage;
- her control of flowers;
- transformations of dead youths into flowers;
- her magical flower’s impregnation of Juno and the conception of Mars;
- the institution and lapse of her games;
- crop failures and the annual Floralia;
- theatrical licence, bright clothing, lights, animals, and beans.

This is primary evidence for Augustan literary religion and festival interpretation—not direct evidence that every story was believed in archaic Rome. [Ovid, *Fasti* 5](https://www.theoi.com/Text/OvidFasti5.html)

#### Ovid, *Metamorphoses*

Contrary to frequent modern summaries, *Metamorphoses* does not provide the canonical Chloris-to-Flora narrative. It contains many flower transformations—Hyacinthus, Narcissus, Crocus, Adonis—which the speaking Flora retrospectively claims as her work in *Fasti*. The complete Flora–Chloris–Zephyrus account belongs to *Fasti* 5.

#### Pliny the Elder

*Natural History* 18.286 connects the Floralia with the Sibylline Books and successful flowering. Pliny is the most explicit ancient chronological authority for 238 BCE, though his arithmetic conflicts with Velleius. [Pliny, *Natural History* 18](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/thayer/l/roman/texts/pliny_the_elder/18%2A.html)

#### Tacitus

*Annals* 2.49 records Flora’s temple among shrines restored by Augustus and dedicated under Tiberius in AD 17. It provides a firm imperial event, not a myth.

#### Martial, Juvenal, Persius, and Seneca

These authors use the Floralia as cultural shorthand for theatre, sexual licence, crowds, or festive distributions:

- Persius, *Satires* 5.177–179, alludes to vetches or chickpeas thrown to crowds.
- Martial refers to Flora’s theatrical environment and the Cato anecdote.
- Juvenal associates the festival with public sexual display.
- Seneca, *Epistle* 97.8, relates that spectators would not request the customary stripping while Cato the Younger was present.

These are valuable witnesses to elite literary perceptions; none is a neutral administrative report.

#### Valerius Maximus

*Memorable Deeds and Sayings* 2.10.8 gives the famous story that Cato left the theatre so spectators could request that mime performers strip without inhibition. The anecdote describes late-Republican moral theatre retrospectively and may be exemplary rather than stenographic history.

#### Festus and Verrius Flaccus

Festus’s abridgment of Verrius preserves material about the Publician aediles and grazing fines. The *Fasti Praenestini*, also associated with Verrius Flaccus and displayed at Praeneste between roughly 6 and 9 CE with later additions, provide epigraphic confirmation for 28 April and Flora’s crop-flowering function.

#### Macrobius

*Saturnalia* discusses the grammatical form *Floralia* and cites Varro on whether the term designates the feast or games. It is late-antique antiquarian evidence, not an independent early cult narrative.

#### Arnobius, Lactantius, and Augustine

These Christian authors are indispensable hostile witnesses:

- Arnobius, *Adversus nationes* 3.23, mocks *Flora genetrix* and the supposed relation between obscene games and flourishing fields. [Latin text](https://www.ancienttexts.org/library/latinlibrary/arnobius3.html)
- Lactantius, *Divine Institutes* 1.20, says Flora was a wealthy prostitute who left money to the Roman people for games; embarrassed Romans supposedly converted her into a flower goddess.
- Augustine uses Varro’s divine taxonomy and condemns theatrical obscenity, but his purpose is Christian refutation.

Their accounts prove that the Floralia remained a prominent polemical example in the third through fifth centuries. They do not establish that the goddess originated as a historical sex worker.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

1. **Flowering cereal crops:** the earliest and most precise domain.
2. **Blossoming vines, olives, and fruit trees:** explicit in Ovid’s account of crop damage.
3. **Flowers generally:** fully developed by the late Republic and Augustan literature.
4. **Spring:** a calendrical and poetic extension.
5. **Ornamental gardens:** especially prominent in Ovid and Renaissance reception.
6. **Theatrical and civic licence:** attached to her festival, not necessarily to her essential divine nature.
7. **Sexual fertility:** metaphorically encouraged by blossoms and festival licence, but no early cult text appoints her a general goddess of human conception.
8. **Transformation of blood into flowers:** Ovidian poetic elaboration, not a documented Roman ritual function.

### Powers and limitations

Ovid’s Flora claims that she:

- colors the once-monochrome earth with seeds and flowers;
- governs the critical flowering phase before fruit;
- can protect or withhold blossom;
- made flowers arise from the blood of Hyacinthus and other dead youths;
- possesses a unique flower capable of making a female conceive without a male;
- rewards observance and punishes neglect through failed blossoming.

These statements belong to **later canonical elaboration** in *Fasti*. Earlier cult evidence supports power over flowering but not the magical garden, flower metamorphoses, or parthenogenetic conception.

### Iconography

Flora lacks the unmistakable ancient iconographic apparatus of Minerva, Diana, or Neptune. A youthful woman with flowers, a garland, or gathered blossoms might be Flora, a Hora, Spring, a nymph, Venus, or a mortal personification. Identification often depends on inscription or context.

Common later attributes include:

- flower crown or wreath;
- flowers held in the hand or scattered from a fold of the garment;
- floral-patterned clothing;
- basket of blossoms;
- youthful, richly dressed female form;
- occasional nudity, particularly in post-classical art influenced by the Floralia’s erotic reputation.

No securely established ancient canon assigns her a special weapon, planet, number, color, gemstone, or zodiac sign.

### “Flora of Stabiae”

A celebrated first-century CE fresco from Villa Arianna at Stabiae shows a female figure seen from behind, gathering or carrying flowers against a green-blue ground. It was found in a room with other isolated female figures. “Flora,” “Primavera,” and “Flora of Stabiae” are conventional modern titles; without an inscription, the identification is plausible but not certain. She may be a spring figure or generic flower-gathering nymph. Calling the fresco a documented cult statue of Flora would exceed the evidence.

### The Flora Farnese or “Flora of Naples”

The colossal marble now in the Museo Archeologico Nazionale di Napoli, inventory 6409, is a second-century CE Roman work based on a late-fifth-century BCE Athenian Aphrodite type. It survived headless and without its extremities. Renaissance restorer Guglielmo della Porta supplied a flower-crowned head and garland, causing or reinforcing its identification as Flora; Carlo Albacini and Filippo Tagliolini later altered the restoration. Thus the “Flora Farnese” is central to Flora’s modern visual identity but is not a securely identified ancient image of the goddess. [Museo Archeologico di Napoli catalogue discussion](https://www.miti3000.it/mito/musei/napoli/pt_col_farn_s13.htm); [Capodimonte on the “Flora effect”](https://capodimonte.cultura.gov.it/litalia-chiamo-capodimonte-oggi-racconta-leffetto-flora/)

### Sacred animals, plants, colors, and festival objects

**Attested:**

- cultivated blossoms and flowering grain;
- multicolored garments, explained by Ovid through flowers’ colors;
- roses and garlands;
- beans, peas, vetches, or chickpeas distributed or thrown at the games;
- hares and *capreae* released in the Circus. *Capreae* means roe deer, though some translations incorrectly or loosely give “goats”;
- nocturnal lights or torches.

**Not securely attested:**

- foxes as Flora’s sacred animal;
- hummingbirds, butterflies, bees, or rabbits as permanent divine familiars;
- rose quartz, emerald, aventurine, a “Flora sigil,” Friday, or a particular moon phase;
- a fixed sacred color such as green or pink.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

### Genealogy

No securely early Roman source supplies Flora with parents, siblings, or children. This absence is material: Roman cult did not require a Greek-style family tree for every divine function.

Ovid calls Chloris a nymph of the blessed fields and says her beauty won her marriage to Zephyrus. He does not name her parents. The Greek mythographic record contains several figures called Chloris, including a daughter of Amphion and Niobe and a Chloris connected with flowers or the Horae; these cannot simply be merged with Roman Flora.

### Consort

**Zephyrus/Favonius:** Ovid’s Flora says the West Wind pursued and seized her. She describes the act as violence—*vim tamen emendat dando mihi nomina nuptae*, “he nevertheless amended the violence by giving me the name of wife.” He granted her dominion over gardens and flowers. This is the sole full ancient account of their marriage and is Augustan poetic syncretism.

### Offspring

None is securely attested. Modern family trees sometimes assign Flora children with Zephyrus, but the standard ancient Flora passage does not.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Flora as Chloris and bride of Zephyrus

**Earliest source:** Ovid, *Fasti* 5.193–214, approximately AD 8.

Flora tells Ovid that she was formerly Chloris, a nymph of the blessed fields. Zephyrus saw her in spring, pursued her, and took her by force. Marriage followed; he compensated for the violence by giving her legal status as wife and a garden filled with flowers. Ovid’s wording should not be softened into a simple consensual romance: the text explicitly uses *vis*, force.

The story combines several layers:

- Greek Chloris, whose name evokes fresh green growth;
- Greek traditions in which Zephyrus is paired with a spring or flower figure;
- Latin Flora’s indigenous name and function;
- Roman marriage language in which marriage is presented as reparation;
- Augustan interest in translating Roman gods into Greek literary mythology.

No Republican text presently demonstrates that Roman worshippers regarded Flora as an abducted Greek nymph. The equation may be Ovid’s invention or an earlier learned identification otherwise unattested.

### Flora and the birth of Mars

**Earliest and only full surviving source:** Ovid, *Fasti* 5.229–258.

Juno resents Jupiter’s production of Minerva without a mother and seeks to bear a child without him. On her way to Ocean she stops with Flora. Flora knows of a unique flower in her garden, received or brought from the Olenian fields, which can cause conception by touch. She hesitates because Jupiter might be angered, but Juno swears secrecy. Flora plucks the flower, touches Juno’s breast, and Juno conceives Mars, later giving birth in Thrace.

This narrative explains Mars’s birth in a manner radically different from the ordinary Greek genealogy in which Ares is the son of Zeus and Hera. It also elevates Flora from crop goddess to possessor of a cosmic reproductive secret.

The tale is:

- not found in early Roman ritual evidence;
- not the ordinary Hesiodic genealogy of Ares;
- likely shaped by Italian traditions surrounding Juno, Mars, spring, and magical flowers;
- an Augustan literary variant, not the uncontested Roman canon.

### Flora’s flower transformations

In *Fasti* 5.215–228 Flora claims the flowers created from Hyacinthus, Narcissus, Crocus, Attis, and Adonis. These transformations have separate Greek literary histories, normally involving Apollo, Aphrodite, Cybele, or self-contained metamorphic causation. Ovid retrospectively incorporates them into Flora’s garden. This is a literary act of jurisdiction: she becomes the divine curator of earlier Greek flower myths.

### Neglect, crop failure, and restoration of the Floralia

In Ovid’s aetiology, the games were once neglected. Flora retaliated—or withdrew protection—so flowering olives were damaged by winds, grain by hail, and vines by rain. The Senate restored the games after consultation. Ovid places the annual institution in 173 BCE.

The historical core is plausible: a previously occasional festival became annual in response to agricultural crisis. The divine first-person explanation is poetic. No surviving senatorial decree confirms Flora’s speech.

### The prostitute Flora

Lactantius reports that Flora was a prostitute who amassed wealth, named the Roman people as heir, and endowed games on her birthday. The Senate, ashamed of the origin, allegedly converted her into a flower goddess. Later Christian and medieval writers repeated the story.

This is a **late-antique hostile rationalization**, not early evidence:

- Flora’s Italic name, Agnone attestation, flamen, and crop function precede Lactantius’s story.
- The narrative resembles euhemerizing attacks on other Roman cults.
- It explains the festival’s erotic performances by inventing or transmitting a human courtesan founder.
- It may preserve cultural memory of prostitutes’ participation, but not Flora’s historical humanity.

The claim that Flora was “originally a prostitute deified by Rome” should therefore be labeled a discredited late-antique polemical theory. [Lactantius, *Epitome* 20](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Ante-Nicene_Fathers/Volume_VII/Lactantius/The_Divine_Institutes/The_Epitome_of_the_Divine_Institutes/Chap._XX)

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### The Quirinal sanctuary

Varro, Vitruvius, and Martial locate an old sanctuary of Flora on or below the Quirinal, near the old Capitoline sanctuary and the temple of Quirinus. Its foundation was traditionally attributed to Titus Tatius.

- **Secure:** a sanctuary existed by the late Republic.
- **Probable:** it was older than the Circus Maximus temple.
- **Traditional, not demonstrated:** foundation during the reign of Romulus and Tatius.
- **Unknown:** exact site, architecture, initial foundation date, destruction date.

Its dedication day may have been 3 May. The distinction between this sanctuary and the later temple near the Circus is essential.

### Temple near the Circus Maximus

The second temple stood on the lower Aventine or along the Circus Maximus zone near the temple of Ceres. It was associated with the plebeian aediles Lucius and Marcus Publicius and reportedly funded from fines levied on illegal use of public pasture.

Possible chronology:

- 241/240 BCE: institution according to Velleius;
- 238 BCE: Pliny’s date for institution on Sibylline advice;
- 28 April: dedication day in the *Fasti Praenestini*;
- 173 BCE: games made annual;
- Augustan period: restoration;
- AD 17: dedication under Tiberius.

The placement near Ceres, plebeian sponsorship, pasture fines, and crop-protection purpose give the institution a marked agrarian and plebeian character. [Study of Republican Floralia](https://www.persee.fr/doc/dha_0755-7256_1977_num_3_1_2694)

### The *flamen Floralis*

Varro lists a *flamen Floralis*, one of Rome’s minor flamines. CIL IX 705 records an imperial-period *flamen Floralis*, M. Numisius M. f. Quintianus. A dedicated priesthood strongly suggests that Flora belonged to an old Roman cultic stratum, though lists attributing all minor flamines directly to King Numa are legendary institutional history.

The evidence does not reveal:

- how the priest was selected in early periods;
- a wife with a complementary title;
- distinctive dress;
- daily duties;
- any secret initiation.

### Arval worship

The Arval Brothers included Flora in sacrificial sequences alongside agricultural and natural powers such as Fons. This confirms continued official recognition under the empire. It does not mean that the Arvals administered the Floralia.

### The Floralia

By the imperial period the public games ran from 28 April through 3 May. Earlier Republican observance may have begun on 27 April before calendrical reform. Components attested or described include:

- theatrical mimes and farces;
- circus performances;
- multicolored clothing;
- nocturnal celebration and lights;
- distribution or throwing of legumes;
- release of hares and roe deer;
- floral crowns and decoration;
- sexually explicit humor and requested undressing by female performers.

Ovid explains the festival’s licence through several analogies: flowers display themselves without shame; Flora is not a stern goddess; and festive freedom suits spring abundance. This is literary interpretation, not a ritual handbook.

### Mimes, nudity, and prostitution

The sexual reputation is multiply attested, but precision matters.

- Valerius Maximus and Seneca tell the story of Cato leaving so the audience could request performers to undress.
- Martial and Juvenal treat the Floralia as a familiar venue of sexual display.
- Lactantius says prostitutes served as mime actresses and stripped at public demand.
- Arnobius denounces the games’ “holy obscenity.”

Modern theatre scholarship generally accepts that mime actresses could appear partly or fully naked, although the extent of actual nudity and whether every performance followed the practice cannot be established. [Cambridge excerpt on Roman mime](https://assets.cambridge.org/97805218/61618/excerpt/9780521861618_excerpt.pdf)

“Temple prostitution,” however, is not documented. There is no evidence of a Flora priesthood operating institutional sacred sex, of intercourse as a formal offering, or of prostitutes belonging to the temple. Prostitutes’ participation in public games is a different claim.

### Sacrifice and offerings

The evidence suggests normal Roman public sacrifice, offerings connected with flowering crops, and Arval offerings. The Agnone tablet prescribes ritual at an altar for *Fluusa Kerríia*, but its Oscan sacrificial terminology is difficult and should not be silently converted into Roman practice.

No human sacrifice is associated with Flora. No ancient text reports children, war captives, or criminals sacrificed to her. The Floralia’s animals were released in performance; sources do not describe them as human substitutes or a blood sacrifice specific to Flora.

### Geography

Documented or probable cult occurs at:

- Rome: Quirinal and Circus Maximus/lower Aventine;
- Agnone/central Samnium: Oscan *Fluusa*;
- Furfo and Vestinian territory;
- Vittorino;
- other parts of central Italy;
- at least isolated imperial evidence outside Italy, including North Africa.

The cult’s concentration in Italy is more conspicuous than a broad empire-wide temple network.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Italic and early Roman formation

Before the third century BCE, Flora’s history must be reconstructed indirectly. Her transparent Italic name, Oscan cognate, specialized flamen, old Quirinal sanctuary, and central-Italian distribution point to an Italic flowering deity older than surviving Roman literature.

The “Sabine origin” is a Roman antiquarian explanation, not a securely datable migration event. The alternatives in scholarship are:

- a specifically Sabine goddess imported to Rome;
- an Osco-Sabellian divine power shared across central Italy;
- a Roman functional deity with close Italic cognates;
- a cult whose multiple local forms preclude a single point of origin.

### Public institutionalization, c. 241–238 BCE

The Sibylline Books, plebeian aediles, a new temple, and public games moved Flora into a highly visible urban framework. This was not necessarily her first arrival at Rome. Rather, it was the monumentalization of an older power in response to agricultural anxiety and the growing culture of public games.

### Annualization in 173 BCE

According to Ovid, repeated damage to blossoms led the Senate to make the Floralia annual. From then onward the festival joined Rome’s recurring civic calendar.

### Late Republic

By Cicero’s aedileship in 69 BCE the games were a magistrate’s recognized responsibility. The festival’s mime, popular audience, and licence became part of Roman political theatre. Elite authors could use the Floralia as a symbol of the plebs, spectacle, sexual display, and temporary relaxation of decorum.

### Augustan systematization

Augustan culture regularized and monumentalized Roman religious memory:

- the temple was restored;
- the *Fasti Praenestini* fixed Flora’s function and date in stone;
- Ovid supplied her with a Greek identity, marriage, garden, and mythic biography;
- Roman writers integrated her into a literary spring procession.

This period created much of the Flora recognizable today.

### Imperial period

The temple’s AD 17 rededication demonstrates continued state support. Inscriptions attest her priesthood; the Arval records attest sacrifice; satirists and epigrammatists show that the games remained culturally legible. Yet she never appears to have rivaled Jupiter, Mars, Venus, Ceres, or Isis in imperial reach.

### Late antiquity

Christian polemicists selected the Floralia as evidence against traditional religion because its mimes and nudity were rhetorically useful. Arnobius and Lactantius do not show that worship had already vanished; their intensity suggests continuing cultural relevance.

A late-fourth-century poem, *Carmen contra paganos*, appears to mention a Symmachus or his heir restoring a temple of Flora or Venus. The identification is disputed, but archaeological and calendrical evidence supports continued pagan presence at Rome in the fourth century. A study of the temple argues for persistence rather than a sudden “pagan revival.” [Late Antique Archaeology study](https://brill.com/view/journals/laaj/7/1/article-p209_9.xml)

No surviving source records a single, Flora-specific closure, destruction, forced conversion, or final Floralia. General imperial restrictions on sacrifice and temple funding in the later fourth century would have undermined the cult, but assigning Flora’s end to one edict is a modern simplification.

### Medieval survival

There is no demonstrated continuous medieval worship of Flora. Her name survived through:

- Latin literature and school texts;
- Christian apologetic accounts of the “prostitute Flora”;
- mythographic compilations;
- seasonal personification;
- the ordinary Latin vocabulary of flowers.

Spring festivals held in medieval Europe cannot be called survivals of the Floralia merely because they involved flowers, dancing, or sexual licence. Continuity requires evidence of transmission, which is generally absent.

### Renaissance recovery

Humanist reading of Ovid returned Flora to elite art and poetry. She became a richly clothed or nude embodiment of spring, beauty, sexuality, gardens, and marriage. This reception profoundly expanded her image beyond the Roman crop-flowering deity.

### Modern worship

Roman polytheist and reconstructionist groups now include Flora and sometimes celebrate the Floralia. Nova Roma explicitly describes its religion as a reconstructed Roman system and distinguishes it from Wicca or merely substituting Roman names into Greek ritual. Such observance is a genuine modern religious practice but not an institutionally continuous survival from antiquity. [Nova Roma, *Religio Romana*](https://www.novaroma.org/religio_romana/)

No reliable census isolates the number of Flora’s worshippers. Claims of a large continuous secret cult, hereditary priestesses, or an unbroken Floralia lineage are unsubstantiated.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Botticelli’s *Primavera*

Sandro Botticelli’s *Primavera*, painted around 1480 on poplar panel and documented in the Medici orbit, is the most influential post-classical image associated with Flora. At the right, Zephyrus seizes Chloris; flowers emerge from her mouth; beside her stands the richly dressed flower-scattering woman conventionally identified as Flora. The pairing visualizes successive states of the same Ovidian figure.

The painting does not simply illustrate one passage. Its Venus, Cupid, Graces, Mercury, orange grove, marriage associations, and possible Neoplatonic program have generated numerous competing interpretations. Even the figures’ identities are not beyond dispute: an alternative reading treats the right-hand women as seasonal Horae and the central figure as Persephone. The Uffizi maintains the conventional Zephyrus–Chloris–Flora identification while acknowledging the work’s complex intellectual setting. [Uffizi catalogue](https://www.uffizi.it/opere/botticelli-primavera); [Frank Zöllner, *Botticelli: Images of Love and Spring*](https://digi.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/diglit/zoellner1998/0042)

### Renaissance and Baroque Flora portraits

From the sixteenth century onward, painters frequently represented attractive women as Flora, sometimes through explicit flower attributes and sometimes by later title:

- Titian’s *Flora*, c. 1515–1520;
- Rembrandt’s portraits of Saskia or other sitters “as Flora”;
- Nicolas Poussin’s *Triumph of Flora*;
- Jan Brueghel and Peter Paul Rubens’s floral allegories;
- numerous court portraits in which Flora signified beauty, marriageability, abundance, or sensuality.

These works made Flora an erotic and nuptial figure to a degree not documented in early Roman cult.

### The “Flora of Naples”

The restored Farnese statue became an icon of Naples and of neoclassical taste. Its antiquarian renaming helped standardize flower crown, bouquet, and monumental female beauty as Flora’s visual code. Ironically, one of the most famous “ancient Floras” is a Renaissance and eighteenth-century reconstruction of a Roman statue derived from an Aphrodite model.

### “Flora” as a botanical term

The divine name became a scientific common noun through early-modern botanical book titles. Simon Paulli’s *Flora Danica* (1648) is often identified as the first regional botanical work whose title uses *Flora* for a region’s plant world. Linnaeus’s *Flora Lapponica* (1737) and *Flora Svecica* (1745) helped establish *flora* as both:

1. the plants collectively inhabiting a region; and
2. a systematic account or catalogue of those plants.

Linnaeus did not create the usage from nothing, but his works made it central to modern botany. The semantic extension from goddess of flowering to collective plant life is early-modern, not classical Roman terminology. [Library of Congress on *Flora Lapponica*](https://blogs.loc.gov/international-collections/2017/07/entering-a-new-world-the-northern-travels-of-carl-linnaeus-in-1732/); [study of Linnaean botanical work](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2720649/)

The later opposition of “flora and fauna” pairs Flora’s name with Fauna, another Roman divine name, but the scientific terms do not imply that ancient Romans worshipped the two as a universal taxonomic couple.

### Occultism and modern esotericism

Flora is not a major named power in the foundational ceremonial systems of the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, Aleister Crowley’s core Thelemic canon, or the standard Solomonic grimoires. Modern correspondence tables that assign her a planet, tarot card, crystal, chakra, or magical direction are eclectic inventions.

Renaissance mythographers and Neoplatonizing artists used Flora allegorically, but that differs from evidence of a formal “Flora mystery cult.” Popular statements that Botticelli encoded an initiatory Floralia rite are speculative.

### Neopaganism and Goddess spirituality

Contemporary devotees may approach Flora as:

- a Roman deity reconstructed from the calendar and literary record;
- an ecological or gardening goddess;
- an archetype of spring, sexuality, or feminine flourishing;
- an eclectic Wiccan or Goddess-movement figure.

The first approach consciously reconstructs; the others usually create new correspondences. Modern offerings of seed packets, cut flowers, perfume, crystals, vegan cakes, or environmental service may be religiously meaningful but cannot be labeled ancient Roman practice without evidence.

### Literature and popular culture

Flora has appeared in:

- Renaissance pageants and masques;
- Spenserian and pastoral poetry;
- ballet and opera personifications of spring;
- decorative sculpture and garden pavilions;
- children’s mythology;
- video games and fantasy works using “Flora” as a plant-magic name;
- commercial perfumes, fashion lines, florists, and botanical brands.

The name’s modern association with gentle nature magic commonly suppresses the ancient cult’s agricultural danger and the Floralia’s theatrical sexuality.

### Political appropriation

Italian nationalism and Fascism extensively appropriated Roman symbols, festivals, architecture, and the Augustan past. Flora herself was not a central or distinctive Fascist emblem on the level of the fasces, eagle, Mars, or Roma. Claims of a major Fascist “cult of Flora” are not supported by the surveyed evidence. Her classical imagery could nevertheless participate in the regime’s generalized Romanità and idealization of fertility, land, and demographic abundance. [Study of Fascist Romanità](https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/nana.12651)

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Antiquarian scholarship

Georg Wissowa treated Flora within the structured institutions of Roman state religion and emphasized the old Italic cult beneath later Greek elaboration. Ludwig Preller, Wilhelm Roscher, and the *Realencyclopädie* assembled the literary, calendrical, and inscriptional evidence, though their categories often assumed sharper distinctions between “native” and “foreign” gods than recent scholarship accepts.

Carl Thulin and scholarship on Etruscan religion encouraged searches for Etruscan antecedents. No securely inscribed Etruscan image has been shown to depict Flora. Etruscan mirrors are crucial for many divine identifications, but the current evidence does not justify claiming an Etruscan Flora equivalent merely because mirrors show flower-bearing women.

### Dumézil

Georges Dumézil included Flora in his reconstruction of archaic Roman religion and its specialized divine functions. His comparative Indo-European framework made Roman functional gods intelligible as an organized system rather than a heap of “primitive” abstractions. Yet Flora does not occupy a clear place in the famous three-function ideology, and broad Indo-European comparison supplies no missing Flora myth.

### Wissowa, Latte, Le Bonniec, Scheid

Key positions include:

- **Wissowa:** Flora’s old Italic nucleus later received Greek coloring.
- **Kurt Latte:** emphasized historical institutions and the dangers of over-systematizing indigitamenta.
- **Henri Le Bonniec:** linked Flora’s cult closely with Ceres and proposed that the obscene or theatrical elements, rather than Flora herself, might reflect Greek influence.
- **John Scheid:** stresses ritual precision and warns that there is no decisive reason to treat Flora as a foreign goddess.

### Major disputes

#### Was Flora originally Sabine?

- **For:** Varro’s Tatius list, the Quirinal sanctuary, central-Italic evidence.
- **Against or qualified:** Tatius is legendary; Roman antiquarians routinely assigned Quirinal cults to Sabines; Oscan distribution proves Italic breadth, not a single Sabine origin.

**Current cautious conclusion:** central-Italic/Italic, with a Roman Sabine-origin tradition.

#### Was Flora imported under Greek influence?

- **For:** Sibylline intervention, public games, theatrical form, and Ovid’s Chloris.
- **Against:** old priesthood, Quirinal cult, transparent Italic name, Oscan evidence.

**Conclusion:** the goddess is older than her Hellenized mythology; Greek theatrical and literary forms affected her public presentation.

#### Was she originally a prostitute?

No. The hypothesis depends on Lactantius’s polemical euhemerism and conflicts with earlier institutional and inscriptional evidence.

#### Did the Floralia require complete nudity?

Ancient literary sources repeatedly associate it with requested undressing. They justify accepting erotic exposure as a real feature of some performances. They do not establish a uniform rule, ritualized intercourse, or nudity by all female participants.

#### Did the cult involve human sacrifice?

No evidence has been found. This is an absence, not a suppressed tradition.

#### Is the Stabiae figure definitely Flora?

No. “Flora” remains a conventional iconographic attribution.

#### Is the Farnese Flora an authentic ancient Flora?

The marble is ancient; its Flora identity is substantially post-antique. Renaissance restoration supplied the decisive floral attributes.

### Primary evidence and repositories

- **Agnone tablet:** British Museum.
- **Fasti Praenestini:** fragments in the Museo Nazionale Romano.
- **Latin inscriptions:** *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum*, especially Roman and Italian volumes; searchable through CIL and EDCS. [CIL database](https://cil-old.bbaw.de/cil_en/dateien/datenbank_eng.php.html); [EDCS description](https://databases.ub.unibe.ch/en/epigraphische-datenbank-clauss-slaby-edcs/470)
- **Flora of Stabiae and Farnese Flora:** Museo Archeologico Nazionale di Napoli.
- **Botticelli’s *Primavera*:** Gallerie degli Uffizi, Florence.
- **Varro’s *Antiquitates*:** lost; surviving fragments and reports must be reconstructed from Augustine and other authors.
- **Sibylline Books:** destroyed in antiquity and repeatedly reconstituted; the actual oracle behind the Floralia does not survive.

| c. AD 8 | Ovid composes the fullest surviving Flora account in *Fasti* 5: Chloris, Zephyrus, Juno and Mars, crop failure, and festival customs. |
