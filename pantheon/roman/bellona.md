# Bellona: Research Dossier

**Class:** Goddess (*dea*) in Roman tradition; specifically a goddess of war, battle-fury, and Rome’s formal transition into war. Under the Empire, her name also designated or absorbed the Cappadocian goddess Mā, whose ecstatic cult was substantially different from Bellona’s older Roman state cult.

**Earliest secure attestation:** A black-glazed *poculum* inscribed **BELOLAI POCOLOM**, “cup of Belola/Bellona” (CIL I² 441 = CIL XI 6708.3 = ILLRP 55), conventionally dated about **300–225 BCE**, probably shortly before the middle of the third century BCE. Its precise findspot is uncertain, and the painted female head cannot securely be identified as Bellona. The earliest datable cult-event is Appius Claudius Caecus’s vow of a temple in **296 BCE**, but this is reported only by Livy, writing three centuries later. The earliest surviving literary occurrence is Plautus, *Amphitruo* 42–43, around **190 BCE**.

### Evidence labels

- **[EA]** securely attested early material
- **[LE]** later literary or canonical elaboration
- **[RV]** regional variant
- **[LS]** learned antiquarian or priestly systematization
- **[SI]** syncretistic identification
- **[MA]** medieval or early-modern accretion
- **[F]** folklore
- **[MSR]** modern scholarly reconstruction
- **[MI]** modern invention or modern religious/literary reception
- **[U]** uncertain, disputed, or unestablished

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

Bellona is the Roman personification and divine agency of organized war and battle-fury. Her securely documented public role is not that of an independently developed mythic protagonist, but of a deity invoked in battle, given a vowed temple, and associated spatially with the Senate’s management of foreign affairs, triumphal claims, and declarations of war. Roman poets subsequently made her a blood-stained companion, sister, wife, or charioteer of Mars. During the late Republic and Empire she was identified with Greek Enyo and Cappadocian Mā; the last identification attached ecstatic priests, prophecy, self-wounding, and new cult titles to her name.

### Names

- **Bellōna**, Latin. **[EA]** The standard form by the late Republic.
- **Duellōna / Duellona**, Old or consciously archaizing Latin. **[EA/LS]** Varro, *De lingua Latina* 5.73 and 7.49, derives the newer name from the older word *duellum*, “war,” which became classical *bellum*: *ab eadem causa facta Duellona Bellona*—“from the same cause Duellona became Bellona.” Augustine, *City of God* 4.24, preserves the same antiquarian explanation.
- **Belola / Belolai**, archaic or nonstandard spelling on the third-century-BCE cup. **[EA]** The genitive *Belolai* corresponds to classical *Bellonae*. The Corpus of Latin Linguistics dates the object to 300–225 BCE.
- Greek **Βελλώνα**, a transcription of the Latin name, occurs in Greek accounts of Roman religion. **[LE]**
- **Ἐνυώ / Enyō. [SI]** Greek Enyo became Bellona’s standard literary equivalent, but the equivalence is not evidence that the original Roman and Greek cults were historically identical.
- **Mā / Μᾶ. [SI/RV]** The Cappadocian goddess worshipped especially at Comana was interpreted as Enyo and Bellona. “Mā-Bellona” is a useful modern designation for this fused imperial cult, not necessarily the exact ordinary ancient name of a single uniform goddess.
- **Virtus Bellona / Dea Virtus Bellona. [SI/RV]** Epigraphically attested in imperial provincial religion, especially in the Rhineland and northern Italy. Lactantius, *Divine Institutes* 1.21.16, says that some called Virtus “the same Bellona.”
- **Bellona Pulvinensis. [RV/SI]** Attested at Rome by CIL VI 490, 2232, and 2233. This title belongs to the Cappadocian/imperial complex. Its derivation is disputed; it should not be casually connected with the *pulvinar* of the Circus Flaminius.
- **Bellona Rufilia. [RV/U]** Attested by CIL VI 2234. It may preserve a founder’s or family name; the old explanation “red/bloody Bellona” is speculative.
- **Bellona Victrix. [LE/U]** Livy 10.19 has Appius ask Bellona for victory, but does not unambiguously give *Victrix* as a formal cult title.
- **pedisequa Matris**, “attendant/follower of the Mother.” **[SI/RV]** Applied to Bellona in CIL VI 490, 2233, and 30851, explicitly subordinating or associating her with Magna Mater.
- **dea pedisequa**, “attendant goddess.” **[RV]** An abbreviated expression of the same imperial relationship.

### Etymology and origin

The transparent Roman etymology connects *Bellona* with *bellum*, “war,” through older *Duellona* and *duellum*. **[EA/LS]** This is supported by actual archaic forms, not merely by late wordplay. The deeper Indo-European etymology of *duellum* is disputed.

A recent alternative derives the root from reconstructed *duenelo-*—“quite good/brave”—related to Old Latin *duenos* and classical *bonus*. **[MSR/U]** Even if correct for the word’s deep history, it does not overturn the Roman-period understanding of Bellona as “War.”

A Sabine origin, frequently repeated since nineteenth-century scholarship, rests chiefly on Bellona’s association with the Claudii, the family’s claimed Sabine ancestry, and comparison with Nerio. **[MSR/U]** No securely identified Sabine inscription names Bellona. “Sabine goddess brought by the Claudii” is therefore a hypothesis, not an ancient fact.

An Etruscan origin has also been proposed from the cup’s Etrusco-Latial manufacture and supposed parallels on Etruscan mirrors. **[MSR/U]** Neither the vessel’s provenance nor generalized armed female figures prove that Bellona was an Etruscan goddess. The head on the *Belolai pocolom* has sometimes been identified as Enyo/Bellona because alleged snakes in its hair suggest a destructive Greek figure; Treccani’s archaeological entry expressly regards that iconographic identification as unacceptable or at least insecure.

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest material evidence

**The Bellona cup, approximately 300–225 BCE. [EA]** The black-glazed vessel bears *Belolai pocolom*, “cup of Bellona.” It proves that a divine name identifiable with Bellona circulated in central Italy by the third century BCE. It does **not** prove a specific ritual, mythology, priesthood, Etruscan origin, or identification with Enyo.

**The temple vow of 296 BCE. [LE reporting an early event]** Livy 10.19.17 relates that Appius Claudius Caecus, in desperate fighting against Samnites and Etruscans, raised his hands and vowed:

> *Bellona, si hodie nobis victoriam duis, ast ego tibi templum voveo*  
> “Bellona, if you grant us victory today, I vow you a temple.”

Livy adds that Appius acted *velut instigante dea*, “as though driven on by the goddess.” This is the foundational narrative of her principal Roman temple, not contemporary documentation of the vow.

**The *devotio* formula. [LE/possible archaic preservation]** Livy 8.9.6 puts Bellona in the prayer used by Decius Mus in 340 BCE: Janus, Jupiter, father Mars, Quirinus, Bellona, the Lares, the Novensiles, and the Indigetes are invoked before the consul devotes himself and the enemy to the gods below. Livy 10.28.13–17 supplies a related version for the younger Decius at Sentinum in 295 BCE. The formula may preserve archaic liturgical matter, but its wording survives only in Augustan historiography.

### Principal corpus

- **Plautus, *Amphitruo* 42–43, approximately 190 BCE; Latin. [EA]** Bellona appears with Virtus, Victoria, and Mars among deities associated with valor and war. This is the earliest surviving literary occurrence.
- **Senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus, 186 BCE, CIL I² 581; bronze inscription. [EA]** The consuls report consulting the Senate *apud aedem Duelonai*, “at the temple of Duellona.” This is the earliest securely dated documentary proof of the temple’s political use and preserves the archaic divine name.
- **Varro, *De lingua Latina* 5.73; 7.49, 40s BCE; Latin. [LS]** Supplies the Bellona/Duellona etymology and antiquarian classification.
- **Cicero, *De natura deorum*, 45 BCE; Latin. [LS]** Bellona belongs to Cicero’s broader evidence for Roman personified powers and inherited divine names rather than receiving an independent myth. The dialogue is philosophical evidence for elite classification, not a cult manual.
- **Virgil, *Aeneid* 8.700–703, 20s BCE; Latin. [LE/SI]** On Aeneas’s shield, Discord strides with torn garment while Bellona follows with a blood-stained whip. This is her most influential literary image.
- **Ovid, *Fasti* 6.201–208, about 8 CE; Latin. [LE]** Records the temple’s June dedication and associates the place with the fetial spear cast toward symbolically hostile territory.
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 5 and related martial passages, 8 CE; Latin. [LE]** Bellona functions as poetic shorthand for war and slaughter, not as the protagonist of a sustained myth.
- **Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 8.9; 10.19; 10.28 and numerous books concerning Senate meetings, composed approximately 27 BCE–17 CE; Latin. [LE]** Principal narrative source for the *devotiones*, Appius’s vow, and the temple’s civic use.
- **Horace, *Satires* 2.3.223–225, approximately 30 BCE; Latin. [LE]** A frenzied “mother of the gods” or Bellona-type devotee supplies a satirical image of religious mania; the passage participates in elite caricature of ecstatic cult.
- **Tibullus 1.6.43–54, late first century BCE; Latin. [LE/SI]** Describes Bellona’s priestess cutting her arms with a double axe and prophesying while blood runs. This is important evidence for Mā-Bellona, not early Republican Bellona.
- **Lucan, *Bellum civile* 1.564–567; 7.568–573, approximately 60 CE; Latin. [LE/SI]** Presents a Bellona-inspired prophet and the goddess of slaughter; the poem sometimes blurs Bellona’s devotees with Cybele’s Galli.
- **Juvenal 4.123 and 6.511–526, late first/early second century CE; Latin. [LE/SI]** Satirical testimony to *fanatici* and oriental ecstatic religion.
- **Statius, *Thebaid* 4.5–9, approximately 90 CE; Latin. [LE]** Bellona herself performs the fetial-style spear action, a poetic transfer of a priestly rite to the goddess.
- **Plutarch, *Sulla* 9 and 18, early second century CE; Greek. [LE/SI]** Reports Sulla’s connection with the Cappadocian goddess Mā and a *servus fanaticus* whose prophecy concerned Sulla’s fortunes. This is crucial for the historical mechanism of Mā’s Roman reception.
- **Apuleius, *Metamorphoses* 8.24–30, late second century CE; Latin. [LE/SI]** Depicts itinerant ecstatic priests in a milieu where Bellona and Magna Mater traditions overlap.
- **Festus/Paulus, *De verborum significatu*, second century CE with later epitome; Latin. [LS]** Preserves antiquarian information about the senaculum and fetial/war-declaration customs.
- **Servius, commentary on *Aeneid* 9.52, late fourth/early fifth century; Latin. [LS]** Explains the *columna bellica* and the legal fiction by which a parcel near Bellona’s temple represented enemy territory.
- **Macrobius, *Saturnalia*, early fifth century; Latin. [LS]** Transmits late-antique antiquarian theology and etymological systematization; references must not be treated automatically as evidence for archaic belief.
- **Augustine, *City of God* 4.24; 6.10, early fifth century; Latin. [LS/hostile witness]** Uses Varro and Seneca to ridicule specialized Roman deities and theatrical cult. Valuable for otherwise lost material, but rhetorically hostile.
- **Arnobius, *Adversus nationes*, approximately 300 CE, and Lactantius, *Divine Institutes* 1.21, early fourth century; Latin. [hostile witnesses]** Preserve divine equations and ritual allegations while arguing against traditional religion. Their claims require corroboration.

The surviving manuscripts of nearly all literary works are medieval, often many centuries later than composition. Inscriptions and archaeological objects therefore have special evidentiary weight.

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Domain

**[EA]** Bellona governs war in its activated, public, and violent dimensions. Her temple’s location outside the *pomerium* made her particularly appropriate to relations with peoples not incorporated into Rome’s sacred civic space.

**[LE]** Poets increasingly make her the embodiment of battle-noise, bloodshed, frenzy, and civil war. Virgil’s bloody whip and Lucan’s destructive Bellona shaped later European iconography more strongly than Republican ritual did.

**[SI]** Mā-Bellona adds ecstatic prophecy, itinerant devotees, self-laceration, and association with Magna Mater. These are imperial transformations; there is no evidence that Appius Claudius’s Republican cult used self-wounding priests.

### Recognizable attributes

- Helmet, cuirass, shield, spear, sword, and military dress. **[LE/iconographic convention]**
- Bloody whip or scourge, from Virgil, *Aeneid* 8.703. **[LE]**
- Double axe, especially in Tibullus’s account of the Mā-Bellona priestess. **[SI/RV]**
- Spear, including the fetial spear and later *hastiferi*, “spear-bearers.” **[EA/SI, in different contexts]**
- Chariot or attendance upon Mars. **[LE]**
- Torch, snakes, or Fury-like appearance in some later art. **[LE/MA/U]** These often derive from conflation with Enyo, Erinys, Discordia, or personified War.

No securely ancient canonical sacred animal, plant, color, number, weekday, planet, or zodiacal sign belongs uniquely to Bellona. Black clothing and caps are reported for some imperial Bellonarii, but these concern Mā-Bellona’s devotees, not a universal divine color. Modern lists assigning Bellona red, Tuesday, iron, particular crystals, or astrological correspondences are **[MI]** unless they cite an actual ancient source.

### Ancient images

The painted female head on the *Belolai pocolom* is the earliest object bearing her name, but the identification of the head itself remains disputed. **[EA/U]**

Bellona appears on Roman reliefs, gems, and coins only where an inscription or distinctive context permits identification; an armed woman may instead be Roma, Virtus, Minerva, Victoria, or an Amazon. **[U]** The *Lexicon Iconographicum Mythologiae Classicae* appropriately treats identification as a contextual problem.

The possible figure on Republican coinage associated with Sulla or his descendants is disputed among Bellona, Venus, and other martial female personifications. Sulla’s documented devotion to Venus and encounter with Mā makes retrospective certainty unsafe.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

Bellona has no securely early birth narrative, parents, offspring, or independent family cycle. **Absence of evidence is decisive here:** the early cult record supplies a function and temple, not a genealogy.

Later Roman authors variously call her Mars’s sister, wife, companion, or charioteer. **[LE]** These versions were never reconciled into a single authoritative genealogy. Identification with Nerio likely encouraged the “wife of Mars” model; Greek equation with Enyo encouraged companion/sister-like martial roles.

### Every documented named divine interaction or joint appearance

- **Mars — companion in war. [EA/LE]** Plautus, *Amphitruo* 42–43, lists Bellona with Mars, Virtus, and Victoria; no narrative action occurs.
- **Mars — invoked jointly in the *devotio*. [LE/possible archaic rite]** Livy 8.9.6 and 10.28: Bellona follows Mars and Quirinus among the invoked gods.
- **Mars — sister. [LE]** Seneca’s lost material, preserved polemically by Augustine, *City of God* 6.10, and later mythographers.
- **Mars — wife or consort. [LE/LS]** Late literary and mythographic tradition; probably encouraged by comparison with Nerio. No early Roman narrative of a marriage survives.
- **Mars — battlefield attendant/charioteer. [LE]** Imperial poetic and artistic convention rather than a discrete early myth.
- **Nerio — identification as Mars’s martial female partner. [SI/LS/MSR]** Antiquarian comparison, especially through traditions preserved by Aulus Gellius and later scholarship. No surviving early ritual text says “Bellona is Nerio.”
- **Enyo — Greek identification. [SI]** Latin poets and glossators use Bellona as Enyo’s Roman name; this is interpretatio, not a narrated meeting.
- **Mā — fusion/equation. [SI/RV]** Developed through Roman contact with Cappadocia, especially around Sulla’s campaigns and imperial cult networks. The deities do not “meet” in a myth; their cult identities merge.
- **Virtus — joint listing. [EA]** Plautus, *Amphitruo* 42–43.
- **Virtus — divine equation. [SI/RV]** *Dea Virtus Bellona* in imperial inscriptions; Lactantius, *Divine Institutes* 1.21.16.
- **Victoria — joint listing. [EA]** Plautus, *Amphitruo* 42–43.
- **Janus — joint invocation. [LE/possible archaic rite]** Livy 8.9.6.
- **Jupiter — joint invocation. [LE/possible archaic rite]** Livy 8.9.6.
- **Quirinus — joint invocation. [LE/possible archaic rite]** Livy 8.9.6.
- **Lares — joint invocation. [LE/possible archaic rite]** Livy 8.9.6.
- **Di Novensiles — joint invocation. [LE/possible archaic rite]** Livy 8.9.6.
- **Di Indigetes — joint invocation. [LE/possible archaic rite]** Livy 8.9.6.
- **Tellus — same *devotio* complex. [LE]** Livy’s formula ultimately consigns enemy lives to Tellus and the Manes; Bellona is an earlier invocant, not Tellus’s antagonist.
- **Di Manes — same *devotio* complex. [LE]** As above.
- **Discordia — joint scene on Aeneas’s shield. [LE]** Virgil, *Aeneid* 8.700–703: Discord advances while Bellona follows with bloody scourge.
- **Magna Mater/Cybele — attendant and cultic neighbor. [SI/RV]** Imperial inscriptions call Bellona *pedisequa Matris*; CIL IX 3146 records restoration of Bellona’s image in a Magna Mater sanctuary at Corfinium.
- **Attis — cultic proximity, not mythic interaction. [SI/RV]** Bellona’s Ostian sanctuary and the *schola* of the *hastiferi* stood within the Campus of Magna Mater alongside Attis’s shrine.
- **Isis and Serapis — topographical proximity only. [RV]** Bellona Rufilia’s sanctuary was described as *ab Isis Serapis*. This does not demonstrate theological interaction.

No securely attested interaction with Minerva, Juno, Venus, Hercules, or Diana has been established merely from their belonging to the Roman pantheon.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Bellona possesses no surviving autonomous myth-cycle comparable to those of Mars, Venus, or Hercules. Her “narratives” are ritual-historical episodes and poetic personifications.

### Appius Claudius’s battle-vow

**[LE reporting 296 BCE]** In Livy 10.19, Appius’s army is faltering against the Etruscan-Samnite coalition. He raises his hands, vows Bellona a temple if she grants victory, and thereafter fights as if inspired by her. The story legitimates both victory and the temple.

The historical core—a Claudian temple vowed in war—is plausible and supported by the temple’s later existence. The precise prayer and divine inspiration are Livian narration, not a contemporary transcript.

### The Decian *devotiones*

**[LE/ritual tradition]** Bellona appears in the invocatory preamble when Decius Mus dedicates himself and the enemy army to death. She neither speaks nor acts independently. Her inclusion situates her among powers capable of enforcing Rome’s sacrificial transfer of destruction.

Scholars dispute how much of Livy’s formula is genuinely archaic. Its language and unusual divine collectives may preserve old ritual, but Livy’s presentation is Augustan.

### Bellona on Aeneas’s shield

**[LE]** Virgil’s ekphrasis of Actium places Bellona with bloody scourge beside Discord. This is not a cult legend but a literary image equating war’s divine splendor with civil bloodshed. It became the main visual template for early-modern Bellona.

### Sulla and Mā

**[LE/SI]** Plutarch reports that Sulla encountered or revered the Cappadocian goddess whom Romans understood as Bellona, and that an ecstatic slave prophesied to him. Later sources connected Sulla’s extraordinary confidence and violence with divine inspiration. The historical contact between Roman forces and Mā’s cult is credible; claims of visions or supernatural commands belong to Sullan self-representation and later biography.

### The fetial spear

**[EA as ritual; LE as divine narrative]** The fetiales originally cast a spear into enemy territory after ritual demands for redress. When wars became geographically remote, Roman antiquarians said land near Bellona’s temple was legally treated as foreign—traditionally purchased from a captive associated with Pyrrhus—and the spear was thrown across the *columna bellica*. Servius and Festus preserve the explanatory tradition. Statius later converts the rite into mythology by making Bellona herself hurl the spear.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Temple near the Circus Flaminius

**[EA]** Appius Claudius Caecus vowed the temple in 296 BCE. Ovid, *Fasti* 6.201–204, gives **3 June** as its dedication day. It stood in the southern Campus Martius, outside the *pomerium*, near the Circus Flaminius and Temple of Apollo.

Its extramural position served constitutional purposes:

- The Senate received foreign embassies whose members could not appropriately enter Rome’s sacred boundary.
- Commanders retaining military *imperium* waited there while the Senate considered triumphal claims.
- The Senate met there on other matters connected with war and foreign policy.
- The bronze *senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus* records a meeting there in 186 BCE.
- Livy records numerous meetings: 26.21; 28.9, 38; 30.21, 40; 31.47; 33.22, 24; 36.39; 38.44; 39.29; 41.6; 42.9, 21, 28, 36.

A *senaculum*, an outdoor senatorial gathering-place, lay nearby according to Festus.

Archaeologists commonly identify the temple with one of the Republican podiums beside the reconstructed Apollo Sosianus temple near the Theatre of Marcellus. **[MSR]** Exact assignment among adjacent remains has changed during modern excavation; it is not warranted to speak as though a complete labeled temple survives.

### The *columna bellica*

**[LE based on Republican ritual]** A column before the temple marked the boundary across which a fetial cast a blood-tipped or fire-hardened spear when Rome declared war. The nearby plot was treated legally as enemy land. Cassius Dio records use or revival of the ceremony under Augustus and Marcus Aurelius. The column therefore joined priestly law, topography, and theatrical statecraft.

### Priests and ordinary worship

The old Roman Bellona had no known *flamen* and no securely documented exclusive archaic priesthood. **[EA/absence]** Magistrates and the fetial college, rather than “priests of Bellona,” performed the state functions associated with her temple.

Votive dedications demonstrate private and provincial worship, but Bellona was never among the best-attested household deities. No reliable evidence establishes a distinct ancient Bellona pilgrimage economy or a standardized initiation.

### Mā-Bellona and the *fanatici*

**[SI/RV]** Imperial sources call Mā-Bellona’s devotees *Bellonarii* and *fanatici*. Tibullus describes a prophetess who wounds her arms with a double axe. Lucan and later satirists depict ecstatic dancing, trumpets or drums, bloodletting, and prophecy.

These accounts combine several kinds of evidence:

- Literary descriptions, often sensational or satirical.
- Inscriptions in which worshippers neutrally call themselves *fanaticus/fanatica*.
- Epigraphic links with Magna Mater, the Galli, and *hastiferi*.
- Christian polemic exploiting blood rituals as evidence of “pagan madness.”

Self-wounding is therefore well attested for the imperial ecstatic complex. Claims that devotees routinely drank human blood, that every sacrifice required self-mutilation, or that Republican Bellona had the same rites are much less secure.

### The *hastiferi*

**[RV/SI]** “Spear-bearers” formed organized collegia associated with Bellona and Magna Mater. Evidence occurs at Ostia, Vienne, and on the Rhine. The earliest evidence at Ostia is approximately **139 CE**. CIL XIII 7281 records *hastiferi* of the *civitas Mattiacorum* restoring a *Mons Vaticanus* in 236 CE.

Their precise function is disputed. A ritual war-dance is plausible but not directly demonstrated. Fishwick showed that the associations were structured collegia rather than merely uncontrolled bands of ecstatic mendicants.

### Ostia

**[RV/SI]** A small Temple of Bellona stood within Ostia’s Campus Magna Mater, opposite or near the *schola* of the *hastiferi*. Inscriptions record rebuilding by Aulus Livius Proculus and Publius Lucilius Gamala. Its placement materializes Bellona’s imperial subordination to or partnership with Magna Mater.

### Other sanctuaries and inscriptions

- **Bellona Pulvinensis at Rome:** CIL VI 490, 2232, 2233; probably imperial and perhaps not earlier than the third century CE.
- **Bellona Rufilia at Rome:** CIL VI 2234; location uncertain.
- **Corfinium:** CIL IX 3146 records restoration of Bellona’s image inside a Magna Mater sanctuary.
- **Caesarea in Mauretania:** AE 1902, 12 records the priestess Scantia Peregrina building a temple to Bellona at her expense on land granted by the municipal council. **[RV]**
- **Rhineland:** dedications to *Dea Virtus Bellona* and collegia of *hastiferi*. **[RV/SI]**
- **Praeneste and other Italian sites:** literary or epigraphic evidence suggests local sanctuaries, but their histories are poorly recoverable.

### Human sacrifice and temple prostitution

No reliable evidence shows that Bellona’s Roman or Cappadocian cult practiced institutional human sacrifice. **[U/negative finding]** Self-shedding of devotees’ blood is not the same as killing a victim.

No evidence establishes temple prostitution in Bellona’s cult. **[U/negative finding]** Generalized claims about “oriental goddesses” cannot substitute for documentation.

## 7. Historical Evolution

### Third–second centuries BCE: emergence in the record

Bellona is epigraphically visible by approximately 300–225 BCE. Her temple was traditionally vowed in 296 BCE and certainly existed by 186 BCE. The cult served Roman warfare and constitutional geography but produced little mythology.

### First century BCE: literary and trans-Mediterranean expansion

Varro fixed the Duellona/Bellona etymology. Roman writers began using Bellona as a personification of war. Sulla’s eastern campaigns brought elite Romans into contact with Cappadocian Mā, facilitating the equation Mā–Enyo–Bellona.

### Augustan age

Livy integrated Bellona into Rome’s archaic ritual past through the *devotio* narratives and Appius’s vow. Virgil made her an icon of bloody warfare; Ovid tied her temple to the calendar and fetial rite. The Greek equation with Enyo became normal literary practice.

### First–third centuries CE: Mā-Bellona

The imperial period produced *fanatici*, *Bellonarii*, *hastiferi*, provincial temples, and fusion with Virtus and Magna Mater. This was Bellona’s widest geographic diffusion, but much of it concerned the transformed Cappadocian deity rather than simple export of the Republican cult.

### Fourth–sixth centuries: displacement

Christian emperors progressively restricted sacrifice and public temple funding. No surviving decree is known to have targeted Bellona alone. **[EA/negative finding]** Her cult declined within the broader dismantling of traditional civic sacrifice and temple institutions.

Augustine, Arnobius, and Lactantius preserved fragments precisely while attacking polytheism. There is no evidence of an underground, continuously transmitted Bellona cult through the Middle Ages.

### Medieval and Renaissance reception

Bellona survived as an allegorical name in Latin poetry, mythography, chronicles, and depictions of War. **[MA]** Renaissance humanists recovered Virgil, Ovid, Livy, and antiquarian sources; Bellona accordingly became a helmeted female personification usable in royal, civic, and military art.

### Modern revival

Contemporary Roman reconstructionists and eclectic Pagans sometimes worship Bellona. **[MI]** This is religiously real as modern practice but historically discontinuous. There are no dependable membership figures for Bellona-specific worship, nor evidence that any present group descends institutionally from ancient *fanatici* or *hastiferi*.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Visual art

Rembrandt’s **Bellona** (1633), now in the Metropolitan Museum of Art, portrays an armored woman with shield. **[MI]** The museum interprets it in the setting of the Dutch Republic’s readiness to renew war with Spain. Its humanized, richly dressed figure is early-modern historical allegory, not reconstruction of an ancient cult statue.

Bellona became common in Baroque and neoclassical palace programs, sometimes paired with Mars, Victory, or Peace. Johann Wilhelm Beyer’s eighteenth-century **Janus and Bellona** group at Schönbrunn is an allegory of war and state power. Auguste Rodin’s helmeted female bust conventionally titled **Bellona** likewise turns the goddess into an embodiment of modern martial violence.

### Literature

Bellona became a conventional poetic synonym for war:

- Shakespeare invokes “the fire-eyed maid of smoky war” and Bellona’s bridegroom in *Macbeth* 1.2. **[MI]**
- Renaissance and eighteenth-century poets used “Bellona” metrically and allegorically without implying living cult.
- Modern fantasy fiction and games frequently make her a fully individualized warrior queen, assign elaborate family trees, or treat her as identical to a Greek goddess in a universal pantheon. **[MI]** Such narrative systems are modern creations.

Rick Riordan’s *Heroes of Olympus* novels materially shaped the popular image of Bellona as an emphatically Roman war goddess and mother of demigods. This parentage system belongs to the novels, not ancient evidence.

Games including *Smite*, *For Honor*, strategy games, comics, and role-playing settings commonly equip Bellona with multiple weapon forms. **[MI]** The ancient sources support martial equipment generally, but not a canonical weapon-switching repertoire.

### Occultism and neopaganism

Bellona did not occupy a major stable place in Renaissance ceremonial magic, the Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn, or classical grimoire demonology. **[negative finding]** Modern websites assigning her planetary seals, crystals, “dark goddess” initiations, menstrual rites, or fixed correspondences generally produce eclectic twentieth- or twenty-first-century systems.

Modern offerings, invocations, and devotional calendars are reconstructionist or creative practices. The June 3 date has ancient support; claims of an ancient March 24 “Day of Bellona’s blood” usually conflate the Magna Mater festival’s *dies sanguinis* and the imperial *hastiferi* with an independent early Bellona festival. **[MI based on SI]**

No substantial evidence was found for distinctive nationalist or extremist appropriation of Bellona comparable to that involving some Germanic, Slavic, or solar symbols. Military and weapons-related commercial use exists, but a company or weapon named Bellona is classical branding, not cult continuity.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Roman-versus-Cappadocian Bellona

Wissowa, Latte, Dumézil, and later historians distinguish the Italian Bellona from Mā-Bellona. This remains the indispensable analytical separation. Older reference works sometimes say the foreign cult “displaced” the Roman goddess; the evidence more securely demonstrates coexistence, name-sharing, and eventual predominance of the ecstatic complex in surviving imperial testimony.

### Archaic antiquity

Bellona’s presence in Livy’s *devotio* formula has led scholars to call her archaic. Against that, she lacks a *flamen*, an early calendar festival, or secure pre-third-century inscription. Her divine name may be old while her monumental public cult is a middle-Republican Claudian foundation. **[MSR]**

Dumézilian interpretation can place Bellona within an Indo-European martial structure centered on Mars. The evidence supports association with war but does not independently prove a reconstructed tripartite theology.

### Sabine, Latin, or Etruscan?

- **Sabine theory:** Claudian ancestry and Nerio comparison.
- **Latin/Roman theory:** transparent Latin name, Roman ritual placement, and lack of external cult evidence.
- **Etruscan/Italic theory:** vessel manufacture and possible iconographic parallels.

No option can presently be demonstrated as an exclusive ethnic origin. The safest formulation is that Bellona is securely Roman/Latial in the surviving record, with proposed Sabine or Etruscan antecedents.

### Enyo

The Bellona–Enyo equation is real as Roman literary translation. It does not establish that Bellona was imported from Greece. The disputed face on the inscribed cup cannot carry that argument by itself.

### Nerio

Nerio’s status as Mars’s consort or personified valor resembles later descriptions of Bellona. Scholars have variously proposed identity, functional overlap, or antiquarian confusion. The surviving sources show comparison, not a recoverable original identity.

### Hostile witnesses

Christian apologists accurately preserve some names and equations, but their descriptions of cult excess serve polemical arguments. Satirists likewise exaggerate. Convergence between inscriptions, Tibullus, Plutarch, and cult architecture confirms an ecstatic Mā-Bellona complex; isolated sensational details remain uncertain.

### State of the evidence

The corpus is fragmented:

- The Bellona cup supplies the earliest name but little theology.
- Republican evidence centers on one temple and later historical narratives.
- No complete temple archive, hymn, liturgy, or indigenous mythology survives.
- The Circus Flaminius temple survives only in disputed archaeological remains.
- Numerous inscriptions are detached, damaged, or known through older publications.
- Varro, Seneca, and Festus survive incompletely or through epitomes and hostile quotations.
- *LIMC*, CIL, ILS, AE, topographical studies, and critical editions remain essential because generic armed female images are easily misidentified.

## 10. Chronology

- **Approximately 300–225 BCE:** *Belolai pocolom* produced; earliest secure inscription naming Bellona.
- **296 BCE:** Appius Claudius Caecus traditionally vows Bellona’s temple during war against Samnites and Etruscans; Livy 10.19.
- **After 296 BCE, dedication date 3 June:** temple erected outside the *pomerium* near the later Circus Flaminius zone.
- **295 BCE:** later Livian account places Bellona in the younger Decius’s *devotio* at Sentinum.
- **186 BCE:** *senatus consultum de Bacchanalibus* records a Senate meeting *apud aedem Duelonai*.
- **Approximately 190 BCE:** Plautus lists Bellona with Virtus, Victoria, and Mars.
- **Second–first centuries BCE:** temple repeatedly used for embassies, commanders, and triumphal deliberations.
- **First century BCE:** symbolic enemy territory and *columna bellica* associated with the fetial declaration of war.
- **80s BCE:** Roman contact with Cappadocian Mā becomes especially visible through Sulla.
- **45 BCE:** Cicero’s *De natura deorum* illustrates elite philosophical treatment of Roman divinity.
- **40s BCE:** Varro records Duellona/Bellona etymology.
- **20s BCE:** Virgil depicts Bellona with bloody scourge.
- **Late first century BCE:** Tibullus describes an ecstatic, self-wounding Bellona priestess.
- **Approximately 8 CE:** Ovid records the temple’s calendar day and the war-declaration site.
- **First century CE:** Lucan makes Bellona an emblem of civil slaughter and prophetic frenzy.
- **Approximately 50 CE:** imperial calendars begin documenting the developing Magna Mater festival complex relevant to later Bellona associations.
- **Approximately 90 CE:** Statius mythologizes the fetial action by assigning it to Bellona.
- **Early second century CE:** Plutarch records Sulla’s Cappadocian Mā/Bellona associations.
- **Approximately 139 CE:** earliest secure Ostian evidence for *hastiferi*.
- **Second–third centuries CE:** Bellona Pulvinensis, Virtus Bellona, and provincial Mā-Bellona cults flourish.
- **236 CE:** CIL XIII 7281 records the Mattiacan *hastiferi* restoring a *Mons Vaticanus*.
- **Approximately 300–430 CE:** Arnobius, Lactantius, Augustine, and other Christian writers preserve and attack Bellona traditions.
- **Fourth–sixth centuries CE:** public cult disappears during the empire-wide suppression and abandonment of sacrificial institutions; no Bellona-specific abolition is known.
- **Middle Ages:** Bellona persists as a literary name and allegory, not a demonstrably continuous cult.
- **Renaissance–eighteenth century:** classical texts restore Bellona to European poetry, painting, sculpture, and state allegory.
- **1633:** Rembrandt paints *Bellona*.
- **Nineteenth–twentieth centuries:** comparative religion debates Sabine, Etruscan, Nerio, Enyo, and Mā origins; CIL and archaeological study consolidate the documentary corpus.
- **Late twentieth–twenty-first centuries:** Roman reconstructionists and eclectic Pagans revive devotional use; novels, games, and popular occult sources create extensive new attributes unsupported by ancient evidence.
- **Present:** Bellona remains active as a classical symbol, fictional character, military/commercial name, and object of small-scale revived worship; continuity from antiquity is unestablished.

## 11. Sources

### Primary and documentary sources

The central primary witnesses are CIL I² 441, CIL I² 581, CIL VI 490, 2232–2234 and 30851, CIL IX 3146, CIL XIII 7281, CIL XIV 69 and associated Ostian texts; Plautus, *Amphitruo* 42–43; Livy 8.9, 10.19, 10.28 and the Senate-meeting passages listed above; Varro, *De lingua Latina* 5.73 and 7.49; Cicero, *De natura deorum*; Virgil, *Aeneid* 8.700–703; Ovid, *Fasti* 6.201–208; Tibullus 1.6.43–54; Horace, *Satires* 2.3; Lucan 1.564–567 and 7.568–573; Statius, *Thebaid* 4; Plutarch, *Sulla*; Juvenal 4 and 6; Apuleius, *Metamorphoses* 8; Festus; Servius on *Aeneid* 9.52; Lactantius, *Divine Institutes* 1.21; Augustine, *City of God* 4.24 and 6.10; and Arnobius, *Adversus nationes*.

### Major modern scholarship

The older foundations remain Georg Wissowa, *Religion und Kultus der Römer*; Kurt Latte, *Römische Religionsgeschichte*; Georges Dumézil, *Archaic Roman Religion*; the *Realencyclopädie* article “Bellona”; Daremberg–Saglio; and Platner and Ashby’s *Topographical Dictionary of Ancient Rome*. These require correction through inscription databases, archaeology, and newer work.

Modern orientation is provided by Mary Beard, John North, and Simon Price, *Religions of Rome*; John Scheid’s studies of Roman ritual and civic religion; the *Oxford Classical Dictionary*; *Brill’s New Pauly*; *LIMC* for iconography; Duncan Fishwick on the *hastiferi*; and recent studies such as John Serrati’s treatment of Bellona, gender, and middle-Republican warfare. The latter usefully challenges the assumption that Bellona was always regarded chiefly as a horrifying or foreign power.
