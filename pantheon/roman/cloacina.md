# Cloacina: Research Dossier

> **Class:** Roman goddess or localized female *numen*; later cultically identified with Venus as **Venus Cloacina / Venus Cluacina**. Ancient evidence does not securely establish an originally Etruscan goddess, despite frequent modern claims to that effect.  
> **Earliest secure attestation:** Plautus, *Curculio* 471, early second century BCE—probably approximately 193–184 BCE—referring to the already-existing **“shrine of Cloacina”** (*apud Cloacinae sacrum*) in the Roman Forum. This proves the shrine and name existed by Plautus’s time but supplies no mythology, ritual, genealogy, or description of the goddess. The surviving archaeological structure cannot securely be dated back to Rome’s regal period.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[EA] Secure early attestation:** contemporary or nearly contemporary literary, material, numismatic, or epigraphic evidence.
- **[LE] Later literary elaboration:** later antiquarian or narrative evidence concerning an allegedly earlier event.
- **[RV] Regional or textual variant.**
- **[LS] Learned systematization:** antiquarian etymology, theological classification, or scholarly harmonization.
- **[SI] Syncretistic identification:** an equation or fusion historically attested but not assumed to have existed from the beginning.
- **[CA] Christian-apologetic testimony:** ancient evidence transmitted in polemical argument; useful but hostile and sometimes derivative.
- **[MSR] Modern scholarly reconstruction:** an inference, not an explicit ancient statement.
- **[MI] Modern invention or reinvention.**
- **[NEG] Negative finding:** a claim for which the searched evidence does not furnish ancient documentation.

## 1. Identification, Names, and Epithets

### Identity

Cloacina is a very sparsely documented Roman female divinity associated with a small open shrine in the Forum, close to the *tabernae novae* and later Basilica Aemilia. Her name was connected in antiquity both with **purification** and with the **Cloaca Maxima**, Rome’s principal drainage channel. By the first century BCE at the latest she had been identified with Venus, producing the cult title **Venus Cloacina** or **Venus Cluacina**. The strongest surviving material representation is a denarius issued by L. Mussidius Longus in 42 BCE: its reverse labels and depicts the shrine, with two female statues on a circular platform.

Descriptions such as “goddess of sewers,” “patroness of plumbing,” or “guardian of sanitation” are understandable modern summaries, but only the first is reasonably close to late-antique explanations. No surviving pagan ritual text appoints her protector of plumbers, toilets, public health, or wastewater workers.

### Documented forms

| Form | Language and meaning | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Cloācīna** | Latin feminine divine name; conventional spelling influenced by *cloāca*, “drain, sewer” | Plautus, Livy, Lactantius, Augustine; coin legend abbreviated **CLOACIN** or **CLOAC** |
| **Cluācīna** | Latin form favored where the name is explained from *cluere/cloare*, “to cleanse, purify” | Pliny, *Natural History* 15.119; Servius on *Aeneid* 1.720; lexicographical tradition |
| **Venus Cloacina / Veneris Cloacinae** | “Venus Cloacina,” conventionally “Venus the Purifier” or “Venus of the Cloaca” | Pliny 15.119; the 42 BCE shrine coin; Servius |
| **Cloacinae sacrum** | “Shrine/sacred place of Cloacina” | Plautus, *Curculio* 471 |
| **signa Veneris Cluacinae** | “Images/statues of Venus Cluacina” | Pliny, *Natural History* 15.119 |
| **sacellum/sacrum Cloacinae** | Modern topographical shorthand based on the ancient wording | Archaeological and topographical literature |

The British Museum specimen of RRC 494/42a carries the abbreviated shrine inscription and identifies the represented monument as the shrine of Venus Cloacina. Its obverse depicts Concordia, not Cloacina. [The British Museum record dates the denarius to 42 BCE.](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/C_R-9224)

### Etymology: purification or sewer?

Two ancient explanations survive.

1. **Purification derivation — *cluere* or *cloare*, “to cleanse.”**  
   **[LE/LS]** Pliny says that Romans and Sabines laid down their arms and were purified with myrtle at the place occupied in his time by the images of Venus Cluacina, adding: *cluere enim antiqui purgare dicebant*—“for the ancients called purification *cluere*.” Servius similarly explains that Cloacina was so named because the ancients used *cloare* for “purify.” This is a real ancient etymological tradition, not merely a modern proposal. [Pliny’s Latin text and context are preserved in *Natural History* 15.119.](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/L/Roman/Texts/Pliny_the_Elder/15%2A.html)

2. **Sewer derivation — *cloaca*, specifically the Cloaca Maxima.**  
   **[CA/LE]** The treatise traditionally attributed to Cyprian says Titus Tatius made a goddess of an image found in a sewer and called it Cloacina. Lactantius is still more explicit: *Cloacinae simulacrum in cloaca maxima repertum Tatius consecravit*—“Tatius consecrated an image of Cloacina found in the Cloaca Maxima”—and named it from its location because he did not know whose image it was. [The Latin survives in *Divine Institutes* 1.20.](https://monumenta.ch/latein/text.php?hide_apparatus=1&inframe=1&level=4&rumpfid=Lactantius%2C+Divinae+Institutiones%2C+1%2C+++20&tabelle=Lactantius)

These explanations need not represent two unrelated word histories: *cloaca*, *Cluacina*, and the purification verb were associated by Roman antiquarians and are commonly connected etymologically. What cannot be established is whether the goddess’s name first meant “Purifier,” first denoted the spirit of a watercourse or drain, or was retrospectively interpreted after the shrine became topographically associated with the Cloaca.

The form **Cluacina** supports the purification explanation, while **Cloacina** invites the sewer explanation. Spelling alone cannot securely recover the cult’s origin because the surviving spellings come from texts transmitted and normalized over centuries.

### Alleged Etruscan origin

**[MSR, weakly supported]** Popular references repeatedly call Cloacina an “Etruscan goddess.” The available evidence does not establish this:

- no securely identified Etruscan inscription names *Cloacina*;
- no securely attributed Etruscan mirror depicts and labels her;
- no known Etruscan theonym supplies an independently attested counterpart;
- the Titus Tatius legend makes her Sabine-associated, not explicitly Etruscan;
- the Cloaca Maxima’s traditional association with Rome’s Etruscan kings does not prove that every divinity associated with it was Etruscan.

Earlier scholarship sometimes used “Etruscan” broadly for obscure Roman religious institutions attributed to the monarchy. That is a historical hypothesis, not an ancient attestation. Thulin’s studies of Etruscan religious survivals and the Etruscan mirror corpus provide comparative context but no securely identified Cloacina.

### Classification

- **Plautus:** a named being possessing a *sacrum*, gendered female by the name’s declension; no classification beyond that. **[EA]**
- **Republican coinage:** a cult figure or figures housed in a shrine; identification of each of the two statues remains uncertain. **[EA]**
- **Pliny and Servius:** Venus under a purificatory title. **[SI/LE]**
- **Cyprian, Lactantius, Augustine, Prudentius:** a Roman goddess invoked as an example of supposedly absurd pagan multiplication of divinity. **[CA]**
- **Modern reference works:** usually “goddess of the Cloaca Maxima” or “sewer goddess.” **[MSR/simplification]**

## 2. Earliest Attestation and Textual Corpus

### Earliest secure evidence: Plautus

Plautus’s *Curculio* contains a comic map of the Forum:

> *qui mendacem et gloriosum, apud Cloacinae sacrum*  
> “If you want a liar and braggart, [look] at the shrine of Cloacina.”

The play was composed in the early second century BCE; an exact performance year is not securely known. The reference is casual, implying that a Roman audience could locate the monument without explanation. It establishes:

- the name **Cloacina**;
- a recognized *sacrum* in the Forum;
- its use as a topographical landmark by Plautus’s lifetime.

It does **not** establish:

- construction by Titus Tatius;
- connection with the rape of the Sabine women;
- an Etruscan origin;
- a purification rite;
- identification with Venus;
- protection of the complete Cloaca Maxima.

[The complete Latin passage is *Curculio* 462–486, with the reference at line 471.](https://wwww.thelatinlibrary.com/plautus/curculio.shtml)

### Ancient corpus

#### Plautus, *Curculio* 471

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Date:** early second century BCE.
- **Role:** shrine as a Forum landmark.
- **Tier:** **[EA]**.
- **Transmission:** medieval manuscript tradition; there is no autograph or Republican papyrus.

#### Lucilius

Fragments of Lucilius’s satires map and mock the Forum’s social geography, and modern discussions sometimes connect them with the Plautine passage. No surviving Lucilian fragment securely adds a myth or ritual of Cloacina. **[NEG]**

#### Cicero, *De natura deorum*

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Date:** 45 BCE.
- **Status:** Cicero discusses the proliferation, interpretation, and philosophical classification of Roman gods, but the searched text does not yield a direct substantive Cloacina dossier.
- **Finding:** Claims that Cicero is a principal source for Cloacina should be qualified. His work supplies intellectual context, not the central evidence presented by Plautus, Livy, Pliny, or Lactantius. **[NEG/contextual]**

#### Coinage of L. Mussidius Longus

- **Language/support:** Latin legends on silver denarii.
- **Date:** 42 BCE.
- **Types:** Crawford RRC 494/42a–b and related 494/43 variants.
- **Obverses:** Concordia on one principal type; Sol appears on a related type.
- **Reverse:** circular, railed shrine labeled **CLOACIN** or **CLOAC**, with two standing female statues, steps or approach, and small associated objects.
- **Role:** earliest surviving image of the shrine and its cult statues; direct contemporary material evidence.
- **Tier:** **[EA]**.

A Berlin specimen of RRC 494/43b shows Sol on the obverse and two statues inside the labeled shrine. [The Berlin Münzkabinett gives its date, measurements, and catalogue references.](https://ikmk.smb.museum/object?id=18233902&lang=en&view=rs)

The pairing of the shrine with **Concordia** is often interpreted as political messaging during the civil wars: reconciliation, purification, and restored civic agreement. This is plausible **[MSR]**, especially given the Sabine reconciliation legend known from Pliny, but the coin itself does not narrate that legend or name Octavian as a purifier.

#### Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 3.48.5

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Composition:** principally late first century BCE.
- **Narrated setting:** traditional fifth century BCE.
- **Role:** topography in the death of Verginia.
- **Textual import:** Verginius draws his daughter aside near Cloacina, beside the shops later called *tabernae novae*, takes a butcher’s knife, and kills her rather than allow Appius Claudius to enslave her.
- **Tier:** shrine location is **[EA for Livy’s own Rome]**; the fifth-century scene is **[LE]**.

Livy does not make Cloacina act, command the killing, receive Verginia as a sacrifice, or protect her. The episode is therefore not evidence for human sacrifice to Cloacina. The location heightens the scene’s public and civic character but does not constitute a ritual offering. [A scholarly analysis notes that Livy uniquely specifies the shrine as the location.](https://publishing.cdlib.org/ucpressebooks/public/book/spectacle-and-society-in-livy-s-history.html)

#### Virgil and Servius

Virgil does not name Cloacina in the *Aeneid*. Servius’s late-antique commentary on *Aeneid* 1.720 does:

- **Date:** Servian commentary chiefly fourth–fifth century CE, with later expanded material in *Servius Danielis*.
- **Role:** explains Cloacina through the old word *cloare*, “to purify,” and relates her to Venus and ritual cleansing.
- **Tier:** **[LE/LS]**.

Virgil is therefore not a direct Cloacina source; Servius is.

#### Ovid, *Fasti* and *Metamorphoses*

Neither work supplies a securely identifiable Cloacina episode. Ovid’s treatment of Venus, Roman festivals, purification, and the Sabine cycle is relevant context, but citing Ovid as direct evidence for Cloacina would overstate the corpus. **[NEG]**

#### Pliny the Elder, *Natural History* 15.119

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Date:** completed circa 77–79 CE.
- **Role:** fullest pagan antiquarian aetiology.
- **Narrative:** Romans and Sabines, after nearly fighting over the abducted women, laid down their arms and were purified with myrtle at the place where the images of Venus Cluacina stood in Pliny’s time.
- **Etymology:** *cluere* meant “to purify.”
- **Tier:** existence of the images in Pliny’s day **[EA]**; the regal-period ceremony **[LE]**.

Pliny is the key evidence joining Venus, myrtle, purification, Roman-Sabine reconciliation, and the shrine.

#### Festus/Paulus

Festus’s epitome of Verrius Flaccus is indispensable for Roman religious vocabulary and preserves related antiquarian material about archaic words, Venus, and purification. No intact Festan entry presently establishes an independent Cloacina myth beyond the traditions more fully preserved by Pliny and Servius. Fragmentary transmission makes arguments from silence cautious. **[NEG/LS context]**

#### Tertullian, *De pallio* 4.7

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Date:** conventionally early third century CE.
- **Text:** Tertullian jokes that Diogenes would have dragged Empedocles into the recesses of the *Cloacinae*.
- **Role:** punning plural or sewer-associated usage rather than cult narrative.
- **Tier:** **[CA/late lexical reception]**.

[Vincent Hunink’s translation and the Latin edition are available together.](https://www.tertullian.org/articles/hunink_de_pallio.htm)

#### *De idolorum vanitate*, traditionally attributed to Cyprian

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Date:** third century CE if genuinely Cyprianic; attribution and dependence within Latin apologetic literature require caution.
- **Narrative:** Titus Tatius allegedly found an image in a sewer and turned it into the goddess Cloacina.
- **Tier:** **[CA/LE]**.
- **Value:** early witness to the “found in the sewer” explanation, but polemically framed to ridicule Roman cult.

#### Arnobius, *Adversus nationes*

Arnobius attacks Roman images, local gods, and the materiality of cult. In the searched surviving text he does not furnish a distinct Cloacina narrative comparable to Lactantius. He is relevant as evidence for the apologetic environment, not as a primary source for an additional episode. **[NEG/contextual]**

#### Lactantius, *Divine Institutes* 1.20

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Date:** approximately 303–311 CE, with later revisions possible.
- **Narrative:** Titus Tatius consecrated an unidentified image found in the Cloaca Maxima and named it from the place.
- **Tier:** **[CA/LE]**.
- **Problem:** Lactantius’s story conflicts in emphasis with Pliny. Pliny explains the name through purification at an already meaningful location; Lactantius explains it through discovery in the sewer.

#### Prudentius, *Apotheosis* 197

- **Language:** Latin verse.
- **Date:** late fourth or early fifth century CE.
- **Text:** *Nemo Cloacinae, aut Eponae super astra deabus…*—roughly, “No one [places] the goddesses Cloacina or Epona above the stars…”
- **Role:** Christian polemical juxtaposition of supposedly low or local goddesses with celestial divinity.
- **Tier:** **[CA]**.

[The surviving Latin places Cloacina beside Epona in the polemic.](https://www.monumenta.ch/latein/text.php?domain=&hide_apparatus=1&inframe=1&lang=0&level=&links=1&rumpfid=Prudentius%2C+Apotheosis&tabelle=Prudentius)

#### Augustine, *City of God* 4.8 and related passages

- **Language:** Latin.
- **Date:** early fifth century CE.
- **Role:** Augustine lists Cloacina among narrowly specialized Roman gods to ridicule Roman polytheism.
- **Tier:** **[CA/LS]**.
- **Relation to Varro:** Augustine repeatedly relies on Varro’s lost *Antiquitates rerum divinarum*, but the exact boundaries between Varronian material and Augustine’s rhetorical arrangement are not always recoverable. Cloacina’s mere inclusion cannot automatically be quoted as Varro’s exact wording.

[Augustine’s list includes Cloacina alongside Volupia, Libentina, Vaticanus, and Cunina.](https://st-takla.org/books/en/ecf/102/1020113.html)

### Calendars, inscriptions, and missing documentation

- No securely identified surviving *fasti* entry assigns Cloacina a festival day. **[NEG]**
- No securely identified priesthood of Cloacina is known. **[NEG]**
- No substantial corpus of private votive inscriptions to Cloacina is presently documented. **[NEG]**
- The coin legend is the most important surviving inscribed use attached to her monument.
- Claims that CIL VI.781–782 concern Cloacina appear to result from confusion with other Venus cults; they should not be used without checking the original CIL entries.
- No hymn, prayer, sacrificial regulation, oracle, magical formula, or temple archive addressed to Cloacina survives. **[NEG]**

## 3. Domain, Attributes, and Iconography

### Securely documented domains

1. **Purification after civic conflict.**  
   **[LE but explicit]** Pliny connects the location and title with purification of Romans and Sabines after their conflict over the abducted Sabine women.

2. **The Forum drainage channel or Cloaca Maxima.**  
   **[LE/MSR]** Lactantius explicitly locates the found image in the Cloaca Maxima. Archaeological topography places the shrine close to the line traditionally associated with the Forum drain. This supports a sewer/drainage connection, although the ancient hydraulic phases are complex.

3. **Venus, marriage, and reconciliation.**  
   **[SI/interpretive]** The myrtle is Venusian; the conflict originated in the seizure and subsequent marriages of Sabine women; Concordia appears on the 42 BCE coin. These elements make matrimonial and civic reconciliation plausible dimensions. No surviving ritual manual names Cloacina as a general protectress of marriage or sexual intercourse.

### Attributes

- **Myrtle:** explicitly associated with the purification in Pliny. Secure as an attribute of the aetiological story; uncertain as a recurring cult offering.
- **Water or drain:** inherent in the shrine’s topography and Lactantius’s version.
- **Purification:** explicit ancient explanation.
- **Concord:** suggested by the coin’s obverse and the Sabine story; not an independently recorded epithet.
- **Sewer sanitation:** modern functional extrapolation.
- **Health, hygiene, plumbing, toilets:** not documented ancient domains.

### The shrine on the 42 BCE denarii

The coins show:

- a circular or oval platform;
- a low balustrade;
- two standing female statues;
- an approach or steps;
- abbreviated labeling **CLOACIN/CLOAC**;
- small upright objects, sometimes interpreted as an altar, cippus, column, or ritual support;
- on some readings, a branch held by one figure.

It is tempting to label the two figures “Venus and Cloacina.” This remains **[MSR]**. They could represent two images subsumed under the plural *signa* of Venus Cloacina; the coin does not label the statues individually. Pliny’s plural *signa* confirms multiple images but not their separate identities.

### Archaeological remains

Between 1899 and 1901 excavators exposed a small circular foundation in front of the Basilica Aemilia. The remains include a round travertine substructure with a marble curb, approximately 2.4 metres in diameter, and match the monument’s literary location and coin design closely enough for the identification to be secure. [The topographical record summarizes the literary, numismatic, and archaeological identification.](https://www.digitalaugustanrome.org/records/cloacina-sacrum/)

The remains establish the monument’s later form, not a regal-period foundation. Stratigraphic and architectural evidence does not verify Titus Tatius as founder.

### Unattested attributes

No ancient source securely assigns Cloacina:

- a sacred animal;
- a celestial body;
- a number, color, weekday, or month;
- a weapon;
- children, attendants, or a mount;
- a standardized human physiognomy;
- a separate Etruscan iconographic type.

## 4. Genealogy and Relations to Other Divine Beings

### Genealogy

**[NEG]** No surviving ancient source gives Cloacina parents, siblings, consort, offspring, birth narrative, residence among the gods, or place in a formal divine hierarchy. She is not integrated into the Olympian family tree.

### Venus

The relation to Venus is identification, not genealogy.

- **Early state:** Plautus says only “Cloacina.”
- **By the late Republic/early Empire:** the shrine is understood as belonging to **Venus Cloacina**.
- **Mechanism:** uncertain. Purification, myrtle, marriage, sexuality, and reconciliation may all have contributed.
- **Historical status:** **[SI]**, not proof that Cloacina was always merely an epithet of Venus.

### Every documented god-god interaction

The corpus is unusually barren of divine narrative. The following exhausts the securely identifiable relations:

| Other being | Interaction | Source and date | Tier |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Venus** | Cloacina is identified with or absorbed into Venus as **Venus Cluacina/Cloacina**; this is a cultic equation, not a narrated meeting between two goddesses. | Coinage of L. Mussidius Longus, 42 BCE; Pliny 15.119, 77–79 CE; Servius on *Aeneid* 1.720, fourth–fifth century CE | **[SI]** |
| **Concordia** | Concordia’s portrait appears on the obverse of a denarius whose reverse depicts Cloacina’s shrine. This is a designed numismatic association suggesting civic concord, not a mythic encounter. | RRC 494/42a, 42 BCE | **[EA; political juxtaposition]** |
| **Sol** | Sol appears on the obverse of a related Mussidius denarius with the Cloacina shrine on the reverse. No ancient text explains a theological relationship. | RRC 494/43b, 42 BCE | **[EA; numismatic juxtaposition only]** |
| **Jupiter/Jove** | In Pope’s *Dunciad*, the bookseller Edmund Curll invokes Jove and Cloacina during the mock-heroic games. This is not ancient religion. | Alexander Pope, *The Dunciad*, Book II, versions from 1728 onward | **[MI/modern literary retelling]** |
| **Epona** | Prudentius names Cloacina and Epona together as examples in Christian polemic; they do not interact. | *Apotheosis* 197, late fourth/early fifth century CE | **[CA; juxtaposition only]** |

**Negative finding:** No ancient myth records Cloacina speaking to, fighting, marrying, deceiving, rescuing, or being judged by another deity.

## 5. Major Myths and Narrative Cycles

Cloacina possesses no surviving epic cycle. Her “mythology” consists chiefly of competing aetiologies for a shrine and a divine name.

### A. Roman-Sabine purification

**Fullest form:** After Romulus’s Romans abducted the Sabine women, Romans and Sabines prepared to fight. The women intervened and reconciliation followed. At the place later occupied by the images of Venus Cluacina, the combatants laid down their weapons and purified themselves with sprigs of myrtle.

- **Earliest surviving source for this Cloacina-specific form:** Pliny, *Natural History* 15.119, 77–79 CE.
- **Purificatory etymology:** *cluere* = “purify.”
- **Founder figures:** Romulus and the Sabine ruler Titus Tatius belong to the wider reconciliation tradition, although Pliny’s surviving wording emphasizes the two peoples rather than formally calling Tatius the shrine’s builder.
- **Tier:** **[LE]** for the regal event; **[EA]** for the story’s existence in the first century CE.

The story explains several features simultaneously: the archaic name, myrtle, Venus, the shrine’s place in the Forum, and Rome’s idealized formation through conflict followed by concord.

It should not be treated as contemporary evidence for an eighth-century BCE ceremony. It is an antiquarian aetiology preserved many centuries after the period it purports to describe.

### B. Titus Tatius and the image found in the sewer

**Fullest form:** Titus Tatius found an unidentified divine image in the Cloaca Maxima, consecrated it, and named it Cloacina from its place of discovery.

- **Sources:** the pseudo-/Cyprianic *De idolorum vanitate*; Lactantius, *Divine Institutes* 1.20.
- **Date:** third to early fourth century CE.
- **Tier:** **[CA/LE]**.
- **Function in the sources:** ridicule of Roman divinization and divine naming.

This version differs materially from Pliny:

- Pliny: the name refers to ritual purification.
- Lactantius: the name derives from the sewer in which the image was found.
- Pliny: Venusian images occupy a site of reconciliation.
- Lactantius: an unidentified image is named after its discovery site.
- Pliny’s account is a Roman antiquarian aetiology; Lactantius uses the story as anti-pagan polemic.

The versions should not be silently combined into a single sequence in which Tatius found the image, established it, and then conducted Pliny’s purification. Ancient texts do not preserve that harmonized narrative.

### C. Verginia’s death beside the shrine

Livy narrates that Appius Claudius manipulated a legal proceeding to obtain Verginia. Her father Verginius took her aside near Cloacina’s shrine and killed her with a butcher’s knife, declaring death preferable to enslavement or sexual dishonor. The act helped ignite the movement against the decemvirs.

- **Source:** Livy 3.44–58, especially 3.48.5.
- **Composition:** late first century BCE.
- **Narrated date:** traditionally 449 BCE.
- **Tier:** **[LE]**.
- **Cloacina’s role:** purely locative. The goddess does not appear as an agent.

Later interpretation sees irony or thematic resonance in a violent killing near a shrine associated with Venus, marriage, purification, and civic reconciliation. That is literary analysis **[MSR]**, not an explicit Livian theology.

### D. The shrine and late-Republican reconciliation

The 42 BCE coin places Concordia on the obverse and Cloacina’s shrine on the reverse during the civil-war era.

- **Evidence:** contemporary coin.
- **Interpretation:** an appeal to concord, purification from civil bloodshed, reconciliation, or triumviral settlement.
- **Tier:** image and pairing **[EA]**; precise political program **[MSR]**.

It is unsafe to state as fact that Octavian personally commissioned the type to proclaim himself purifier of Caesar’s murder. The issuer was the moneyer L. Mussidius Longus, and the exact authorized political message is not explained by an accompanying text.

## 6. Cult, Temples, and Ritual Practice

### Cult center

Only one secure cult center is known: the small shrine in the Roman Forum, before the later Basilica Aemilia and near the *tabernae novae*. The building apparently stood near or above a significant stretch or access point of the Forum drainage system.

The literary coordinates agree:

- Plautus: a recognizable Forum landmark.
- Livy: near the *tabernae novae*.
- Coins: small circular railed structure.
- Archaeology: circular foundation before the Basilica Aemilia.

### Foundation and reconstruction

- **Regal foundation by Titus Tatius:** **[LE]**, recorded only much later.
- **Existence by early second century BCE:** **[EA]**, Plautus.
- **Late-Republican appearance:** **[EA]**, 42 BCE coins.
- **Imperial-period statues:** **[EA]**, Pliny.
- **Excavated masonry:** belongs to later architectural phases and cannot by itself date the cult’s origin.

The shrine was an open *sacellum* or platform rather than a conventional roofed temple with a large cella.

### Rituals

#### Purification with myrtle

Pliny is the only clear source for a ritual connected to Cloacina:

- weapons were laid down;
- Romans and Sabines were purified;
- myrtle sprigs were used;
- the rite marked reconciliation after threatened violence.

Whether this was a repeatedly performed cult rite or a once-only legendary precedent is unknown. Pliny presents it as tradition, not as an annual ceremony he observed.

#### Sacrifice and offerings

**[NEG]** No securely attested sacrificial animal, food offering, libation, votive formula, or prescribed prayer survives. No ancient evidence supports claims of human sacrifice, temple prostitution, ritual defecation, sewage offerings, or sexual initiation at the shrine.

Verginia’s killing nearby was homicide within Livy’s political narrative, not a sacrifice to Cloacina.

### Priesthood

**[NEG]** No priest, priestess, collegium, or flamen of Cloacina is securely documented. The shrine may have been maintained through broader civic religious administration, but the responsible office is unknown.

### Festival and calendar

**[NEG]** No securely identified date in the surviving Roman *fasti* is labeled as Cloacina’s festival. Modern observance dates assigned to her are reconstructive or invented.

### Divination, magic, and healing

**[NEG]** No ancient oracle, dream incubation, curse tablet, magical papyrus, amulet, healing testimony, or divinatory procedure invokes Cloacina.

### Economics and politics

The shrine occupied prime civic space but was physically tiny. Its appearance on coinage proves that its image could carry public political meaning disproportionate to its scale. There is no evidence for extensive temple estates, treasuries, pilgrimage income, or a major votive economy.

## 7. Historical Evolution of the Cult and Figure

### Possible archaic background: before the second century BCE

The Forum basin was originally wet and crossed by watercourses that were progressively drained and canalized. A localized divine power connected with moving or drained water is possible. The shrine may have marked a hydrological boundary, crossing, or outlet.

This is **[MSR]**. Neither archaeology nor an archaic inscription supplies the goddess’s name in the regal period.

The association with Titus Tatius could preserve a belief that the cult was Sabine or that it commemorated the political incorporation of Sabines. It may equally be an aetiology constructed from the reconciliation legend.

### Early second century BCE: first secure attestation

Plautus shows that the shrine was already a familiar landmark. Nothing indicates that Cloacina was then a major state goddess. Her cult was local, physically small, and topographically distinctive.

### First century BCE: Venus identification and civic symbolism

By 42 BCE the shrine could be deployed on state coinage. The inscription and paired statues show its recognized public identity. The Concordia obverse favors an interpretation centered on civic union after conflict.

This is the clearest period of public visibility in the surviving evidence, though “peak of worship” would overstate what one coin issue can prove.

### First century CE: antiquarian stabilization

Livy embeds the shrine in the Verginia narrative; Pliny records its statues and the Roman-Sabine purification. By this point the following complex was established:

- Cloacina/Cluacina;
- Venus;
- myrtle;
- purification;
- Roman-Sabine reconciliation;
- the Forum monument.

### Third–fifth centuries CE: Christian polemic

Cyprian or the author writing under his name, Tertullian, Lactantius, Prudentius, and Augustine recast Cloacina as evidence of the supposed absurdity or material baseness of Roman religion. Their references are historically valuable because they preserve names and aetiologies, but their purpose is refutation.

This polemical reception helped reduce Cloacina to “the sewer goddess,” often severed from Pliny’s purification and concord themes.

### Suppression and disappearance

No decree or recorded act specifically closes Cloacina’s shrine. The cult’s end must be situated in the broader late-antique decline and prohibition of public sacrifice and state-supported traditional cult, especially in the fourth and early fifth centuries CE. A Cloacina-specific destruction, forced conversion, martyr narrative, or church replacement is not documented. **[NEG]**

The physical monument disappeared into the accumulated and rebuilt Forum landscape. Its exact ancient end date is unknown.

### Medieval to early-modern survival

There is no demonstrated continuous folk worship. Cloacina survived textually through Latin authors, manuscripts, dictionaries, antiquarian works, and the word *cloaca*. Medieval or Renaissance occult manuals do not supply a demonstrable Cloacina tradition.

### Archaeological rediscovery

Excavation in 1899–1901 uncovered the circular remains in front of the Basilica Aemilia. Their plan and location matched the coins and literary testimony, allowing the monument to be identified securely. [Platner and Ashby’s topographical account summarizes the identification and earlier sources.](https://penelope.uchicago.edu/Thayer/E/Gazetteer/Places/Europe/Italy/Lazio/Roma/Rome/_Texts/PLATOP%2A/Sacrum_Cloacinae.html)

### Present

The site is an archaeological feature of the Roman Forum. Individual modern polytheists sometimes address Cloacina in household or environmental practice, but no reliable demographic evidence establishes an organized, continuous, or large-scale cult. Such practice is revival, not survival.

## 8. Modern Reception and Reinvention

### Antiquarian and lexicographical reception

Early-modern and nineteenth-century dictionaries systematized the scattered texts into a compact identity: Cloacina as a Roman goddess of the sewer, later united with Venus. This remains the standard popular profile. Lewis and Short use **Cluacina**, derive it from *cluo*, and gloss it as “the purifier,” while listing Plautus, Pliny, Livy, Servius, Prudentius, and Lactantius. [The dictionary entry preserves this source list.](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn%3Acite2%3Ascaife-viewer%3Adictionary-entries.atlas_v1%3Alat.ls.perseus-eng2-n8657/)

### Jonathan Swift

Swift uses “Cloacine/Cloacina” as a mock-classical goddess of the privy and excrement. In poems concerning the urban body and sanitation, she becomes a comic divine patron of defecation. This is literary satire, not transmitted Roman ritual. [The digitized edition includes the line “Here gentle goddess Cloacine.”](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3AThe_Works_of_the_Rev._Jonathan_Swift%2C_Volume_8.djvu/56)

### Alexander Pope, *The Dunciad*

In Book II, the bookseller Edmund Curll invokes Cloacina during a mock-heroic contest amid Fleet Ditch’s filth. Pope makes her a grotesque muse and helper of obscene publishing. The passage materially shaped the English image of Cloacina as a comic excrement goddess. [The Grub Street Project explains the Fleet Ditch setting and Cloacina’s relation to Curll.](https://www.grubstreetproject.net/essays/fleet-ditch/)

This is **[MI]**: Pope creatively transformed an ancient local goddess into a personification of filthy literature and urban sewage.

### “Temple of Cloacina” as euphemism

From the eighteenth century onward, “temple of Cloacina” became a mock-learned euphemism for a privy or toilet. It belongs to English classical satire, not Roman cult terminology.

### The attributed “Prayer to Cloacina”

A frequently circulated rhyme begins:

> “O Cloacina, Goddess of this place…”

and asks that bodily offerings be neither too swift nor too slow. It is often attributed to Byron. A secure Byronic source is difficult to establish, and the attribution should be treated as **[MI/disputed attribution]**, not quoted as authenticated Byron without an edition and textual history.

### Occultism and ceremonial magic

**[NEG]** The searched historical record does not show a significant Cloacina tradition in Renaissance Hermetism, the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, canonical grimoires, or established ceremonial-magic lineages. Modern web pages may assign her correspondences, offerings, colors, or ritual functions, but these are contemporary constructions unless individually traced.

### Neopagan and reconstructionist practice

Contemporary Roman polytheists and eclectic pagans occasionally invoke Cloacina for:

- plumbing and household sanitation;
- wastewater treatment;
- ecological purification;
- release of emotional “toxicity”;
- bathroom or sewer workers;
- urban infrastructure.

These extensions are **[MI]** or modern theological developments. They draw intelligibly on the Cloaca and purification traditions, but ancient evidence does not document them. An individual’s modern worship is evidence for present reception, not for ancient continuity.

### Politics and extremism

**[NEG]** No significant nationalist, fascist, racist, or violent extremist appropriation focused specifically on Cloacina was located. Her principal modern political-literary uses are satire, urban commentary, and environmental or infrastructural symbolism.

### Popular culture and commerce

Cloacina appears intermittently in humorous lists of “strange gods,” plumbing jokes, sanitation branding, fiction, games, and online devotional material. These uses usually reduce her to “Roman goddess of toilets.” That phrase is a modern comic compression: the ancient evidence concerns a Forum shrine, a drain, Venus, myrtle, purification, and civic reconciliation—not the flush toilet.

## 9. Historiography and Scholarly Debate

### Major interpretive traditions

#### Cloacina as sewer divinity

Wissowa and much older scholarship classified Roman gods strongly by narrowly delimited functions and topographical domains. Cloacina accordingly became the *numen* of the Cloaca Maxima.

Strengths:

- supported by the shrine’s location;
- explicit in Lactantius’s aetiology;
- consistent with the name *Cloacina*.

Weaknesses:

- Lactantius is late and polemical;
- the ancient Cloaca was originally part of a drainage and water-management system, not simply a modern fecal sewer;
- this interpretation can obscure Pliny’s purification narrative.

#### Cloacina as “Purifier”

Ancient etymology and scholars emphasizing Roman ritual language read **Cluacina** from *cluere/cloare*, “cleanse.”

Strengths:

- explicitly stated by Pliny and Servius;
- connects myrtle, Venus, disarmament, and reconciliation;
- avoids reducing the cult to waste disposal.

Weaknesses:

- the etymology may itself be antiquarian wordplay;
- it does not by itself explain the shrine’s hydraulic location.

The most responsible conclusion is not to choose one ancient explanation absolutely. Drainage and purification could overlap in Roman religious thought, while different periods may have emphasized different meanings.

#### Original goddess or epithet of Venus?

- Plautus’s **Cloacinae sacrum** may imply an independent Cloacina.
- The later **Venus Cloacina** can be read as absorption into Venus.
- Alternatively, Cloacina may already have been a local title of Venus before the earliest explicit “Venus” wording.

Because Plautus supplies no cult image or theology, the sequence “independent drain goddess, later arbitrarily fused with Venus” remains a reconstruction, albeit a plausible one.

#### Why Venus?

Proposals include:

- myrtle, Venus’s plant;
- purification;
- marriage after the Sabine women’s intervention;
- sexuality and lawful union;
- civic concord generated through intermarriage;
- a pre-existing Venus cult assimilating a local water power.

No ancient source offers a single explicit historical explanation for the fusion.

### Dumézil

Georges Dumézil’s structural interpretations of archaic Roman religion prioritize inherited Indo-European functional patterns, ritual structures, and the transformation of archaic divinities. Cloacina is peripheral to his strongest tri-functional demonstrations. Dumézilian categories do not supply missing primary evidence for her genealogy or ritual.

### Schilling

Robert Schilling’s *La religion romaine de Vénus* gives the Venusian context greater weight, particularly myrtle, purification, sexuality, marriage, and the development of Venus’s Roman cult titles. His work remains central to explaining why an apparently hydraulic divinity could be expressed as Venus Cloacina.

### Latte, Scheid, Beard–North–Price

Later scholarship is generally more cautious about reconstructing an invariant divine “essence.” It emphasizes:

- ritual and civic location;
- historical change;
- the evidentiary difference between Republican monuments and imperial antiquarian explanations;
- the fluidity of Roman divine naming;
- the danger of turning every divine title into a fully autonomous personality.

This approach suits Cloacina especially well: the evidence documents a shrine and changing explanations better than a developed mythology.

### Topographical debate

The identification of the excavated circular base is strong because location, form, and coin imagery converge. More uncertain are:

- the precise ancient phase of the Cloaca directly beneath or beside it;
- whether the shrine originally marked an open brook, drain entrance, boundary, or later sewer access;
- the dates of successive reconstructions;
- whether rebuilding for the Basilica Aemilia displaced or altered it.

### The two statues

Major possibilities include:

1. Venus and Cloacina as separate figures;
2. two images of Venus Cloacina;
3. Roman and Sabine figures symbolizing reconciliation;
4. cult statues of different dates accumulated at the shrine.

The coin and Pliny’s plural *signa* establish multiplicity, not individual identities. Calling the pair “Venus and Cloacina” without qualification converts hypothesis into fact.

### Discredited or unsupported popular claims

- **“Cloacina is securely an Etruscan goddess.”** Unsupported by a named Etruscan inscription or image.
- **“She was the Roman goddess of toilets.”** Anachronistic simplification.
- **“She protected plumbers.”** Modern extrapolation.
- **“Titus Tatius’s foundation is historical fact.”** Late legendary aetiology.
- **“Verginia was sacrificed to Cloacina.”** False; Livy describes a killing nearby, not cult sacrifice.
- **“She had an annual festival recorded in the Roman calendar.”** No secure evidence located.
- **“The two coin statues are certainly Venus and Cloacina.”** Possible but unproved.
- **“The cult continued secretly through the Middle Ages.”** No documentation.
- **“Ancient occult manuals prescribe offerings to her.”** No documentation.
- **“The Byron prayer is securely authentic.”** Attribution requires proof and is often repeated without one.

### State of the evidence

The primary record is exceptionally small:

- one early literary landmark reference;
- a late-Republican coin series;
- Livian topography;
- Pliny’s aetiology;
- late commentaries and Christian polemics;
- a securely identified but scant archaeological foundation.

That scarcity is itself a historical result. Cloacina was not a richly narrated literary goddess; she was a durable local cult-name whose monument acquired several competing explanations.

### Reconstructable historical profile

Cloacina’s documented history begins not in regal Rome but in an early second-century BCE theatrical reference to an already familiar Forum shrine. A coin issue of 42 BCE supplies the clearest material image: a tiny circular sanctuary containing two female statues. Livy confirms its location, while Pliny supplies the central pagan aetiology—Venus Cluacina, myrtle, purification, and Roman-Sabine reconciliation. Several centuries later, Christian writers preserve a competing story in which Titus Tatius found an unidentified image in the Cloaca Maxima and named it after the sewer.

The evidence therefore supports neither a seamless biography nor a single timeless identity. It records a sequence:

1. a locally named shrine;
2. identification with Venus;
3. deployment as an image of civic concord or purification;
4. antiquarian explanations based on both *cluere* and *cloaca*;
5. Christian reduction to an example of allegedly ridiculous pagan specialization;
6. early-modern satirical rebirth as the goddess of privies and literary filth;
7. archaeological rediscovery and limited modern devotional reinvention.

No surviving evidence establishes genealogy, an annual festival, dedicated clergy, sacrifices, human sacrifice, temple prostitution, a magical tradition, an Etruscan counterpart, or continuous worship. Those absences are not defects to be filled imaginatively: they define the actual limits of Cloacina’s record.
