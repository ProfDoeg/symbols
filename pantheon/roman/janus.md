# Janus: Research Dossier

**Class:** Roman god (*deus*), specifically a god of beginnings, entrances, exits, passages, ritual commencement, and—in developed Roman theology—time’s transitions. He is not securely attested as a primordial creator in the earliest evidence; that cosmic status is principally late-Republican and Augustan systematization.

**Earliest secure attestation:** the earliest surviving, independently datable material representations conventionally identified as Janus are Roman Republican cast-bronze *asses* showing a laureate, bearded double head, securely catalogued around 225–217 BCE. An older Janus cult is probable: C. Duilius reportedly built or dedicated a temple after his naval victory at Mylae in 260 BCE, and the archaic *Carmen Saliare* addresses Janus as *divom deus*, “god of gods.” But the Duilius notice survives only in Tacitus, writing in the early second century CE, while the Salian words survive only as fragments quoted by much later authors. The hymn is archaic in language and ritual setting, but no archaic physical manuscript survives. The coin is therefore the earliest secure extant object; the hymn and temple notice are evidence for an earlier cult mediated by later transmission. A British Museum specimen, RRC 35/1, is dated 225–217 BCE and bears Janus/prow types.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Core identity

[**Secure Roman cultic structure; attested by the first century BCE**] Janus is the divine guarantor of passage from one state, place, or period into another. Latin ritual consequently placed him at the beginning of sacrificial formulas. Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.67, says that beginnings and endings are of greatest consequence and that sacrifices therefore begin with Janus. Varro’s lost *Antiquitates rerum divinarum*, reconstructed chiefly from Augustine, likewise placed Janus first among the “select gods.”

This primacy did not mean that Janus was the Roman equivalent of Zeus/Jupiter. His priority was procedural and liminal: access must be opened before the intended recipient can be approached. Ovid formulates this memorably in *Fasti* 1.125–126: even Jupiter’s coming and going operates through Janus’s office.

[**Augustan literary theology**] Ovid presents Janus as the double-faced god who sees forward and backward and opens or closes the universe, the year, peace, and war. This is the fullest surviving ancient self-description, but it is a poetic interview composed around the turn of the era, not a transcript of archaic doctrine.

### Name and spellings

- **Classical Latin:** *Iānus*. Latin spelling used *I*, not modern *J*.
- **Archaic form:** *Ian* may lie behind the obscure Salian text, but reconstruction of the fragment is disputed.
- **Greek transcription:** Ἰανός, occurring mainly in Greek-language accounts of Roman religion.
- **English and French:** Janus.
- **Italian and Spanish:** Giano; Jano.
- **German:** Janus.

### Etymology

1. **Movement/passage derivation** — [**Ancient etymology; broadly supported in modern linguistics**]. Cicero derives *Ianus* from *ire*, “to go,” connecting it with *iani*, covered passages or archways, and *ianuae*, doors. Ovid makes Janus derive his name from his function in regulating divine “goings and returnings.” Robert Schilling argued from the Indo-European root *ei-*, “go,” through an enlarged Italic stem, understanding *ianus* as an abstraction of passing. This remains more persuasive than many solar or Etruscan derivations.

2. **Door derivation** — [**Ancient popular etymology; direction of derivation disputed**]. It was natural to connect *Ianus* with *ianua*, “door.” The historical problem is whether the god’s name produced the common noun, the common noun produced the divine name, or both belong to a passage/movement root. Cicero explicitly makes *iani* and *ianuae* derivative from “going”; Ovid rhetorically reverses or blurs the relationship.

3. **Dianus/Diana theory** — [**Late-Republican learned systematization; not established**]. Nigidius Figulus, reported by Macrobius, connected *Ianus* with a hypothetical *Dianus* and paired him with Diana/Iana, sometimes interpreting them as sun and moon. Phonological proposals involving *Divianus > Dianus > Ianus* have had supporters, but the divine pairing is not an early securely documented cult pair.

4. **Etruscan origin** — [**Modern scholarly reconstruction; disputed**]. Etruscan Culśanś is a youthful, often two-faced deity associated with gates; Culsu is a female underworld gatekeeper. Some scholars, including Larissa Bonfante, have treated Culśanś as evidence for an Etruscan source or close analogue. Dumézil and others instead regarded Janus as Latin/Italic and allowed borrowing or convergence in the opposite direction. Similar iconography proves contact or interpretive assimilation, not which community originated Janus. Roman Janus/prow coinage is earlier than some of the cited Etruscan bifrontal representations.

5. **Greek derivations and alleged Greek origin** — [**Late antiquarian legend; historically unverified**]. Some late sources make Janus an immigrant from Greece, sometimes from Perrhaebia. No linguistic or archaeological evidence securely establishes this, and Ovid explicitly says that Greece has no deity corresponding to Janus.

### Documented titles and epithets

The principal list comes from Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 1.9.15–16, with parallels in Servius, John Lydus, late lexicography, and fragments of ritual-antiquarian literature.

- **Ianus Pater**, “Father Janus” — [**Cultic/ritual title**]. Common in prayer language and provincial dedications.
- **Divom deus**, “god of gods” — [**Archaic formula transmitted late**]. Salian hymn fragment. It expresses precedence or exaltation, but does not by itself prove that Janus once ruled Rome’s pantheon as a sovereign sky-god.
- **Geminus**, “twin/double” — attached both to the two-faced god and to the gated passage called *Ianus Geminus*.
- **Bifrons/Biceps**, “two-fronted/two-headed” — chiefly literary-iconographic descriptions.
- **Quadrifrons**, “four-fronted” — four-faced form associated especially with a Faliscan statue brought to Rome and with four-way passage architecture. The surviving late-antique “Arch of Janus” in the Forum Boarium is a *quadrifrons* arch but is not securely a temple of Janus; its modern name reflects architectural form.
- **Patulcius/Patulcius**, “the opener” — from *patere*, “stand open.”
- **Clusius/Clusivius**, “the closer” — from *claudere*, “close.”
- **Consivius**, “sower/begetter” — Macrobius explains it through *conserere*, human propagation. This belongs to learned theology but may preserve a genuine cult title.
- **Iunonius**, “Junonian” or “associated with Juno” — explained from Janus’s guardianship of the entrance of every month, while Juno received the Kalends.
- **Quirinus** — used of the Janus whose gates represented war and peace. Ancient explanations relate it to Quirinus/Romulus, the Sabine *curis* (“spear”), or the citizen body; its exact cultic force is disputed.
- **Matutinus Pater**, “Morning Father” — Horace, *Satires* 2.6.20, invokes him at the opening of the day’s business. It need not make Janus a solar god.
- **Portae custos / liminum custos**, “guardian of gates/thresholds” — descriptive rather than necessarily formal cult titles.
- **Caelus, mundus, chaos** — [**Antiquarian philosophical equations**]. Varro reportedly identified Janus with the world; Ovid’s Janus says the ancients called him Chaos; other authors make him heaven. These are competing learned interpretations, not interchangeable early dogmas.
- **Apollo/Sol** — [**Syncretistic or learned identification**]. Nigidius and Macrobius transmitted solar interpretations. They are not evidence that ordinary Republican worshippers treated Janus simply as Apollo.
- **Culśanś** — [**Modern comparative identification based on ancient Italic contact**]. Comparable Etruscan gate deity, not securely a translation-equivalent.

### No Greek equivalent

[**Explicit Augustan evidence**] Ovid asks in *Fasti* 1.89–90 what god Janus is, because “Greece has no divinity like you.” Romans could explain him using Greek cosmology, identify him with Chaos, or assimilate his imagery to Hermes or Apollo, but no stable *interpretatio Graeca* made him equivalent to one Greek god. “Roman Hermes” and “Roman Chronos” are therefore misleading if stated as identities.

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### Evidence hierarchy

1. **Archaic composition, late transmission: the *Carmen Saliare*.**  
   The hymn of the Salii was written in archaic Saturnian Latin and had become difficult for Romans themselves to understand. A fragment gives Janus the title *divom deus*. Its ritual and linguistic archaism make early composition likely, conventionally in the regal or early Republican religious stratum, but the surviving words are quotations and manuscript copies of later authors. Exact reconstruction remains contested.

2. **Reported temple of 260 BCE.**  
   Tacitus, *Annals* 2.49, says that C. Duilius erected a temple of Janus near the Forum Holitorium after becoming the first Roman commander to win a naval victory. Tacitus wrote roughly 370 years after Mylae, but his account probably used annalistic and monumental records. Tiberius dedicated or restored the temple in 17 CE. Its exact position among the Republican temples beside the vegetable market remains debated.

3. **Material image, 225–217 BCE.**  
   Janus’s bearded double head appears on the Roman bronze *as*, conventionally paired with a ship’s prow. The British Museum specimen 1867,0212.70 is securely catalogued to 225–217 BCE. The Janus/prow design became one of Rome’s most persistent monetary types.

4. **First-century BCE texts.**  
   Varro, Cicero, Virgil, Horace, and Augustan antiquarianism provide the earliest substantial literary discussions, but they already interpret an older and partly obscure cult.

### Primary corpus

- **Varro, *De lingua Latina* 5 and 7** — Latin, 40s BCE; surviving directly but damaged. Preserves religious vocabulary and Salian material.
- **Varro, *Antiquitates rerum divinarum*** — Latin, 40s BCE; lost. Fragments and summaries survive especially in Augustine, *City of God* 6–7, and in Christian apologists. Varro ranked Janus among the select gods and variously explained him as the world, heaven, or a god of beginnings. Because Augustine is an adversarial witness, one must separate Varro’s reported positions from Augustine’s ridicule.
- **Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.67** — Latin, 45 BCE. Gives the “going/passage” etymology and Janus’s precedence in sacrifice. The speaker is the Stoic Balbus; the dialogue does not simply declare Cicero’s own creed.
- **Virgil, *Aeneid* 7.601–622; 8.357–358; 12.198** — Latin, composed approximately 29–19 BCE. Book 7 describes the solemn opening of war’s gates; Juno herself opens them when Latinus refuses. Book 8 points to Janiculum and Saturnia as ruined settlements associated with Janus and Saturn.
- **Horace, *Satires* 2.6.20–23; *Epistles* 2.1.255** — Latin, later first century BCE. “Morning Father Janus” begins human work; peace is imagined as enclosed when the gates are shut.
- **Livy, *Ab urbe condita* 1.19.1–3** — Latin, late first century BCE. Numa builds or establishes the Janus at the bottom of the Argiletum as an index of peace and war; Livy says it had been closed only under Numa and after the First Punic War before Augustus. This is a foundation narrative written centuries after the supposed regal event.
- **Augustus, *Res Gestae* 13** — Latin and Greek monumental inscription, composed by 14 CE and preserved notably at Ankara. Augustus states that the Janus Quirinus, which ancestors wished shut when peace had been achieved throughout Roman dominion, had been closed only twice before his birth but three times under his principate. This is first-person imperial political testimony, not a neutral chronicle.
- **Ovid, *Fasti* 1.63–288, 317–336; 6.101–130** — Latin, composed around 2–8 CE and revised in exile. Book 1 stages the great interview with Janus and relates his cosmic nature, two faces, key and staff, first offerings, January, money, Saturn, Janiculum, hot spring, and gates. Book 6 narrates his pursuit and sexual coercion of Cranaë/Carna and his gift of hinge power.
- **Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 14.778–804** — Latin, completed by about 8 CE. In the Sabine attack Janus releases scorching sulphurous water through the gate, saving Rome.
- **Tacitus, *Annals* 2.49** — Latin, early second century CE. Records Tiberius’s temple dedications/restorations, including the Forum Holitorium Janus attributed to Duilius.
- **Suetonius, *Augustus* 22** — Latin, early second century CE. States that Augustus closed Janus Quirinus three times, after only two earlier closings since Rome’s foundation.
- **Plutarch, *Numa* 19–20; *Roman Questions*** — Greek, late first/early second century CE. Discusses the gates and offers several speculative explanations of Janus, including culture-bringer and immigrant king.
- **Festus, *De verborum significatu*** — Latin, probably second century CE, surviving in an incomplete medieval manuscript and Paul the Deacon’s eighth-century epitome. Preserves glosses on Janus, *iani*, ritual vocabulary, and the disputed temple meeting concerning the Fabii.
- **Arnobius, *Adversus nationes* 3.29 and related passages** — Latin Christian polemic, approximately 300 CE. Lists contradictory pagan genealogies and reports Janus as father of Fontus by Juturna. Valuable as a hostile compiler, not an unfiltered cult record.
- **Servius, commentary on *Aeneid* 7.607–610 and 8.357** — Latin, fourth/early fifth century CE, with later expanded material. Preserves titles, explanations of the gates, ritual dress, the Faliscan four-faced statue, and king-Janus traditions.
- **Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 1.7–9, 1.13–15** — Latin, early fifth century CE. The fullest late-antique antiquarian synthesis: Janus and Saturn as early kings, the coin type, epithets, cosmic and solar interpretations, monthly rites.
- **Augustine, *City of God* 7.7–10, 7.28** — Latin, early fifth century CE. Quotes or paraphrases Varro, asking why Janus and Terminus are separate if Janus is the world and why Jupiter remains sovereign. His hostile purpose is explicit, but the lost Varronian material makes him indispensable.
- **Procopius, *Gothic War* 1.25.18–25** — Greek, mid-sixth century CE. Describes the bronze Janus shrine and ancient statue during the Gothic siege of 536 CE and tells how Romans secretly tried to open the gates. This establishes survival of the monument and custom-memory, not continuing public pagan worship.
- **John Lydus, *De mensibus*** — Greek, sixth century CE. Preserves late antiquarian calendrical and theological explanations.
- **Roman calendars:** *Fasti Praenestini*, *Fasti Antiates*, *Fasti Amiternini*, and related inscriptions document festival dates and temple anniversaries. The January Agonium is calendrically secure; identifying all Agonalia with Janus is not.
- **Epigraphy:** CIL and EDCS contain dedications including *Iano Patri*, *Iano Gemino*, and *Iano Augusto*, especially in Dalmatia, Dacia, Gaul, and North Africa. A striking feature is the comparative scarcity of uncontested private dedications from Italy itself.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Domains

[**Secure cultic core**]

- openings and closures;
- doors, gates, covered passages, and thresholds;
- beginnings of rituals, prayers, months, days, enterprises, and journeys;
- entry into communication with other gods;
- the transition between war and peace.

“God of endings” is defensible only as the other side of closure and completed passage. Roman sources more consistently give *ends* to Terminus. Augustine’s criticism depends precisely on this division: beginnings to Janus, ends to Terminus.

[**Late-Republican/Augustan elaboration**]

- the opening of the year and January;
- cosmic gates and movement through the heavens;
- past and future vision;
- time in a broad sense;
- primordial chaos becoming articulated cosmos;
- origins of agriculture, coinage, and civilized Latium.

[**Modern simplification**] “God of choices,” “duality,” or “all opposites” extrapolates from his two faces. These may be useful modern symbolism but are not formal ancient cult portfolios.

### Form

The canonical form is a mature, bearded male head with two faces turned in opposite directions. Republican coinage normally shows the paired profiles without a visible rear skull. Ovid’s Janus carries:

- a **key** in the left hand: authority to open and close;
- a **staff** in the right: guidance or control of passage.

The quadriform version faces four directions. Servius and Macrobius associate a four-faced statue with Falerii and say it was brought to Rome after the capture of that city. The four faces were interpreted as the four quarters of the world or four seasons, but these are late learned readings.

### Earliest and principal images

- **Janus/prow *aes grave* as, 225–217 BCE:** earliest securely dated extant Janus image. Bearded bifrontal head; prow on reverse.
- **Continuing Republican bronze asses:** the Janus/prow pattern persisted at changing weight standards.
- **Sextus Pompey coinage, approximately 45–36 BCE:** adapts the bifrontal type with features recalling Pompey the Great, using Janus imagery for lineage and political legitimacy.
- **Neronian bronzes, especially 64–66 CE:** depict the *Ianus Geminus* with closed doors and legends such as *PACE P R VBIQ PARTA IANVM CLVSIT*—“peace having been secured for the Roman people everywhere, he closed Janus.” These are the clearest surviving ancient pictures of the shrine, but their architectural precision is debated.
- **Hadrianic and later imperial issues:** reuse Janus, temple, and transition imagery.
- **Commodus medallions:** assimilate the emperor’s bifrontal portrait to Janus, making imperial command span temporal and cosmic order.
- **Provincial relief from the Alburnus Maior/Dalmatian-Dacian environment:** a youthful two-headed *Ianus Geminus* may reflect interpretatio Romana of a local deity; inscriptions must be read as provincial syncretism, not as pristine Roman iconography.

### Associations often overstated

- **Ship/prow:** extremely well attested numismatically. Ovid explains the ship by Saturn’s arrival in Italy; this is an aetiological story, not proof that Janus began as a sea god.
- **Coins/commerce:** Janus appears on the *as*, and Ovid mentions coin imagery. This does not make him straightforwardly the Roman god of money.
- **Number two:** inferred from two faces, not documented as a sacred number in cult calendars.
- **Colors, crystals, zodiac signs:** no stable ancient system is attested. Lists assigning black/white, labradorite, garnet, Capricorn, or particular tarot cards are modern inventions.
- **Snakes, wolves, ravens:** not securely established as Janus’s sacred animals.
- **January 1:** closely associated with him in Ovid and Roman New Year practice, but his principal named festival was January 9.

## 4. Genealogy and relations to other divine beings

Janus has no single canonical genealogy. The early cult evidence does not name parents. Genealogies occur mainly in antiquarian, poetic, and polemical sources.

### Origin versions

- **Self-existent primordial/cosmic Janus** — [**Augustan literary theology**]. In *Fasti* 1 Janus identifies himself with ancient Chaos before the elements separated. No parents are supplied.
- **The world, heaven, or earth** — [**Varronian learned systematization**, preserved by Augustine]. These are philosophical explanations, not genealogical births.
- **Mortal king of Latium** — [**Euhemeristic antiquarian tradition**]. Janus rules Janiculum and civilizes early Italy, sometimes jointly with Saturn.
- **Greek immigrant** — [**Late regional/antiquarian variant**]. Some sources give him a Greek origin. Ovid’s insistence that Greece has no Janus stands against this.
- **Child of Apollo/Creusa or other Hellenizing pedigrees** — [**Late mythographic accretion**]. Such genealogies lack early Roman cult support.

### Consorts and offspring

- **Camese/Camesene** — [**Late antiquarian variant**]. Macrobius reports that Janus shared rule with Camese; some later genealogies turn Camese into spouse or sibling. The exact relationship varies.
- **Juturna** — [**Late reported genealogy**]. Arnobius reports Janus as father, by the fountain goddess Juturna, of Fontus/Fons.
- **Venilia** — [**Late antiquarian genealogy**]. Some Servian material makes Janus and Venilia parents of Canens; other traditions attach Venilia to Neptune or Daunus. This is not a universally Roman family tree.
- **Carna/Cranaë** — not a wife in Ovid. Janus sexually overcomes her and compensates or rewards her with control of hinges.
- **Fontus/Fons** — son of Janus and Juturna in Arnobius and later compilations; a fountain deity with a festival, the Fontinalia.
- **Canens** — in one genealogy, daughter of Janus and Venilia; Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* instead emphasizes her as the wife of Picus without foregrounding Janus as father.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

The following list distinguishes an actual scene or reported relationship from mere membership in the same pantheon.

- **Saturn — hospitality and joint rule:** Janus receives the fugitive Saturn in Latium; Saturn teaches agriculture; the two reign in concord. Ovid, *Fasti* 1.233–240; Virgil, *Aeneid* 8.357–358; Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 1.7. [**Augustan narrative with late-antique elaboration**]
- **Saturn — coin explanation:** the ship on early bronze records Saturn’s arrival, while Janus’s head occupies the other face. Ovid, *Fasti* 1.229–240; Macrobius 1.7. [**Literary aetiology**]
- **Jupiter — Janus controls divine access:** even Jupiter goes and returns through Janus’s office. Ovid, *Fasti* 1.125–126. [**Augustan literary theology**]
- **Jupiter — precedence without sovereignty:** Cicero makes Janus leader at sacrifices while Jupiter remains chief divine ruler; Augustine, arguing against Varro, asks how both can be cosmic gods. Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.67; Augustine, *City of God* 7.9. [**Late-Republican ritual explanation / Christian polemical analysis**]
- **Juno — coordinated Kalends rites:** the *rex sacrorum* and minor pontiff sacrifice to Janus while the *regina sacrorum* sacrifices to Juno; Macrobius 1.15.9, 19. [**Cultic system, late description**]
- **Juno — opens the war gates:** Latinus refuses to open the gates; Juno descends and forces them open. Janus himself is named as guardian in the surrounding description but does not personally confront her. Virgil, *Aeneid* 7.601–622. [**Augustan epic; indirect interaction at Janus’s institution**]
- **Vesta — framing sacrificial order:** Roman prayer convention invoked Janus first and Vesta last, opening and completing access to the gods. Cicero and later antiquarian testimony; Ovid, *Fasti* 6, treats Vesta as the power of the hearth’s center. [**Ritual structural relation; no shared mythic episode securely attested**]
- **Terminus — divided control of beginnings and ends:** Varro assigns beginnings to Janus and ends to Terminus; Augustine attacks the division. Augustine, *City of God* 7.7. [**Varronian learned/civic theology preserved by a hostile witness**]
- **Carna/Cranaë — pursuit, sexual coercion, and grant of power:** Janus’s two faces defeat the nymph’s trick of sending suitors ahead and doubling back; after taking her, he gives her authority over hinges and a white-thorn branch. Ovid, *Fasti* 6.101–130. [**Augustan poetic narrative; earliest extant source**]
- **Juturna — sexual/genealogical union:** Janus fathers Fontus with Juturna. Arnobius, *Adversus nationes* 3.29. [**Late testimony, hostile Christian witness**]
- **Fontus/Fons — paternity:** Janus is Fontus’s father. Arnobius 3.29; later antiquarians. [**Late reported genealogy**]
- **Venilia — consort or co-parent in one tradition:** Janus and Venilia produce Canens. Servian/late mythographic tradition. [**Regional or late antiquarian variant**]
- **Canens — paternity:** Janus is named as Canens’s father in the foregoing variant. [**Late variant; absent from the principal early cult record**]
- **Camese/Camesene — shared sovereignty:** Janus and Camese rule together; relationship variously interpreted as colleague, sibling, or spouse. Macrobius 1.7. [**Late antiquarian preservation**]
- **Apollo — learned identification:** Nigidius, reported by Macrobius, associates Janus with Apollo/Sol and Diana with Iana/Luna. No narrative meeting occurs. [**Syncretistic learned systematization**]
- **Diana/Iana — paired linguistic-theological construction:** see Apollo above. [**Learned identification, not secure early cult**]
- **Culśanś — comparative equation:** modern scholarship compares or historically identifies the Etruscan gate god with Janus. There is no surviving ancient narrative in which the two meet. [**Modern reconstruction from iconography and functions**]
- **Mars — institutional proximity but no documented mythic encounter:** the Salii of Mars sang the hymn invoking Janus, and war passed through Janus’s gates. No surviving early story has Janus and Mars meet. [**Absence of a narrative interaction**]
- **Bellona — shared war-opening setting but no direct Janus episode:** Virgil includes war powers around the gate ceremony; no secure personal encounter is recorded. [**Absence of evidence**]

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Janus before the cosmos

[**Earliest extant version: Ovid, approximately 2–8 CE**]

In *Fasti* 1.89–144 Janus appears physically to Ovid and answers the poet’s questions. He says the ancients called him Chaos. When air, fire, water, and earth separated, his once-formless substance assumed divine shape. His two faces preserve his ability to see what lies before and behind. He watches the universe’s two gates and regulates access even for Jupiter.

This is not demonstrably an archaic Roman creation myth. Its vocabulary draws on Greek cosmological *Chaos*, Roman door theology, philosophical elemental theory, and Augustan calendrical poetics. It is best classified as an Augustan synthesis built around an older cultic god.

### Janus and Saturn’s Golden Age

[**Augustan canonical literary form; later elaboration**]

Saturn, displaced from heaven, arrives by ship in Latium. Janus receives him and shares his kingdom. Saturn teaches agriculture, and the age is remembered as peaceful and prosperous. Janus rules from Janiculum; Saturn’s settlement is Saturnia. The ship on Roman coinage commemorates Saturn’s arrival, while Janus’s double head appears on the other side.

Sources differ:

- Virgil merely points to the ancient settlements of Janus and Saturn.
- Ovid integrates hospitality, agriculture, kingship, and coin iconography.
- Macrobius expands the account and then allegorizes it, warning that fables conceal religious truth.

The narrative rationalizes place-names, early kingship, farming, and money. Nothing in the surviving third-century BCE coins themselves identifies the prow as Saturn’s ship.

### The Sabine attack and the boiling spring

[**Augustan poetic aetiology**]

During the war following the rape of the Sabine women, Tarpeia or another betrayer admits the Sabines toward Rome. Janus saves the city by causing a torrent of hot, sulphurous water to erupt from the gate, driving the attackers back. Ovid tells the fullest version in *Metamorphoses* 14.778–804 and alludes to it in *Fasti* 1.261–276.

The story explains the Porta Janualis and the practice of keeping Janus’s gate open in war: once Janus’s active aid had saved the city through an open passage, the passage remained open when Rome needed martial assistance. Livy’s account of the Sabine war does not make Janus a protagonist, demonstrating that this was not the only version.

### Numa and the gates of peace

[**Roman foundation legend, first extant in late Republican/Augustan sources**]

Livy says Numa established the Janus at the bottom of the Argiletum as an index of peace and war. Open gates meant Rome was under arms; closed gates meant peace had been achieved among surrounding peoples. Numa’s peaceful reign allowed closure. Livy says the second closure occurred after the First Punic War, in the consulship of T. Manlius, conventionally 235 BCE.

The institution is regularly called a “temple,” but the *Ianus Geminus* was more nearly a small gated passage or shrine containing a statue. Its traditional foundation by Numa cannot be independently verified.

### Janus and Carna

[**Ovidian narrative; approximately 8 CE**]

Cranaë is a huntress-like nymph who avoids suitors by agreeing to enter a cave and then hiding after they pass ahead of her. Janus desires her. Because he can see behind himself, her stratagem fails. Ovid states that he embraces or possesses her; in modern terms the episode is sexual coercion, not a consensual courtship. He then gives her control over hinges and a white-thorn branch capable of excluding harmful beings. Ovid connects Cranaë with Carna, a goddess whose older domain may instead have involved bodily vitality or flesh.

The story may be Ovid’s playful or violent conflation of similarly named powers: *Carna*, *Cardea* (“hinge goddess”), and Cranaë. It should not be projected uncritically into archaic religion.

### Janus as mortal king

[**Euhemeristic antiquarian tradition**]

Janus is said to have reigned in Latium before or alongside Saturn, welcomed immigrants, introduced social order or money, and founded Janiculum. Some versions make him native; others Greek. The king narrative made cult, geography, and technological beginnings intelligible as achievements of a deified ruler. It is not independent evidence for a historical King Janus.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### The *Ianus Geminus* or *Ianus Quirinus*

The most famous shrine stood near the Argiletum at the northeast side of the Forum, although exact placement remains disputed because no universally accepted archaeological foundation survives.

Livy attributes it to Numa. Procopius describes a small, wholly bronze, rectangular structure just large enough for a bronze, two-faced statue approximately five cubits high, with gates facing east and west. Neronian coins show a compact structure with arched openings, doors, and latticed side treatment. Literary description and coin image do not agree in every detail, possibly because of rebuilding or artistic compression.

The gate’s ritual meaning is itself contested:

- Livy and the Augustan political formulation treat closure as proof of universal peace.
- Virgil’s gate restrains War and the Furies; opening releases battle.
- Ovid and Horace can picture Peace as shut safely inside.
- Some modern reconstructions suppose an originally military passage through which troops ritually departed and returned. This is plausible but not directly described for the earliest period.

### Recorded closures

- Under **Numa** — legendary.
- **235 BCE**, after the First Punic War — reported by Livy and Plutarch.
- **Augustan first closure**, after Actium and associated settlements, conventionally 29 BCE.
- **Second Augustan closure**, commonly connected with the Cantabrian settlement, approximately 25 BCE.
- **Third Augustan closure** — exact date disputed; ancient and modern proposals include approximately 13 BCE and 1 BCE. Augustus states the total but does not date each closure.
- Later emperors, including Nero and Vespasian, reused closure imagery. Because Rome’s frontiers were rarely wholly at peace, claims of universal peace were political constructions as well as ritual acts.

Augustus’s phrase in *Res Gestae* 13 is especially important: he says the Senate ordered the closure three times during his principate. Suetonius confirms three. Attempts to force every closure into an exact modern war chronology remain disputed.

### Forum Holitorium temple

Tacitus reports a Janus temple erected by C. Duilius after Mylae in 260 BCE and restored or dedicated by Tiberius in 17 CE. Calendars record a dedication day near the Portunalia, August 17, though another calendar tradition gives October 18, perhaps reflecting restoration or a different shrine. The usual identification places it among the three Republican temples incorporated into or near San Nicola in Carcere, but assignment of the individual foundations is debated.

### Other structures and cult sites

- **Janus Quadrifrons shrine:** traditionally founded after the capture of Falerii and containing a four-faced image.
- **Janiculum:** ancient authors connected the hill with Janus’s city. No secure historical Janus temple on the hill confirms the legend.
- **Numerous *iani* in Rome:** passage arches called *iani*, including *Ianus medius* in the Forum’s commercial district. These need not all have been temples.
- **Provincial dedications:** *Iano Patri*, *Iano Gemino*, and *Iano Augusto* are particularly visible along the eastern Adriatic and in mining/military regions. At Alburnus Maior, *Ianus Geminus* may have absorbed a local double or gate deity.
- **“Temple of Janus” at Autun:** the surviving Gallo-Roman tower acquired this name in post-antique tradition; its ancient dedication is unknown. It is not evidence for a Janus cult.

### Priesthood

No specialized *flamen Ianualis* or securely documented permanent priesthood of Janus is known. His principal officiant was the **rex sacrorum**, the republican successor to royal religious functions. This is significant: a god without his own flamen could nevertheless receive rites from the highest-ranking priest in the traditional hierarchy.

### Ritual precedence

Janus was invoked first in prayers and sacrifices, enabling approach to the other gods. Vesta was often placed last, enclosing the ritual through hearth and completion. This should be understood as syntax of ritual access, not necessarily a ranking of divine power.

### Kalends

Macrobius says that at the beginning of the month the *rex sacrorum*, assisted by the minor pontiff, sacrificed to Janus in the Curia Calabra, while the *regina sacrorum* sacrificed to Juno. Janus and Juno therefore governed complementary aspects of calendrical entry.

### January 1

Ovid describes New Year’s greetings, auspicious speech, dates, gifts of figs, honey, and money, and work begun symbolically so that idleness would not omen the whole year. Janus explains that sweet offerings seek a sweet course for the year and coins express hopes for prosperity.

The Julian calendar fixed January 1 as the civil year’s opening, but January’s place at the head of the calendar developed historically. Roman tradition attributed January and February to Numa; historically, January 1 became the regular consular inauguration date in 153 BCE. The month’s name is ancient, but a single act by Numa is not demonstrable.

### Agonium, January 9

Roman calendars mark an Agonium on January 9. Ovid says Janus must be propitiated on the Agonal day and describes the *rex sacrorum* sacrificing a ram. Festus confirms that on Agonal days the king sacrificed a ram in the Regia. Janus is the probable recipient on January 9, but the calendars themselves do not always spell out the recipient, and there were other Agonalia for other gods.

### Offerings

Attested or textually reported offerings include:

- wine;
- incense;
- sacrificial cakes or *ianual* made with grain and salt;
- January sweets such as dates, figs, and honey;
- a ram at the January Agonium;
- ordinary votive altars and images in the provinces.

There is no sound evidence for a Janus mystery cult, temple prostitution, or regular human sacrifice. Early comparative-religion writers sometimes inferred a sacrificed divine king or double-headed “fetish”; those reconstructions are not supported by Roman documentation.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Archaic formation, before the third century BCE

The cult’s age is supported by archaic ritual language, Janus’s place in prayer, association with the *rex sacrorum*, and the obscurity of his theology to later Romans. Yet virtually all explanatory narratives are later. Claims that Janus was once Rome’s supreme sky-god are reconstructions from *divom deus* and later cosmic interpretations, not an established historical stage.

### Middle Republic, third–second centuries BCE

This is the first materially secure period:

- Duilius’s reported temple after 260 BCE;
- the 235 BCE peace closure;
- Janus/prow bronze coinage from the late third century onward;
- increasing use of monumental and monetary imagery during Rome’s Mediterranean expansion.

The paradox is characteristic: the guardian of peace’s closed gates became publicly prominent through a state engaged in continual war.

### Late Republic

Varro and Cicero systematized an inherited cult whose older language was already difficult. Janus became an example in debates about Roman etymology, natural theology, and ritual structure. Coin types were politicized: Sextus Pompey could reshape the double head in memory of Pompey.

### Augustan transformation

This was Janus’s greatest ideological expansion.

- Augustus made three closures proof of world peace.
- Virgil placed the war gates inside Rome’s destined imperial history.
- Ovid turned Janus into the articulate god of the calendar, cosmos, money, peace, and imperial present.
- The *Res Gestae* monumentalized the closures across the empire in Latin and Greek.

Augustan sources did not invent Janus, but they created much of the coherent figure familiar today.

### High Empire

Emperors reused Janus as a sign of peace, restored his temples, and placed shrine images on coinage. Provincial dedications demonstrate geographic extension but sometimes also local syncretism. Janus never developed the large trans-Mediterranean mystery institutions associated with Isis, Mithras, or Jupiter Dolichenus.

### Late antiquity and Christianization

Christian apologists made Janus a favored example of pagan contradiction:

- Tertullian, Arnobius, and Augustine juxtaposed incompatible claims that he was world, heaven, king, or door god.
- Their accounts are hostile but preserve lost pagan learning.
- Imperial restrictions on sacrifice and temple cult in the late fourth century affected Janus as part of public paganism; no known suppression edict or martyr narrative targets his cult alone.

Macrobius still treated Janus as an intellectually rich symbol in the early fifth century. Procopius found the bronze shrine and statue surviving in 536 CE. During the Gothic siege, people allegedly tried secretly to open its doors. That episode demonstrates the tenacity of civic ritual memory; it does not prove an uninterrupted priesthood.

The shrine subsequently disappeared. Its metal was likely removed or reused, but no secure account dates its destruction.

### Medieval transformation

Janus survived primarily in manuscripts, calendars, grammatical glosses, universal histories, and moralized mythology. Medieval writers could treat him as:

- a deified ancient king;
- a symbol of prudence looking to past and future;
- a pagan anticipation of temporal duality;
- an ancestor in fabricated histories.

Annius of Viterbo’s late-fifteenth-century pseudo-historical works identified Janus with Noah and made him foundational to Etruscan and Italian civilization. These claims depended on forged or manipulated “ancient” authorities and are early-modern invention, not recovered Roman tradition.

### Renaissance to Enlightenment

Mythographic handbooks by Giraldi, Natale Conti, and Vincenzo Cartari systematized Janus’s images and allegories. Artists increasingly rendered him as Prudence, Time, Peace/War, or the old/new year. This visual tradition often combines genuinely ancient keys and faces with Renaissance philosophical attributes.

January imagery spread Janus far beyond religious cult. Encyclopedias, emblem books, architecture, and political allegory turned him into a universal symbol of retrospection and foresight.

### Modern revival

Modern Roman reconstructionist religion emerged through several discontinuous currents rather than an unbroken lineage:

- revolutionary and Romantic proposals for revived pagan civic religion;
- nineteenth-century classicism and occult antiquarianism;
- early-twentieth-century Italian traditionalism, including Arturo Reghini and Julius Evola;
- late-twentieth- and twenty-first-century *Religio Romana* or *Cultus Deorum Romanorum* groups.

Modern devotees commonly invoke Janus first, celebrate January 1 and January 9, and reconstruct offerings from Ovid, Macrobius, and Roman ritual studies. These are conscious reconstructions. Reliable membership numbers are unavailable, and no continuous Janus congregation can be traced from antiquity.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### Political language

“Janus-faced” came to mean duplicitous or two-sided. This is a post-classical moralization. Ancient two-facedness principally meant simultaneous vision and gatekeeping, not dishonesty.

Political cartoons regularly give parties, treaties, or rulers opposed Janus faces. “Janus” also names policies that combine incompatible aims. Such use has often displaced the god’s cultic identity.

Janus has occasionally entered Italian nationalist or traditionalist discourse through the claim that Roman religion embodies an indigenous, anti-Christian national spirituality. The documented twentieth-century Roman pagan revival includes strands connected with Reghini and Evola. Janus’s antiquity and lack of a Greek equivalent make him attractive as an emblem of specifically Roman identity. This is modern political appropriation, not evidence of an unbroken secret cult.

### Occultism and esotericism

Janus was never central to the Golden Dawn, Theosophy, or standard Hermetic Qabalah in the manner of Egyptian, Greek, Hebrew, or planetary beings. Modern occult literature nevertheless recruits him for:

- opening and closing rites;
- transition magic;
- New Year workings;
- backward/forward divination;
- guardian-of-the-threshold symbolism;
- pairing with tarot cards, zodiacal signs, colors, stones, or “portals.”

No stable ancient evidence supports most of those correspondences. Claims that Janus was a horned witch-god surviving secretly through medieval Europe derive especially from Margaret Murray-style witch-cult theories and related Frazerian comparison. The historical witch-cult hypothesis is discredited; its Janus/Dianus continuity is modern invention.

Chaos-magic and eclectic grimoire practices sometimes treat Janus as an archetypal “liminal current.” These are legitimate modern religious creations as such, but not ancient liturgies.

### Psychology and philosophy

Jungian and post-Jungian writing employs Janus as an archetype of dual perception, transition, ambivalence, and integration of past and future. This is a twentieth-century psychological interpretation. Carl Jung did not uncover an ancient Roman doctrine of a “Janus archetype”; later writers applied his vocabulary to the god.

The “Janus principle” appears in organizational studies, chemistry, immunology, computing, and philosophy for things with two directions or opposed functions. The metaphor descends from iconography rather than cult.

### Literature and art

- Shakespeare invokes “two-headed Janus” in *The Merchant of Venice* 1.1 as an emblem of opposed temperaments.
- Renaissance and Baroque emblem books portray Janus with key, staff, serpent-circle, year, seasons, or Peace and War.
- T. S. Eliot’s *Four Quartets* repeatedly makes ending and beginning interpenetrate. It is often described as Janus-like, although Janus is not the poem’s governing named deity; explicit dependence should not be asserted without further evidence.
- Modern fantasy commonly reduces Janus to a god of “duality,” choices, or time portals.
- In the James Bond film and game *GoldenEye* (1995/1997), “Janus” is the name of a criminal syndicate led by a man with divided allegiance and a scarred face: a modern play on duplicity.
- In comics and games, bifrontal characters and gatekeepers are often named Janus even where Roman religious material is minimal.

### Science and commerce

Saturn’s moon **Janus**, discovered in the twentieth century and confirmed through later observations, shares an orbit with Epimetheus in a remarkable co-orbital exchange. The name invokes transition and paired identity, not an ancient Janus–Epimetheus myth.

“Janus particles,” “Janus kinases,” “Janus materials,” software systems, companies, locks, financial products, and security projects all use the two-sided or gateway metaphor. These commercial and scientific usages materially sustain the name but are not continuations of worship.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Foundational modern studies

- **Georg Wissowa, *Religion und Kultus der Römer***: treated Roman cult through institutions, calendars, priesthoods, and historically disciplined comparison. Janus emerges as a god of entrances and beginnings rather than a free-floating mythic archetype.
- **Kurt Latte, *Römische Religionsgeschichte***: emphasized historical development and warned against excessive reconstruction.
- **Georges Dumézil, *La religion romaine archaïque* / *Archaic Roman Religion***: made Janus the god of “firstness,” passages, and opening within a structural Roman system. Dumézil’s approach is powerful for ritual relations but does not prove that every structural symmetry was consciously archaic.
- **Robert Schilling**: influential studies of Janus as *dieu introducteur* and of the epithets, emphasizing passage and the *ei-/iā-* etymology.
- **Mary Beard, John North, Simon Price, *Religions of Rome***: situates Janus within Roman ritual practice, political change, and the limits of reconstructing belief from later literary accounts.
- **John Scheid**: stresses correct performance, civic institutions, and ritual ordering over a search for private creeds.
- **LIMC**: provides the indispensable iconographic catalogue, though identifications of anonymous bifrontal heads remain context-dependent.
- **Brill’s New Pauly and the Oxford Classical Dictionary**: concise modern reference syntheses anchored in literary, ritual, and material evidence.

### Main controversies

#### Was Janus originally supreme?

Support:

- Salian *divom deus*;
- precedence in prayer;
- Varro’s cosmic identifications;
- title *Pater*.

Objections:

- precedence can reflect access, not sovereignty;
- the Salian phrase’s text and meaning are uncertain;
- Jupiter is consistently the principal sovereign god in securely documented Roman public religion;
- cosmic identifications belong to late philosophical systematization.

Conclusion: a former “supreme Janus” is possible but unproved.

#### Latin or Etruscan?

The Roman movement/door etymology and archaic ritual integration favor an old Latin or Italic god. Culśanś supplies a genuine Etruscan functional and iconographic comparison. Direction of borrowing remains unsettled. Treating Culśanś simply as “Etruscan Janus” erases Etruscan particularity.

#### Solar god?

Ancient scholars equated Janus with Sol/Apollo, and morning belongs to him. Yet key and threshold functions explain the evidence without requiring a lost solar essence. The solar theory is a learned ancient interpretation later amplified by comparative mythologists.

#### Why two faces?

Ancient answers include:

- watching entrance and exit;
- seeing past and future;
- surveying east and west;
- memory and foresight;
- civilized Janus versus incoming Saturn;
- two gates or directions.

No surviving early inscription explains the image. The oldest material image already has two faces, but its intended theology cannot be read directly from the coin.

#### War gates: what was inside?

Sources can imply that War is confined, Peace is protected, or Janus’s aid remains accessible. Rather than harmonize these, they should be read as changing metaphors. Augustus could exploit closure as peace while epic dramatized opening as the release of destructive force.

#### Human sacrifice

No primary source securely documents human sacrifice to Janus. The theory entered Janus scholarship through nineteenth- and early-twentieth-century divine-king and comparative “primitive religion” models. It remains speculation and should not be reported as Roman practice.

#### The Janiculum

Ancient etymology derives Janiculum from Janus or his city. This is part of Roman place-making, not proof that the god historically founded a settlement there. Archaeology has not produced an archaic “palace of Janus.”

### State of the evidence

What survives is asymmetrical:

- many late literary explanations;
- fragments of archaic prayer embedded in later books;
- numerous coins;
- very few securely identified early statues;
- no surviving Janus temple securely excavated with an intact cult assemblage;
- scattered provincial dedications;
- monumental calendars;
- no priestly manual of his rites.

Varro’s *Antiquitates rerum divinarum* is the greatest loss. Augustine’s quotations preserve crucial material but filter it through Christian polemic. The CIL, EDCS, museum coin catalogues, and topographical archaeology have corrected older accounts that relied almost entirely on literary myth.

### Critical assessment

The dossier’s firmest foundation is the combination of late-third-century BCE coinage, Republican/Augustan texts, the *Res Gestae*, calendars, imperial coins, and provincial inscriptions. Regal-era events, complete archaic theology, genealogies, and the historical King Janus cannot be established independently. Ovid supplies the fullest mythology, but precisely because his account is late, literary, and self-consciously synthetic, it must not be mistaken for an untouched archaic canon.
