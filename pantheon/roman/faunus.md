# Faunus: Research Dossier

**Class:** Roman/Latin god (*deus*); in late Republican and imperial literature also the singular ancestor or representative of the plural *Fauni*, woodland prophetic spirits. In Roman legendary historiography he is additionally rationalized as an early king of Latium who became a god after death.

**Earliest secure attestation:** The earliest surviving direct material attestation is **FAUNO** in the *Fasti Antiates Maiores*, a painted Republican calendar from Antium, dated approximately **84–46 BCE**, under the Ides of February. The calendar registers a public observance but supplies no theology or mythology. An earlier cult is historically probable: Livy records that an *aedes Fauni* was commissioned in 196 BCE and dedicated on Tiber Island in 194 BCE, but Livy wrote roughly two centuries after that event and the temple itself has not survived. Ennius’s second-century-BCE phrase “the verses which once the Fauns and seers sang,” preserved by Cicero and Varro, is the earliest surviving literary language, but its plural *Fauni* need not designate the fully developed singular god.

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Early/direct]** Contemporary or nearly contemporary material, inscriptional, calendrical, or early literary evidence.
- **[Later canonical]** The Augustan and early imperial literary synthesis that became standard.
- **[Regional variant]** A geographically limited form or identification.
- **[Learned systematization]** Antiquarian, philosophical, grammatical, or genealogical organization rather than demonstrably old cult belief.
- **[Syncretistic identification]** A historically datable equation with another deity.
- **[Late accretion]** Late-antique, medieval, or early-modern development.
- **[Modern reconstruction]** A scholarly inference from incomplete evidence.
- **[Modern invention/reception]** A modern religious, literary, artistic, commercial, or popular-cultural creation.

## 1. Identification, names, and epithets

### Faunus

**[Early/direct]** Latin **Faunus**, genitive *Faunī*, is the god’s ordinary name. The calendar form **FAUNO** is dative, “[a rite] for Faunus.” Varro, *De lingua Latina* 7.36, calls the *Fauni* “gods of the Latins” and says that both a male **Faunus** and female **Fauna** existed. Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.6 and *De divinatione* 1.101, treats voices of the *Fauni* as familiar examples of traditional Roman divine manifestation.

The evidence supports several overlapping conceptions:

1. a Latin god receiving calendrical and temple cult;
2. a prophetic presence or voice in uncultivated woodland;
3. a rustic protector connected with livestock and country estates;
4. a legendary pre-Trojan king and ancestor of Latinus;
5. under Greek influence, the Roman counterpart of Pan;
6. in the plural, *Fauni*, a species of woodland beings increasingly assimilated to Greek satyrs and Panes.

These are historical layers, not necessarily parts of a single original personality.

### Etymology

**[Ancient learned systematization]** Varro derives *Fauni* from *fari*, “to speak”: woodland beings were said to utter oracles in Saturnian verse, *a quo fando Faunos dictos*. This is an ancient punning etymology and is important evidence for how late Republican antiquarians understood the god, but it is not secure historical linguistics.

**[Ancient learned systematization]** Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 1.12.21–22, explains *Fauna* from *favere*, “to favor, benefit,” because the earth favors living creatures. Comparable derivations encouraged an interpretation of *Faunus* as “the favorable one.”

**[Modern reconstruction]** A currently defensible linguistic analysis derives the name from Proto-Italic forms such as *\*fawono-* or *\*fawe/o-* and associates it with favor or benevolence; Umbrian *foner*, “favorably/mercifully,” is commonly cited. This is compatible with, but not proved by, the ancient *favere* explanation.

**[Disputed modern reconstruction]** A different proposal derives *Faunus* from Indo-European *\*dhau-no-* or a related “strangler/wild beast” formation, making him originally wolf-like and connecting him with the *Luperci*. The theory depends heavily on accepting Faunus as the original god of the Lupercalia—precisely the point the ancient evidence leaves uncertain. Proposed comparisons with Greek *thaûnon*, “wild beast,” Daunian ethnonyms, Pan, and Vedic Rudra remain comparativist hypotheses, not attested identifications.

There is no demonstrated derivation from Etruscan. Etruscan **Fufluns** is Dionysian, not an Etruscan Faunus, and similarity of names is insufficient evidence. Etruscan mirrors illustrate the circulation of Greek and Italic mythic figures, but no securely labeled Etruscan image establishes a native Faunus cult.

### Principal titles and associated names

- **Fatuus / Fatuclus.** **[Learned systematization]** Connected by grammarians and commentators with *fari*, prophecy, and inspired speech. The evidence often makes these alternate names or explanatory doubles rather than securely independent cult epithets.
- **Fatua.** **[Learned systematization]** Feminine prophetic counterpart, frequently identified with Fauna or Bona Dea.
- **Fauna.** **[Attested but unstable classification]** A female deity whom sources variously make Faunus’s wife, sister, daughter, or feminine counterpart. No single relationship is canonical.
- **Inuus.** **[Syncretistic identification/disputed title]** Livy 1.5.2 says the Romans called Pan Lycaeus **Inuus** in the context of the Lupercalia. Later commentators connect the name with *inire*, “to enter/mount,” and animal or human sexual generation. Livy does not call Inuus “Faunus”; that equation is later.
- **Lupercus.** **[Disputed identification]** Often presented in modern summaries as “Faunus Lupercus,” protector of flocks from wolves. The ancient evidence does not securely establish this as an early cult title of Faunus. Modern scholarship has variously treated Lupercus as Faunus, as an independent deity, as a secondary formation from *Luperci/Lupercalia*, or as no ancient god at all.
- **Lycaeus.** **[Syncretistic identification]** Ovid’s “Lycaean Faunus” is Faunus translated into the position of Arcadian Pan Lycaeus. It is literary evidence for Roman-Greek equivalence, not evidence that an archaic Latin cult used the Greek title.
- **Silvicola**, “woodland-dweller,” and similar poetic descriptions. **[Later literary]** Descriptive rather than securely epigraphic cult titles.
- **Agrestis**, “rustic/country.” **[Later canonical]** Ovid, *Fasti* 2.193, calls him *agrestis Faunus* at his Tiber Island altar.
- **Deus Faunus Ausecus.** **[Regional variant]** A late Roman dedication on the Thetford treasure, conventionally cited as AE 1982, 670b, invokes *Dei Fauni Auseci*. The epithet **Ausecus** is otherwise obscure; its meaning cannot be established. It may represent a local Romano-British cult form.
- **Faunigeni**, “Faunus-born.” **[Poetic genealogy]** Applied to Latin peoples or figures descended from Faunus; it is not necessarily a cult title.

### Faunus and Pan

**[Syncretistic identification]** The equation developed through Hellenizing interpretation:

- Livy 1.5.2: the Lupercalia honors **Pan Lycaeus**, called **Inuus** by Romans.
- Virgil, *Aeneid* 8.343–344: Evander’s Arcadians establish a rite to **Lycaean Pan** at the Lupercal.
- Dionysius of Halicarnassus 1.32: Arcadians establish rites connected with Pan.
- Ovid, *Fasti* 2.267–424: calls the festival Faunus’s, narrates Pan’s Arcadian introduction, and speaks of “Lycaean Faunus.”

Ovid therefore supplies the clearest surviving literary merger. It is methodologically unsafe to carry that Augustan equation backward and call every Lupercalian recipient “Faunus.”

## 2. Earliest attestation and textual corpus

### The material and calendrical record

**Fasti Antiates Maiores, ca. 84–46 BCE — Latin, painted plaster calendar from Antium.**  
**[Early/direct]** The calendar marks the Ides of February with **FAUNO**. It establishes a recognized observance to Faunus by the late Republic. It does not identify him with Pan, Inuus, Lupercus, or the Lupercalia, which occurred two days later.

**Lost Temple of Faunus, Tiber Island — vowed 196, dedicated 194 BCE.**  
**[Historical record preserved in later text]** Livy 33.42.10 says the plebeian aediles Gnaeus Domitius Ahenobarbus and Gaius Scribonius Curio built a temple of Faunus from fines imposed on three fraudulent public-pasture contractors; Livy 34.53.4 records its dedication by Domitius in 194 BCE. Ovid and surviving calendars place its anniversary on February 13. Vitruvius 3.2.3 lists it as a prostyle temple. No architectural remains have been securely identified, and its precise position—north or south end of Tiber Island—has been disputed in topographical literature.

The funding is unusually appropriate to the later pastoral god: penalties concerning public grazing financed his sanctuary. That connection is documented; whether it motivated the dedication is an inference.

### Archaic poetic memory

**Ennius, *Annales*, fragment preserved in Cicero and Varro, early second century BCE — Latin.**  
**[Early literary]** Ennius contrasts his poetic practice with *versibus quos olim Fauni vatesque canebant*, “the verses which once the Fauns and seers sang.” The line shows that *Fauni* could symbolize native, prophetic, pre-Hellenic poetic antiquity. It does not by itself prove cult of a single god named Faunus.

### Republican prose

**Cicero, *Brutus* 71, 75 and *Orator* 171, 46 BCE — Latin.**  
**[Early literary]** Cicero quotes or evokes Ennius’s Fauns and seers while discussing archaic verse. These passages document literary memory, not ritual.

**Cicero, *De natura deorum* 2.6.14–17; 3.15, 45 BCE — Latin.**  
**[Early philosophical testimony]** Balbus cites repeatedly heard “voices of the Fauns” as evidence for divine beings. In book 3 the Academic critic Cotta questions why satyrs, nymphs, Fauns, and similar beings should not count as gods if the premises of popular theology are accepted. The dialogue therefore records belief and philosophical dispute rather than Cicero’s unqualified testimony.

**Cicero, *De divinatione* 1.101, 114; 44 BCE — Latin.**  
**[Early philosophical testimony]** Quintus says Fauns have been heard in battle and that truthful disembodied voices have issued during crises. The opposing book disputes unreliable divination. Again, the work preserves a claim under debate.

**Varro, *De lingua Latina* 7.36, ca. 47–45 BCE — Latin.**  
**[Early antiquarian testimony]** Varro calls the Fauns Latin gods, associates Faunus and Fauna, woodland prophecy, and Saturnian verse, and derives their name from speech. This is the central Republican statement about the prophetic Fauns.

### Augustan poetry and history

**Virgil, *Eclogues* 6.27, ca. 40 BCE — Latin.**  
**[Later canonical literary]** Fauns dance to Silenus’s song. The plural beings inhabit the Greek pastoral landscape and are already close to satyrs.

**Virgil, *Georgics* 1.10–12, 29 BCE — Latin.**  
**[Later canonical literary]** “Fauns, present gods of the countryside,” together with Dryads, are invoked to favor an agricultural poem. Their function is rustic and collective.

**Virgil, *Aeneid* 7.45–106, ca. 29–19 BCE — Latin.**  
**[Later canonical synthesis]** Faunus becomes an ancestral Latin god-king: Latinus is son of Faunus and the Laurentine nymph Marica; Faunus is son of Picus, Picus of Saturn. Latinus consults his father’s oracle at Albunea by sacrificing sheep and sleeping on their skins. A woodland voice warns him not to marry Lavinia to a Latin suitor because foreign descendants will rule widely. This is the fullest ancient representation of Faunus as ancestral oracle.

**Virgil, *Aeneid* 8.314–344 — Latin.**  
**[Later canonical/syncretistic]** Evander recounts early Italian kings, including Saturn, and points out the Lupercal associated with Arcadian Pan. Virgil does not here call Pan Faunus.

**Horace, *Odes* 1.17, ca. 23 BCE — Latin.**  
**[Later canonical poetic cult]** Faunus exchanges Arcadian Lycaeus for Horace’s Sabine Mount Lucretilis and protects the poet’s goats from heat and snakes. The poem makes a Greek-named god locally present on an Italian estate.

**Horace, *Odes* 3.18, ca. 23 BCE — Latin.**  
**[Later canonical poetic cult]** Faunus is invoked as lover of fleeing nymphs. The poet promises a kid when December’s Nones return; cattle rest, the village celebrates, and the wolf moves harmlessly among lambs. This poem is the principal evidence for a rustic December Faunalia, but it may portray a private or local estate rite rather than a universal public festival.

**Livy, books 1, 33, and 34, composed ca. 27 BCE–17 CE — Latin.**  
**[Later historical tradition]** Book 1 connects the Lupercalia with Pan Lycaeus/Inuus and Evander; books 33–34 preserve the temple’s construction and dedication. Livy never securely says that the Lupercalia was originally Faunus’s festival.

**Ovid, *Fasti* 2, ca. 8 CE — Latin.**  
**[Later canonical/syncretistic]** Records the February 13 altar observance; calls the Lupercalia Faunus’s festival; identifies Faunus with Lycaean Pan; narrates his attempted sexual assault on Omphale and humiliation by Hercules; and connects a goat oracle with Roman fertility rites.

**Ovid, *Fasti* 3.285–392 — Latin.**  
**[Later canonical elaboration]** Numa traps Faunus and Picus after drugging their spring with wine. They help him summon Jupiter Elicius and negotiate a nonhuman expiation for lightning.

**Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 13.750–897, ca. 8 CE — Latin.**  
**[Later canonical genealogy]** Acis is the son of Faunus and the river-nymph Symaethis. Faunus plays no active role in Acis’s death and transformation.

### Imperial and late-antique systematization

**Dionysius of Halicarnassus, *Roman Antiquities* 1.31–44, late first century BCE — Greek.**  
**[Learned historiography]** Treats Faunus as a king in the line of indigenous rulers. Dionysius reports that Latinus “passed for” Faunus’s son but was, in an alternative rationalizing account, actually Hercules’s son. He also gives the Arcadian foundation narrative of the Lupercalia.

**Festus/Paulus, *De verborum significatu*, imperial epitome of Verrius Flaccus — Latin.**  
**[Learned systematization]** Preserves definitions and etymological material about *Fauni*, *Fatuus*, *Inuus*, and Lupercalian terminology. Because Festus survives incompletely and Paulus is a much later epitome, individual formulations must not automatically be treated as archaic cult doctrine.

**Servius and Servius Auctus, commentaries on Virgil, principally fourth–fifth centuries CE — Latin.**  
**[Late learned systematization]** These scholia preserve alternate genealogies, explanations of Faunus, Pan, Inuus, Fatuus, Marica, and Lupercus, and lost antiquarian learning. They are invaluable but frequently harmonize divergent traditions.

**Macrobius, *Saturnalia* 1.12, early fifth century CE — Latin.**  
**[Late learned systematization]** Identifies Bona Dea with Earth, Bona, Fauna, Ops, Fatua, Proserpina, Hecate, or Semele; also reports the disturbing tradition that she was Faunus’s daughter and resisted his incestuous assault before he beat her with myrtle and coupled with her after becoming a serpent.

**Arnobius, *Adversus nationes*, ca. 300 CE; Lactantius, *Divinae institutiones*, early fourth century; Augustine, *City of God*, 413–426 — Latin.**  
**[Hostile Christian testimony]** These authors use pagan genealogies, euhemerism, sexual violence, multiplication of gods, and rustic spirits polemically. They preserve otherwise lost antiquarian material but cannot be read as neutral descriptions. Augustine’s Fauns or “incubi,” for example, belong to Christian demonological argument as much as to documentation of pagan belief.

## 3. Domain, attributes, and iconography

### Woodland speech and prophecy

**[Early/direct]** Faunus’s best-attested early domain is not goat-legged sexuality but the uncanny voice of uncultivated places. Varro locates Faunic speech in *silvestria loca*, woodland places; Cicero speaks of heard voices; Virgil monumentalizes the idea at Albunea.

At Virgil’s oracle:

- the site is a sacred grove around a sulfurous or mephitic spring;
- a priest brings sacrificial animals;
- the consultant sleeps on the victims’ fleeces;
- dreams, apparitions, disembodied voices, communication with gods, and contact with the underworld are possible;
- Faunus speaks to Latinus as ancestor and god.

This resembles incubation, but the passage is epic representation rather than an archaeological report of a continuously documented institution. The traditional identification with the sulfurous springs near Tibur is plausible, though exact localization remains disputed.

### Agriculture, herds, and protection

**[Later canonical]** Horace and Virgil firmly establish Faunus/Fauni as gods of fields, hills, herds, and rural boundaries between settlement and wilderness. Horace makes him protect young goats and suspend predation during his feast.

The Tiber Island temple’s financing from fines against *pecuarii* provides independent historical contact with pastoral administration. It does not prove that protection from wolves was Faunus’s original function.

### Fertility and sexuality

**[Syncretistic/later canonical]** The sexual Faunus emerges most strongly when he is assimilated to Pan and Inuus. Ovid’s Faunus pursues Omphale; Horace calls him lover of fleeing nymphs; late commentators relate *Inuus* to penetration or mounting.

The sources do not present this sexuality benignly in every case. Ovid’s episode is an attempted nocturnal assault, and Macrobius’s Fauna story describes incestuous coercion and violence. These are literary and antiquarian narratives, not evidence for institutional “sacred sexuality.”

There is **no reliable evidence for temple prostitution in Faunus’s cult**.

### Powers and limitations

- **Prophecy:** woodland voices, dreams, and ancestral oracles.
- **Protection:** livestock and rural property in Horatian poetry.
- **Fertility:** attributed through the Pan/Inuus/Lupercalia complex.
- **Fear and disturbance:** plural Fauns can be uncanny, noisy beings.
- **Divine limitation:** in *Fasti* 3 Faunus admits that rustic gods cannot themselves command Jupiter’s lightning; they can only help Numa summon him.
- **Fallibility and comic defeat:** Hercules easily throws him from the bed in *Fasti* 2.
- **Metamorphosis:** Macrobius’s late account says he became a serpent to assault Fauna.

### Physical form

**[Early uncertainty]** No surviving archaic or Republican image can be identified beyond reasonable doubt as the individual Roman Faunus. A bearded or horned rustic figure without inscription may be Pan, Silvanus, a satyr, a generic faun, or another pastoral being.

**[Syncretistic iconography]** Once identified with Pan, Faunus can appear:

- horned;
- bearded or youthful;
- nude or wearing an animal skin;
- with goat legs or otherwise theriomorphic;
- carrying a shepherd’s crook, drinking vessel, cornucopia, or pipes;
- accompanied by goats, nymphs, satyrs, or rural vegetation.

The **syrinx/panpipes** are Pan’s inherited Greek attribute, not securely an original Latin emblem. Likewise, goat legs became standard for generic “fauns” through the Pan/satyr tradition.

A Roman statue associated with the Lupercal reportedly showed a nude male wearing goatskin. Identification as Faunus is interpretive; the figure may represent Pan, a Lupercus, or a personification of the ritual.

### The fauns of art

**[Later canonical to modern reception]** Latin singular *faunus* became a common noun through plural *fauni*. Roman art and later European art increasingly treated fauns as the Roman equivalent of Greek satyrs. This produced a major semantic split:

- **Faunus:** the named Latin god and ancestor-oracle;
- **a faun:** one of a class of sensuous, playful, frightening, or lascivious woodland beings.

Renaissance and modern catalogues often call satyrs “fauns” without implying that the image represents the god Faunus. Famous works such as the Barberini Faun are therefore evidence for the reception of the species, not secure portraits of the god.

### Unattested popular correspondences

No ancient evidence establishes a canonical color, number, weekday, planet, or zodiacal sign for Faunus. Modern tables assigning him green or brown, the number 3 or 5, a particular crystal, tarot card, or planet are **[Modern invention]** unless explicitly presented as a practitioner’s new system.

## 4. Genealogy and relations with other divine beings

### Competing genealogies

| Source and date | Genealogy | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|
| Virgil, *Aeneid* 7.45–49, late first century BCE | Saturn → Picus → Faunus → Latinus, with Marica as Latinus’s mother | Later canonical Latin-national genealogy |
| Dionysius 1.44, late first century BCE | Latinus merely “passed for” Faunus’s son; Hercules was supposedly his biological father | Rationalizing Greek-language variant |
| Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 13.750, ca. 8 CE | Faunus + Symaethis → Acis | Sicilian poetic genealogy |
| Macrobius 1.12.24, early fifth century CE | Fauna/Bona Dea is Faunus’s daughter; he desires and assaults her | Late antiquarian accretion |
| Servian traditions | Marica may be wife, mother, or local goddess connected with Faunus and Latinus | Late learned variants |
| Various late sources | Fauna is wife, sister, daughter, or female equivalent of Faunus | Unstable systematization |

“Flora as wife of Faunus,” common in modern summaries, is weakly supported and probably results from confusion among Fauna, Bona Dea, and Flora. It should not be treated as canonical.

### Pantheon position

Faunus never occupies the central civic rank held by Jupiter, Juno, or Minerva. His single securely reported Roman temple was peripheral on Tiber Island; his strongest literary landscapes lie outside the city—in groves, springs, farms, hills, and primordial Latium.

Augustan epic elevates him genealogically: as father of Latinus, he becomes an ancestor of the polity into which Aeneas will marry. This is political-poetic promotion, not proof of a correspondingly powerful Roman priesthood.

### Every documented interaction with another named divine being

The list separates direct narrative interaction from genealogy or equation.

- **Picus — father and ritual companion.** Faunus is Picus’s son in Virgil, *Aeneid* 7.48–49; in Ovid, *Fasti* 3.289–344, Faunus and Picus drink together, are captured by Numa, and jointly summon Jupiter. **[Later canonical]**
- **Saturn — grandfather.** Virgil makes Saturn father of Picus and therefore grandfather of Faunus, *Aeneid* 7.48–49. No direct meeting is narrated. **[Later canonical genealogy]**
- **Marica — co-parent or disputed relation.** Virgil makes Latinus the child of Faunus and the Laurentine nymph Marica, *Aeneid* 7.47. No courtship or meeting is narrated. Servian commentary preserves alternate interpretations. **[Later canonical with learned variants]**
- **Latinus — son and oracle-consultant.** Latinus consults his divine father at Albunea and receives the warning against a local marriage for Lavinia, Virgil, *Aeneid* 7.81–101. **[Later canonical]**
- **Symaethis — co-parent.** Ovid calls Acis the child of Faunus and the river-nymph Symaethis, *Metamorphoses* 13.750. No encounter is narrated. **[Later canonical/Sicilian poetic genealogy]**
- **Acis — son.** Ovid identifies Acis as Faunus’s son; Faunus does not intervene in the conflict with Polyphemus, *Metamorphoses* 13.750–897. **[Later canonical]**
- **Pan — explicit equation rather than encounter.** Ovid overlays Faunus on Pan Lycaeus in *Fasti* 2.267–424. Virgil and Livy retain Pan in the Lupercalian foundation narrative. No story has Faunus and Pan meet as two separate gods. **[Syncretistic identification]**
- **Inuus — alternate Roman name or conflated deity.** Livy calls Pan Lycaeus *Inuus*; later sources fold Inuus into Faunus. No interaction between two independent figures is attested. **[Syncretistic/learned identification]**
- **Lupercus — disputed equation.** Late and modern sources treat Lupercus as Faunus or his epithet. No securely early narrative stages them together. **[Disputed learned identification]**
- **Hercules — attempted sexual encounter and defeat.** Faunus mistakes Hercules, dressed in Omphale’s garments, for Omphale; Hercules throws him from the bed, Ovid, *Fasti* 2.303–358. **[Later canonical comic myth]**
- **Omphale — object of attempted assault.** Faunus follows Hercules and Omphale, enters their cave at night, and tries to reach the sleeper he thinks is Omphale; exchanged clothing defeats him, Ovid, *Fasti* 2.303–358. Omphale is generally mortal or heroic rather than a Roman goddess, but belongs in the divine interaction register because the episode explains ritual conduct. **[Later canonical]**
- **Jupiter/Jupiter Elicius — superior deity summoned with Faunus’s help.** Faunus and Picus acknowledge Jupiter’s exclusive authority over lightning and help Numa draw him down; Jupiter negotiates the expiation, Ovid, *Fasti* 3.285–392. **[Later canonical elaboration]**
- **Juno/Lucina — indirect Lupercalian connection.** Ovid’s fertility narrative says Juno answered sterile Roman women through a grove-oracle: “Let the sacred he-goat penetrate the Italian mothers”; an Etruscan augur interpreted this as striking them with goatskin. Faunus is not made a speaking participant in that episode, despite its placement in his festival. **[Later canonical; no direct interaction securely attested]**
- **Fauna/Bona Dea/Fatua — counterpart, daughter, and victim in incompatible accounts.** Varro recognizes Faunus and Fauna as male and female Latin gods. Macrobius identifies Fauna with Bona Dea and reports that Faunus desired, beat, intoxicated, and—in serpent form—assaulted his daughter. **[Republican classification; late violent accretion]**
- **Ops, Terra, Proserpina, Hecate, Semele — equations applied to Fauna, not encounters with Faunus.** Macrobius 1.12.21–24 gives these learned identifications. **[Late learned syncretism]**
- **Silenus — joint poetic scene.** The Fauns dance to Silenus’s cosmic song in Virgil, *Eclogues* 6.27. This concerns plural Fauni rather than necessarily the singular god. **[Later canonical pastoral]**
- **Dryads — joint invocation.** Virgil invokes Fauns and Dryads together at *Georgics* 1.10–12; no discrete action between them occurs. **[Later canonical pastoral]**
- **Nymphs — erotic pursuit.** Horace calls Faunus *Nympharum fugientum amator*, “lover of fleeing nymphs,” *Odes* 3.18. They are unnamed, so no named god-god interaction can be extracted. **[Later canonical poetic]**

No securely attested narrative interaction between Faunus and Silvanus survives. Their overlapping woodland domains produced later comparison and iconographic confusion, not a documented meeting.

## 5. Major myths and narrative cycles

### Faunus, Hercules, and Omphale

**Source:** Ovid, *Fasti* 2.303–358, ca. 8 CE.  
**Tier:** **[Later canonical; Greek-derived comic elaboration]**

Hercules and Omphale travel with their attendants. From a mountain Faunus sees them and becomes inflamed with desire for Omphale. The pair exchange clothing for amusement or ritual preparation: Omphale puts on Hercules’s lion skin and carries his club; Hercules wears her fine garments. They retire separately because they are preparing for a rite requiring sexual abstinence.

Faunus enters the dark cave. Feeling the lion skin, he assumes he has found Hercules and withdraws. Feeling soft clothing on the other couch, he climbs into what he thinks is Omphale’s bed, only to discover Hercules’s hairy legs. Hercules throws him down; the company laughs. Ovid makes this humiliation the reason Faunus’s worshippers or the Luperci reject clothing.

Nothing establishes the story as archaic Roman cult myth. Its ingredients—Hercules’s servitude to Omphale, exchanged dress, Pan-like lust, and comic mistaken identity—are Hellenistic. Ovid uses an already syncretized Faunus/Pan to provide an entertaining ritual aetiology.

### Numa captures Faunus and Picus

**Source:** Ovid, *Fasti* 3.285–392, ca. 8 CE; partial parallels in Plutarch and later tradition.  
**Tier:** **[Later canonical elaboration]**

After ominous lightning, the nymph Egeria tells Numa that Picus and Faunus know how to expiate the thunderbolt. Numa places wine at the spring from which the two rustic gods drink. They become intoxicated and fall asleep; he binds them. When they wake, supernatural resistance makes the bonds tighten.

Faunus explains that rustic divinities cannot reveal or control Jupiter’s mysteries independently, but he and Picus can help summon Jupiter. They call him down as Jupiter Elicius. Numa then negotiates Jupiter’s escalating verbal demands:

- Jupiter asks for “a head”; Numa offers an onion head.
- Jupiter asks for “a man’s”; Numa supplies human hair.
- Jupiter asks for “life”; Numa offers a fish.

Jupiter accepts the substitutions and gives a ritual for averting lightning. The story dramatizes Roman ritual ingenuity: precise language and negotiation prevent human killing.

This is not evidence that Faunus’s own cult practiced human sacrifice. On the contrary, Ovid’s story is an aetiology of nonhuman substitution in a Jupiter rite.

### Faunus’s oracle to Latinus

**Source:** Virgil, *Aeneid* 7.45–106, ca. 29–19 BCE.  
**Tier:** **[Later canonical Roman-national epic]**

Portents trouble Latinus as he considers marrying Lavinia to Turnus. He consults Faunus at Albunea according to ancestral procedure, sacrificing one hundred sheep and sleeping on their skins. A voice from the high grove addresses him as “my son” and forbids marriage to a Latin husband. Foreign sons-in-law will come whose descendants will raise the Latin name to world rule.

The prophecy makes Faunus a guarantor of the Trojan-Latin union and retrospectively of Roman empire. It is simultaneously religious narrative and Augustan political teleology. The passage cannot be treated as a verbatim ritual manual, but its combination of sacrifice, incubation, dream-vision, woodland voice, and ancestral authority probably draws on real Italian divinatory concepts.

### Faunus the king of Latium

**Sources:** Virgil, Dionysius, Justin, Lactantius, Servius, and other antiquarian accounts.  
**Tier:** **[Learned euhemerism and legendary historiography]**

Faunus is placed among a sequence of primordial Italian rulers, commonly Saturn, Picus, Faunus, and Latinus. Different writers credit such kings with civilizing activities, agriculture, law, worship, or hospitality.

The tradition works in two opposite directions:

- a god is historicized as an ancient king later deified;
- an invented royal genealogy integrates Latin religion with Trojan and Greek universal history.

Dionysius’s claim that Latinus was really Hercules’s son but was reputed to be Faunus’s demonstrates that ancient writers knew and preserved incompatible genealogies.

No contemporary record establishes a historical King Faunus.

### Faunus, Fauna, and Bona Dea

**Source:** chiefly Macrobius 1.12.21–24, early fifth century CE, drawing on earlier antiquarians.  
**Tier:** **[Late learned accretion; hostile or sensational material may underlie transmission]**

Macrobius first offers theological equations: Earth is Bona Dea, Fauna, Ops, and Fatua. He then relates a story in which Fauna is Faunus’s daughter. Faunus desires her; she resists even after wine; he beats her with myrtle; finally he changes into a serpent and has intercourse with her.

The narrative was used to explain features of Bona Dea’s women-only rites: exclusion of men, prohibition or euphemistic naming of wine, avoidance of myrtle, and the snake motif. Its late attestation and programmatic ritual explanations make it unsafe evidence for early Faunus cult, but the sexual violence must not be removed from the documented tradition.

### Acis

**Source:** Ovid, *Metamorphoses* 13.750–897.  
**Tier:** **[Later canonical/Sicilian localization]**

Acis, beloved of Galatea and killed by Polyphemus, is identified as son of Faunus and Symaethis. Faunus’s role is genealogical only. The genealogy naturalizes a Greek-Sicilian myth within a Roman rustic divine family.

## 6. Cult, temples, and ritual practice

### Tiber Island temple

**[Historically attested]** This is the only securely documented temple of Faunus in Rome:

- commissioned in 196 BCE by plebeian aediles;
- financed from fines on dishonest public-pasture contractors;
- dedicated in 194 BCE by Domitius;
- dedication anniversary February 13;
- described by Vitruvius as prostyle;
- mentioned by Ovid at the point where the island divides the Tiber;
- archaeologically unlocated.

The claim that Faunus also possessed a round temple on the Caelian depends on late or uncertain antiquarian notices and should not be placed on the same evidentiary footing.

### February 13 observance

**[Direct calendrical]** Republican and imperial calendars mark an observance to Faunus on the Ides of February. Ovid writes:

> *Idibus agrestis fumant altaria Fauni,  
> hic ubi discretas insula rumpit aquas.*

“On the Ides the altars of rustic Faunus smoke, here where the island divides the parted waters.”

This is the temple’s *dies natalis*, not automatically “the first Faunalia.”

### December Nones

**[Literary/local]** Horace, *Odes* 3.18, is the basis for a rural festival on December 5. It includes wine, a sacrificed kid, rest for cattle, village leisure, and protection of flocks. The term **Faunalia Rustica** is convenient but not securely attested as the official ancient name of a state festival. No priestly calendar or ritual law survives for it.

### The Lupercalia: what is actually documented

The Lupercalia was celebrated on February 15 around the Palatine and Lupercal. Sources describe:

- sacrifice of goats and a dog;
- two young men touched on the forehead with the bloody knife;
- blood wiped away with wool dipped in milk;
- ritual laughter;
- Luperci running with strips cut from goatskins;
- women presenting themselves to be struck, understood in imperial sources as promoting conception or childbirth;
- separate colleges traditionally called Fabiani and Quinctiales, with a Julian college briefly added under Caesar.

**[Firm conclusion]** The ritual is securely ancient and pastoral/urban-boundary in character. Its meaning was already disputed in antiquity.

**[Disputed divine recipient]**

- Livy: Pan Lycaeus, Roman Inuus.
- Virgil: Pan Lycaeus.
- Dionysius and Plutarch: Greek/Arcadian comparisons and uncertainty.
- Ovid: Faunus, explicitly translated through Pan.
- Later sources: Pan, Faunus, Inuus, Lupercus, and sometimes other gods.

Ovid alone among the main early accounts unequivocally calls it Faunus’s festival, and he does so within a Pan-equation. Thus “the Luperci were priests of Faunus” is possible but not securely established for the early rite.

### Sacrifice and the human-sacrifice question

**Animal sacrifice:** Securely present. Goats and a dog belong to Lupercalian ritual; sheep appear in Virgil’s Faunus incubation; a kid is promised by Horace.

**Human sacrifice:** **Not documented for Faunus.** Some scholars once read the blooding of Luperci and their subsequent cleansing as a survival or symbolic substitution for human sacrifice. This is conjectural. No ancient Lupercalia narrative says that a human victim had formerly been killed, and the best detailed historical study explicitly noted that absence.

Ovid’s Jupiter Elicius story substitutes onion, hair, and fish for apparently human demands, but it concerns lightning expiation and demonstrates avoidance, not performance, of human sacrifice.

### Oracles and incubation

**[Literary but internally detailed]** Virgil provides the principal ritual:

1. approach Faunus’s grove;
2. purificatory preparation;
3. sacrifice sheep;
4. lie upon the fleeces;
5. receive apparitions, voices, divine conversation, or underworld communication.

Varro and Cicero independently corroborate the broader association between Fauns, woodland voices, and prophecy. The convergence makes an Italic oracular conception secure; the exact operation of a permanent oracle at Albunea remains less certain.

### Priesthood

No separately organized, continuously attested **flamen Fauni** is securely known. The Luperci were ritual sodalities, but identifying them simply as “priests of Faunus” prejudges the divine-recipient dispute. Virgil refers generically to a priest at Albunea without supplying an institutional title.

### Votives and inscriptions

Faunus’s epigraphic corpus is modest compared with Jupiter, Silvanus, Mercury, or Mithras. Relevant evidence includes:

- calendar entries for February 13;
- imperial or provincial dedications using *Fauno* or *Deo Fauno*;
- the Romano-British **Deus Faunus Ausecus** dedication;
- inscriptions and literary evidence for temples or altars whose physical remains are lost.

The modest corpus cautions against imagining a vast centrally organized cult. Faunus may have been especially at home in household, estate, local, and literary religion, where inscriptions are less abundant.

## 7. Historical evolution

### Before the second century BCE: hypothetical Italic background

**[Modern reconstruction]** Faunus is generally treated as an indigenous Latin or Italic god because Varro calls the Fauns Latin gods, the name has plausible Italic etymology, and the god is embedded in Latin landscapes and genealogies. Yet no securely identified archaic inscription or image survives. Claims about an Indo-European wolf god, Etruscan origin, or direct Rudra parallel exceed the evidence.

### 196–194 BCE: entry into the surviving civic record

The Tiber Island temple supplies the earliest datable cultic event reported by historical sources. Its funding connects public regulation, pastoral economy, and divine cult.

### Late Republic: prophecy and native antiquity

By Ennius, Cicero, and Varro, *Fauni* represented:

- pre-literary Saturnian song;
- Latin antiquity;
- woodland prophecy;
- audible divine presence.

The *Fasti Antiates* proves a calendrical rite for singular Faunus.

### Augustan period: synthesis and promotion

Virgil, Horace, Livy, and Ovid created most of the Faunus recognizable today:

- ancestral father of Latinus;
- oracle of Italy’s Trojan future;
- protective estate god;
- lustful Pan-equivalent;
- participant in Numa’s ritual wisdom;
- putative god of the Lupercalia.

This was not simple preservation. Greek Pan, Roman rustic religion, national genealogy, and antiquarian etymology were being recombined.

### Imperial period: plural fauns and visual Hellenization

The individual god’s identity increasingly overlapped with Pan, satyrs, Silvanus, and plural fauns. Art favored the visibly legible Greek-derived horned or goatlike rustic being. Provincial worship could produce localized titles such as Ausecus.

### Fourth–fifth centuries: antiquarian preservation and Christian recoding

Servius and Macrobius preserved and multiplied equations. Christian apologists treated Faunus in several ways:

- a deified mortal king;
- one among absurdly multiplied pagan gods;
- a sexually immoral being;
- a woodland demon or incubus.

These are polemical transformations, but they helped transmit the material after public pagan institutions disappeared.

### The end of the Lupercalia under Gelasius

The festival survived Christian imperial rule longer than many public pagan rites. Augustine still knew it; the Christian calendar of Polemius Silvius, 448/449, includes it.

Around **494–496 CE**, Pope Gelasius I wrote *Adversus Andromachum* against senators who defended the Lupercalia as necessary for Rome’s protection. Gelasius mocks the refusal of elites to perform the degrading rite themselves and argues that the festival no longer provides its alleged benefits. The exchange is strong evidence that the rite persisted or that influential Romans sought its continuation.

It is reasonable to associate the Lupercalia’s final disappearance with Gelasius’s campaign, but three qualifications are essential:

1. Gelasius does not identify the festival primarily as Faunus worship.
2. His letter does not institute St Valentine’s Day in its place.
3. It does not replace the Lupercalia with Candlemas.

The Valentine/Candlemas replacement story is a **modern historical construction**, not a fifth-century fact.

### Medieval survival

**[Late accretion/folklore]** Faunus ceased to be the recipient of public Roman cult. The plural fauns survived through:

- Latin school texts and mythography;
- Christian demonology;
- bestiaries and marvel literature;
- confusion with satyrs, incubi, woodland demons, and wild men.

This is literary and folkloric survival, not documented continuous worship.

### Renaissance to nineteenth century

Classical recovery returned fauns to painting, sculpture, garden ornament, pastoral poetry, and antiquarian collections. Artists usually represented the generalized faun/satyr rather than the Latin oracle and king. Panpipes, goat legs, erotic pursuit, drunkenness, and Bacchic companionship came to dominate.

### Twentieth and twenty-first centuries

Faunus is again invoked in Roman reconstructionist religion, eclectic Paganism, and nature-centered spirituality. Organizations such as Nova Roma explicitly describe reconstruction of Roman religion, but there is no reliable census of Faunus-specific worshippers and no demonstrated unbroken lineage from antiquity. These are revivals based on texts and scholarship.

## 8. Modern reception and reinvention

### The Symbolist faun

Stéphane Mallarmé’s **“L’Après-midi d’un faune”**, published in its definitive illustrated form in **1876**, made the faun a figure of unstable memory, desire, artifice, and sensual imagination.

Claude Debussy’s **Prélude à l’après-midi d’un faune**, premiered in **1894**, transformed Mallarmé’s figure into an orchestral landmark. Vaslav Nijinsky’s **1912 ballet** further emphasized erotic physicality and provoked controversy. This modern faun descends from the Pan/satyr complex far more than from Faunus the Latin king and oracle.

### Literature and popular culture

- Nathaniel Hawthorne’s *The Marble Faun* (1860) uses the faun as an image of innocence, animality, sexuality, and moral ambiguity.
- Arthur Machen and other fin-de-siècle writers used Pan/faun figures for nature’s terror and transgressive desire.
- C. S. Lewis’s Mr Tumnus in *The Lion, the Witch and the Wardrobe* (1950) established a gentle, scarf-wearing faun for modern children’s fantasy.
- Fantasy games and role-playing systems commonly treat fauns as a species interchangeable with satyrs; this is not ancient Roman classification.
- “Faunus” appears as a name for characters, businesses, software, music projects, and commercial products with little historical content.

### Occultism and esotericism

There is no major medieval or Renaissance grimoire tradition in which Faunus is a stable named demon with a seal, rank, planet, and legion. Lists presenting such a system as ancient are **[Modern invention]** unless supported by a specific text.

Renaissance Neoplatonists, mythographers, and natural-magical writers could allegorize Pan, satyrs, fauns, and sylvans as world-nature, elemental powers, lust, imagination, or lower spirits. Those interpretations generally concern the class of fauns or the Greek Pan, not a continuous initiatory cult of Roman Faunus.

Modern occult systems may assign Faunus correspondences—green, earth, goat, forest, fertility, sexuality, divination—but those are modern syntheses.

### Modern Pagan practice

Roman reconstructionists can reconstruct offerings from Horace, Ovid, calendars, and general Roman ritual procedure. Practices advertised today may include:

- offerings of wine, incense, milk, grain, or cakes;
- observance on February 13 or December 5;
- prayers for livestock, gardens, woodland, sexuality, or divination;
- Lupercalia-inspired rites stripped of animal sacrifice and public flagellation.

Such practices are valid evidence for modern reception but must not be projected backward. “Faunalia Rustica” liturgies circulating online are usually contemporary reconstructions.

### Political appropriation

The Lupercalia has sometimes been romanticized as a virile, pre-Christian, ethnonational Roman rite. Modern depictions that make it a timeless festival of racial vigor, heterosexual fertility, or resistance to Christianity are ideological reconstructions. Ancient evidence instead shows a changing civic ritual whose meaning and divine recipient were disputed even by Romans.

No substantial Faunus-centered extremist movement is documented. Broader Roman and European pagan imagery can, however, be selectively appropriated by nationalist subcultures.

## 9. Historiography and scholarly debate

### Was Faunus the original god of the Lupercalia?

This is the central dispute.

**Arguments for:**

- Ovid directly calls it Faunus’s festival.
- Faunus’s pastoral and sexual traits fit goats, fertility, and running Luperci.
- Later tradition equates him with Pan, Inuus, and Lupercus.
- His temple anniversary falls two days earlier.

**Arguments against:**

- Livy and Virgil name Pan/Inuus, not Faunus.
- Ovid’s Faunus is transparently a translation of Pan.
- The ritual’s name and offices derive from *Lupercal/Luperci*, not Faunus.
- No early inscription calls the Luperci Faunus’s priests.
- The February 13 temple anniversary and February 15 Lupercalia remain distinct calendar entries.

Wissowa and Fowler were skeptical of making Faunus the original recipient. Other scholars, including T. P. Wiseman, have reopened the case. The responsible conclusion is that **Faunus was certainly associated with the festival by Ovid’s time; original ownership is unproved**.

### One god or many Fauns?

Cicero and Varro give the plural *Fauni* substantial antiquity. Scholars disagree whether:

- singular Faunus generated the plural;
- plural woodland powers were primary and later personified;
- the forms coexisted;
- singular and plural converged through Pan/satyr influence.

The surviving evidence cannot decide securely.

### God first or king first?

Ancient euhemerists made Faunus a mortal king later worshipped. Modern historians usually treat that as learned historiography, not preserved biography. The divine name and cult do not require a historical king.

### Prophecy versus fertility

Older presentations often define Faunus principally as a fertility god modeled on Pan. Republican evidence gives unusual prominence to voice, prophecy, and archaic verse. Modern scholarship therefore tends to distinguish an oracular Latin core from later Pan-like sexuality, although even that chronological reconstruction remains inferential.

### Dumézil and comparative religion

Dumézil explored Faunus and Lupercalian material within wider Indo-European structures, including sovereignty, wilderness, initiation, and Rudra-like figures. His comparisons were influential but depend on reconstructed systems that are not directly stated by Roman sources. They should be treated as heuristic models, not recovered prehistoric fact.

### Wissowa, Latte, Scheid, and civic religion

- **Georg Wissowa** stressed cult institutions and was cautious about literary Greek overlays.
- **Kurt Latte** emphasized the difficulty of recovering an original Faunus after Pan-identification.
- **Georges Dumézil** sought comparative Indo-European structure.
- **John Scheid** and **Mary Beard, John North, and Simon Price** emphasize ritual practice, civic context, multiple interpretations, and the danger of turning Roman religion into a fixed mythological system.
- **T. P. Wiseman** has treated the Lupercal and Lupercalia as historically changing Roman institutions embedded in political memory.
- **LIMC** is indispensable for visual comparanda but cannot eliminate the identification problem where objects lack inscriptions.

### Etruscan and Italic claims

Etruscan religious scholarship, including Thulin, shows that Roman divination and Italic religion developed amid intense cultural exchange. It does not supply a securely attested Etruscan Faunus. Etruscan mirrors labeled with other figures cannot be reassigned to Faunus merely because they contain rustic or prophetic imagery.

### Iconographic problem

Faunus is especially difficult to identify visually because:

- Pan already has a strong Greek iconography;
- satyrs and silens share animal traits;
- Silvanus shares Roman woodland attributes;
- “faun” became a generic museum label;
- inscriptions identifying the individual god are rare.

Claims that a particular horned rustic statue is “the earliest image of Faunus” require more proof than resemblance.

### Lost evidence

Varro’s *Antiquitates rerum divinarum*, Verrius Flaccus’s original lexicon, pontifical books, early calendars, local ritual regulations, and most sanctuary furnishings are lost. Augustine, Festus, Macrobius, and Servius preserve fragments through their own polemical or scholarly agendas. Consequently, the dossier is disproportionately dependent on Augustan poetry.

### Discredited or unsupported claims still circulating

- Faunus was universally and from earliest times identical with Pan.
- Lupercus is a securely attested archaic epithet of Faunus.
- The Lupercalia was straightforwardly “Faunus’s fertility festival.”
- Lupercalian flagellation was certainly a survival of human sacrifice.
- Faunus’s cult involved temple prostitution.
- Gelasius replaced the Lupercalia with St Valentine’s Day.
- Candlemas was created to replace the Lupercalia.
- Etruscan Fufluns was Faunus.
- Modern occult correspondences assigned to Faunus are ancient.
- Modern Pagan practice proves continuous underground worship.

None is established by the surviving evidence.
