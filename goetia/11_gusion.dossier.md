# Gusion: Research Dossier

## Editorial method and evidence labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **Documented text** — directly stated in a manuscript, early printed book, or identifiable edition.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a modern conclusion based on textual comparison, palaeography, bibliography, or historical context.
- **Tradition** — a claim transmitted within Jewish, Christian-kabbalistic, or ceremonial-magical literature, without implying empirical truth.
- **Esoteric correspondence** — an occult attribution added to the catalogue: planet, metal, zodiac, tarot, angel, color, incense, and similar data.
- **Disputed claim / legend** — a claim whose sources disagree, whose derivation is conjectural, or which survives as religious or magical narrative rather than demonstrable history.
- **Modern invention** — a recent addition absent from the older source chain.

Spellings are retained when quoting. Dates refer to editions or manuscripts, not to the legendary Solomonic authorship.

# The name and its origins

## 1. The earliest reconstructable form: Gazon

### Mid- or late fifteenth century: *Livre des Esperitz*

**Documented text.** The earliest presently identifiable ancestor is most probably **Gazon**, a Duke in the French *Livre des Esperitz*, preserved in Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29. The manuscript has been dated to the second half of the fifteenth century, with some catalogues allowing an early-sixteenth-century date. Its entry gives precisely the characteristic complex later associated with Gusion: true answers about time, universal favor, elevation to honor and dignity, and forty legions.

The manuscript begins the notice:

> “Gazon est ung grant duc qui donne vroye responce…”

In modern English: Gazon is a great Duke who gives true answers concerning past, future, and present things; grants grace and love among people; raises persons to great honors and dignities; and possesses forty legions. The transcription is available in the [complete *Livre des Esperitz*](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre_des_esperitz/Texte_entier); Jean-Patrice Boudet prints it on p. 107 of his critical study and edition, [“Les who’s who démonologiques…”](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/pdf/1171).

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Identity with Gusion is extremely strong because several independent details travel together:

1. Duke.
2. Answers concerning past, present, and future.
3. Favor or reconciliation.
4. Honors and dignities.
5. Forty legions.

A coincidental duplication of that entire profile is less economical than textual descent or descent from a common catalogue.

### The competing *Livre* name Gorsay/Gorsin

**Disputed.** The *Livre* also contains a late entry variously transcribed **Gorsay** or **Gorsin**. Some modern tables equate this with Gusion because of superficial spelling. That entry, however, has different functions. Gazon is the much better match in title, office, and legion count.

Accordingly:

- **Gazon → Gusoyn/Gusoin/Gusion** is a strong scholarly reconstruction.
- **Gorsay/Gorsin → Gusion** is a weak or conflated modern identification.

This distinction matters because modern demonological sites frequently list all these forms as interchangeable without explaining that they occur as separate entries in the French catalogue.

## 2. Cason in the Munich handbook

**Documented text with reconstructed identification.** The fifteenth-century Munich necromancer’s handbook, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek CLM 849, contains a Duke called **Cason**. Joseph Peterson’s comparison table and Skinner and Rankine align Cason with Gazon/Gusoyn/Gusoin. The handbook also has closely parallel forms for other familiar catalogue spirits: *Barbarus*, *Otius*, *Curson*, and *Alugor*.

The manuscript can be consulted through the [Bavarian State Library facsimile](https://www.digitale-sammlungen.de/de/view/bsb00037155). Richard Kieckhefer edited and studied it in *Forbidden Rites: A Necromancer’s Manual of the Fifteenth Century*.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The sequence **Cason → Gazon → Gusoyn** is plausible as a history of copying across languages and scripts, but no surviving document proves each stage of transmission.

## 3. Weyer’s Gusoyn

**Documented text.** Johann Weyer’s fifth Latin edition of *De praestigiis daemonum* in 1577 appended the *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*. Here the spirit is **Gusoyn**, number 8.

This is the first known printed occurrence. The parent work appeared in 1563, but the *Pseudomonarchia* appendix belongs to the enlarged 1577 edition; dating “Gusoyn” to 1563 is therefore misleading. The 1577 scan places the entry across printed columns 915–916, PDF p. 6. See the [facsimile](https://booksofmagick.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/Pseudomonarchia-Daemonum-1577.pdf).

## 4. Scot’s Gusoin

**Documented text.** Reginald Scot translated and adapted Weyer in Book XV, chapter 2 of *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584). He writes **Gusoin**, still number 8.

Scot’s first-edition pagination places the notice on p. 378; it appears on p. 315 of Brinsley Nicholson’s 1886 reprint. The [Gutenberg transcription](https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/60766/pg60766-images.html) retains both pagination systems.

## 5. The seventeenth-century Goetia spellings

The manuscript witnesses do not agree:

| Witness | Approximate date | Reading |
|---|---:|---|
| Sloane 3825 | seventeenth century | **Gusoin** |
| Sloane 3648 | after c. 1665 | **Gusion** |
| Sloane 2731 | begun 1686/87 | **Gusion** |
| Sloane 3805 | 1685, fragmentary | probably Goetia-family reading |
| Harley 6483 | Peter Smart transcript, 1712–13 | **Gusoin** |
| Wellcome 3203 | Hockley/Lea copy, nineteenth century | **Gusoin** |
| Northwestern University MS 65 | later witness | **Gusoin** |

Peterson’s [comparative table](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia_spirits.pdf) is especially useful here. He judges Sloane 3825 the most accurate witness when it can be compared with known source material; Sloane 3648 frequently modernizes or corrupts readings. Thus the modern canonical **Gusion** may itself reflect a copying variation that became standard through Sloane 2731 and Mathers.

## 6. Gusayn and other later forms

**Documented late variant.** A. E. Waite records “Gusion, otherwise **Gusayn**” and mentions **Pirsoyn** from another manuscript. These forms did not displace Gusion in the twentieth-century tradition.

**Modern instability.** Forms such as **Augusyon** occur in modern summaries but have no comparable standing in the principal source chain.

## 7. Etymology

### No demonstrated ancient god, hero, or place

**Negative finding.** Unlike Baal, Astarte/Astaroth, Amun/Amon, Asmodeus/Ashmedai, Belial, or Phoenix, Gusion has no securely demonstrated derivation from a named ancient deity, biblical being, classical hero, or geographical place.

The earliest evidence is a series of late-medieval catalogue names—Cason and Gazon—followed by Gusoyn. No ancient inscription, classical text, rabbinic demon list, or late-antique magical papyrus has been connected to it by a defensible philological argument.

### Possibilities that remain unproved

- French **gazon**, “turf/sod,” is a real word of Germanic origin. **Disputed:** nothing in the spirit’s office connects it to turf, and the form may simply be accidental.
- Parthian **gōsān**, “minstrel/poet-musician,” is an ancient word discussed by Mary Boyce in [*Encyclopaedia Iranica*](https://www.iranicaonline.org/articles/gosan/). **Disputed:** phonetic resemblance is interesting, but there is no known transmission route or semantic connection to the divinatory Duke.
- Greek-looking interpretations based on *xenos* belong to the appearance-word *zenophali/Xenophilus*, not securely to the name Gusion.

### Modern unsupported meanings

The following are **modern inventions or unsupported conjectures**, not early etymologies:

- “Little dove.”
- “Gluttony.”
- “The great.”
- A derivation from *Gasam*, “craftsman.”
- Identification with Typhon, Thoth, Tiamat, Ma’at, Themis, Nemesis, or an unnamed “primal evolutionary force.”
- A connection to Daʿath based on the spirit’s alleged “hidden will.”
- A fixed pronunciation such as “GOO-see-on” presented as ancient.

Such claims appear in recent practitioner compilations, including the [Devil’s Library entry](https://www.thedevilslibrary.com/t/gusion-gusoin-gusoyn-gusayn-cason-gazon-gorsay-gorsin/409), but no medieval or early-modern evidence is supplied.

# The description across the sources

## 1. *Livre des Esperitz*: Gazon

**Documented text, fifteenth century.** Boudet’s critical transcription gives Gazon as a great Duke who answers truly concerning past, future, and present things, gives grace and love among all persons, elevates people to great honors and dignities, and has forty legions.

What is already present:

- Duke.
- Temporal knowledge.
- Social favor.
- Honor and dignity.
- Forty legions.

What is absent:

- *Zenophali/Xenophilus* appearance.
- A seal.
- A demanded second form.
- Planet, metal, zodiac, tarot, or angel.
- A Gusion-specific ritual hour.

This is the essential textual nucleus from which the later description developed.

## 2. Munich CLM 849: Cason

**Documented name; reconstructed equivalence.** Cason occurs in a short *liber officiorum* group. The name is aligned with Gusion because other names in the same sequence correspond to spirits in the Weyer/Goetia family. The Munich form is also important for the obscure appearance word: Peterson reports **senaphali**, supporting the view that Scot’s “Xenophilus” arose from an already-corrupt or technical term.

## 3. Weyer, 1577: Latin

**Documented text.** Weyer’s entry begins:

> “Gusoyn Dux magnus & fortis, apparet in forma zenophali.”

He continues that the spirit answers explicitly and truly concerning present, past, future, and hidden things; restores or produces the favor of friends and enemies; confers dignities and confirms honors; and presides over forty-five legions.

A close English rendering is:

> Gusoyn is a great and strong Duke, appearing in the form of a *zenophalus*. He answers explicitly and truly about present, past, future, and hidden matters. He restores the favor of friends and enemies, confers dignities, confirms honors, and rules forty-five legions.

Edition: Johann Weyer, *De praestigiis daemonum*, fifth edition, Basel, 1577, *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*, cols. 915–916; [Latin transcription and apparatus](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

### Notable Weyer readings

- **fortis** — strong or powerful.
- **occultis** — hidden matters, absent or weakened in some later English versions.
- **amicorum et inimicorum gratiam** — favor of friends and enemies. Scot compresses this to reconciling friendship.
- **dignitates confert et honores conformat** — confers dignities and confirms or establishes honors.
- **quadragintaquinque legionibus** — forty-five legions.

## 4. Scot, 1584: English

**Documented text.** Scot writes:

> “Gusoin is a great duke, and a strong, appearing in the forme of a Xenophilus…”

He says that Gusoin answers all things present, past, and future; expounds questions; reconciles friendship; distributes honors and dignities; and rules forty legions of devils.

Edition: Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (London: William Brome, 1584), Book XV, ch. 2, first-edition p. 378; Nicholson reprint p. 315. See [Scot’s text at Esoteric Archives](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/scot16.htm) and the [1886 reprint](https://gutenberg.org/cache/epub/60766/pg60766-images.html).

### Scot’s changes

- **Gusoyn → Gusoin.**
- **zenophali → Xenophilus.**
- Weyer’s hidden matters disappear explicitly, although “all things” can be read broadly.
- Favor of friends and enemies becomes the narrower “reconcileth freendship.”
- Forty-five becomes **forty**.

The bracketed “[and five]” sometimes found in modern transcriptions is an editorial restoration from Weyer, not Scot’s printed reading.

## 5. Sloane 3825 and the Goetia text

**Documented manuscript text.** Peterson’s principal transcription reads in substance:

> “The 11th spirit is a great & strong duke called Gusoin…”

It continues: he appears like a Xenophilus; tells all things past, present, and to come; shows the meaning of every question; reconciles friends; gives honor and dignity; and rules forty legions. The seal is to be worn “as aforesaid.”

Source: British Library Sloane MS 3825, fol. 102r in Peterson’s transcription, [*Lemegeton*, Part I](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm#gusion).

This is much closer to Scot than to Weyer and demonstrates direct or indirect dependence on the English translation.

### Sloane 2731 and Sloane 3648

**Documented variation.**

- Both favor **Gusion** rather than Gusoin.
- The offices remain essentially the same.
- Both preserve a seal.
- Sloane 2731 was begun in January 1686/87 and was compiled from multiple exemplars.
- Sloane 3648 contains numerous readings Peterson regards as secondary errors.

The manuscripts are described in Peterson’s [Lemegeton source survey](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm).

## 6. Harley 6483: the Rudd recension

**Documented text and esoteric expansion.** Skinner and Rankine print:

> “The Eleventh Spirit is a great and strong Duke called Gusoin…”

The remainder again states the Xenophilus appearance, temporal knowledge, interpretation of questions, reconciliation of friendships, granting of honor and dignities, forty legions, and the wearable seal.

Edition: Stephen Skinner and David Rankine, eds., *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (Golden Hoard Press, 2007), p. 113.

Harley 6483 adds what the ordinary Sloane Goetias do not: the paired angelic name and seal, its psalm, Hebrew lettering, and an elaborate apparatus intended to place each demon under divine and angelic authority.

## 7. Mathers–Crowley, 1904

**Documented printed text.** The 1904 edition begins:

> “GUSION.—The Eleventh Spirit in order is a great and strong Duke, called Gusion.”

It says he appears like a Xenopilus; tells all things past, present, and to come; shows the meaning and resolution of all questions; conciliates and reconciles friendships; gives honor and dignity; rules forty legions; and has a seal to be worn as previously directed.

Edition: S. L. MacGregor Mathers, trans.; Aleister Crowley, ed., *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King* (Foyers, Inverness: Society for the Propagation of Religious Truth, 1904), printed p. 16; [facsimile PDF](https://lyricaltokarev.com/public/dynamic/sync/stuff/pdf-documents-library/ac_goetia.pdf); [Wikisource scan](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/File:The_Book_of_the_Goetia_(Mathers-Crowley,_1904).djvu).

Mathers’ distinctive contributions include:

- Standardizing **Gusion**.
- Printing **Xenopilus**, without *h*.
- Adding “resolution” to the explanation of questions.
- Using “conciliateth and reconcileth,” which combines related source readings.
- Presenting a carefully redrawn printable seal.

## 8. Peterson, 2001

**Scholarly edition.** Joseph H. Peterson’s *The Lesser Key of Solomon: Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis* (Weiser, 2001) collates the principal British Library manuscripts and avoids treating Mathers’ composite spelling as a single authoritative manuscript reading. Its Gusion/Gusoin text follows Sloane 3825 while recording Weyer, Scot, and other manuscript variants.

The publisher identifies the edition as a manuscript-based critical presentation; see [Red Wheel/Weiser](https://redwheelweiser.com/book/the-lesser-key-of-solomon-9781578632206/) and Peterson’s continually corrected [online edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm).

## 10. Offices not belonging to Gusion

**Negative finding.** The source chain does not give Gusion:

- Languages.
- Liberal or mechanical sciences.
- Treasure-finding.
- Familiars.
- Invisibility.
- Metallurgical transmutation.
- Healing.
- Love or sexual compulsion.
- Transportation.
- Necromancy.
- Weather control.

Those offices belong to other Goetic spirits. Modern lists sometimes aggregate generic Goetic powers into Gusion’s entry; that is not supported by Gazon, Weyer, Scot, or the Lemegeton.

## 11. Second form

**Negative finding.** Gusion has no “first animal form, then human form” clause. The catalogues simply say *zenophali/Xenophilus*. General Goetic conjurations demand that spirits appear in a “fair and comely shape,” but that ritual formula is not a second iconographic description specific to Gusion.

# The seal

## Earliest status

**Documented text.** Neither the *Livre des Esperitz*, the Munich list, Weyer, nor Scot gives Gusion a seal. The surviving seals belong to the seventeenth-century English Goetia tradition.

## Basic printed design

In Mathers–Crowley’s 1904 form, Gusion’s seal is a circular lamen containing a bilaterally organized linear figure. Its most conspicuous features are:

- A long barred upper structure.
- Curled or hooked terminals.
- Descending supports or strokes.
- A lower connecting bar.
- Small loop or ring-like nodes.
- The spirit’s identifying lettering arranged in the enclosing border in the printed recension.

The public-domain printed figure can be inspected directly at [From Old Books](https://www.fromoldbooks.org/Mathers-Goetia/pages/011-Seal-of-Gusion/) or [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:11-Gusion_seal.png).

## Manuscript variation

**Documented scribal variation.** Sloane 3825, Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, and Harley 6483 preserve recognizably related figures, but proportions, curls, crossings, terminal loops, and surrounding circles differ. There is no evidence that these tiny graphic differences encode separate Gusions or separate offices. They are normal variations produced by hand-copying a nonalphabetic character.

**Scholarly caution.** Mathers’ figure is an editorially regularized print design, not a photographic reproduction of a single “original seal.”

## Rudd’s double-sided construction

**Documented esoteric practice.** In Harley 6483:

- The demon’s seal is placed outward.
- The corresponding Shem angel’s seal and name appear on the reverse, toward the magician’s heart.
- Hebrew forms and divine names surround or accompany the figures.
- The angelic side is intended to constrain or balance the spirit.

Skinner and Rankine interpret this as Rudd’s central operational innovation: each demon is checked by a specific angelic authority. This construction is not found in Weyer, Scot, or the ordinary Sloane Goetias.

## 2. The 72-fold name

### Biblical raw material

**Documented interpretive tradition.** Exodus 14:19–21 contains three Hebrew verses of seventy-two letters each in the Masoretic consonantal text. In the standard construction:

1. Verse 19 is written in one direction.
2. Verse 20 is written in reverse.
3. Verse 21 is written in the first direction.
4. The vertically aligned letters produce seventy-two triplets.

This arrangement is commonly called boustrophedonic. Rashi’s commentary on Sukkah 45a explicitly connects *Ani* and *Va-Ho* with names extracted from these verses; see [Rashi on Sukkah 45a](https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Sukkah.45).

**Terminological caution.** It is more exact to call this a **216-letter construction divided into seventy-two three-letter names** than one name containing only seventy-two letters.

### From divine triplets to angels

**Tradition and historical development.** The triplets were not originally a list of seventy-two Goetic counter-angels. Western Christian kabbalists expanded the forms by adding divine suffixes:

- **-El**, “God.”
- **-Yah/-Iah**, a divine-name element.

Thus the triplet לאו becomes a name written לאויה and transliterated Laviah/Lauviah.

The attributed English meaning is not a literal dictionary translation of the three consonants. It is an occult divine epithet attached to the name.

## 3. Historical transmission

### Rashi, the *Bahir*, and medieval Jewish tradition

- **Documented Jewish exegesis:** Rashi, d. 1105, describes the extraction from Exodus.
- **Scholarly reconstruction:** the *Sefer ha-Bahir*, circulated in Provence around 1150–1200, is among the earliest extant kabbalistic works to develop the seventy-twofold construction systematically.
- **Tradition:** *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* incorporates collections of divine and angelic names and helped transmit the 72-name complex. Its surviving form is composite and medieval; the book’s legendary attribution to the angel Raziel and Adam is not historical authorship.
- **Negative finding:** these Jewish sources do not contain a Goetic demon named Gusion or the Rudd pairing.

### Reuchlin and Agrippa

**Documented Christian-kabbalistic development.**

- Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517) helped popularize the 72 names in Christian kabbalah.
- Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia*, Book III, chapter 25, presents the angelized names with suffixes and zodiacal/angelological applications.

A modern scholarly study of Reuchlin and the seventy-two angels is [“72 in His Name”](https://e-univers.ru/upload/iblock/919/1d14cc5s1qdrw3vc1f1360nmgkaohtru.pdf).

### Blaise de Vigenère

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Material associated with Blaise de Vigenère preserves angelic names and seals that were subsequently copied or adapted by Rudd. Skinner and Rankine regard Vigenère’s papers as an important proximate source for the Rudd angelic figures.

### Kircher, 1652–54

**Documented Christian-kabbalistic synthesis.** Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* reproduces extensive Christianized Cabala, including the seventy-twofold name. Kircher’s treatment is polemical and syncretic: he records the system while condemning magical abuses of it. See his [*Cabala Hebraeorum*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/kircher/cabala.htm) and the [1652–54 bibliographic record](https://onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu/webbin/book/lookupid?key=olbp67641).

Kircher is important to Lenain’s later synthesis, but there is no evidence that Kircher paired Lauviah with Gusion.

### Thomas Rudd / Harley 6483

**Documented esoteric correspondence.** The first securely attested scheme that places one of the seventy-two angels against each of the seventy-two Goetic spirits is the material attributed to Thomas Rudd.

Harley 6483 is not Rudd’s autograph. It is a fair copy made by the lawyer Peter Smart in 1712–13 from material attributed to or owned by Rudd, who died in 1656. The degree to which every element is personally Rudd’s therefore remains uncertain.

For spirit 11, Harley 6483 gives:

- Demon: **Gusoin**.
- Angel: **Laviah/Lauviah**.
- Psalm formula beginning *Vivit Dominus…*.
- Angelic seal on the reverse of the demon’s lamen.

**Traditional rationale.** The magician invokes divine and angelic authority before evoking the demon. The angel is treated as the demon’s “antidote,” ruler, or constraining intelligence.

**Historical boundary.** This is a Rudd-layer correspondence, not a feature of Weyer, Scot, or the base Sloane Goetia.

### Lenain, 1823

Lazare Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* gives the eleventh angel as **Lauviah**:

- Attribute: *Dieu loué et exalté*.
- Sacred name: *Deus*, in the language of the Latins.
- Ray: degrees 51–55 inclusive.
- Associated decan genius: **Rombomaré**.
- Planetary influence: **Saturn**.
- Five recurring calendar dates: March 30, June 10, August 20, November 1, January 12.
- Favorable daily time: 3:20–3:40.
- Psalm: identified as the fiftieth verse of Psalm 17.
- Latin formula: *Vivit Dominus et benedictus Deus meus, et exaltetur Deus salutis meae.*

See Lenain, *La science cabalistique* (Amiens, 1823), p. 52, in the [IAPSOP scan](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1823__lenain___science_cabalistique.pdf) and [Google Books record](https://books.google.com/books/about/La_science_cabalistique_ou_l_art_de_conn.html?id=BqzyzEEX3MAC).

**Important distinction.** Lenain’s five separated dates and 51st–55th “degrees of the sphere” are not yet the simplified modern rule “May 11–15, 20°–25° Taurus.”

### Golden Dawn

**Documented esoteric correspondence.** The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn attached pairs of Shem names to the thirty-six tarot decans, beginning the sequence at 0° Leo. Consequently:

- Lauviah 11 rules 20°–25° Virgo.
- Hahaiah 12 rules 25°–30° Virgo.
- Together they rule the third decan of Virgo.
- Its card is the **Ten of Pentacles**, Mercury in Virgo, “Lord of Wealth.”

*Book T* states that Lauviah and Hahaiah are set over this decan as angel rulers; see the [Golden Dawn/Book T text](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/publication/english/Liber_LXXVIII.html).

This differs from Gusion’s separate *777* placement in Cancer.

### Papus

**Tradition.** Papus reproduced and popularized Christian-kabbalistic angel tables in works such as *La Cabbale* (1903), drawing substantially on earlier occult compilations including Lenain. His importance is transmission and popularization, not creation of the Gusion–Lauviah pairing. A scan is available [here](https://www.cercle-papus.com/ouvrages/papus-la-cabbale.pdf).

### Ambelain

**Modern esoteric system.** Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) systematized the angels by choirs, archangels, dates, offices, and ritual prayers. In this structure, angel 11 belongs among the Cherubim under Jophiel or Raziel, depending on the table used. Ambelain helped normalize the continuous five-day guardian-angel calendar and expanded moral and practical offices.

These are twentieth-century occult correspondences, not medieval Jewish definitions.

### Haziel

**Modern angelology.** François Bernard-Termes, writing as **Haziel** (1925–2015), popularized a devotional guardian-angel system derived substantially from Kabaleb and the French occult tradition. In this literature Lauviah 11 becomes an angel of victory, success, fame, constructive realization, memory, and protection, commonly assigned May 11–15.

These expansive offices postdate Lenain and Rudd. They should not be retrojected into the medieval Shem tradition. A representative attribution citing Haziel appears [here](https://soleilverseau.com/2021/08/28/priere-a-lange-du-jour-28-aout-lauviah-cherubin/amp/).

## 4. Psalm problem

The Rudd/Lenain formula corresponds to:

> “The LORD liveth; and blessed be my rock; and let the God of my salvation be exalted.”

In common modern English numbering this is **Psalm 18:46**. In the Greek/Vulgate sequence, where Psalms 9 and 10 are combined, it belongs to **Psalm 17**.

However, Lenain calls it the “50th verse” of Psalm 17. That does not match the usual modern verse division, where *Vivit Dominus* is verse 47 in the Vulgate. Possible explanations include:

- Counting the superscription or liturgical lines differently.
- Dependence on a source with a nonstandard division.
- A copying or editorial mistake.

Therefore the safest citation is:

- **Textual incipit:** *Vivit Dominus et benedictus Deus meus…*
- **Modern Bible reference:** Psalm 18:46.
- **Lenain/Rudd label:** Psalm 17, verse 50.
- **Status:** documented discrepancy, unresolved.

## 5. Two Lauviahs

**Documented ambiguity.** The traditional sequence contains another angel commonly transliterated **Lauviah** at number 17. The two are distinguished by number, Hebrew spelling traditions, meaning, psalm, choir, and office. Modern tables frequently regularize one as Laviah, Lauviah, Laoviah, or Lauvuel to reduce confusion.

For Gusion, the relevant angel is unequivocally **number 11**, not number 17.

## Gusion in Crowley’s *777*

Table V, columns CLVII–CLVIII of *Liber 777* distributes the spirits across the thirty-six decans:

- Spirits 1–36 are assigned “by day.”
- Spirits 37–72 are assigned “by night.”
- Gusion 11 and Uvall 47 occupy the same succedent Cancer decan.
- The entry preserves Gusion’s Venus/Duke glyph and “Xenopilus” image.

The source is [*Liber 777*](https://hermetic.com/_media/93beast.fea.st/files/section1/777/liber_777.pdf); the night table is also legible in [*777 Revised*](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/aa/documents/Student_Reading_Curriculum/02_Liber_777_DCCLXXVII_Revised.pdf).

### Why “quinance” causes confusion

A decan is ten degrees; a quinance is five. *777* gives Gusion and Uvall the same full decan distinguished by day and night. Later tables sometimes split it:

- Gusion: 10°–15° Cancer.
- Uvall: 15°–20° Cancer.

That division is mathematically tidy but is not printed in the original *777* table. The strongest historical statement is therefore **Cancer II by day**, not an exclusive five-degree span.

## Tarot pip

Cancer II is the **Three of Cups**:

- Sign: Cancer.
- Decan: 10°–20°.
- Decan ruler: Mercury.
- Golden Dawn title: **Lord of Abundance**.
- Day spirit: Gusion.
- Night spirit: Uvall.

This “two spirits per pip” system concerns demons. It is independent of *Book T*’s separate pairing of two Shem angels with each tarot decan.

## Angelic zodiac alternatives

### Golden Dawn system

Starting angel 1 at 0° Leo puts Lauviah 11 at:

- 20°–25° Virgo.
- Ten of Pentacles.
- Mercury in Virgo.
- First angelic half of the decan.
- Hahaiah 12 governs the second half.

### Aries-zero system

Starting angel 1 at 0° Aries puts Lauviah 11 at:

- 20°–25° Taurus.
- Seven of Pentacles.
- Saturn in Taurus.
- Later calendar equivalents around May 10/11–14/15.

Skinner and Rankine explicitly discuss competing starting points, including 0° Aries, 0° Leo, and a Sirius-related starting point. Consequently no five-degree zodiac span should be described as the unique ancient attribution.

## Astrological timing of conjuration

A historically layered answer is necessary:

1. **Goetia manuscript rule:** Duke, sunrise to noon, clear weather.
2. **Planetary rank overlay:** Venus, hence copper.
3. **Later practice:** Friday or an hour of Venus.
4. ***777* decan:** Cancer 10°–20°, by day.
5. **Rudd/angelic practice:** invoke Lauviah’s authority before Gusion.
6. **Lenain angelic practice:** 3:20–3:40 with the assigned psalm.
7. **Modern guardian-calendar practice:** May 11–15.

No single early source combines all seven. A contemporary table that presents them together is a synthetic magical system.

## Enochian overlays

**Later esoteric synthesis.** The original Gazon–Weyer–Scot chain contains nothing Enochian. Enochian connections enter through later English ceremonial magic:

- Rudd’s related Harley material attempts to coordinate Solomonic workings with John Dee’s tables and the *Tabula Sancta*.
- Crowley added material to his 1904 publication that was not part of the base Goetia, including a preliminary invocation with a separate ritual history.
- Lon Milo DuQuette’s *Tarot of Ceremonial Magick* deliberately synthesizes tarot decans, Goetic spirits, Shem angels, and Enochian rulers.

These overlays are historically interesting but should not be described as seventeenth-century essentials unless a particular Rudd manuscript supports them.

# Iconography

## 1. *Zenophali*, Xenophilus, Xenopilus

The source sequence is:

1. Munich CLM 849: **senaphali**.
2. Weyer, 1577: **zenophali**.
3. Scot, 1584: **Xenophilus**.
4. English Goetia manuscripts: **Xenophilus**.
5. Mathers–Crowley: **Xenopilus**.

### The philosopher Xenophilus

Xenophilus of Chalcidice was a fourth-century BCE Pythagorean philosopher and musician. **Disputed:** nothing in Weyer suggests that Gusoyn appears as this historical man. Skinner and Rankine explicitly warn against the identification.

### Dog-headed or baboon interpretation

Peterson tentatively suggests comparison with **cynocephalus/zenocephalus**, “dog-headed.” This could explain modern images of:

- A cynocephalic man.
- A baboon.
- A dog-headed humanoid.
- An ape.

But no surviving source actually says *cynocephalus*. The dog-headed reading is a scholarly or occult emendation of a corrupt word, not an established translation.

### “Xenophile” and extraterrestrial hybrids

Modern sources occasionally reinterpret “Xenophilus” as “xenophile” or an extraterrestrial hybrid. That is a **modern invention** based on resemblance to a modern word.

## 2. Collin de Plancy

The *Dictionnaire infernal* first appeared in 1818 and went through multiple ideological and editorial revisions. In the illustrated sixth edition of 1863, de Plancy writes of **Gusoyn**:

> “Gusoyn, grand-duc aux enfers. Il apparaît sous la forme d’un chameau.”

He continues that Gusoyn answers concerning present, past, and future, discovers hidden things, increases dignities, establishes honors, and commands forty-five legions.

Edition: J.-A.-S. Collin de Plancy, *Dictionnaire infernal*, sixth edition (Paris, 1863), p. 316; [Wikisource page image](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Jacques_Collin_de_Plancy_-_Dictionnaire_infernal.pdf/324); [complete scan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e0/Jacques_Collin_de_Plancy_-_Dictionnaire_infernal.pdf).

### The camel

**Documented de Plancy variant.** The camel is absent from Gazon, Weyer, Scot, and the Goetia.

**Disputed origin.** It may be:

- An unexplained editorial reinterpretation.
- A confusion with a nearby camel/dromedary-riding spirit.
- A source no longer identifiable.
- A simple dictionary-entry error.

It should not be harmonized automatically with *zenophali*.

### No Louis Le Breton portrait

**Negative finding.** Although the 1863 edition contains Louis Le Breton’s famous engraved portraits, Gusion/Gusoyn does not receive one. The verified entry is text-only. Images advertised as “de Plancy’s portrait of Gusion” are therefore misattributed unless they come from another artist or edition.

## 3. Manuscript seals as iconography

Before the modern period, Gusion’s only stable individual image is the seal. No medieval manuscript portrait has been identified. The seal consequently became the basis for:

- Talismans and jewelry.
- Tattoo designs.
- Magical card decks.
- Vectorized occult symbols.
- Decorative “demonic alphabets.”
- Game and comic design references.

## 4. Modern visual reinventions

**Modern invention.** Recent artists variously render Gusion as:

- A baboon or ape, reflecting the cynocephalus conjecture.
- A dog-headed noble.
- A handsome crowned Duke.
- A camel.
- A robed oracle or judge.
- A many-animal chimera.
- A blue or purple humanoid.
- An abstract Venusian intelligence.

None is an “original Goetia portrait.”

The *Bloodstained* art books depict the game’s ape-like form, while *Mobile Suit Gundam: Iron-Blooded Orphans* converts the name and number into a heavily armored machine.

# People and works

## Grimoire ancestors and related traditions

### Testament of Solomon

**Documented ancient/late-antique Solomonic tradition; negative Gusion finding.** The Greek *Testament of Solomon* narrates Solomon compelling demons, learning their names and offices, and discovering the angels who thwart them. Its date and compositional history are disputed, with material ranging from late antiquity into medieval recensions.

It is an important conceptual ancestor for:

- Solomon as demon-master.
- The magical ring.
- Demonic self-identification.
- Angelic adversaries.
- Compelled labor on the Temple.

But **Gusion, Gusoyn, Gazon, and Cason do not appear in its known demon lists**. See the [Conybeare translation](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Testament_of_Solomon) and [McCown’s manuscript edition record](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL6652835M/The_Testament_of_Solomon).

### Ashmedai traditions

The Babylonian Talmud’s Ashmedai story in Gittin 68 is another Solomonic demon-binding narrative. It does not supply Gusion’s name or offices.

### Munich manual

**Probable direct family evidence.** CLM 849’s Cason is the earliest Latin cognate presently identified.

### *Livre des Esperitz*

**Strongest direct ancestor.** Gazon supplies the distinctive office complex and forty-legion count.

Boudet concludes that the *Livre* and Weyer’s catalogue are distinct versions of a common type of text rather than one being a simple French translation of the other. Thirty-five spirits correspond, sometimes principally through functions where the names are badly deformed.

### *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

Weyer says his material came from, or resembled, a *Liber officiorum spirituum* or Book of the Offices of Spirits. Several fragmentary and derivative witnesses survive. The title denotes a textual family more than one recoverable pristine book.

Cason’s short Munich list is one such precursor. Sloane 3824 and later English books preserve related materials. See Peterson and Daniel Harms’ work on the tradition and the historical summary in [the *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* record](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Liber_Officiorum_Spirituum).

### Abramelin

**Negative finding.** No secure Gusion/Gusoyn equivalent was located in the principal Abramelin spirit lists. Similar-looking forms in large corrupted name catalogues are insufficient without matching offices or a textual bridge.

### *Book of Oberon*

Related sixteenth-century English spirit material shares names and practices with the *Liber Officiorum* family, but no strong Gusion-specific witness comparable to Gazon or Cason was identified.

## Historical authors and editors

### Johann Weyer

Weyer was a physician and critic of witch persecution, not simply a compiler advocating demon evocation. His *Pseudomonarchia* was printed within a work attacking credulous and cruel witchcraft doctrines.

**Historical irony:** the catalogue he printed as evidence of conjurors’ folly became one of the most influential practical demon lists.

### Reginald Scot

Scot translated and reorganized Weyer within another skeptical work. He intended to expose magical pretensions and oppose witch persecution. His lucid English nevertheless furnished later magicians with a ready-made spirit catalogue.

### Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

Rudd is credited with integrating the Goetic spirits, seventy-two Shem angels, psalms, and counter-seals. The surviving Harley witnesses complicate personal attribution:

- Material is attributed to Rudd.
- Peter Smart copied it after Rudd’s death.
- Some components may reflect Smart, an intermediate exemplar, or a broader magical school.

### Collin de Plancy

De Plancy repackaged demonology for a nineteenth-century dictionary-reading public. His editions changed with his religious position. The camel and restoration of Weyer’s forty-five legions are his main Gusion-specific deviations.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers

Mathers collated several English manuscripts and prepared the Goetia translation during the Golden Dawn period, probably by 1898. He standardized names and seals and integrated Golden Dawn terminology.

There is no documentary evidence that Mathers personally evoked Gusion.

### Aleister Crowley

Crowley published Mathers’ text in 1904, supplied prefatory and ritual material, and later placed Gusion in the decanic tables of *777*. His psychological essay on demons as portions of the human brain is one interpretation among several, not the view of Weyer or the manuscript compiler.

There is no surviving Gusion-specific magical record comparable to Crowley’s records for some other operations.

### Frater Achad

**Negative finding.** Frater Achad—Charles Stansfeld Jones—wrote extensively on Thelemic Qabalah, but no reliable Gusion-specific working or interpretation was located. Merely appearing within the broader Crowley milieu does not establish work with this spirit.

### Poke Runyon

Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* presents a modern operational Goetia incorporating mirror skrying and reconstructed Solomonic ritual. Its Gusion table uses:

- 20°–24° Taurus.
- Lauiah.
- Venus/copper by rank.
- A modernized ritual framework.

Runyon is important to late-twentieth-century practical revival, but the Taurus quinance is a later systematic distribution, not part of Weyer or the base Goetia.

### Lon Milo DuQuette

DuQuette co-authored *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* and developed a broader synthesis in *Tarot of Ceremonial Magick*. His work helped circulate:

- Decanic Goetia.
- Shem angels.
- Enochian rulers.
- Tarot pips.
- Planetary attributions.

These are deliberately synthetic correspondences.

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 edition restored the whole Lemegeton to manuscript-based study and made comparison among Sloane witnesses, Weyer, Scot, and related texts much easier. His online edition continues to record corrections and newly noticed parallels.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their 2007 *Goetia of Dr Rudd* brought Harley 6483’s demon–angel system into print and emphasized that the reverse-side angelic seals materially change how the magic is structured.

They also caution against easy Hebrew etymologies for unstable demon names: most of Rudd’s Hebrew spellings are conjectural transliterations, not recoverable ancient Semitic originals.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s *Encyclopaedia Goetica*, *Geosophia*, and related writings argue for understanding grimoire spirits within a much longer history of *goēs*, necromancy, hero cult, and chthonic religion.

That is a broad historical-esoteric reconstruction. It does not establish that Gusion specifically was an ancient god or hero. Gusion’s demonstrable name history still begins in the late-medieval catalogue family.

### Owen Davies

Davies’ *Grimoires: A History of Magic Books* and later work situate Weyer and Scot in the history of print, skepticism, censorship, and the democratization of learned magic. This context is essential: the two men who transmitted Gusoyn/Gusoin most effectively did so while criticizing magical belief.

### Jan Veenstra and Julien Véronèse

Veenstra’s work on Solomonic manuscripts and the metamorphosis of magic illuminates how the Lemegeton was assembled from earlier textual blocks. Véronèse’s studies of medieval ritual magic provide the larger manuscript context.

For Gusion’s particular genealogy, however, the most directly relevant published study located is **Jean-Patrice Boudet’s** edition and comparison of the *Livre des Esperitz* and Weyer.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Was Gusion first attested in 1577?

**Answer:** not if Gazon and Cason are accepted as cognates.

- **Certain printed attestation:** Gusoyn, 1577.
- **Strong earlier cognate:** Gazon, fifteenth-century *Livre des Esperitz*.
- **Probable earlier cognate:** Cason, fifteenth-century Munich manual.
- **No ancient attestation:** none presently known.

The source-critical answer is therefore: **the spelling Gusoyn begins in Weyer, but the spirit’s textual profile is older.**

## 2. Is the name connected to an ancient god?

No defensible connection has been established. Claims involving Typhon, Thoth, Tiamat, Ma’at, or an ancient “Gusion” deity are modern speculative theology.

## 3. What is a Xenophilus?

Unresolved.

Possibilities include:

- A corrupt technical creature-name.
- An attempted Latinization of a dog-headed form.
- A scribal development of *senaphali/zenophali*.
- Scot’s mistaken interpretation as a proper noun.
- Less plausibly, the philosopher Xenophilus.

The dog-headed reading is possible, not certain. The ape/baboon image is one step further removed.

## 4. Why 40 versus 45 legions?

The likely direction is:

- *Livre des Esperitz*: 40.
- Weyer: 45.
- Scot: 40.
- Goetia: 40.
- de Plancy: 45, following Weyer.

Weyer may have had a variant exemplar, deliberately altered the figure, or made a printing/copying error. The recurrence of forty before and after him favors forty as the older and more persistent reading, but no autograph source settles the matter.

## 5. Is de Plancy’s camel authentic?

It is authentic **to de Plancy’s 1863 text**, but not to the older Gusion tradition. Its source is unknown. There is no Le Breton portrait to clarify what sort of camel was intended.

## 6. Did a misprint create “Gusion”?

Possibly a copying modernization, but “misprint” is too strong without proof.

- Gusoin is the best-supported English source reading.
- Gusion appears in Sloane 3648 and 2731.
- Mathers standardized it.
- The printed 1904 book made it internationally dominant.

Thus **Gusion may be a secondary manuscript spelling that became canonical**, not necessarily a compositor’s one-time error.

## 7. Are Gorsay and Gorsin variants of Gusion?

Probably not. The *Livre* already contains Gazon with Gusion’s offices. Treating its separate Gorsay/Gorsin as the same spirit creates a conflation of two entries.

## 8. Did Scot say 45 legions?

No. Scot’s printed text says forty. Modern editions sometimes add “[and five]” editorially to restore Weyer’s Latin.

## 9. Is the Shem angel pairing Jewish antiquity?

No evidence supports that.

- The 72 divine triplets have medieval Jewish roots.
- Angelized names were developed and circulated through Jewish and Christian-kabbalistic channels.
- The one-to-one opposition of Lauviah to Gusion is attested in the Rudd material.
- It is absent from the early Jewish sources, Weyer, Scot, and ordinary Goetia manuscripts.

## 10. Is Lauviah 20°–25° Taurus or Virgo?

Both occur in later systems:

- Taurus: Aries-zero sequence.
- Virgo: Golden Dawn’s Leo-zero sequence.
- Lenain: degrees 51–55 of a sphere plus dispersed calendar days.
- Rudd manuscripts do not eliminate the starting-point problem.

Any claim that one is the uncontested ancient placement is false.

## 11. Is the psalm Psalm 17:50 or Psalm 18:46?

The words match Psalm 18:46 in common modern numbering and Psalm 17 in Vulgate numbering. “Verse 50” appears in Lenain/Rudd-derived tradition but conflicts with standard modern verse divisions.

## 12. Does Gusion seduce women and drive them to suicide?

**Modern invention.** This claim circulates in French-language web summaries but is absent from:

- Gazon.
- Weyer.
- Scot.
- Sloane 3825.
- Sloane 2731.
- Harley 6483.
- Mathers–Crowley.
- De Plancy.

It may result from confusion with spirits whose offices concern sexual love, or from unsourced modern folklore.

## 13. Is Gusion “friendly,” “safe,” or morally benevolent?

These are modern practitioner judgments. The historical catalogues state offices and ritual controls; they do not provide a stable personality profile. Contemporary reports range from benevolent counselor to dangerous infernal Duke and are personal testimony rather than independently verifiable history.

## 14. Is the “enn” ancient?

No. The formula commonly printed as **“Secore vesa anet Gusion”** belongs to modern demonolatry, particularly systems popularized in the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries. It is absent from every early source covered here.

## 15. Are Friday, sandalwood, green candles, water, and the west original?

Only Venus/copper has a direct base in the Goetia’s rank table. Friday, green, Venus hours, and related materials are normal planetary deductions. Sandalwood, particular candles, elements, directions, offerings, and stones vary among modern authors.

# Open questions

1. **Exact textual genealogy:** Did Weyer use a lost Latin version directly related to the French *Livre des Esperitz*, or did both descend from an earlier *Liber Officiorum*?

2. **Cason’s precise relation:** Is Munich Cason directly ancestral to Gazon/Gusoyn, or a collateral witness from the same catalogue family?

3. **Meaning of *zenophali*:** No secure medieval zoological or magical term has yet been demonstrated. The cynocephalus emendation remains attractive but unproved.

4. **Forty-five legions:** Why did Weyer depart from the apparent forty-legion antecedent?

5. **Seal archetype:** Which surviving Goetia manuscript most closely preserves the lost prototype of Gusion’s character?

6. **Camel source:** Did de Plancy possess another source, confuse Gusion with a camel-associated spirit, or simply improvise?

7. **Rudd’s authorship:** How much of Harley 6483’s angelic apparatus derives from Thomas Rudd, how much from Vigenère or another exemplar, and how much from Peter Smart?

8. **Psalm numbering:** What exact Bible or liturgical division produced Lenain’s “Psalm 17, verse 50”?

9. **Degree system:** Did Rudd intend 0° Aries, 0° Leo, a Sirius-related zero point, or no uniquely fixed tropical starting point?

10. **First use of “Gusayn”:** Waite records it, but the exact manuscript ancestry of the spelling requires further codicological work.

11. **Early de Plancy editions:** The 1863 wording is verified. A line-by-line comparison with the 1818 first edition could establish exactly when the camel first entered the entry.

12. **Modern media:** Hellboy and several Japanese uses need exact issue, episode, or game citations before more detailed claims can be made.

---

# Conclusions

The recoverable Gusion is not an ancient named god demoted into a demon. The earliest convincing evidence is the fifteenth-century Duke **Gazon**, accompanied by **Cason** in a related Latin handbook. Gazon already possesses nearly all of Gusion’s enduring offices and forty legions.

Weyer’s 1577 **Gusoyn** adds the enigmatic *zenophali* form and changes the legions to forty-five. Scot’s 1584 **Gusoin** transforms *zenophali* into “Xenophilus,” returns the count to forty, and supplies the English wording from which the seventeenth-century Goetia developed. The Goetia moves him from number 8 to number 11 and adds a seal. Mathers’ 1904 printing makes **Gusion** the standard spelling.

The paired angel **Lauviah 11** is not part of that early demonological ancestry. Its three-letter root belongs to the medieval Jewish seventy-twofold interpretation of Exodus; its expanded angelic personality developed through Christian kabbalah. The direct Gusion–Lauviah opposition is a Rudd-layer innovation preserved in Harley 6483. Lenain, the Golden Dawn, Papus, Ambelain, Haziel, and modern ceremonial magicians subsequently supplied differing degrees, dates, tarot placements, planets, offices, and devotional meanings.

The source record therefore contains several distinct Gusions:

- The late-medieval Duke Gazon.
- Weyer’s *zenophali* Gusoyn.
- Scot’s Xenophilus-shaped Gusoin.
- The sealed spirit 11 of the English Goetia.
- Rudd’s demon counterbalanced by Lauviah.
- De Plancy’s camel-shaped Gusoyn.
- Crowley’s day demon of Cancer II and the Three of Cups.
- Modern apes, judges, guardian spirits, Gundams, and game monsters.

They are historically connected, but they are not interchangeable.
