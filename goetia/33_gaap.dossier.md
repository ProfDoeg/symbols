# Gäap: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **[D—documented]** What a named manuscript or printed source actually says.
- **[SR—scholarly reconstruction]** A historical or philological identification proposed from textual comparison.
- **[T—tradition]** Material transmitted as authoritative within the magical or angelological tradition, without independent historical verification.
- **[EC—esoteric correspondence]** Astrological, planetary, tarot, or ritual assignments made within an occult system.
- **[Dis—disputed]** A contested reading, identification, attribution, or chronology.
- **[L—legend]** Narrative presented by a grimoire or later author but unsupported as history.
- **[MI—modern invention]** A post-nineteenth-century elaboration, creative adaptation, or new ritual overlay.

A note on quotations: the linked sources contain the complete public-domain texts. To keep quotation within reproduction limits, I give exact short excerpts, manuscript folios or printed pages, and complete clause-by-clause summaries rather than reproducing several hundred consecutive words from each online edition.

## The name and its origins

### 1. The earliest possible ancestor: Taob, fifteenth century

**[D]** The earliest currently recoverable form plausibly connected with Gaap is **Taob**—sometimes normalized or misreported as **Toab**—in Munich, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Clm 849, the so-called *Munich Handbook of Necromancy*. Richard Kieckhefer dates the relevant collection to the first half of the fifteenth century.

The text calls Taob great and a prince. It says that he appears “*in signo medici*” when assuming human form; is an excellent physician or instructor of women; makes women burn with love for men; can change their form until they reach their lovers; can make them sterile; and has twenty-five legions. See Kieckhefer’s edition of Clm 849 in [*Forbidden Rites*](https://www.degruyter.com/document/doi/10.1515/9780271065441/html?lang=en).

**[SR]** Taob is identified with Gaap because:

1. *Taob* is graphically close to Weyer’s alternate name *Tap*.
2. Both are princes.
3. Both assume a human form.
4. Both have erotic offices.
5. The phrase *in signo medici* resembles Weyer’s *in signo meridiei* closely enough to suggest copying or corruption.

**[Dis]** The identification is probable, not certain. Taob’s medical, transformative, and sterilizing offices, and his twenty-five legions, differ markedly from Gaap’s later sciences, invisibility, transportation, and sixty-six legions. Either the entry was substantially rewritten, or Taob and Gaap were originally separate names that became conflated.

The Latin phrase is itself problematic:

- *in signo medici* = “in the sign/form of a physician”;
- *in signo meridiei* = “in a meridional/southern sign,” or possibly “at midday.”

One reading may have developed from the other through scribal corruption.

### 2. Caap and Coap in the *Livre des Esperitz*

**[D]** The mid-sixteenth-century French *Livre des Esperitz*, Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29, contains two nearby names that matter:

- **Caap**, no. 26, is a great prince appearing as a knight. He gives true answers, brings gold and silver from wherever commanded, and has twenty legions.
- **Coap**, no. 23, is a great prince who procures women, causes them to come where desired, and has twenty-seven legions.

Jean-Patrice Boudet prints and compares these entries in [“Les who’s who démonologiques de la Renaissance et leurs ancêtres médiévaux”](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/1019).

**[SR]** Boudet’s catalogue comparison places **Caap** behind Weyer’s Gaap. Caap’s name and princely rank support the connection, while Coap’s erotic office resembles Taob.

**[Dis]** The evidence may point to contamination between two originally distinct entries. Later catalogues could have inherited:

- the name and princely rank from Caap;
- erotic functions from Coap or Taob;
- new philosophical and transportation offices from an intermediate *Liber officiorum spirituum*.

Caap’s appearance as a knight is not preserved in Weyer or the *Goetia*.

### 3. Gaap alias Tap in Weyer, 1577

**[D]** The first secure occurrence of the spelling **Gaap** is Johann Weyer’s *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*, first appended to the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum* in **1577**. The heading is:

> “Gaap, aliàs Tap, Præses magnus & Princeps”

That is, “Gaap, otherwise Tap, a great President and Prince.” It is entry **36**, printed at columns 923–924 in the 1577 edition. The complete Latin is available in [Peterson’s transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

**[Dis]** It is often stated that the *Pseudomonarchia* dates from 1563. That was the year of the first edition of *De praestigiis*, but the demon catalogue was added only in the 1577 fifth edition.

### 5. Is Goap the same being?

**[D]** Weyer’s preliminary ritual material names four directional kings:

- Amoymon, king of the East;
- Gorson, king of the South;
- Zymymar, king of the North;
- **Goap**, king and prince of the West.

The *Lemegeton* rearranges or corrupts the directions, commonly giving Amaymon East, Corson West, Ziminiar North, and Goap South.

**[Dis]** Gaap and Goap may be connected, but the texts do not identify them unequivocally:

- **Gaap** is a President and Prince with sixty-six legions.
- **Goap** is a directional King.
- Weyer says Gaap guides four principal kings. If Gaap were Goap, this might make him one of the kings he guides.
- Similar spellings could nevertheless represent one name divided into two offices during textual transmission.

Peterson cautions against automatically identifying them. Boudet treats the resemblance as significant; later occult writers, particularly those interested in directional kings, are more willing to conflate them.

### 6. Etymology

**[Negative finding]** No ancient god, hero, nation, or place has been securely identified behind Gaap’s name.

Unlike Baal, Astarte, Amun, Asmodeus/Ashmedai, Belial, or Phoenix, **Gaap has no demonstrated ancient-language etymology or ancient cultic antecedent**. The evidence currently supports a medieval catalogue name transmitted as Taob/Tap/Caap/Gaap, rather than a demonized ancient deity.

**[Dis]** Modern web pages and practitioner literature propose derivations from:

- alleged Hebrew words meaning “body,” “valley,” “chasm,” or “wing”;
- a purported Sumerian *Gubba/Gubbu*;
- Elagabalus or forms such as *Elelogap*;
- a lost pagan earth, gate, or transport god.

No cited Hebrew, Akkadian, Sumerian, Greek, or Latin textual chain connects these words to the manuscript forms. These proposals should be treated as speculative folk etymology.

The title *Goetia* has a genuine Greek derivation—from Greek **γοητεία**, sorcery or jugglery, associated with **γόης**, an enchanter or lamenting magician—but that explains the book’s title, not the name Gaap.

## Number and order: Weyer’s 69 and the Goetia’s 72

**[D]** Weyer has **69** spirits and places Gaap at **36**. The *Ars Goetia* has **72** and places him at **33**.

The difference is produced by:

- omission of Weyer’s no. 4, **Pruflas/Bufas**;
- addition of **Vassago** as Goetia no. 3;
- addition of **Seere**, no. 70;
- addition of **Dantalion**, no. 71;
- addition of **Andromalius**, no. 72;
- local reorderings and spelling changes.

Thus, relative to Weyer: 69 − 1 + 4 = 72.

**[D]** Scot does not print ordinal numbers and omits Pruflas. Gaap is therefore about the thirty-fifth spirit actually encountered in Scot’s text.

**[Dis]** A note in Peterson’s online *Goetia* has sometimes been read as saying Gaap is no. 26 in Weyer and Scot. Peterson’s own Weyer transcription and comparison table give the correct Weyer number, **36**. Number 26 belongs to **Caap in the *Livre des Esperitz***. The online “26” is best understood as an editorial cross-reference error or accidental carryover from the French ancestor.

## The description across the sources

## Clm 849: Taob

**[D]** Kieckhefer’s fifteenth-century text presents Taob as:

- great and a prince;
- assuming a human form, apparently under the sign or appearance of a physician;
- expert in matters concerning women;
- able to make women burn with love for men;
- able to change women’s forms until they come before their beloved;
- able, according to a difficult reading, to make them sterile;
- ruler of twenty-five legions.

This is a possible textual ancestor, not the complete later Gaap personality.

## *Livre des Esperitz*: Caap and Coap

**[D]** Caap:

- great prince;
- appears as a knight;
- gives true answers;
- brings gold and silver;
- rules twenty legions.

**[D]** Coap:

- great prince;
- procures women and causes them to come where commanded;
- has an obscure further office described by *horchaingnes*;
- rules twenty-seven legions.

**[Dis]** Boudet notes that the obscure French could indicate making women strangers, outsiders, disordered, or otherwise altered. It should not be confidently translated as one precise magical power.

## Weyer’s Latin, 1577

The entry appears at *De praestigiis daemonum*, fifth edition, columns **923–924**, no. 36. Its opening identifies Gaap as a great President and Prince who appears:

> “in signo meridiei … quum humanam assumit faciem”

Literally, “in a meridional sign … when he assumes a human face.”

**Complete contents, in sequence [D]:**

1. Gaap, also called Tap, is a great President and Prince.
2. He appears *in signo meridiei*.
3. When he assumes a human face, he is guide of the four principal kings.
4. He is as powerful as Byleth.
5. Certain necromancers offered him libations and burnt offerings.
6. They claimed that Solomon composed the art used to evoke him.
7. Weyer rejects that attribution.
8. The narrative instead says Cham, son of Noah, first invoked evil spirits after the Flood.
9. Cham invoked Byleth and composed an art and book in Byleth’s name.
10. Exorcists mixed burnt offerings, gifts, and impious acts with the holy names of God.
11. An epistle concerning those names was attributed to Solomon and mentioned by “Elias of Jerusalem” and “Eliseus.”
12. Weyer refuses to reveal part of the method of constraining Byleth because it is abominable.
13. Gaap makes a person admirable in philosophy and all liberal arts.
14. He produces love and hatred.
15. He causes invisibility.
16. He consecrates things belonging to Amaymon’s dominion.
17. He gives or transfers familiars from another exorcist’s power.
18. He answers perfectly about present, past, and future things.
19. He transports a person with extreme speed into different regions.
20. He commands sixty-six legions.
21. He formerly belonged to the order of Powers, *Potestates*.

The full Latin is in [Peterson’s Weyer edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

### The Cham/Noah note

**[L]** Weyer’s account that Cham, son of Noah, began post-Flood spirit invocation is not in Genesis and has no historical evidence. It belongs to the widespread pseudepigraphic strategy of assigning magical books to biblical antiquity.

**[D]** Weyer does not straightforwardly say that Cham first summoned Gaap. In the Gaap entry he says Cham invoked **Byleth** and made the art in Byleth’s name. Later summaries frequently blur that distinction.

**[D]** Weyer’s larger purpose was polemical. He accepted a Christian demonology but attacked witch prosecutions and presented the catalogue partly to expose necromantic fraud. He deliberately withheld some ritual instructions.

## Reginald Scot’s English, 1584

Scot’s translation appears in *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, Book XV, chapter 2, page **222** of the 1584 edition. It begins:

> “Gaap, alias Tap, a great president and a prince, he appeareth in a meridionall signe…”

The full early-modern English is available in [Peterson’s transcription of Scot](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/scot16.htm); scans are held by [Cornell](https://digital.library.cornell.edu/catalog/witchcraft081) and the [Wellcome Collection](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/egqkfghn).

**Complete contents and significant differences [D]:**

- Scot closely follows Weyer’s rank, meridional appearance, four kings, Bileth comparison, sacrifices, Cham legend, sciences, love and hatred, familiars, answers, transportation, sixty-six legions, and former membership in the Powers.
- Where Weyer has *invisibilitatem*, Scot prints both **“insensibilitie” and “invisibilitie.”**
- Scot duplicates “consecration,” probably through translation or typesetting.
- Scot says familiars may be delivered from the possession of other conjurers.
- He renders the Latin *mathematici* as “mathematicians,” a term that could then encompass astrologers and occult practitioners.
- The translation may have been prepared or assisted by Abraham Fleming, though the extent of Fleming’s contribution remains debated.

The appearance of **insensibility** here is important. It is not merely a modern interpretation: it is already in Scot, although Weyer’s surviving Latin at that point gives invisibility.

## Sloane 3825 and the manuscript *Ars Goetia*

The manuscript entry is in Sloane 3825, approximately fols. **105r–105v**, spirit 33. Peterson uses Sloane 3825 as his principal copy-text and records variants from Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, and Harley 6483 in [his critical online edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

It opens:

> “The 33d spirit is Gaap. he is a great president & a mighty Prince…”

**Complete contents, in sequence [D]:**

1. Gaap is spirit 33.
2. He is a great President and mighty Prince.
3. He appears when the Sun is in some of the Southern Signs.
4. He appears in human shape.
5. He goes before four great and mighty kings, guiding them on their way.
6. He makes people knowledgeable in philosophy and the liberal sciences.
7. He causes love or hatred.
8. He makes people insensible.
9. He teaches the magician to consecrate things belonging to the dominion of Amaymon, his king.
10. He delivers familiars from the custody of other magicians.
11. He answers truly about things past, present, and future.
12. He can carry and return men or things rapidly from one kingdom to another at the exorcist’s will.
13. He rules sixty-six legions.
14. He was of the order of Potestates or Potentates.
15. His seal is to be made and worn as a lamen.

### What changed from Weyer and Scot?

**[D]**

- The long Cham, Noah, Byleth, sacrifices, and false-Solomon digression disappears.
- *In signo meridiei* becomes the explicit astrological phrase “when the Sun is in some of the Southern Signs.”
- Weyer’s “human face” becomes “human shape.”
- “Insensibility” is retained.
- Weyer’s explicit invisibility disappears.
- The spirit now “teaches” consecration rather than simply performing it.
- Amaymon is explicitly called **“his king.”**
- A drawn seal and lamen instruction are added.
- The catalogue is integrated into a full ritual of circle, triangle, conjurations, constraints, license to depart, and planetary-metal rules.

**[SR]** The English *Goetia* seems to depend more directly on Scot’s English than on Weyer’s Latin. “Insensibility,” the syntax, and several expansions support that conclusion.

## Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, and Harley 6483

**[D]**

- Sloane 3648 is among the older surviving *Lemegeton* witnesses.
- Sloane 2731 bears a date of 18 January 1687.
- Harley 6483 belongs to 1712–1713 and transmits the form associated with “Dr Rudd.”
- All retain **Gaap**, number 33, the combined President/Prince rank, and sixty-six legions.

The manuscripts do not support a separate spelling **Gäap**.

**[Dis]** “The Goetia of Dr Rudd” is a convenient description of the Harley material, not proof that Thomas Rudd personally wrote Harley 6483. The hand, compilation history, and relationship between Harley 6482 and 6483 make direct authorship uncertain.

## Mathers–Crowley, 1904

The 1904 *Goetia: The Lesser Key of Solomon the King*, edited by Aleister Crowley from a translation prepared by S. L. MacGregor Mathers, prints the entry at approximately page **22**:

> “The Thirty-third Spirit is Gäap. He is a Great President and a Mighty Prince.”

The complete edition is available through [Hermetic Library](https://hermetic.com/_media/93beast.fea.st/files/section1/crowley_-_goetia.pdf) and [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/The_Lesser_Key_of_Solomon).

**Complete contents and editorial features [D]:**

- **Gäap** replaces manuscript *Gaap*.
- He appears when the Sun is in some Southern Signs.
- He takes human shape and guides four great kings.
- Mathers rearranges the educational clause: Gaap makes people “Insensible or Ignorant,” but makes them knowledgeable in philosophy and the liberal sciences.
- Love, hatred, Amaymon’s consecrations, release of familiars, truthful answers, rapid transportation, sixty-six legions, and former Potentate status are retained.
- Mathers specifies that Gaap carries and recarries **men**, resolving one manuscript ambiguity.
- The seal is printed beneath the entry.

**[Dis]** “Ignorant” is Mathers’s interpretation, not a separate power securely present in Weyer’s Latin. It glosses *insensible*.

## Peterson, 2001

**[D]** Joseph H. Peterson’s *The Lesser Key of Solomon: Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis* was published by Weiser in 2001. The Gaap material occurs in Part I’s catalogue, within the section spanning roughly pp. **18–22**; the web edition identifies the manuscript folio directly.

Peterson:

- returns the name to **Gaap**;
- follows Sloane 3825 rather than simply reprinting Mathers;
- supplies variant readings from Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, and Harley 6483;
- restores manuscript language such as “make men knowing”;
- brackets obvious copyist omissions or corrections;
- separates manuscript evidence from editorial emendation.

The bibliographical record is available through [Google Books](https://books.google.ca/books?id=wfIrE3QtmFEC), and Peterson maintains [online emendments](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lkey_cor.htm).

## Collin de Plancy, 1863

De Plancy’s sixth edition, p. **651**, heads the entry **“Tap ou Gaap.”** It begins:

> “grand président et grand prince aux enfers”

The full French entry is available at [French Wikisource](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.%2C_1863/Tap).

**[D]** De Plancy:

- interprets the appearance as occurring **at noon**;
- says Gaap commands four principal kings;
- retains the Byleth comparison and Cham legend;
- mentions libations, holocausts, holy names, and the alleged books of Solomon, Elijah, and Elisha;
- gives love and hatred;
- places demons of Amaymon’s power under him;
- retains rapid transportation;
- reduces the legions from **66 to 60**;
- omits most of the philosophical, scientific, familiar, and prophetic offices.

**[Dis]** “Commands four kings” is stronger than Weyer’s “guide.” “At noon” is a possible but not necessary interpretation of *meridiei*. The reduction to sixty legions has no support in the principal earlier witnesses and is likely abridgment, error, or normalization.

The first edition of the *Dictionnaire infernal* was published in **1818**, but it was extensively revised. The existence of the 1818 work is certain; without a verified page-level comparison, the wording of the 1863 Tap entry should not be projected unchanged into the first edition.

## Appearance, second form, and offices

### Appearance

**[D]** No principal source gives Gaap a composite animal or monstrous first form.

- Taob: human form, perhaps as a physician.
- Caap: a knight.
- Weyer: a human face in a “meridional sign.”
- Scot: the same.
- *Goetia*: human shape while the Sun is in Southern Signs.
- De Plancy’s engraving: a later winged, horned giant transporting a person.

### Second form

**[Negative finding]** There is no Gaap-specific second form that the conjurer must demand.

**[D/T]** The general *Goetia* ritual allows the exorcist to command a spirit that appears in an alarming or unsuitable form to assume a fair and comely human shape. That general rule is sometimes applied to every spirit, but Gaap’s individual entry already says he appears human.

## The four kings, Byleth, and Amaymon

Three groups are frequently conflated.

### The four kings Gaap guides

**[D]** Weyer and the *Goetia* say that Gaap goes before or guides four principal kings. They do not name those four in the Gaap entry.

### The cardinal kings

**[D]** Separate ritual passages name Amaymon, Gorson/Corson, Zymymar/Ziminiar, and Goap as directional kings.

**[SR/Dis]** These are plausible candidates for the four guided kings, but the identification is not explicit and becomes awkward if Goap equals Gaap.

### The four chief spirits in the brass vessel

**[L]** The *Goetia* says Solomon confined seventy-two spirits in a brass vessel and that **Belial, Bileth, Asmoday, and Gaap** were chiefs. It adds that the Babylonians broke the vessel while seeking treasure and released them.

This is a late Solomonic legend, not evidence for ancient Babylonian belief.

**[Dis]** The four chiefs in the vessel may be the “four great kings” of Gaap’s procession, but the text never says so. Modern summaries often treat the two groups as identical without evidence.

### Amaymon

**[D]** Weyer links Gaap’s consecrations to Amaymon’s dominion. The *Goetia* calls Amaymon “his king.”

**[EC/T]** Later practitioners therefore place Gaap under Amaymon’s eastern rulership. This follows the *Lemegeton* configuration; Weyer’s own directional list has a different order.

## Rank, planets, metals, and time

### Rank metals

The *Goetia*’s general instructions give:

| Rank | Planet | Metal |
|---|---|---|
| King | Sun | Gold |
| Duke | Venus | Copper |
| Prince/Prelate | Jupiter | Tin |
| Marquis | Moon | Silver |
| President | Mercury | Mercury/quicksilver |
| Earl/Count | Mars and Moon in later tables | Copper and silver |
| Knight | Saturn | Lead |

**[D/EC]** Gaap’s two ranks therefore imply:

- **President → Mercury → quicksilver**
- **Prince → Jupiter → tin**

**[Dis]** The manuscript does not state whether a dual-ranked spirit requires:

- two separate seals;
- an alloy or combination;
- the metal of the first-listed rank;
- the metal appropriate to the office requested.

Peterson suggests that combinations may be considered, but this is editorial reasoning. Modern practitioners variously prefer mercury’s symbolic substitute because actual elemental mercury is toxic, tin for the princely rank, or paper/parchment.

### Rank time

**[D/T]** The general *Goetia* says:

- Princes or Prelates may be called during the day.
- Presidents may be called at any daytime hour except twilight or night, unless permission is obtained from their king.

The practical intersection for Gaap is therefore ordinary daylight outside twilight. No individual fixed clock hour is supplied.

### “Southern Signs”

**[D]** The *Goetia* says Gaap appears when the Sun is in “some of the Southern Signs.”

**[SR]** Astronomically, “southern signs” could mean the half of the zodiac in which the Sun lies south of the celestial equator, broadly Libra through Pisces in a tropical framework.

**[Dis]** Other possibilities are:

- a corrupted remnant of Taob’s *signo medici*;
- Weyer’s word for a meridional or southern quarter;
- an instruction connected with the directional king Goap;
- “midday,” as de Plancy understood it.

The sources do not identify precisely which signs are meant.

### Later planetary timing

**[EC]** A later Golden Dawn-style operator might select:

- Wednesday or an hour of Mercury for the presidential office;
- Thursday or an hour of Jupiter for the princely office;
- the Sun’s transit through 20°–30° Aquarius for Crowley’s decan attribution;
- or daylight while the Sun is in a southern sign.

No early source harmonizes these conditions. Combining all of them is a modern synthesis.

## The seal

### Manuscript status

**[D]** Weyer and Scot give no seal for Gaap. Spirit seals enter the recoverable Gaap tradition with the seventeenth-century *Lemegeton* manuscripts. Peterson notes that comparable sigils are absent from the earlier *Offices of Spirits* catalogues known to him.

### Form

**[D]** The ordinary Gaap seal is a line drawing rather than a verbal heraldic design. In broad terms it consists of:

- a vertical central stem crossed near the middle;
- four small circles or ring-dots grouped about the central region;
- rounded or bracket-like surrounding strokes;
- an extended horizontal arm;
- a squared or hooked terminal at the right.

Because it is a hand-drawn character, a verbal description is less exact than the manuscript image.

### Variants

**[D]**

- Sloane 3825 preserves the principal form used by Peterson.
- Sloane 2731 and Sloane 3648 retain the same recognizable skeleton but vary in line curvature, proportions, ring-dot size, and joins.
- Harley 6483 copies the demonic seal as one face of a protective lamen.
- Mathers–Crowley regularizes it into a cleaner printed figure.
- Later vectors often impose perfect symmetry and uniform stroke width not found in manuscript hands.

**[Dis]** Small graphic differences should not automatically be treated as separate seals. Most are ordinary copyist and engraver variation.

### Rudd’s double lamen

**[D/T]** In the Harley-Rudd arrangement, the demon’s seal is placed on one side and the corresponding Shem angel’s seal or name on the reverse, facing the magician’s heart. This embodies the claim that a fallen or adverse spirit is constrained by an angel of the corresponding celestial order.

## The angel: Iehuiah / Yehuiah

## Construction of the seventy-two names

**[D/T]** Exodus 14:19–21 comprises three consecutive Hebrew verses traditionally counted as seventy-two letters each. The later procedure is:

1. write verse 19 in normal order;
2. write verse 20 in reverse;
3. write verse 21 in normal order;
4. read the resulting seventy-two vertical groups of three letters.

This alternating direction is commonly described as boustrophedon. Rashi’s commentary on Sukkah 45a describes the seventy-two-letter construction; see [Rashi on Sukkah 45a](https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Sukkah.45).

**[D]** The thirty-third triplet is **יחו**:

- י — *yod*
- ח — *ḥet*
- ו — *vav*

**[T]** Adding a theophoric ending, normally *-el* or *-yah*, turns the triad into an angelic name. In the Rudd/Lenain line it is commonly **יהויה**, transliterated *Iehuiah, Jehuiah,* or *Yehuiah*.

### What “Shem ha-Mephorash” meant

**[D/SR]** In early rabbinic usage *shem ha-meforash* means the “explicit,” “distinctly pronounced,” or “fully expressed” divine Name and does not exclusively denote this list of seventy-two angels.

**[T]** Medieval Kabbalah applied the expression to several expanded Names—of twelve, forty-two, or seventy-two letters. The seventy-two triads became especially influential through the *Sefer ha-Bahir*, *Sefer Raziel*, practical-Kabbalistic manuscripts, and Christian Cabala.

**[Dis]** It is anachronistic to say that Exodus itself names seventy-two guardian angels. Exodus supplies the letters; the individuation, vocalization, suffixes, offices, zodiacal degrees, and demon pairings are later constructions.

## From Jewish Kabbalah to Christian Cabala

### *Sefer ha-Bahir* and *Sefer Raziel*

**[D/T]** The *Bahir*, in its extant medieval form, discusses the seventy-two-fold divine Name. The *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* became a major vehicle for angelic names, divine-name permutations, seals, and practical angelology.

**[Dis]** The exact modern persona “Yehuiah, angel 33, ruler of 10°–15° Virgo and opponent of Gaap” is not simply found intact in an ancient *Sefer Raziel*. It is the product of several later layers.

### Reuchlin, 1517

**[D]** Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* helped transmit a seventy-two-name angelology into Christian Cabala. Appending divine suffixes and treating the names as intelligible angelic powers became standard in this environment.

### Agrippa, 1533

**[D]** Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa explains the seventy-two-fold Name and its extraction in *De occulta philosophia*, Book III, chapter 25. His text is available through [Esoteric Archives](https://www.esotericarchives.com/agrippa/op3.htm).

Agrippa is important to the genealogy of the angelic list, but he does not provide the later ordinal Gaap–Yehuiah pairing.

### Kircher, 1652–1654

**[D]** Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* visually organized the seventy-two names around divine and astrological structures. His famous diagram may be viewed at [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_72_Names_of_God_(Oedipus_Aegyptiacus,_1652%E2%80%9354).jpg).

**[T/EC]** Kircher helped normalize the Christian-Hermetic presentation of the names as ordered celestial powers. He did not originate Rudd’s one-to-one system of controlling the *Goetia*.

## Rudd’s pairing of Gaap and Iehuiah

**[D]** Harley 6483 pairs the thirty-third demon, Gaap, with the thirty-third Shem angel, **Iehuiah**. Stephen Skinner and David Rankine reproduce this in *The Goetia of Dr Rudd*.

**[T]** The operating principle is ordinal and oppositional:

```text
Angel 33: Iehuiah / Yehuiah
                 ↓ divine constraint
Demon 33: Gaap
```

The magician invokes or places confidence in the angelic name and relevant psalm, then uses Gaap’s seal while keeping the angelic counterpart toward the heart. The angel provides divine authority over the spirit rather than functioning as Gaap’s ally or “good twin.”

**[D]** The Harley compilation also integrates angelic names with the brass vessel and other ritual equipment.

**[Dis]** Skinner and Rankine reconstruct the working system by reading Harley 6483 together with Harley 6482, Sloane 3824, and related manuscripts. Every procedural detail is not written consecutively in one Gaap entry.

---

## Lenain’s Iehuiah, 1823

Lazare Républicain Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* gives one of the most influential complete descriptions, at pp. **66–67**. The scan is available from [IAPSOP](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1823__lenain___science_cabalistique.pdf) and [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_ms05365).

The entry identifies the angel as:

> “Iehuiah … Dieu qui connaît toutes choses.”

**[D/T/EC]** Lenain assigns:

- number **33**;
- attribute: “God who knows all things”;
- corresponding holy name: **Agad**, allegedly in the language of the Hesperides;
- zodiacal ray: degrees **161–165**, inclusive;
- seventeenth five-degree division;
- subordinate or zodiacal genius **Ouestucati**;
- influence of **Venus**;
- five dispersed annual days: **21 April, 2 July, 12 September, 23 November, 3 February**;
- fifth angelic order, the **Powers**;
- daily invocation time: **10:40–11:00**;
- a verse he labels Psalm 33:11;
- detection of traitors;
- destruction of their plans and machinations;
- protection of Christian princes;
- maintenance of subjects in obedience;
- dutifulness in people born under the influence;
- contrary genius: insubordination, sedition, and revolt.

### The zodiacal degrees

**[EC]** If the zodiac begins at 0° Aries:

- degrees 151–180 are Virgo;
- degrees 161–165 are **10°–15° Virgo**.

The exact conventional statement is therefore 10°00′ through 14°59′ Virgo, with 15° marking the next boundary.

### The date discrepancy

**[Dis]** Modern guardian-angel books commonly convert this to a continuous period such as 3–7 September or 30 August–3 September. Lenain does not give one continuous five-day birth interval. He lists five separate dates distributed around the year. The continuous dates are a later calendrical simplification based on the zodiacal quinance, and authors differ because of inclusive counting and degree/date conventions.

### The psalm problem

Lenain’s Latin is:

> “Dominus scit cogitationes hominum quoniam vanæ sunt.”

**[D]** This is Vulgate **Psalm 93:11**, corresponding to Psalm **94:11** in most modern Protestant and Hebrew-based numbering: “The Lord knows the thoughts of man, that they are vain.”

**[Dis]** Lenain calls it Psalm 33:11. Psalm 33:11 has different wording. The same incorrect reference passes into later angelological tables and the Rudd edition’s note. Some modern writers silently correct it to 94:11; others substitute a genuine verse from Psalm 33, creating a second tradition.

## Papus, the Golden Dawn, Crowley, Ambelain, and Haziel

### Papus

**[D/T]** Gérard Encausse, “Papus,” reproduced and reorganized much of the French Christian-Kabbalistic angelology in works such as *La Cabbale*. The material is accessible in [Gallica’s scan](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k114146g).

Papus helped carry Lenain’s attributes into French occultism. This is transmission, not independent confirmation.

### Golden Dawn

**[EC]** The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn incorporated:

- the seventy-two Shem names;
- zodiacal divisions;
- decans and quinances;
- Hebrew divine names;
- tarot pips;
- planetary and elemental correspondences;
- Solomonic and Enochian ritual materials.

**[Dis]** Different Golden Dawn papers and successor orders begin or arrange the Shem zodiacal cycle differently. The straightforward Lenain calculation gives Yehuiah 10°–15° Virgo; alternative tables may shift dates or use different starting points. These variations should not be retrojected into Rudd.

### Crowley’s *777*

**[EC]** Crowley’s *777*, first published in 1909 from Golden Dawn-derived correspondence papers, places **Gaap**, not Yehuiah, in the daytime half of the third decan of Aquarius:

- **20°–30° Aquarius**
- daytime spirit: **Gaap**
- nighttime spirit: **Decarabia**
- tarot: **Seven of Swords**
- planetary dignity of the card: **Moon in Aquarius**
- Golden Dawn title: **Lord of Unstable Effort**

The relevant tables are available in [*Liber 777*](https://hermetic.com/_media/93beast.fea.st/files/section1/777/liber_777.pdf) and [*777 Revised*](https://hermetic.com/_media/93beast.fea.st/files/section1/777/liber_777_revised.pdf).

Each of the thirty-six minor-arcana pips from Twos through Tens receives two Goetic spirits—one diurnal and one nocturnal—producing 36 × 2 = 72.

### Ambelain

**[T/EC]** Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* systematized Lenain-style angelic names, regencies, qualities, adverse genii, psalms, and ritual applications for twentieth-century French occultism. An English translation is available at [Mark Foster’s archive](https://www.markfoster.net/rn/texts/practicalkabbalah-part2.pdf).

Ambelain is a major transmitter, not an early witness to Gaap.

### Haziel

**[MI/T]** “Haziel,” the pen name of François Bernad-Termès, popularized the seventy-two angels as personal guardian angels in late-twentieth-century French devotional and New Age publishing. His books expanded the calendar, personality, prayer, prosperity, and relationship interpretations. The publisher’s Haziel catalogue is maintained by [Éditions Bussière](https://editions-bussiere.com/brand/33-haziel).

These individualized biographies should not be attributed to the medieval Kabbalists or Rudd.

### Three planets must not be conflated

Gaap’s correspondence produces three distinct planetary layers:

## Enochian and later overlays

**[Negative finding]** Weyer, Scot, and the ordinary *Ars Goetia* give Gaap no Enochian name, tablet square, aethyr, or Watchtower assignment.

**[D/SR]** The manuscripts surrounding Rudd combine several bodies of material associated with John Dee, angelic tables, the *Tabula Sancta*, the Shem angels, and Solomonic spirit magic. Skinner and Rankine discuss this as a broader Rudd synthesis.

**[MI]** Twentieth-century magicians—including Poke Runyon and the authors of *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia*—integrated Enochian Calls, mirror scrying, colored triangles, tarot, and Goetic seals. Such systems may be internally coherent, but an Enochian Call assigned to Gaap is not part of Weyer’s or the seventeenth-century Gaap description.

Modern demonolatry “enns,” dedicated colors, incense lists, pathworkings, personality profiles, and claims of direct patronage are likewise later constructions unless their authors explicitly identify them as personal revelation.

## Iconography

## The manuscript seal

**[D]** Gaap has no known portrait in Weyer or Scot. His first durable visual identifier is the *Lemegeton* seal. It is abstract and gives no evidence for bodily appearance.

The seal’s four circular elements have sometimes been interpreted as the four kings, four directions, or four transported persons. **[Dis]** No manuscript caption explains them that way.

## De Plancy and Louis Le Breton

**[D]** The illustrated 1863 sixth edition of Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* includes a portrait captioned **Tap**. The image is conventionally attributed to Louis Le Breton and may be viewed as [“Gaap/Tap” on Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Gaap.jpg).

The image shows:

- a large airborne anthropomorphic demon;
- broad bat-like wings;
- horns and a tail;
- a much smaller human figure carried on or across its shoulders/back;
- a clouded or aerial setting suggesting rapid flight.

**[SR]** The picture visualizes Gaap’s power to transport people quickly between countries. It does not follow the textual statement that he appears in human shape and should not be treated as an earlier traditional apparition.

**[D]** De Plancy’s title page advertises hundreds of engravings and portraits of demons. Counts quoted for the Le Breton demon series vary depending on whether later recuttings and ancillary illustrations are included.

## Modern art

**[MI]** Modern depictions generally descend from one of two sources:

1. the de Plancy/Le Breton flying carrier;
2. the abstract Goetic seal.

Contemporary artists also portray Gaap as:

- a robed philosopher;
- a Mercurial scholar;
- a winged transporter;
- one of four kings;
- an androgynous or female demon;
- a masked official or military commander.

None is a recoverable premodern iconographic tradition unless it reproduces the seal or de Plancy engraving.

## People and works

### Johann Weyer

**[D]** Weyer printed the first secure Gaap spelling in 1577. He was a physician and opponent of witch executions, although he accepted much of Christian demonology. The catalogue was presented critically and with dangerous operational material withheld.

### Reginald Scot and Abraham Fleming

**[D/Dis]** Scot reproduced the catalogue in 1584 as part of his assault on witchcraft credulity and persecution. The exact division of translation work between Scot and Abraham Fleming is uncertain.

### The anonymous *Lemegeton* compilers

**[SR]** Seventeenth-century English compilers converted the Weyer–Scot catalogue into a ritual handbook, adding seals, circles, triangles, metals, conjurations, and a seventy-two-spirit structure.

### Thomas Rudd and the Harley compiler

**[Dis]** Thomas Rudd is traditionally associated with the angel–demon counterbalancing system. Harley 6483 is not securely his autograph; “Rudd” may identify a textual lineage or attribution rather than sole authorship.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers

**[D]** Mathers prepared the translation underlying the 1904 edition, introduced the form **Gäap**, regularized the prose, and interpreted insensibility as ignorance.

### Aleister Crowley

**[D/EC]** Crowley edited and published Mathers’s text in 1904, added his own prefatory and magical framing, and later placed Gaap in the Aquarius/Seven of Swords system reproduced in *777*.

### Frater Achad

**[Negative finding]** No securely documented Gaap-specific working or original Gaap teaching by Charles Stansfeld Jones, Frater Achad, was located. Generic claims that every major Thelemite worked individually with Gaap are unsupported.

### Poke Runyon

**[MI]** Poke Runyon’s *Book of Solomon’s Magick* and related Church of the Hermetic Sciences work integrate Goetic spirits with mirror scrying, the Almadel, Shem angels, directional rulers, and an operative temple system. His publishing description is available at [Poke Runyon’s site](https://www.pokerunyon.com/books/the-book-of-solomons-magick-second-edition).

Runyon is influential in modern practical Goetia, but his ritual synthesis is not evidence for medieval Gaap worship.

### Lon Milo DuQuette, Christopher Hyatt, and David P. Wilson

**[MI/EC]** *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* popularized practical tables listing Gaap with Aquarius, the Seven of Swords, Mercury, Jupiter, metals, dates, and Enochian Calls. These tables clarify modern practice but combine several historical layers.

### Joseph H. Peterson

**[D/SR]** Peterson’s 2001 edition and continuing online work established the modern manuscript-critical baseline: Sloane 3825 as copy-text, manuscript variants, comparison with Weyer and Scot, and separation of Mathers’s editorial wording from the seventeenth-century text.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

**[D/SR]** Their 2007 *Goetia of Dr Rudd* brought Harley 6483’s angelic counterseals and the Gaap–Iehuiah pairing into print, reconstructed the ritual system across several manuscripts, and emphasized the angelic safety mechanism. See the [publisher’s edition page](https://www.llewellyn.com/product.php?ean=9780738723556).

### Jake Stratton-Kent

**[SR/MI]** Stratton-Kent argued broadly for reading spirit catalogues in the context of earlier European, Mediterranean, and chthonic traditions and drew attention to directional kings and grimoire genealogy. His work encourages examination of Gaap/Goap, but it does not establish a particular ancient god as Gaap’s source.

### Owen Davies, Jan Veenstra, Julien Véronèse, and Jean-Patrice Boudet

These scholars supply historical context rather than operative teachings:

- Davies situates grimoires socially and materially.
- Veenstra examines learned magic and spirit traditions.
- Véronèse studies medieval ritual magic and Solomonic textual production.
- Boudet provides the especially important catalogue comparison connecting Caap, Taob, Weyer, and related lists.

## Grimoire ancestors and comparanda

### *Testament of Solomon*

**[D/SR]** The Greek *Testament of Solomon*, composed and revised in late antiquity over a disputed period, contains demons interrogated by Solomon and constrained through angelic names. It is an important structural ancestor of Solomonic demonology.

**[Negative finding]** Gaap does not appear by that name. A general similarity—demons having offices and opposing angels—is not proof of direct identity.

### Michael Scot and early spirit lists

**[D/SR]** Medieval works associated with Michael Scot and other learned-magical traditions provide planetary and spirit catalogues. They establish the environment from which the *Offices of Spirits* developed but do not securely name Gaap.

### Clm 849

**[SR]** Taob is the strongest fifteenth-century candidate for a direct Gaap ancestor.

### *Liber officiorum spirituum*

**[D/SR]** Weyer says his catalogue came from a *Liber officiorum spirituum* or comparable necromantic book. Trithemius mentions books under related titles. No single surviving manuscript is demonstrably Weyer’s exact exemplar.

### *Livre des Esperitz*

**[SR]** Caap and Coap show that the name-family and several offices existed before Weyer. The French catalogue is the clearest securely pre-1577 bridge.

### *Book of Abramelin*

**[Negative finding]** Abramelin manuscripts contain extensive spirit-name lists, but no securely established Gaap entry with the later offices has been identified. Similar-looking names in modern alphabetical concordances should not be assumed identical without manuscript context.

### Solomon’s brass vessel

**[L]** The seventy-two-spirit vessel story is part of the *Goetia*’s internal legendary history. It should not be confused with the earlier *Testament of Solomon* or archaeological evidence.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Does Gaap exist before Weyer?

**Finding:** The exact spelling **Gaap** is first securely attested in Weyer, 1577. **Taob** and **Caap** are credible pre-Weyer ancestors.

- **[D]** Taob: first half of the fifteenth century.
- **[D]** Caap/Coap: mid-sixteenth century.
- **[SR]** Both belong to the textual ancestry.
- **[Dis]** Neither is identical in every office or legion count.

The strongest formulation is: **Gaap as a stabilized spelling begins with Weyer; the catalogue entity or name-family is older.**

### 2. Taob or Toab?

**[D/Dis]** Kieckhefer’s reading is generally **Taob**. Secondary tables sometimes reverse the middle letters as **Toab**. This is transcriptional variation, not evidence for two securely separate spirits.

### 3. Caap and Coap

**[Dis]** The *Livre des Esperitz* treats them separately. Their later convergence is possible but not demonstrated. The erotic and transformative powers may have migrated between entries during copying.

### 4. Gaap and Goap

**[Dis]** Similar spelling and the “four kings” problem favor connection; different rank, function, and separate placement favor distinction. Neither absolute identity nor absolute independence can be proven from the surviving witnesses.

### 5. *Meridiei*: south or noon?

Possible readings are:

- meridional or southern sign;
- noon;
- southern quarter;
- corruption of *medici*, physician.

The *Goetia* favors an astrological/southern reading; de Plancy favors noon; Clm 849 preserves the physician-like reading. No surviving archetype resolves it.

### 6. Insensibility, invisibility, or ignorance?

- **Weyer Latin:** invisibility.
- **Scot:** insensibility and invisibility.
- **Goetia manuscripts:** insensibility, generally not invisibility.
- **Mathers:** insensibility or ignorance.

This is a traceable translation and redaction sequence. Modern lists that give all three without qualification combine witnesses.

### 7. Sixty-six or sixty legions?

Sixty-six is supported by Weyer, Scot, the *Goetia*, Rudd, and Mathers–Crowley. De Plancy alone in the principal chain gives sixty. Sixty-six is therefore the stronger transmitted reading.

### 8. The four kings

The texts leave three possibilities:

1. the four directional kings;
2. Belial, Bileth, Asmoday, and Gaap from the vessel legend;
3. another unnamed group.

No witness explicitly settles the question.

### 9. Was Gaap once a king?

**[Dis]** Skinner and Rankine note the possibility, principally because of Goap and the four-king material. It is a reasonable textual hypothesis, not a documented original rank.

### 10. Is Yehuiah’s meaning linguistic?

**[T/Dis]** “God who knows all things” is Lenain’s transmitted divine attribute. It is not a straightforward dictionary translation of the consonants יחו. The final *-yah* invokes the divine name; the full English phrase is theological exegesis.

### 11. Which psalm?

The quoted Latin is unambiguously Psalm 93:11 Vulgate / 94:11 modern numbering. “Psalm 33:11” is a transmitted citation error. Later authors who use an actual verse from Psalm 33 create a revised system.

### 12. Did Rudd invent the 72 angel–demon pairs?

**[Dis]** The seventy-two divine names predate Rudd; the demons’ catalogue predates the Harley manuscript; ordinal opposition of the two lists is characteristic of the Rudd material. Whether Thomas Rudd personally invented it, inherited it, or merely received the attribution remains open.

### 13. Is there an ancient god behind Gaap?

No demonstrated one. Claims of Hebrew, Sumerian, Roman, or Near Eastern derivation lack a manuscript and philological chain.

### 14. Misprints becoming names

The Gaap tradition contains several candidates:

- *Taob → Tap*;
- *Caap → Gaap*;
- *medici → meridiei*;
- *Gaap ↔ Goap*;
- Peterson’s accidental no. 26 cross-reference;
- Mathers’s decorative or pronunciation-marking **Gäap**;
- 66 → 60 in de Plancy.

These illustrate how copying, typesetting, translation, and editorial normalization can create apparently meaningful distinctions.

## Open questions

1. What exact manuscript of the *Liber officiorum spirituum* did Weyer use?
2. Is Clm 849’s *Taob* the direct ancestor of Gaap or only a cognate entry?
3. Did *medici* precede *meridiei*, or did a scribe convert “meridional” into “physician”?
4. Were Caap and Coap originally one entry split into two, or two entries later conflated?
5. Is the cardinal king Goap a higher-rank version of Gaap?
6. Who are the four kings Gaap guides?
7. Why did the legion count shift from 25 or 20/27 to 66?
8. Where did Gaap’s philosophical and liberal-science office first enter the tradition?
9. Did Scot deliberately add insensibility, or was it already in a lost Latin witness?
10. What is the immediate graphic source of the *Lemegeton* seal?
11. Was the double-lamen system devised by Thomas Rudd, by the Harley compiler, or by an earlier unknown author?
12. Why did Lenain or his source attach the correct text of Psalm 93/94:11 to the incorrect reference Psalm 33:11?
13. Are Ouestucati and “Agad in the language of the Hesperides” inherited from an identifiable earlier table?
14. How were the conflicting Mercury, Jupiter, Moon, and Venus correspondences practically reconciled before modern synthetic systems?
15. Does an unexamined manuscript preserve an intermediate form between Taob/Caap and Weyer’s Gaap?

## Conclusions

The recoverable history does not begin with an ancient god named Gaap. It begins with a mutable late-medieval spirit-catalogue complex:

```text
Taob, fifteenth century
        ↘
Caap / Coap, sixteenth century
        ↓
Gaap alias Tap, Weyer 1577
        ↓
Scot 1584
        ↓
Gaap, seventeenth-century Lemegeton manuscripts
        ├── Rudd: Gaap opposed by Iehuiah
        ├── de Plancy: Tap/Gaap and Le Breton portrait
        └── Mathers–Crowley: Gäap
                ↓
Golden Dawn / 777 / modern ritual and fiction
```

The stable core from Weyer onward is a powerful President and Prince, associated with human appearance, four kings, philosophy, liberal sciences, love and hatred, Amaymon, familiars, truthful answers, rapid transport, sixty-six legions, and the fallen order of Powers. Invisibility belongs most clearly to Weyer and Scot; insensibility enters through Scot and becomes dominant in the *Goetia*; “ignorance” is Mathers’s interpretation.

The pairing with Yehuiah is not an ancient Jewish demonological doctrine. It is an early-eighteenth-century Christian-Hermetic synthesis built from the much older seventy-two-letter Name, Renaissance Christian Cabala, the seventy-two-spirit *Goetia*, and Rudd-associated protective magic. Lenain later supplied the most influential meaning, zodiacal quinance, invocation time, psalm, and moral offices—along with a persistent psalm-number error.
