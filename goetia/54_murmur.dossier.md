# Murmur: Research Dossier

## Evidentiary key

- **[DT — documented text]** Directly stated or visible in a cited primary source or manuscript edition.
- **[SR — scholarly reconstruction]** An inference or textual history proposed by an editor or scholar.
- **[TR — tradition]** A teaching transmitted in later magical, kabbalistic, or occult literature.
- **[EC — esoteric correspondence]** A deliberately constructed symbolic attribution rather than a historical or linguistic fact.
- **[DIS — disputed]** A claim for which sources conflict or the evidence remains incomplete.
- **[LEG — legend]** Traditional narrative not independently established as history.
- **[MI — modern invention]** A recent expansion, reinterpretation, fictional use, or practitioner revelation absent from the early sources.

Because online quotations are subject to reproduction limits, the dossier supplies short exact excerpts, complete clause-by-clause translations or paraphrases, page references, and links to the full public-domain texts. The early descriptions can therefore be checked directly without confusing editorial modernization with manuscript wording.

## The name and its origins

### The documented spellings

The following source chain can be reconstructed:

| Date and source | Form |
|---|---|
| 1577, Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* | **Murmur** |
| 1584, Scot, *Discoverie of Witchcraft* | **Murmur** |
| Seventeenth century, Sloane 3825 | **Murmur** |
| Seventeenth century, Sloane 2731 | **Murmur**, with **Murmus** as a variant |
| ca. 1665 or later, Sloane 3648 | **Murmus** |
| 1712–1713, Harley 6483/Peter Smart | **Murmus** |
| 1904, Mathers–Crowley | **Murmur, Murmus, or Murmux** |
| 2001, Peterson critical edition | **Murmur [Murmus]**, with manuscript variants documented editorially |
| Modern occult reference works | **Murmuur**, usually without an early textual witness |

**[DT]** *Murmus* is therefore a genuine manuscript variant, not an invention by Mathers or Crowley. **[DIS]** *Murmux* has not been located in the consulted manuscripts and appears to enter the printed tradition through Mathers–Crowley in 1904. Peterson warns generally that several picturesque alternate names in that edition lack manuscript support. **[MI]** *Murmuur* appears to be a recent orthographic expansion; Theresa Bane’s 2012 encyclopedia helped circulate it, but her listed sources do not establish a premodern witness.

### Latin etymology

**[SR]** The simplest and strongest etymology is Latin *murmur, murmuris*, “murmur,” “low continuous sound,” “muttering,” “roar,” “rumbling,” “rustling,” or “hum.” It is an old sound-symbolic or onomatopoeic formation, with comparanda such as Greek μορμύρω, “to roar, surge, or boil.” The name is thus intelligible without recourse to an ancient deity.

**[SR]** Murmur’s trumpeters and noisy advent may have encouraged the retention or reinterpretation of the Latin name as a speaking name. That is an inference: Weyer does not supply an etymology, and Latin *murmur* does not specifically mean “trumpet.”

There is no secure evidence that the name descends from:

- an ancient god;
- a hero;
- a geographical place;
- Hebrew;
- Arabic;
- a corrupt form of a known divine name.

Unlike Baal, Astarte, Amun, Asmodeus/Ashmedai, Belial, or Phoenix, Murmur presently has no demonstrated ancient cultic referent.

### Murmur and Mormo

**[DIS]** Some modern occultists identify Murmur with Greek **Mormo** or **Mormolyke**, a female child-frightening spectre associated in ancient and later literature with Lamia, Empusa, or Hecate. The argument is principally phonetic: *Murmur/Murmus* resembles *Mormo*.

No consulted Weyer, Scot, Lemegeton, Rudd, de Plancy, or nineteenth-century kabbalistic source makes that identification. Ancient Mormo is normally female and belongs to a different mythic complex; Murmur is grammatically and iconographically a male military count-duke. The phonetic resemblance is insufficient to demonstrate descent.

**[MI]** The conflation has nevertheless produced a substantial modern mythology. V. K. Jehannum and derivative practitioner literature describe Murmur/Mormo as a son or consort of Hecate, a resurrected human, a vampiric or underworld initiator, and sometimes assign a pre-fall name such as “Matthias.” These are modern revelations or personal gnosis, not readings from the historical Goetic manuscripts.

### Earliest attestation: a newly reopened question

Until very recently, the earliest readily verifiable attestation was Weyer’s 1577 *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*.

A complication appeared with Stephen Skinner and Daniel Clark’s 2025 publication of Books III–IV of Berengarius Ganelli’s *Summa Sacre Magice*, a work whose colophon dates it to Perpignan in 1346. The publisher verifies the medieval work and the 2025 edition. A reader of the new edition reports that it contains two Murmur entries:

- Murmur as a “leader and count,” a ducal-crowned soldier riding a buffalo and holding a weapon;
- Murmur as commanding 25 legions, teaching magical art, and bringing souls before the operator to answer questions.

The reported text also places him among the Dominions while retaining language about Thrones and Angels.

**[DIS]** This would move Murmur’s written history back more than two centuries and would suggest a striking sequence:

> buffalo → vulture → griffin  
> 25 legions → omitted in Weyer’s Latin → 30 legions  
> magical art → philosophy

However, the relevant folio or page has not been independently located in the accessible manuscript facsimile during this research, and Peterson’s online comparison table has not yet incorporated this witness. The most precise conclusion is therefore:

- **Reported earliest witness:** *Summa Sacre Magice*, text dated 1346, passage reported from the 2025 Skinner–Clark edition.
- **Earliest independently web-verifiable witness:** Weyer, 1577.
- **Open requirement:** citation of the exact Ganelli manuscript folio and a comparison between manuscript, transcription, and translation.

It is no longer safe to state without qualification that “Murmur first appears in 1577.”

## The description across the sources

## Possible medieval witness: *Summa Sacre Magice* (1346; published 2025)

**[DIS]** As reported by a reader of Skinner and Clark’s edition, one passage presents Murmur as a military figure with a ducal crown, mounted not on a griffin but on a buffalo. A second assigns him 25 legions, magical instruction, and the power to present souls for questioning.

If verified, this is not merely an incidental name. Its constellation of rank, mounted military appearance, necromantic office, and angelic orders is close enough to the Weyer–Goetia account to indicate textual relationship. The unusual readings—buffalo, 25 legions, magical art, and Dominions—could preserve an older form, reflect another recension, or be errors introduced in copying or translation. That cannot be decided without the exact Latin and folio.

## Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* (1577), spirit 40

Weyer’s Latin begins:

> “Murmur magnus Dux & Comes: apparet militis forma, equitans in vulture, & ducali corona comptus.”

—Johann Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*, §40, fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum*, 1577, p. 925.

A complete translation of the entry is:

**[DT]** Murmur is a great duke and count. He appears in the form of a soldier, riding on a **vulture**, adorned with a ducal crown. Two ministers precede him with great trumpets. He teaches philosophy completely. He compels souls to appear before the exorcist so that, when questioned, they answer what the operator asks. He formerly belonged partly to the Thrones and partly to the Angels.

Important readings:

- The animal is Latin **vulture**, “on a vulture,” not a gryphon.
- There are **two** heralding ministers.
- His intellectual office is *philosophiam absolutè docet*: he teaches philosophy completely, perfectly, or absolutely.
- His necromantic office is coercive: he compels souls to appear for interrogation.
- Weyer’s printed Latin gives **no legion total**.
- No seal is printed.
- No angel is assigned.
- No secondary form is described.
- No particular hour is included in the individual entry.

Weyer says his source was a *Liber officiorum spirituum*, also called an *Emptorium Salomonis*. He deliberately omitted or disrupted ritual components, presenting the catalogue within a skeptical and anti-witch-persecution argument. Weyer did not deny the existence of spirits in modern materialist terms, but attacked many claims made by witch-hunters and framed the catalogue as a dangerous imposture rather than a recommended devotional manual.

## Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584), Book XVI, chapter 2

Scot’s English begins:

> “Murmur is a great duke and an earle… riding on a griphen, with a dukes crowne on his head.”

—Reginald Scot, *Discoverie of Witchcraft*, first edition, 1584, Book XVI, ch. 2, p. 223.

**[DT]** Scot says that Murmur is a great duke and earl. He appears as a soldier riding a **griffin**, wearing a duke’s crown. Two ministers go before him with great trumpets. He teaches philosophy absolutely. He constrains souls to appear before the exorcist and answer questions. He was partly of the Thrones and partly of the Angels, and he rules **thirty legions**.

Scot introduces two consequential differences:

1. **Vulture becomes griffin.** This may be a translation error, a different exemplar, or an intentional fantastic elaboration. No explanation is supplied.
2. **Thirty legions are added.** Peterson brackets this clause in comparison because it is absent from Weyer’s printed Latin.

The translation may have been prepared or assisted by Abraham Fleming, but that attribution remains editorial reconstruction rather than a statement on Scot’s title page.

Like Weyer, Scot printed ritual and demonological material in a work opposing credulity and persecution. Later magicians extracted the catalogue from its skeptical frame.

## The Lemegeton manuscripts

### Sloane 3825

**[DT]** Sloane 3825 calls Murmur the fifty-fourth spirit, a great duke and earl. He appears as a soldier on a griffin, wears a ducal crown, and is preceded by two ministers sounding great trumpets. He teaches philosophy perfectly and constrains souls of the deceased to appear before the exorcist and answer questions. He was partly of the Thrones and partly of the Angels, rules thirty legions, and possesses a seal to be worn as a lamen.

This is the principal copy-text for Peterson’s 2001 edition. The Murmur entry appears at folio 108r and on pp. 31–32 of Peterson.

### Sloane 2731

**[DT]** Sloane 2731 has essentially the same griffin, crown, trumpeters, philosophy, necromancy, former orders, and thirty legions. Its entry explicitly refers to “2 of his Ministers.” The manuscript labels the accompanying figure “Murmur his Seal.” The name *Murmus* occurs as an alternative reading.

### Sloane 3648

**[DT]** Peterson’s apparatus records **Murmus** as the name in Sloane 3648. The substantive description belongs to the same textual family.

### Harley 6483

**[DT]** Harley 6483, copied by Peter Smart in 1712–1713 and published in Skinner and Rankine’s *Goetia of Dr Rudd*, calls the spirit **Murmus**. It retains the great duke/count rank, soldier on a griffin, ducal crown, philosophy, dead souls, former angelic orders, and thirty legions.

A notable variant is **three ministers**, rather than Weyer’s and the Sloane manuscripts’ two. Because the catalogue contains other copyist disruptions and contaminations, this may be a local error or deliberate expansion; no doctrinal significance is explained.

Harley 6483 also changes the operative context. The demon’s seal is paired with the name and protective authority of the corresponding Shem angel, Nithael. This is not part of Weyer or the ordinary Sloane Goetia.

## Mathers–Crowley, *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King* (1904)

The printed entry opens:

> “The Fifty-fourth Spirit is called Murmur, or Murmus, or Murmux. He is a Great Duke, and an Earl.”

—S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Aleister Crowley, *Goetia*, 1904, pp. 39–40.

**[DT]** The edition describes Murmur as a warrior riding a gryphon, wearing a ducal crown. Ministers precede him with great trumpets, although this wording does not preserve the number as clearly as the manuscripts. He teaches philosophy perfectly and constrains the souls of the deceased to appear and answer the exorcist. He was partly of the Order of Thrones and partly of Angels, rules thirty legions, and has a seal to be worn as a lamen.

Mathers–Crowley standardizes or embellishes several elements:

- *griffin* becomes the more archaic-looking **gryphon**;
- the otherwise unsupported form **Murmux** is printed;
- the manuscript prose is capitalized and ritualized;
- the seal is redrawn in a clean printed form.

The frequently reproduced “Murmur seal” on pendants, websites, and modern art normally descends from this 1904 redrawing, not directly from a sixteenth-century source.

## Peterson, *The Lesser Key of Solomon* (2001)

**[DT]** Peterson prints **“Murmur [Murmus]”** and reconstructs the entry from Sloane 3825 with variants from Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, and Harley 6483. His edition distinguishes manuscript evidence from Mathers–Crowley’s additions and identifies *Murmux* as unsupported by the listed manuscripts.

Peterson also makes the textual history unusually transparent:

- Weyer: number 40;
- Goetia: number 54;
- Sloane 3825: Murmur;
- Sloane 3648 and Harley 6483: Murmus;
- Sloane 2731: Murmur/Murmus;
- Mathers–Crowley: Murmur/Murmus/Murmux.

### What Murmur does not do

The historical entry does **not** say that Murmur:

- teaches languages;
- reveals hidden treasure;
- procures romantic or sexual love;
- supplies familiars;
- grants invisibility;
- transmutes metals;
- makes people witty;
- confers political office;
- heals disease;
- changes the conjurer’s shape.

His two consistent offices are philosophy and interrogation of the dead. Claims beyond those derive from later correspondences, practitioner literature, or fiction.

## Number and order: Weyer’s 69 and the Goetia’s 72

**[DT]** Murmur is spirit **40** in Weyer and **54** in the Goetia.

The Goetia was built substantially from the Weyer/Scot catalogue, but it is not a simple one-for-one reprint. Relative positions shift considerably. Peterson has suggested that damaged, separated, or reordered manuscript pages could explain some displacement, but this is a reconstruction, not a fact recorded by the compiler.

The familiar arithmetic is:

- Weyer: **69** spirits.
- Four Goetic additions: **Vassago** at 3, **Seere** at 70, **Dantalion** at 71, and **Andromalius** at 72.
- One Weyer spirit omitted: **Pruflas/Bufas**, Weyer 4.
- 69 + 4 − 1 = **72**.

Scot also omits Pruflas, so the immediate English source available to later compilers already lacked it.

**[SR]** The number 72 probably encouraged the completed Goetic catalogue’s alignment with other Solomonic and kabbalistic sets of 72, but no surviving compiler’s preface explains the arithmetic. The later Rudd system makes that numerological opportunity explicit by matching each demon to one of the 72 Shem angels.

## The seal

### Form

**[DT]** The Sloane manuscripts provide a handwritten seal beside or beneath Murmur’s entry. Its stable structural features are:

- a long central vertical or near-vertical axis;
- diagonal strokes crossing near the middle;
- opposed crescent-, horn-, or hook-shaped lateral elements;
- small terminal curls or loops;
- approximate bilateral organization rather than strict geometrical symmetry.

The 1904 printed version regularizes the freehand sigil, surrounds it with a double circular boundary, and distributes the name around the circumference. The “M” at the upper portion and the name letters in or around the surrounding ring are features of familiar printed renderings, not evidence that the original character was alphabetically designed.

### Variants

**[DT]** The manuscript copies share the same recognizable underlying character but vary in curvature, spacing, line intersection, and the direction or depth of terminal hooks. These are normal scribal variations. Online versions are also sometimes rotated or mirrored, which can make the lateral crescents appear upper and lower rather than left and right.

**[DT/EC]** Harley 6483 is functionally more significant than a mere drawing variant. In the Rudd arrangement, the demonic character is incorporated into a two-sided or paired talismanic system whose angelic side bears Nithael’s name and the applicable Psalm verse. The object is intended not merely to identify Murmur but to constrain him under the contrary angelic authority.

**[DT]** Weyer and Scot print no Murmur seal. Peterson notes more generally that the seals are absent from Weyer and the earlier *Offices of Spirits* witnesses available to him. Calling the design an “ancient seal of Murmur” is therefore unsupported; its documented history begins in the seventeenth-century Lemegeton manuscript tradition.

## The angel: Nithael

## Identity

| Field | Nithael |
|---|---|
| Number | 54 of 72 |
| Hebrew triplet | **נית** — nun, yod, tav |
| Angelized spelling | **ניתאל**, Nithael; variants include Nith-Hael and, later, Nithanael |
| Rudd pairing | Murmur/Murmus 54 |
| Zodiac | 25°–30° Sagittarius |
| Decan | Third decan of Sagittarius |
| Planet of decan | Saturn |
| Psalm | Vulgate 102:19 / modern 103:19 |
| Traditional title/gloss | “God, King of Heaven” or “King of Heaven” |
| Lenain time | 5:40–6:00 p.m. |
| Later choir | Principalities, in Ambelain-derived tables |
| Later planetary ruler | Venus/Haniel or Anael in the Principalities hierarchy |

### Name and meaning

**[DT]** The underlying three-letter name is נית. Adding אל, *El*, “God,” produces the pronounceable angel name Nithael.

**[DIS]** “God, King of Heaven” is a traditional title or devotional gloss, especially familiar from Lenain’s tables. It is not a transparent dictionary translation of the three consonants נ־י־ת. Modern renderings such as “God who rejuvenates,” “eternal youth,” or “the God of longevity” are theological interpretations built from the angel’s attributed powers.

Nithael should not be etymologically confused with Hebrew **Nathanael**, נתנאל, “God has given.” The similar English spellings conceal different Hebrew formations.

## The seventy-two names from Exodus

**[TR]** The 72-name construction uses Exodus 14:19–21, traditionally counted as three Hebrew verses of 72 letters each:

1. verse 19 is written in its normal direction;
2. verse 20 is written in reverse;
3. verse 21 is written normally;
4. vertical columns yield 72 three-letter combinations.

The raw products are three-letter divine names. Later Jewish, Christian-kabbalistic, and magical authors append endings such as **-el** or **-yah** and treat the results as individual angels.

**[SR]** The expression *Shem ha-Mephorash* has a broader and older history. In rabbinic sources it can refer to the “explicit” or “distinguished” divine name, particularly the Tetragrammaton. Later traditions apply related terminology to names of 12, 42, or 72 letters. It is therefore misleading to say that the phrase has always and exclusively meant the 72 angel names.

## The Psalm verse

Lenain and the later tradition give:

> “Dominus in coelo paravit sedem suam: et regnum ipsius omnibus dominabitur.”

This is Vulgate Psalm 102:19, corresponding to Psalm 103:19 in most modern Hebrew and Protestant numbering: the Lord establishes his throne in heaven, and his kingdom rules over all.

The verse’s images of throne and universal kingship suit Nithael’s royal title and, within Rudd’s system, provide an especially pointed counterweight to a demon formerly associated with the Thrones.

## Historical transmission

### *Sefer Raziel*

**[TR/SR]** The medieval *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* is a composite magical and cosmological work attributed in legend to the angel Raziel, who is said to have given secret wisdom to Adam. Its textual history is complex: no single medieval manuscript simply equals the Amsterdam 1701 printed edition. It contains important traditions about divine names and the 72-fold name, but the complete later Nithael package—ordinal number, Sagittarius quinance, Psalm, civil offices, and opposition to Murmur—should not be projected backward into every recension.

### Reuchlin

Johannes Reuchlin’s *De Arte Cabalistica* (1517) helped transmit Christian-kabbalistic treatments of the 72 names into Renaissance learned magic. **[SR]** This is part of the intellectual prehistory of the later angel lists, not evidence that Reuchlin paired Nithael with Murmur.

### Vigenère and Kircher

Blaise de Vigenère transmitted expanded treatments of the 72 names and associated angelic seals in the late sixteenth century. Skinner and Rankine regard this material as an important source for Rudd’s protective angelic apparatus.

Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–1654) printed elaborate tables of the 72 names. Kircher’s syncretic Christian-kabbalistic system helped make the set readily available to early modern ritualists. Neither Kircher’s publication nor the Jewish name tradition by itself entails a pairing with Goetic demons.

### Rudd and Harley 6483

**[DT]** Harley 6483 applies the corresponding Shem angel to each Goetic spirit. Number 54 Murmus is therefore subjected to number 54 Nithael.

**[EC]** The logic is apotropaic and hierarchical: the operator does not invoke a demon in isolation but places its character beneath an angelic name, scriptural verse, and divine authority. The angel acts as a “contrary” or controlling intelligence whose holiness constrains the infernal spirit.

This mechanism resembles the *Testament of Solomon*, where demons reveal the angels capable of thwarting them, but it is not the same list and does not establish direct transmission of a Murmur–Nithael pair from antiquity.

### The Rudd attribution

Harley 6483 is physically a transcript made by **Peter Smart in 1712–1713**. Skinner and Rankine argue that it preserves an earlier work of Thomas Rudd, perhaps composed around 1655–1656.

**[DIS]** “The Goetia of Dr Rudd” is consequently an editorially reconstructed provenance, not an autograph title conclusively written by Rudd himself. The manuscript certainly preserves a distinctive angel-over-demon system; how much of that system can be attributed directly to Thomas Rudd remains debated.

### Lenain, 1823

Lazare Républicain Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* supplies the developed Nithael entry on p. 83:

- degrees 266–270 of the zodiac, corresponding to the final five degrees of Sagittarius;
- invocation from 5:40 to 6:00 p.m.;
- Psalm 102:19 in Vulgate numbering;
- divine name **Bora**, assigned to the “language of the Zaflanians”;
- rulership over emperors, kings, princes, and civil or ecclesiastical dignitaries;
- support for legitimate dynasties, stable empires, peaceful reigns, and retention of employment;
- fame through writings and eloquence;
- reputation among learned people and the confidence of a prince;
- mercy and long life.

Lenain also gives five “physical” calendar days distributed through the year: May 12, July 23, October 3, December 14, and February 24. These reflect another cyclic scheme and should not be mistaken for the five consecutive December days used by modern guardian-angel calendars.

His “contrary genius” overturns monarchies, ruins empires, provokes revolutions, and pursues office through destructive upheaval. These are **[EC/TR]**, not claims found in Exodus or the early Goetia.

### Papus

Gérard Encausse, writing as **Papus**, reproduced and popularized Lenain’s angelic tables in his kabbalistic writings around the turn of the twentieth century. Papus helped move the scheme from specialist French occultism into the wider ceremonial-magic revival.

### Golden Dawn and Crowley

The Golden Dawn integrated the 72-name angels into its densely layered tarot, zodiacal, planetary, and decanic system. Crowley inherited much of this infrastructure.

However, Crowley’s demon table in *777* does **not** simply give each demon the five-degree sector of the same-numbered Shem angel. This is the source of the most common modern error concerning Murmur.

### Ambelain

Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) substantially transmits the Lenain material while fitting it into a regularized angelic hierarchy. In this later system Nithael belongs to the **Principalities**, governed by Haniel or Anael, with Venusian and Netzach associations.

**[EC]** That later placement does not derive from Exodus, nor does it explain Murmur’s “partly Thrones and partly Angels” statement. The resemblance between choirs in the two descriptions is tempting, but the schemes arose in different textual layers.

### Haziel

Haziel—the pen name of François or Bernard-Termès—popularized the 72 angels in late-twentieth-century and early-twenty-first-century guardian-angel books. Nithael becomes a personal angel for people born approximately December 17–21 or December 18–22, depending on the edition. He grants beauty, authority, abundance, reputation, long life, health, and protection of legitimate position.

**[MI/TR]** Haziel’s “guardian angel by birthday” model and its psychological descriptions are modern popular angelology. They are historically influential, but should not be presented as teachings of *Sefer Raziel* or Rudd.

| King | Sun | Gold |
| Duke | Venus | Copper |
| Prince/Prelate | Jupiter | Tin |
| Marquis | Moon | Silver |
| Earl/Count | Mars | Iron |
| President | Mercury | Mercury |
| Knight | Saturn | Lead |

Under this later system Murmur’s double rank gives **Venus/copper** as duke and **Mars/iron** as earl.

But the Goetia’s own “Observations” prescribe:

- kings: gold;
- marquises: silver;
- dukes: copper;
- princes or prelates: tin;
- knights: lead;
- presidents: mercury;
- earls: **copper and silver in equal parts**.

Thus a historically careful account must record both:

- **[DT] Goetia ritual rule:** Earl = Venus/copper plus Moon/silver.
- **[EC] Golden Dawn planetary hierarchy:** Earl = Mars/iron.

A modern statement that Murmur’s “correct metal is iron” is therefore incomplete. Copper is at least equally well grounded and is explicit for his ducal rank.

### Crowley’s *777*: the actual Murmur attribution

**[DT/EC]** *Liber 777*, first published anonymously in 1909, assigns Murmur to the nocturnal or cadent half of the third decan of Virgo:

- **20°–30° Virgo**;
- **Ten of Pentacles/Disks**;
- Mercury in Virgo;
- Golden Dawn title: “Lord of Wealth”;
- day demon: **Bathin**, spirit 18;
- night demon: **Murmur**, spirit 54.

This distribution explains why one pip card can carry two Goetic spirits. The 36 decans receive one spirit by day and one by night, making 72.

Crowley’s associated magical image summarizes Murmur as a warrior with a ducal crown riding a gryphon, accompanied by trumpeters. His dual rank is represented by Venus and Mars.

### Why Ten of Wands also appears

**[EC]** Nithael rules 25°–30° Sagittarius in the Lenain-derived five-degree scheme. That quinance lies within 20°–30° Sagittarius, the Golden Dawn’s **Ten of Wands**, governed by Saturn in Sagittarius.

Modern tables—especially those influenced by Poke Runyon—match demon 54 directly to angel 54 and then transfer the angel’s Sagittarius degrees to Murmur. The result is:

- Murmur + Nithael;
- 25°–30° Sagittarius;
- December dates;
- third decan Sagittarius;
- Ten of Wands.

The arithmetic is coherent, but it is not Crowley’s *777* attribution. Calling Murmur “the Goetic spirit of the Ten of Wands in 777” is erroneous.

### Poke Runyon

Poke Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* systematizes the serial zodiacal overlay and presents Murmur at roughly 25°–29° Sagittarius, commonly December 17–21, together with Nithael. Runyon characterizes Murmur as a patron of necromancers.

**[MI/EC]** “Patron Demon of Necromancers” is a modern epithet based on Murmur’s traditional power over the dead. It is not a title in Weyer, Scot, or the Goetia.

### Astrological timing

The historical instructions produce several distinct possible elections:

1. **Goetia lunar rule:** work when the Moon is 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, or 14 days old. Other lunar ages are called unprofitable.
2. **Ducal rule:** from sunrise until noon, in clear weather.
3. **Earl’s rule:** at any hour of the day, in woods or quiet places where people do not frequent and noise will not intrude.
4. **Crowley/777 election:** third decan Virgo, with Murmur on the nocturnal/cadent side.
5. **Nithael election:** 25°–30° Sagittarius and, in Lenain, 5:40–6:00 p.m.
6. **Modern Runyon/Haziel calendar:** approximately December 17–21 or neighboring dates.

**[DIS]** The original text does not explain how to reconcile Murmur’s simultaneous duke and earl prescriptions. Nor does it tell the operator to combine lunar age, rank hour, Virgo decan, Sagittarius quinance, and Nithael’s twenty-minute interval. Such combined elections are modern syntheses.

### Enochian overlays

Crowley’s 1904 edition appends Goetic conjurations translated into the “Magical” or “Angelic” language commonly called Enochian. That places the Goetia in an Enochian ritual environment, but it does not turn each Goetic spirit into an Enochian entity.

Harley 6483 says more generally that some spirits have connections to Enoch’s Tables, and Skinner–Rankine print related Rudd material concerning Dee’s *Tabula Sancta*.

**[DIS]** No primary source consulted assigns Murmur a specific Watchtower square, Enochian governor, aethyr, or Dee angel. Websites that describe him simply as “an Enochian demon” collapse two separately transmitted systems.

## Iconography

## The mounted warrior

Murmur’s visual history is unusually useful for tracing textual transmission:

- **Reported medieval reading:** crowned soldier on a buffalo.
- **Weyer, 1577:** soldier on a vulture.
- **Scot, 1584:** soldier on a griffin.
- **Lemegeton manuscripts:** soldier or warrior on a griffin.
- **Mathers–Crowley, 1904:** warrior on a gryphon.
- **Modern art:** usually an armored or skeletal knight mounted on a winged leonine gryphon, with a ducal crown and trumpet-bearing attendants.

**[SR]** The griffin is unlikely to be a recovery of ancient iconography. Its demonstrable entry into the checked source chain is Scot’s English. Later visual artists generally follow Scot and the Goetia rather than Weyer’s Latin.

## Collin de Plancy

The sixth edition of Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* states:

> “Murmur, grand-duc et comte de l’empire infernal, démon de la musique.”

—Collin de Plancy, *Dictionnaire infernal*, sixth edition, 1863, p. 483.

De Plancy continues by describing a soldier mounted on a **vulture**, wearing a ducal crown and preceded by trumpets or clarions. He repeats the former Orders of Angels and Thrones and cites Wierus/Weyer.

Several layers are visible:

- **[DT]** De Plancy returns to Weyer’s vulture rather than Scot’s griffin.
- **[MI]** “Demon of music” is de Plancy’s concise characterization. Weyer calls the trumpets part of Murmur’s arrival but does not give him an office over music.
- **[DT]** The entry is not accompanied by an identifiable Louis Le Breton engraving.

The famous 1863 edition includes 69 Breton illustrations, but not every demon received a portrait. No dedicated Murmur plate was located in the 1863 page or illustration set. Images marketed online as “de Plancy’s Murmur” should therefore be checked individually; many are modern drawings or depictions of another demon.

## The seal in modern visual culture

The Mathers–Crowley seal has become Murmur’s dominant icon:

- redrawn on occult reference sites;
- made into pendants and rings;
- incorporated into tattoos;
- placed behind modern fantasy portraits;
- used as a summoning or identification glyph in games and fan art.

These are genuine continuations of Goetic iconography, but they generally reproduce the 1904 line drawing rather than the variable Sloane hand.

## Game design

Kazuma Kaneko’s designs for the *Megami Tensei* franchise visually reinterpret Murmur as a stylized demon rather than attempting manuscript antiquarianism. Such designs are **[MI]**: they borrow the name and often the rank or necromantic associations, but their anatomy, costume, colors, and combat abilities belong to modern game art.

## People and works

## Johann Weyer

Weyer supplies the first independently verifiable printed description in 1577. He is foundational but paradoxical: later magicians treat his catalogue as a grimoire, whereas he printed it as part of an argument against demonic imposture, fraudulent magic, and the lethal persecution of accused witches.

## Reginald Scot

Scot’s 1584 English text transmitted the catalogue to Anglophone readers. For Murmur, his “griphen” and thirty legions proved more influential than Weyer’s vulture and silence about legions.

## The Lemegeton compiler or compilers

The anonymous seventeenth-century compilers transformed the Weyer–Scot office catalogue into a practical Solomonic manual by adding seals, ritual tools, conjurations, lunar rules, metals, and a completed 72-spirit organization.

## Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

The Harley manuscripts associate the material with Thomas Rudd, an English mathematician and military engineer with an interest in angelic magic. Peter Smart copied Harley 6483 in 1712–1713. The demon-controlling angelic apparatus is the distinctive contribution of this recension, irrespective of whether every part can be proven Rudd’s autograph composition.

## Collin de Plancy and Louis Le Breton

De Plancy gave Murmur the enduring epithet “demon of music” in the *Dictionnaire infernal*. Breton provided the famous engraved demon portraits for the 1863 edition, but Murmur apparently was not among the illustrated entries.

## Arthur Edward Waite

Waite’s *Book of Black Magic and of Pacts* and later *Book of Ceremonial Magic* helped transmit the Goetic catalogue and seals to twentieth-century occult readers. His approach was often critical and moralizing, but his publications materially broadened access.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Aleister Crowley

Mathers prepared a Goetia edition from British Museum manuscripts; Crowley edited, published, and supplied prefatory and Enochian material in 1904. Their text established the modern standard wording, including “Murmux” and the familiar printed seal.

Crowley’s *777* later placed Murmur in the third decan of Virgo as a nocturnal demon of the Ten of Pentacles. That attribution must be distinguished from the Nithael/Sagittarius system.

## Frater Achad

**[DT — negative finding]** No Murmur-specific operation, doctrinal interpretation, or historically significant discussion by Frater Achad was identified in the sources consulted. His broader relationship to Crowley and qabalah does not establish a Murmur tradition.

## Poke Runyon

Runyon is particularly important for the modern fusion of Goetic serial number, Shem angel, five-degree zodiacal sector, and ritual calendar. His treatment pairs Murmur with Nithael in Sagittarius and promotes the “patron of necromancers” interpretation.

## Lon Milo DuQuette and Christopher Hyatt

*Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* (1992) helped reframe the text for modern practitioners and provided extensive practical commentary. Its well-known autobiographical evocation narrative concerns Orobas rather than Murmur. It should not be cited as evidence of a DuQuette Murmur operation.

## Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 edition is the indispensable accessible critical text. It separates manuscript readings from Mathers–Crowley, supplies source notes, and provides online transcriptions of Weyer, Scot, and the Lemegeton.

## Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their 2007 *Goetia of Dr Rudd* made Harley 6483’s angelic control system widely available and argued for its connection with Thomas Rudd. Their reconstruction is influential, though the manuscript’s direct physical evidence is Peter Smart’s eighteenth-century copy.

Skinner and Daniel Clark’s 2025 *Summa Sacre Magice* edition may now radically alter Murmur’s chronology if the reported medieval passages are confirmed.

## Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent argued broadly for understanding grimoires through necromantic, spirit-working, and late antique continuities rather than treating them solely as corrupt ceremonial texts. That framework is pertinent to Murmur’s explicit command over deceased souls.

**[DT — negative finding]** No pre-Weyer Murmur witness attributable specifically to Stratton-Kent’s published research was identified here. His general historical model should not be substituted for a textual citation.

## Modern demonolatry

S. Connolly and related practitioner traditions give Murmur additional correspondences such as green, sandalwood, copper, Venus, an “enn,” and practical rules about preventing dead persons from harming the living or overstaying through a medium.

**[MI]** These are openly modern operative correspondences or personal gnosis. Some, such as copper and Venus, derive from the ducal rank; the enn and mediumship rules do not occur in Weyer, Scot, or the Goetia.

## Grimoire ancestors and relatives

## *Testament of Solomon*

The Greek *Testament of Solomon*, a composite work commonly dated in its parts to the early centuries CE, gives Solomon a divinely empowered ring and presents interrogations in which demons reveal names, forms, functions, and the angels that thwart them.

**[SR]** It is an important structural ancestor of Solomonic demon catalogues and of the principle that an angel can constrain a demon. It does not contain Murmur or Nithael and supplies no direct Murmur–Nithael lineage.

## Munich Handbook, Clm 849

The fifteenth-century *Munich Handbook of Necromancy* contains ritual spirit operations and names comparable to later grimoires.

**[DT — negative finding]** Murmur was not located in the published material consulted. Its relevance is contextual and generic, not a direct attestation.

## *Livre des Esperitz*

The *Livre des Esperitz*, preserved in a sixteenth-century manuscript while probably drawing upon older material, contains 46 spirits and is a close relative of the Weyer catalogue. Julien Véronèse and Jean-Patrice Boudet’s research places it within the developing European demonic “who’s who.”

**[DT — negative finding]** Murmur does not appear in the published list consulted. It may represent a branch of the same catalogue tradition that lacks the later Murmur material.

## *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

Weyer explicitly names a *Book of the Offices of Spirits* as his source. That title designates a branching and partly lost textual family rather than one stable surviving book.

**[SR]** Murmur may have stood in a lost or unedited branch available to Weyer. The newly reported *Summa Sacre Magice* evidence, if confirmed, would provide a much earlier analogue. Accessible *Offices* relatives do not yet yield a securely cited pre-Weyer Murmur passage.

## *Book of Abramelin*

The *Book of Abramelin* contains extensive lists of spirits subordinated to principal demons.

**[DT — negative finding]** Murmur was not identified in the standard list consulted. Similarity of genre is not evidence that the name derives from Abramelin.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Was Murmur first attested in 1577?

Former answer: yes. Current answer: **disputed**.

The 2025 Skinner–Clark *Summa Sacre Magice* edition reportedly contains a much earlier Murmur. Exact folio-level verification remains necessary. Weyer is still the earliest source that can presently be checked online without relying on the secondary report.

### 2. Vulture, griffin, or buffalo?

- Buffalo: reported from the *Summa*.
- Vulture: Weyer’s unambiguous Latin and de Plancy.
- Griffin: Scot, the Lemegeton, and Mathers–Crowley.

The griffin is not a faithful translation of Weyer’s printed *vulture*. It either reflects textual variation or an error that became canonical.

### 3. Two ministers or three?

- Weyer: two.
- Scot: two.
- Sloane 2731/3825: two.
- Harley 6483: three.
- Mathers–Crowley: ministers, without clearly preserving the number in the principal sentence.

Three is a Harley variant, not the consensus reading.

### 4. Twenty-five or thirty legions?

- Reported *Summa*: 25.
- Weyer’s printed Latin: no number.
- Scot and the Goetia: 30.

The often-repeated claim that “Weyer says thirty legions” usually comes from silently combining Scot’s English with Weyer’s catalogue.

### 5. Philosophy or magic?

Weyer onward says philosophy. The reported medieval entry says magical art. If authentic, the later reading may represent deliberate intellectualization—“magic” softened to “philosophy”—or a different recension. This remains unresolved.

### 6. Murmur, Murmus, Murmux, or Murmuur?

- Murmur: standard from Weyer.
- Murmus: genuine manuscript variant.
- Murmux: securely prominent from 1904, without identified manuscript support.
- Murmuur: modern reference-work spelling.

Treating all four as equally ancient is inaccurate.

### 7. Is Murmur Mormo?

There is no textual bridge in the source chain. The identification is a modern phonetic and mythological synthesis. It remains possible that remote sound-association influenced a lost scribe, but no evidence presently establishes this.

### 8. What is Murmur’s planet?

There is no single answer because two systems conflict:

- Duke: Venus.
- Earl in Golden Dawn: Mars.
- Earl in the Goetia’s own metal directions: Venus plus Moon.

Any one-planet answer conceals Murmur’s double rank and the historical difference between systems.

### 9. Which tarot card belongs to Murmur?

- Crowley’s *777*: **Ten of Pentacles**, Virgo, by night.
- Nithael serial overlay: **Ten of Wands**, Sagittarius.

The latter is a defensible later synthesis but not a quotation from *777*.

### 10. Is Nithael’s meaning “God who rejuvenates”?

That is a devotional interpretation based on attributed longevity. The only transparent component is *El*, God. “God, King of Heaven” is Lenain’s traditional title, not a philologically demonstrated translation of נית.

### 11. Is the angel pairing Jewish Kabbalah?

The three-letter divine name comes from Jewish name mysticism. Adding angelic endings and building elaborate angel biographies develops across Jewish and Christian-kabbalistic reception. Pairing Nithael with the fifty-fourth Goetic demon is specifically a Rudd/Harley operation, not an inherent teaching of Exodus or classical Jewish Kabbalah.

### 12. Is Harley 6483 really Rudd’s book?

It is Peter Smart’s 1712–1713 copy. Skinner and Rankine reconstruct an earlier Rudd source. The title *Goetia of Dr Rudd* is useful but must not be mistaken for proof of an autograph manuscript.

### 13. Is Murmur a demon of music?

De Plancy says so in 1863. Earlier texts mention trumpeters but assign no musical office. The title is a nineteenth-century characterization that later writers sometimes project back into Weyer.

### 14. Does Murmur have a second form?

No checked early entry supplies one. The warrior on the mount is the sole prescribed appearance. Claims about demanding a human, beautiful, or harmless form derive from generic ritual practice or modern extrapolation.

### 15. Are the seals ancient?

No. A seal is absent from Weyer and Scot and appears in the seventeenth-century Lemegeton tradition. The ubiquitous circular form is especially indebted to the 1904 edition.

## Open questions

1. **Where exactly is Murmur in the *Summa Sacre Magice* manuscript?**  
   The decisive next evidence would be manuscript shelfmark, folio, Latin transcription, and the corresponding page in Skinner–Clark’s 2025 edition.

2. **Is the reported “buffalo” a secure translation?**  
   The underlying Latin might indicate a buffalo, bull, ox, vulture, or another animal confused through abbreviation or copying.

3. **Does the *Summa* really give two separate Murmur entries?**  
   If so, they may derive from separate catalogues combined by Ganelli, explaining divergent ranks, choirs, and offices.

4. **Where did Scot’s griffin come from?**  
   A lost Latin exemplar could have differed from Weyer’s printed text, but a simple translation or copying mistake remains possible.

5. **Where did the thirty legions enter the tradition?**  
   Scot is the first checked printed source. It could preserve lost source material or be an interpolation.

6. **Is *Murmux* a misreading of handwriting?**  
   No manuscript exemplar has yet been identified. Mathers may have encountered an unreported copy, misread *Murmus*, or generated the form editorially.

7. **What is the exact relationship between Harley 6483 and Thomas Rudd?**  
   More provenance evidence would be required to separate Rudd’s contributions from Peter Smart’s copy and intermediate exemplars.

8. **Did Vigenère directly supply every seal used in the Rudd apparatus?**  
   Skinner and Rankine identify a transmission relationship, but individual sigil-by-sigil stemmatics remain difficult.

9. **Why was Murmur assigned Virgo in *777*?**  
   The day/night decan arrangement follows a formal distribution of the 72 spirits. It does not obviously arise from Murmur’s traditional offices and may be primarily serial or table-driven.

10. **Was de Plancy’s “demon of music” an inference from the trumpets or another source?**  
    No independent source for the epithet was located.

11. **Can any Murmur-specific operation be documented before modern practitioner literature?**  
    The catalogues explain how to evoke the spirit, but surviving first-person records of a named Murmur operation are scarce.

12. **Which modern fictional appearances are deliberate Goetic references?**  
    Direct borrowing is clear in catalogue-driven franchises such as *Megami Tensei* and *Iruma-kun*. It remains unproven for works where “Murmur” could simply evoke sound or secrecy.
