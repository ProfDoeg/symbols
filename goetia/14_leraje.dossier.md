# Leraje: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes the kinds of claims in the following way:

- **[Documented text]** — stated in a manuscript, early printed grimoire, or identifiable historical edition.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** — a conclusion drawn by modern textual historians from manuscript relationships, chronology, or philology.
- **[Tradition]** — received ritual or religious teaching whose historical or empirical status is not thereby established.
- **[Esoteric correspondence]** — an occult attribution imposed by a later system, such as the Golden Dawn or *777*.
- **[Disputed]** — proposed but unsupported, textually uncertain, or contradicted by other evidence.
- **[Legend]** — supernatural narrative reported as tradition rather than historical fact.
- **[Modern invention]** — a feature first found in recent occult literature, fiction, games, or popular reference sites.

The evidence supports a comparatively simple conclusion: **Loray/Oray first appears securely in Johann Weyer’s 1577 demon catalogue.** Its archer, battle, putrefaction, thirty-legion, and marquis attributes remain remarkably stable. Green clothing, Sagittarius, the seal, angelic counter-power Mebahel, tarot, decans, and most personality or devotional material are later additions.

## The name and its origins

### The attested spellings

The name changed repeatedly during copying and translation:

| Date/source | Form |
|---|---|
| 1577, Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* | **Loray**, alias **Oray** |
| 1584, Scot, *Discoverie of Witchcraft* | **Leraie**, with **Loray** reported as the source reading; alias **Oray** |
| c. 1665+, Sloane 3648 | **LERAJE** |
| begun 1686, Sloane 2731 | **LERAJE** |
| seventeenth century, Sloane 3825 | **Leraye**, with “or **Leraje**” added above the line |
| 1712–13, Harley 6483 | **Leraic** |
| 1863, Collin de Plancy | **Oray ou Loray** |
| 1904, Mathers–Crowley | heading **LERAJE, or LERAIKHA**; prose **Leraje (or Leraie)** |
| 2001, Peterson | base reading **Leraye (or Leraje)**, with full variant collation |

**[Documented text]** Peterson’s 2001 note at p. 13 reads:

> S1, S2: “LERAJE”; H: “Leraic”; C: “Leraje, or Leraikha”; W: “Loray, alias Oray”; “Leraie” in Scot’s translation.

Here Peterson’s sigla are S1 = Sloane 2731, S2 = Sloane 3648, H = Harley 6483, C = Crowley/Mathers, and W = Weyer. His base manuscript, S3, is Sloane 3825. A searchable copy of the edition is available through this [Peterson PDF](https://jamesparsonslakebucket.s3-us-west-1.amazonaws.com/text/Peterson_Joseph_H_The_Lesser_Key_of_Solomon.pdf).

### Is there an etymology?

**[Finding]** There is no secure etymology.

Unlike **Baal**, **Astarte/Astaroth**, **Amun/Amon**, **Asmodeus/Ashmedai**, **Belial/Beliyaʿal**, or **Phoenix**, the name Loray/Oray has not been securely connected to an ancient god, hero, place, Hebrew common noun, Greek word, or Latin word. No ancient inscription, classical literary source, Jewish demonological catalogue, or medieval theological work presently supplies a persuasive cognate.

The earliest secure form is **Loray**, not Leraje. Any proposed etymology must therefore explain the change:

> Loray / Oray → Leraie → Leraje / Leraye → Leraic / Leraikha

That looks at least as much like ordinary scribal and editorial variation as the survival of an ancient name.

### Suggested derivations and their evidentiary status

- **[Disputed] Liber Juratus connection.** The *Sworn Book of Honorius* includes a Venus-day angel named **Leyraiell** among a much longer list: “Patnelin, Keyalin, Nailin, Leyraiell, Ablayeill…” The text can be checked in Peterson’s [*Liber Juratus* transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/juratus/juratus.htm). The resemblance to *Leraie* has encouraged speculation, but no manuscript stemma, shared description, office, rank, or intermediary spelling demonstrates that Leyraiell became Loray.

- **[Disputed] Hebrew derivations.** Internet sources sometimes derive the name from expressions glossed “to see evil,” “evil sight,” or “the evil one.” These generally begin from the late spelling *Leraje* rather than Weyer’s *Loray*, and no historical Hebrew spelling accompanies them.

- **[Disputed] Latin derivations.** Connections with *laedere*, “to injure,” are semantically attractive because of putrefying wounds, but phonologically and historically unproved.

- **[Disputed] Akkadian or Mesopotamian roots.** Claims that the name denotes a warrior, archer, or battle spirit in Akkadian are not supported by cited lexical or cuneiform evidence.

- **[Disputed] French derivations.** Loray is a French place-name and surname, but there is no evidence that Weyer’s demon was named for the commune or a family. Resemblance alone is insufficient.

- **[Modern invention] Lerna, the Hydra, Robin Hood, or a primordial “green archer.”** These are imaginative associations produced from the later iconography. They do not occur in Weyer, Scot, the Lemegeton manuscripts, de Plancy, or the Rudd material.

### Is Loray present before Weyer?

**[Finding]** Not under that name in any currently verified pre-1577 source.

There are older catalogues of spirits, but the distinction between **ancestry of the genre**, **ancestry of a description**, and **attestation of a name** matters:

1. **Michael Scot’s *Liber introductorius***, composed in the early thirteenth century, supplies an early learned demon catalogue but not Loray.

2. The **Munich Handbook**, Clm 849, a fifteenth-century necromancer’s manual, contains spirit names comparable with some later Weyer entries but no identifiable Loray.

3. The French **Livre des Esperitz**, preserved in Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29, belongs to the same catalogue tradition and shares numerous demons with Weyer. Its surviving form is normally dated to the mid-sixteenth century. It does not provide a secure Loray/Oray. See Jean-Patrice Boudet’s study, [“Les who’s who démonologiques de la Renaissance et leurs ancêtres médiévaux”](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/1019).

4. Trithemius’s 1508 *Antipalus maleficiorum* names a **Liber officiorum spirituum**. Weyer later said that his catalogue came from a book of spirit offices. **[Scholarly reconstruction]** This makes a lost *Offices of Spirits* a plausible immediate ancestor, but it does not prove that Loray occurred in the 1508 book.

5. **Sloane 3853** contains a spirit named **Berci**:

> Berci is a gret revler & wyll apere in þe liknes of an archer berynge A bowe, & A quivor of Iroz [arrows] & is þe begynner of warres & makethe to shote nere the marke & hathe under hym 30 legion of spryts.

This is available in Peterson’s [*Offices of Spirits* material](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/offices.htm). A related Folger text has **Barsy**.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Berci/Barsy is a strong descriptive relative: archer, war, thirty legions. It may preserve another branch of the textual unit that became Loray. But because the names are not close and the wound-putrefaction office is absent, it is evidence for the **description’s ancestry**, not a pre-Weyer occurrence of **Loray**.

6. The **Book of Abramelin** contains extensive lists of spirits, but no securely identifiable Leraje/Loray has been demonstrated.

7. The **Testament of Solomon** supplies the ancient literary model of Solomon questioning demons and learning their thwarting angels. It does not name Leraje or Mebahel and does not contain this archer’s description.

The most defensible statement is therefore:

> **Loray is first securely attested in Weyer’s printed *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* of 1577. An older spirit-office text probably lies behind Weyer, while Berci/Barsy may be a descriptive cousin, but no surviving pre-Weyer witness has yet supplied the same name.**

## The description across the sources

## 1. Johann Weyer, 1577

### Context

**[Documented text]** Johann Weyer or Wier (1515–1588), a physician and former pupil of Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa, appended the *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* to the 1577 fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum*. He opposed the judicial torture and execution of accused witches but did not simply deny the existence of spirits. His catalogue contains **69 entries**.

Peterson observes that Weyer omitted operative components, apparently so the material could not readily be used as a working conjuration manual. The text and its source history are presented in [Peterson’s bilingual *Pseudomonarchia*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

### Weyer’s Latin, verbatim

Entry 13, approximately p. 915 in the 1577 printing:

> **Loray, aliàs Oray, magnus Marchio, se ostendens in forma sagittarii pulcherrimi, pharetram & arcum gestantis: author existit omnium præliorum, & vulnera putrefacit quæ à sagittariis infliguntur, quos obijcit optimos tribus diebus. Triginta dominatur legionibus.**

A close translation is:

> Loray, otherwise Oray, a great Marquis, shows himself in the form of a most handsome archer, carrying a quiver and bow. He is the author of all battles and causes wounds inflicted by archers to putrefy. *Quos obijcit optimos tribus diebus.* He rules thirty legions.

The italicized sentence fragment is textually difficult and discussed below.

### What Weyer actually says

- **[Documented text]** Name: Loray, alias Oray.
- **[Documented text]** Number: 13.
- **[Documented text]** Rank: great Marquis.
- **[Documented text]** Appearance: an exceptionally handsome archer with quiver and bow.
- **[Documented text]** Office: author of all battles.
- **[Documented text]** Office: causes arrow wounds made by archers to putrefy.
- **[Documented text]** Command: thirty legions.
- **[Finding]** Weyer does **not** say that the archer is dressed in green.
- **[Finding]** Weyer does **not** assign Sagittarius.
- **[Finding]** Weyer supplies no seal, controlling angel, tarot card, decan, personality, love power, healing function, or second form.

### The corrupt “three days” clause

The phrase is:

> *quos obijcit optimos tribus diebus*

Peterson places a tentative gloss in Scot’s text: “which he shoots best within three days.” The Latin is awkward. *Quos* requires an antecedent; *obijcit* can mean throws, casts, exposes, or opposes; *optimos* is grammatically plural; *tribus diebus* means “in/for/within three days.”

**[Disputed]** Possible intentions include:

- the arrows or archers he sends are most effective for three days;
- the wounded become most seriously affected within three days;
- he confronts or dispatches excellent archers for three days;
- a copying omission has destroyed the original sense.

No conjecture has achieved textual confirmation. The responsible course is to retain the Latin and mark the uncertainty.

This corrupt clause probably generated several later inventions:

- de Plancy’s “deadliest javelins”;
- claims that Leraje can “drive away mobs”;
- claims of a three-day disease or three-day deadline;
- translations treating *quos* as arrows, archers, wounds, or crowds.

None is a secure literal recovery.

## 2. Reginald Scot, 1584

### Context

**[Documented text]** Reginald Scot incorporated an English version of Weyer’s catalogue into Book XVI, chapter 2 of *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584). The translation has sometimes been attributed to Abraham Fleming, but the precise division of responsibility is not entirely certain. Scot’s broader purpose was skeptical and anti-persecution.

Scot omits Weyer’s fourth spirit, **Pruflas/Bufas**, but otherwise follows the sequence. Loray is still the thirteenth description in the inherited series.

### Scot’s English, verbatim

Original pagination approximately p. 379:

> **Leraie [Loray], alias Oray, a great marquesse, shewing himselfe in the likenesse of a galant archer, carrieng a bowe and a quiver, he is author of all battels, he dooth putrifie all such wounds as are made with arrowes by archers, Quos optimos obijcit tribus diebus, [which he shoots best within three days] and he hath regiment over thirtie legions.**

The bracketed editorial material is supplied in Peterson’s transcription; the Latin itself is retained because the translator evidently could not confidently resolve it. The full text appears beside Weyer at [Esoteric Archives](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

### Changes from Weyer

- **Loray → Leraie**, although Loray remains recognized as the source form.
- *sagittarii pulcherrimi* becomes a “galant archer”: handsome, splendid, or fine-looking.
- *omnia prælia* becomes “all battels.”
- Thirty legions remain unchanged.
- The problematic Latin remains embedded rather than silently normalized.

Scot introduces neither green clothing nor a seal.

## 3. The Lemegeton manuscripts

### The manuscript family

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The *Ars Goetia* is not an ancient Solomonic book preserved intact from antiquity. Its first section was assembled in seventeenth-century England, drawing heavily on Weyer/Scot and on broader spirit-office literature.

Peterson’s [manuscript survey](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm) describes the principal witnesses:

- **Sloane 3648**, copied c. 1665 or later, is among the earliest important witnesses.
- **Sloane 3825**, seventeenth century, is Peterson’s principal base manuscript because of its relative completeness and accuracy.
- **Sloane 2731** was begun on 18 January 1686 in old-style dating and is a composite, sometimes careless copy.
- **Harley 6483**, dated 1712–13, is a handsome but late, innovative text carrying “Dr. Rudd” material.

### Why Leraje becomes number 14

Weyer has 69 spirits. The *Goetia* reaches 72 by:

- adding **Vassago** at no. 3;
- omitting Weyer’s **Pruflas/Bufas**;
- adding **Seere**, **Dantalion**, and **Andromalius** at nos. 70–72.

The arithmetic is:

> 69 − 1 omitted spirit + 4 additions = 72.

Vassago’s insertion before the old Loray position contributes to **Weyer 13 → Goetia 14**. The whole ordering is not a simple one-position shift, because the seventeenth-century compiler also rearranged parts of the catalogue.

### Sloane 2731

The independent manuscript transcription reads:

> **The 14th spirit is called Leraje, he is a Marquis great in power shewing himself in the Likeness of an Archer Clothed in Green, Carrying a bow and quiver, he causeth all great Battles and Contestations, and maketh wounds to putrefie with Arrows by Archers; this belongs to [Sagittarius] &c he Governeth 30 Legions of spirits; this is his seale of Obedience &c**

See the [Sloane 2731 transcription and manuscript images](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/goetia-sloane2731/read/).

This witness contains the defining Goetic additions:

- **[Documented text]** green clothing;
- **[Documented text]** “great Battles and Contestations”;
- **[Documented text]** Sagittarius;
- **[Documented text]** a seal of obedience.

### Sloane 3825

Peterson’s base text, from approximately fol. 102v, reads:

> **The 14th spirit is called Leraye (or Leraje). he is a Marquize great in power shewing him selfe in ye likeness of an archer, cladd in green carring a Bow and quiver, he causeth all great Battles & contests, & causeth ye wounds to putrifie that are [made] wth arrows by archers this belongeth to [Sagittarius] he governeth 30 Legions of spirits, & his seale of obediance is this.**

In Sloane 3825, “or Leraje” is written above the line. The transcription and seal appear in Peterson’s [online *Goetia*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

### What “this belongeth unto Sagittarius” means

**[Documented text]** The phrase belongs to the seventeenth-century *Goetia* text, not Weyer.

**[Scholarly caution]** The antecedent of “this” is not explicitly defined. It may classify:

- the archer’s appearance;
- the bow-and-arrow office;
- the spirit astrologically;
- or the seal under a zodiacal heading.

Because the manuscripts provide only the terse sentence, it is unsafe to treat Sagittarius as Leraje’s domicile, rulership, decan, or date of manifestation. Later *777* tables in fact put Leraje in Leo, showing that occultists did not preserve a single astrological interpretation.

### No second appearance

**[Finding]** Leraje has only one spirit-specific manifestation: the archer.

Some Goetic entries say that a spirit first appears as an animal or monster and must assume human form at the magician’s command. Leraje’s entry does not. The generic ritual says that the magician may show Solomon’s hexagram to compel a spirit into human shape, but that is not a separate Leraje description.

### No other offices

**[Finding]** The early texts do not say that Leraje:

- teaches languages, arts, sciences, philosophy, astronomy, rhetoric, or handicrafts;
- reveals treasures;
- grants dignities;
- reconciles or separates lovers;
- gives familiars;
- bestows invisibility;
- transmutes metals;
- answers questions;
- heals wounds;
- controls weather;
- imparts archery skill;
- commands forests or animals.

Those belong to other spirits or to modern elaborations.

## 4. Harley 6483 and the Rudd recension

**[Documented text]** Harley 6483 calls the spirit **Leraic**. It is dated 1712–13 and bears the titles *Liber malorum Spirituum, seu Goetia* and *Lemegeton, Secretum Secretorum*. Peterson describes it as a late, attractive manuscript with extensive additional “Dr. Rudd” material, Hebrew lettering, and circles around many spirit characters. See the [Lemegeton manuscript description](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm).

**[Tradition]** The recension attributes its elaborations to Dr. Thomas Rudd, the seventeenth-century military engineer and mathematical writer.

**[Disputed attribution]** Harley 6483 is not a surviving Rudd autograph. Thomas Rudd died in 1656, whereas the manuscript is dated more than half a century later. Related papers identify the copyist Peter Smart. Modern examination has therefore distinguished:

- an older body of material possibly transmitted under Rudd’s name;
- Peter Smart’s copying or editorial activity;
- and the unsupported assumption that every innovation is Thomas Rudd’s own composition.

An extended critical discussion is available in Celephaïs Press’s [“Addenda et Scholia 2”](https://celephaispress.blogspot.com/2021/06/addenda-et-scholia-2.html).

## 5. Mathers–Crowley, 1904

### Publication

Samuel Liddell MacGregor Mathers prepared the English text from British Museum manuscripts; Aleister Crowley edited and published it as *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King* in 1904. Crowley supplied an introductory “initiated interpretation” and Enochian or “Angelic” renderings of ritual material.

Bibliographical details are preserved by [100th Monkey Press](https://www.100thmonkeypress.com/biblio/acrowley/books/goetia_1904/goetia_1904.htm). A scan is available [here](https://lo2.org/pdf/celepha%C3%AFs/mathers/Mathers-and-Crowley.Goetia.pdf), with mirrors at [Sacred Texts](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/) and [Hermetic Library](https://hermetic.com/norton/classics/goetia/index).

### Complete 1904 entry

Original printed p. 16, PDF p. 29:

> **(14.) LERAJE, or LERAIKHA.—The Fourteenth Spirit is called Leraje (or Leraie). He is a Marquis Great in Power, showing himself in the likeness of an Archer clad in Green, and carrying a Bow and Quiver. He causeth all great Battles and Contests; and maketh wounds to putrefy that are made with Arrows by Archers. This belongeth unto Sagittar. He governeth 30 Legions of Spirits, and this is his Seal, etc.**

This version modernizes spelling but does not add a new office. “Leraikha” appears to be a manuscript/editorial variant rather than a recoverable ancient form.

## 6. Peterson, 2001

Joseph H. Peterson’s *The Lesser Key of Solomon: Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis* (York Beach, Maine: Weiser, 2001) established the most useful modern collation.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Peterson uses Sloane 3825 as his base and compares other manuscripts rather than silently reproducing the 1904 wording. Consequently his entry, p. 13, preserves:

- **Leraye**, with supra-linear **Leraje**;
- Sloane **LERAJE**;
- Harley **Leraic**;
- Crowley **Leraje/Leraikha**;
- Weyer **Loray/Oray**;
- Scot **Leraie**.

Peterson’s appendix reproduces Weyer at approximately p. 232. His online source editions make the genealogy directly inspectable rather than relying on later demonological summaries.

## Rank, metal, planet, legions, and timing

### Marquis, silver, and the Moon

**[Documented text]** The *Goetia* directs that spirit seals be made in metals assigned by rank:

| Rank | Goetic seal metal | Conventional planetary identification |
|---|---:|---|
| King | Gold | Sun |
| Duke | Copper | Venus |
| Prince or Prelate | Tin | Jupiter |
| Marquis | **Silver** | **Moon** |
| President | Mercury | Mercury |
| Knight | Lead | Saturn |
| Earl/Count | Copper and silver in equal parts | Venus–Moon alloy |

The instructions can be read in Peterson’s [*Goetia* apparatus](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm) and the [Sacred Texts rank-and-seal section](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks07.htm).

**[Esoteric correspondence]** “Leraje’s planet is the Moon” is therefore true in the limited sense that a Marquis uses silver. Weyer did not assign a planet, and the spirit’s *777* decan is ruled by Jupiter, not the Moon.

Crowley speculated elsewhere that the missing classical Mars metal might belong to Earls, but that editorial restoration does not alter the manuscript rule for Marquises.

### Thirty, not thirty-three, legions

The count is completely stable:

- Weyer: *Triginta… legionibus*.
- Scot: “thirtie legions.”
- Sloane 2731: “30 Legions.”
- Sloane 3825: “30 Legions.”
- Mathers–Crowley: “30 Legions.”
- de Plancy: *Trente légions*.

**[Modern error]** The frequently repeated figure **33 legions** has no support in this source chain. It appears to be a modern copying error or conflation.

### Hours

**[Documented text]** The general Goetic rubric assigns Marquises two periods:

> from three in the afternoon until nine at night, and from nine at night until sunrise.

This is not stated inside Leraje’s individual entry; it follows from rank.

**[Documented text]** The same ritual apparatus recommends operating when the Moon is two, four, six, eight, ten, twelve, or fourteen days old, in clear weather and with the Moon visible.

These prescriptions should not be merged automatically with the later *777* classification of Leraje as a “day” decan spirit. They come from different textual layers.

## The seal and its variants

### What the early sources contain

- **Weyer, 1577:** no seal.
- **Scot, 1584:** no seal.
- **Sloane 2731:** a “seale of Obedience.”
- **Sloane 3825:** a seal of obedience reproduced by Peterson.
- **Harley 6483:** a Goetic character in the Rudd recension, where seals are embedded in a more elaborate angelic apparatus.
- **Mathers–Crowley, 1904:** a redrawn printed seal beneath the entry.
- **Peterson, 2001:** a manuscript-based reproduction rather than a wholly modern symbolic redesign.

### Verbal description

**[Documented image; descriptive transcription]** In the Sloane 3825-derived image, the character is predominantly horizontal. Its principal features are:

1. a long central shaft;
2. an angular, arrowhead-like terminal at the right;
3. a central vertical crossing with short diagonal or radiating strokes;
4. a large lower curved or bow-like stroke;
5. small circular terminals at the ends of the lower curve.

A verbal description cannot encode the exact angles, stroke order, proportions, or relations of overlapping lines. The [facsimile-derived image](https://www.esotericarchives.com/gifs/g14.jpg) is therefore the exact reference.

### Variant caution

**[Finding]** Published collations record name variants much more precisely than they record every graphical change to this seal. Handwritten sigils naturally vary in:

- length and angle of the shaft;
- openness of the lower curve;
- size of terminal circles;
- number and direction of short central strokes;
- enclosure inside an added circle;
- line weight and copying errors.

Harley 6483 often encloses characters and adds angelic/Hebrew material, but the accessible source record does not justify declaring one small geometric difference in Leraje’s Harley seal to be doctrinally meaningful. Mathers himself warned generally that the “Rudd” codex contained errors in some sigils; he did not thereby prove that Leraje’s particular figure was corrupt.

## Collin de Plancy

### 1818 and 1863

Jacques-Albin-Simon Collin de Plancy first published his *Dictionnaire infernal* in 1818. The work passed through substantial ideological and editorial changes. The famous sixth edition appeared in 1863 with sixty-nine engravings associated with Louis Le Breton.

**[Finding]** An Oray/Loray entry is securely verified in the **1863 sixth edition**, p. 508. I did not locate page-level evidence establishing that the wording is identical in the 1818 first edition. Modern databases often collapse the editions and backdate sixth-edition content to “1818.”

### Complete 1863 entry

> **Oray ou Loray, grand marquis des enfers, qui se montre sous la forme d’un superbe archer portant un arc et des flèches ; il anime les combats, empire les blessures faites par les archers, lance les javelines les plus meurtrières. Trente légions le reconnaissent pour dominateur et souverain.**

English gloss:

> Oray or Loray, a great marquis of hell, who appears in the form of a superb archer carrying a bow and arrows; he excites battles, worsens wounds made by archers, and hurls the deadliest javelins. Thirty legions acknowledge him as ruler and sovereign.

See the [1863 Oray article](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.%2C_1863/Oray) and [page scan](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page:Jacques_Collin_de_Plancy_-_Dictionnaire_infernal.pdf/516).

The entry cites Wierus, that is, Weyer.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** “Hurls the deadliest javelins” is probably de Plancy’s effort to make sense of Weyer’s corrupt *quos obijcit optimos tribus diebus*. It is not an independent ancient tradition about spears.

### Is there a Le Breton portrait?

**[Finding]** No. The Oray entry on p. 508 has text but no portrait. Although the 1863 edition contains many famous Le Breton demon engravings, Leraje/Oray was not among the illustrated spirits. Images presented online as “de Plancy’s Leraje” should therefore be treated as misattributions unless their page can be produced.

### Mebahel is not named in Exodus

**[Documented text]** Exodus 14:19–21 consists, in the traditional consonantal Hebrew text, of three verses of seventy-two letters each. The later method writes:

1. verse 19 in its normal direction;
2. verse 20 in reverse;
3. verse 21 in its normal direction;

then reads vertical columns to obtain seventy-two three-letter groups.

Mebahel is the fourteenth group, **מבה**. Adding the divine element **אל**, *El*, produces **מבהאל**.

**[Tradition]** The triplets are commonly called the “seventy-two names,” “seventy-two-letter Name,” or angels of the Shem ha-Mephorash.

**[Historical caution]** *Shem ha-mephorash* originally means the explicit, distinguished, or fully expressed divine Name and can refer in rabbinic literature to the Tetragrammaton. It does not always mean this particular seventy-two-triplet table.

### From Jewish sources to Christian occultism

#### Rabbinic and medieval Jewish background

Rashi’s commentary on *Sukkah* 45a and the *Sefer ha-Bahir*, §110, attest medieval knowledge of the seventy-two-letter construction. The *Bahir* describes the three seventy-two-letter verses and their arrangement; see this [translation of §§109–110](https://hekhal.org/texts/sefer-ha-bahir-109-110-targum/).

Joshua Trachtenberg summarizes the alternating-direction method in [*Jewish Magic and Superstition*](https://sacred-texts.com/jud/jms/jms09.htm). For the textual derivation in Rashi and *Sefer Raziel*, see Laura Joffe’s study, [“The Answer to the Meaning of the Seventy-Two Names of God”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/030908920202700205).

#### Sefer Raziel

**[Tradition/documented medieval reception]** *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* is a layered medieval magical compendium, not a demonstrably primordial book dictated to Adam. It circulates the seventy-two-fold divine name and related angelic material. Its first printed edition appeared at Amsterdam in 1701, although constituent material and manuscripts are older.

The association “from *Sefer Raziel*” therefore means transmission through a medieval textual complex, not that the full later French guardian-angel calendar appears there unchanged.

#### Reuchlin

Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517) helped transmit Hebrew and kabbalistic materials into Christian learned occultism. The addition of endings such as **-el** and **-yah** made the triplets look like a roster of individual angels.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Reuchlin is an important intermediary between Jewish name speculation and the Christian seventy-two-angel catalogues later used by Kircher and ritual magicians. He is not evidence for a Leraje–Mebahel pairing.

#### Kircher

Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–54) printed elaborate kabbalistic tables, divine names, angelic orders, zodiacal divisions, and versions of the seventy-two names. His presentation helped standardize the material for Christian occult readers. See Peterson’s [Kircher cabala materials](https://www.esotericarchives.com/kircher/cabala.htm) and a [scan of *Oedipus Aegyptiacus*](https://www.esonet.org/wp-content/uploads/2010/06/Oedipus_Aegyptiacus02.pdf).

Again, Kircher does not document the medieval existence of Leraje’s specific opposition by Mebahel.

## Mebahel in Rudd’s scheme

### The pairing

**[Documented text/tradition]** In the Harley 6483 or “Dr. Rudd” arrangement, the seventy-two Goetic spirits are paired serially with the seventy-two Shem angels:

> spirit 1 with angel 1, spirit 2 with angel 2 … spirit 14 with angel 14.

Accordingly:

> **Leraic/Leraje 14 ↔ Mebahel 14.**

The controlling angel is **Mebahel**, not **Mehahel**. Mehahel is a transposition found in modern copying.

### What the angel does in the ritual structure

**[Documented ritual prescription]** The Rudd recension does not merely give the demon an abstract “guardian angel.” It uses the corresponding Shem angel as a superior divine or angelic authority:

- the angel’s name and character are associated with the demon’s seal;
- angelic names are placed on protective equipment, including the magician’s breastplate;
- the names participate in the construction or control of the Brass Vessel;
- the corresponding psalm verse is used as a divine textual authority;
- the angel functions as a constraint upon the demonic power.

This resembles the logic of the *Testament of Solomon*, where demons disclose the angels who frustrate them, but no direct textual line from the Testament’s named demons to Leraje/Mebahel has been demonstrated.

### Skinner and Rankine

Stephen Skinner and David Rankine published *The Goetia of Dr Rudd: The Angels and Demons of Liber Malorum Spirituum seu Goetia Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis* through Golden Hoard Press in 2007. A later Llewellyn edition appeared in 2010.

The editors present Harley 6483 with related material from Harley 6482, Sloane 3824, and Wellcome MS 3203. Bibliographic records are available from [Atlantic Books](https://atlanticbooks.com/products/goetia-of-dr-rudd-9780954763923) and [Llewellyn](https://www.llewellyn.com/product.php?ean=9780738723556).

**[Disputed]** Skinner and Rankine’s title and interpretation foreground Thomas Rudd. The manuscript’s date and scribal evidence make “the Harley/Smart Rudd recension” more historically cautious than “Rudd’s autograph book.”

## Lenain’s Mebahel

Lazare Lenain published *La science cabalistique* at Amiens in 1823. The complete scan is available through the [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_ms05365) and as a [direct PDF](https://archive.org/download/BIUSante_ms05365/BIUSante_ms05365.pdf).

For Mebahel, Lenain gives:

- **[Documented text]** fourteenth genius;
- **[Documented text]** *Dieu conservateur*, “God the Preserver”;
- **[Documented text]** influence over the 66th through 70th zodiacal degrees;
- **[Calculation]** 65°–70° from 0° Aries equals **5°–10° Gemini**;
- **[Documented text]** a five-day cycle: 2 April, 13 June, 24 August, 4 November, and 15 January;
- **[Documented text]** daily time 4:20–4:40 a.m.;
- **[Documented text]** invocation against the usurpation of another’s fortune;
- **[Documented text]** justice, truth, liberty, aid to the oppressed, discovery of truth;
- **[Documented text]** an inclination toward jurisprudence or the bar;
- **[Documented text]** a contrary spirit of calumny, false witness, and contentious proceedings.

### The Psalm

Lenain’s Latin verse is:

> **Et factus est Dominus refugium pauperis: adjutor in opportunitatibus, in tribulatione.**

A conventional English rendering is:

> **The Lord also will be a refuge for the oppressed, a refuge in times of trouble.**

The reference is:

- **Psalm 9:9** in the Masoretic/KJV numbering;
- **Psalm 9:10** in the Vulgate-derived numbering used by Lenain and later French occultists.

This numbering difference, rather than a different verse, explains apparently contradictory modern references.

### “Meaning” of the name

**[Tradition]** “God the Preserver” is Lenain’s divine epithet or occult gloss.

**[Philological caution]** Only the ending **-El**, “God,” has a transparent ordinary Hebrew meaning here. The triplet מבה is generated from Exodus by position, not necessarily by parsing it as a Hebrew word. Elaborate translations such as “God who liberates the oppressed through truth” summarize the angel’s assigned offices rather than literally translating the consonants.

## Golden Dawn, Papus, Ambelain, and Haziel

### Golden Dawn

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn absorbed both the seventy-two angels and astrological decans into its late nineteenth-century correspondence system.

Its *Book T* starts the Shem sequence at **0° Leo**, associated with Regulus or Cor Leonis, rather than at 0° Aries. The accessible Golden Dawn manuscript compilation describes the sequence and assigns **Yezalel and Mebahel** to the first decan of Libra; see the [Golden Dawn manuscripts](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/gd/publication/english/golden_dawn_manuscripts.html).

Therefore:

- Yezalel governs approximately 0°–5° Libra;
- Mebahel governs approximately **5°–10° Libra**.

This differs from Lenain’s **5°–10° Gemini**.

**[Finding]** Neither is a textual fact from Exodus. They are products of different occult methods for fitting seventy-two names to the zodiac.

### Papus

Gérard Encausse, “Papus,” transmitted Lenain’s angelic system through French occultism. His *La Cabbale* (1903) and his preface to the later Durville reprint of Lenain helped restore the scarce 1823 text to circulation.

**[Tradition]** Papus is an important popularizer of Lenain’s scheme, not its originator and not a witness to Rudd’s Leraje pairing.

### Robert Ambelain

Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) repeats much of Lenain’s organization and wording. Its Mebahel notice gives the “God the Preserver” epithet, zodiacal degrees, psalm, judicial virtues, and contrary influence. An English translation is available in this [Ambelain PDF](https://www.markfoster.net/rn/texts/practicalkabbalah-part2.pdf).

Ambelain belongs to the twentieth-century French occult transmission, not to medieval Jewish angelology.

### Haziel

The author known as Haziel popularized a devotional guardian-angel system in books including:

- *Notre Ange Gardien existe* (1994);
- *Communiquer avec son Ange Gardien* (1995).

Haziel presents Mebahel as the angel for approximately **26–31 May**, associated with truth, liberty, justice, lawful vindication, and protection from false accusation. This converts the Gemini degrees into a familiar birthday calendar.

**[Modern esoteric development]** Haziel adds spiritual psychology, prayers, celestial hierarchies, planetary “energies,” and guardian-angel personality profiles. These are not found in Exodus or Weyer, and they are not required by the Rudd method.

Likewise, identifying Mebahel as a Cherub under Raziel and combining “Uranian” and Venusian forces belongs to modern occult angelology, not the earliest Shem tradition.

### Crowley’s 777

Crowley’s *777* was first published in 1909; *777 Revised* appeared posthumously in 1955. Table V distributes the first thirty-six Goetic spirits through the thirty-six decans as day spirits and the remaining thirty-six through them as night spirits.

Number 14 falls by sequence into:

> **Leo II, 10°–20° Leo — day/succedent — Jupiter in Leo — Six of Wands.**

The *777 Revised* entry describes Leraikha as:

> “An archer in green.”

The relevant table is available in [*777 Revised*](https://cleaves.lingama.net/news/attachments/may2009/liber777revised.pdf), Table V, columns CLV–CLXVI.

Number 50, Furcas, is 36 places later and consequently shares the same decan as its night spirit. This is why a tarot pip carries two Goetic spirits in the *777* overlay.

### Six of Wands

Golden Dawn *Book T* identifies 10°–20° Leo as:

- Jupiter in Leo;
- Six of Wands;
- “Lord of Victory”;
- victory or gain after strife, with pride and insolence among its less favorable potentials.

The battle association makes the assignment symbolically neat, but it is generated by Leraje’s list position, not by an early manuscript statement.

### A crucial non-equivalence

The Six of Wands’ Golden Dawn Shem angels are the angels numbered **3 and 4**—usually Sitael and Elemiah in modern spelling—not Mebahel.

Thus the following two claims are both true inside their respective tables:

- Leraje no. 14 is paired with Mebahel no. 14 by Rudd’s ordinal method.
- Leraje’s *777* decan, Leo II, carries Shem angels 3 and 4 in the Golden Dawn zodiacal method.

They do not produce a single coherent ancient system. They are separate overlays.

### The four different zodiacal answers

A practitioner asking “What sign belongs to Leraje?” may encounter four answers:

1. **Sagittarius** — written in the *Goetia*, probably because Leraje is an archer.
2. **Leo II** — Crowley’s decan assignment generated from Goetic number 14.
3. **Gemini 5°–10°** — Lenain’s zodiacal span for Mebahel.
4. **Libra 5°–10°** — Golden Dawn’s Regulus-start placement of Mebahel.

None should be substituted silently for another.

## Enochian and later ritual overlays

**[Documented modern editorial layer]** Crowley stated that he supplied “Enochian” or “Angelic” versions of Goetic conjurations in the 1904 book. This joined John Dee’s angelic language to a seventeenth-century spirit catalogue.

**[Finding]** There is no demonstrated Dee diary entry, Enochian Watchtower square, Aethyr, or original Enochian name corresponding specifically to Leraje. Calling Leraje an “Enochian demon of Leo” conflates systems.

Harley 6483 likewise participates in a broad early modern synthesis of Goetia, angelic names, ritual circles, and divine constraints. That does not make the name Loray Enochian in origin.

Later practices variously add:

- Moon incense or silver implements because of the Marquis rank;
- Jupiter or solar/Leo material because of the decan;
- Sagittarian symbolism because of the archer;
- green candles because of the robe;
- the Six of Wands as a talisman;
- Mebahel’s psalm as a controlling prayer;
- archery, military, infection, conflict-resolution, or legal-justice symbolism.

These are **modern or post-Golden-Dawn syntheses**, not a single prescription found in the manuscripts.

## Iconography

### The manuscript image

The oldest secure visual identifier is the seal in the seventeenth-century *Goetia*. Weyer and Scot provide only prose.

The seal’s arrow-like right terminal makes an archer interpretation tempting, but:

**[Scholarly caution]** Sigils are highly stylized copying traditions. Resemblance to a bow or arrow does not prove that the designer intentionally drew a pictogram.

### The green archer

The iconographic development is chronological:

1. **1577:** “most handsome archer,” bow and quiver.
2. **1584:** “galant archer.”
3. **seventeenth-century Goetia:** archer “cladd in green.”
4. **1863 de Plancy:** “superb archer,” bow, arrows, and deadly javelins, but no green.
5. **1904:** green-clad archer standardized in print.
6. **1909/1955 *777*:** compressed to “an archer in green.”
7. **modern art:** forest archer, elven huntress, military bowman, green-skinned demon, plague archer, or female sniper.

Green is therefore documented, but only from the *Goetia* layer onward.

### No de Plancy portrait

The absence of an engraving is a positive finding. A supposed “Louis Le Breton portrait of Leraje” is not in the 1863 Oray entry. The familiar modern images cannot be authenticated merely by reproducing the *Dictionnaire infernal* title on a webpage.

### Modern occult art

**[Modern invention]** Contemporary demon dictionaries, oracle decks, sigil shops, tattoos, and AI or digital illustrations commonly show:

- a hooded ranger;
- an androgynous or female elf;
- green armor;
- diseased or poisoned arrows;
- battlefields and corpses;
- lunar silver;
- a Sagittarius bow;
- serpents or rot around an arrow wound.

Only the green archer, bow, quiver, battles, wounds, and putrefaction have direct roots in the Goetic entry. The rest is interpretive art.

## People and works

### Johann Weyer

Weyer is the earliest secure witness to Loray. His importance is twofold:

- he preserved a catalogue drawn from ritual-magic material;
- he printed it within a polemic against the persecution of alleged witches.

He should not be described simply as either a credulous demonologist or a modern rationalist; both his spirit cosmology and his humanitarian legal argument matter.

### Reginald Scot

Scot carried the catalogue into English print in 1584. “Leraie” is the first secure English spelling. His work greatly increased the later accessibility of the Weyer material.

### The anonymous Lemegeton compiler and scribes

The seventeenth-century compiler transformed Weyer’s short notice by adding:

- number 14 in a 72-spirit structure;
- green clothing;
- Sagittarius;
- a seal of obedience;
- integration into a complete conjuration manual.

The individual scribes of Sloane 3648, 2731, and 3825 preserved slightly different spellings and drawings.

### Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

Rudd is the traditional source of the angel-versus-demon system. Peter Smart is important because the surviving “Rudd” witnesses are later copies associated with him. Historical attribution remains open.

### Collin de Plancy and Louis Le Breton

De Plancy transmitted Oray/Loray to nineteenth-century French demonology. Louis Le Breton illustrated many demons in the 1863 edition but apparently not Oray.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers

Mathers collated British Museum manuscripts and prepared the English Goetia later published by Crowley. The variants “Leraje,” “Leraie,” and “Leraikha” entered wide occult circulation through this edition.

### Aleister Crowley

Crowley:

- published Mathers’s work in 1904;
- added an initiatory/psychological interpretation;
- supplied Enochian ritual versions;
- placed Leraikha in Leo II as a day spirit in *777*;
- connected the decan to the Six of Wands.

None of those correspondences belongs to Weyer.

### Frater Achad

**[Finding]** No verifiable Leraje-specific operation or substantial historical claim by Charles Stansfeld Jones, “Frater Achad,” was located. He belongs to the wider Crowleyan milieu but should not be inserted into Leraje’s history without a text.

### Poke Runyon

Poke Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* and related Church of the Hermetic Sciences material reinterpret Solomonic evocation through modern ceremonial practice, mirrors, and imaginative visualization.

**[Finding]** Runyon is relevant to modern Goetic reception, but no distinct Leraje revelation or alternative historical pedigree was verified.

### Lon Milo DuQuette

DuQuette has written and taught extensively about ceremonial and Goetic magic in a humorous autobiographical mode.

**[Finding]** He is part of the modern reception history; no source-driven Leraje-specific doctrine comparable to Weyer, Rudd, or *777* was located.

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 critical edition and Esoteric Archives transcriptions are decisive for distinguishing manuscripts from the 1904 printed recension. His collation exposes “Leraikha,” “Leraic,” and “Leraje” as variants rather than separate ancient beings.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their 2007 edition made the Harley 6483/Rudd system—and consequently the Leraje–Mebahel opposition—widely accessible. Their attribution of the recension to Rudd remains more confident than some manuscript critics accept.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s *Encyclopaedia Goetica*, especially *Geosophia*, argues that later spirit catalogues retain material connected to older Mediterranean necromantic and chthonic traditions.

**[Scholarly/esoteric reconstruction]** This is valuable as a broad genealogy of Goetia, but it does not supply an attested ancient deity from whom Loray can be derived.

### Owen Davies

Davies’s *Grimoires: A History of Magic Books* (Oxford University Press, 2009) supplies historical context for the creation, circulation, publication, and reception of grimoires. It does not establish a pre-Weyer Leraje.

### Jean-Patrice Boudet, Jan Veenstra, and Julien Véronèse

Their scholarship on medieval learned magic, spirit catalogues, necromantic manuals, and the *Livre des Esperitz* clarifies the world from which Weyer’s source emerged.

**[Finding]** This scholarship supports a prehistory for the **genre and catalogue tradition**, not a documented ancient or high-medieval Loray.

### The Grand Grimoire Loray

A French *Grand Grimoire* hierarchy says of Sargatanas:

> **Il a sous lui Loray, Valefar & Foraii.**

That is, “He has under him Loray, Valefar, and Foraii.”

See this [French scan of *Le Grand Grimoire*](https://booksofmagick.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/08/Le_grand_grimoire_avec_la_grande_clavicu.pdf); an Italian witness is available at [Esoteric Archives](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/Il_Grand_Grimoire.pdf).

**[Documented later tradition]** This preserves the older **Loray** spelling and reorganizes the Weyer spirits into a military infernal hierarchy.

**[Historical caution]** Claims that the *Grand Grimoire* dates from 1522 are part of its legendary self-presentation. The available printed tradition is much later, conventionally eighteenth or nineteenth century. It is not evidence that Loray was known in 1522.

**[Disputed conflation]** Some modern sites say Leraje “is Satanachia.” The *Grand Grimoire* does not say that. It places Loray under **Sargatanas**, while Satanachia is a different superior spirit. Confusion between the similar names Sargatanas and Satanachia produced the identification.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Is the correct name Loray or Leraje?

**Answer:** Loray is the earliest secure form; Leraje is the canonical *Goetia* form.

There is no reason to regard them as different spirits. The description, rank, offices, and thirty legions show direct textual continuity.

### 2. Is Leraikha an ancient or Hebrew form?

**[Finding]** No evidence supports that claim. It first gains wide currency through Mathers–Crowley’s 1904 heading. Peterson treats it as a Crowley/Mathers variant.

### 3. Does Leraje command 30 or 33 legions?

**Answer:** Thirty. Thirty-three is a modern error.

### 4. Does Leraje attract or repel lovers?

**[Modern invention/conflation]** No primary Leraje text gives this power. It may have been borrowed from spirits whose offices concern love, women, reconciliation, or separation.

### 5. Can Leraje “drive away mobs”?

**[Disputed mistranslation]** This does not appear in Weyer’s clear Latin, Scot’s English, the *Goetia*, or de Plancy. It likely arose from efforts to decipher *quos obijcit optimos tribus diebus*. Websites such as [Occult World’s Leraje entry](https://occult-world.com/leraje/) illustrate how the claim has spread, but do not supply an older source.

### 6. Does Leraje heal wounds?

**[Modern inversion]** The sources say the opposite: Leraje makes arrow wounds rot or worsens them.

A practitioner might invoke Mebahel protectively against Leraje or reinterpret command over putrefaction as a power to remove it, but that is magical extrapolation rather than the documented office.

### 7. Does putrefaction mean poison, gangrene, or infection?

**[Scholarly interpretation]** Weyer’s *putrefacit* and the English *putrifie* literally concern rotting or corruption. “Gangrene,” “infection,” and “poisoned arrows” are reasonable modern visualizations but narrower medical or mechanical interpretations than the text itself supplies.

### 8. Is Leraje Sagittarius?

**[Documented but ambiguous]** The *Goetia* says “this belongeth unto Sagittar.” Weyer does not.

Calling Leraje “a Sagittarius demon” is possible within the Goetic sentence, but exact claims about domicile, dates, elemental triplicity, or Sagittarius rulership exceed the text.

### 9. Is Leraje Leo?

**[Esoteric correspondence]** Only in Crowley’s *777* decan scheme: Leo II. That placement results from list order.

### 10. Is Leraje lunar?

**[Esoteric correspondence]** By rank metal: Marquis → silver → Moon. It is a ritual material attribution, not an early statement about temperament or domicile.

### 11. Is Mebahel in Gemini or Libra?

Both occur, in different systems:

- Lenain: Gemini 5°–10°.
- Golden Dawn: Libra 5°–10°.

Calling either the uniquely “original” Shem zodiac assignment is misleading.

### 12. Is Mebahel’s Psalm 9:9 or 9:10?

Both references point to the same sentence under different numbering systems.

### 13. Does Mebahel literally mean “God the Preserver”?

**[Tradition, not secure lexical translation]** It is Lenain’s received epithet. The generated triplet מבה is not transparently the Hebrew word “preserver.”

### 14. Did Thomas Rudd write Harley 6483?

**[Disputed]** The manuscript is too late to be his autograph. It may transmit material associated with him, but Peter Smart and other copyists or compilers stand between the historical Rudd and the surviving witness.

### 15. Was Loray already in Trithemius’s lost *Offices of Spirits*?

**[Open reconstruction]** Weyer used such a source tradition, so it is possible. The loss of the precise exemplar prevents proof.

### 16. Is Berci the same spirit?

**[Disputed]** Berci has the archer, war, and thirty-legion profile. That makes it textually related. The dissimilar name and lack of wound-putrefaction language prevent a confident identity.

### 17. Does Leraje derive from Leyraiell?

**[Disputed]** The phonetic resemblance is interesting, and both occur in ritual-magic catalogues. But Leyraiell is an angel of Venus in the *Liber Juratus*, whereas Loray is a demonic marquis and warlike archer. No connecting manuscript has been found.

### 18. Is Leraje Satanachia?

**[Modern conflation]** No. The *Grand Grimoire* places Loray under Sargatanas. Sargatanas and Satanachia are different names and offices in that hierarchy.

### 19. Is there an ancient deity behind Leraje?

**[Finding]** None has been demonstrated. Claims otherwise presently belong to speculative occult genealogy or modern mythmaking.

### 20. Is the green archer a fallen angel?

**[Legend/modern invention]** Neither Weyer nor the *Goetia* gives a fall narrative. Dungeons & Dragons and modern occult writers have supplied biographies, loyalties, wars in heaven, and personality traits.

---

## Open questions

1. **What did Weyer’s corrupt Latin mean?**  
   A better manuscript of his immediate source might recover the missing or damaged wording behind *quos obijcit optimos tribus diebus*.

2. **Was Loray in Weyer’s exact *Liber officiorum spirituum* exemplar?**  
   Almost certainly if Weyer copied the entry faithfully, but the lost exemplar prevents independent confirmation.

3. **How are Berci, Barsy, and Loray related?**  
   The shared archer/war/thirty-legion complex is too close to dismiss, yet the names and some offices differ. A full stemmatic comparison of every surviving spirit-office manuscript is needed.

4. **Where did “green” enter the text?**  
   It is present in the seventeenth-century *Goetia* witnesses but absent from Weyer and Scot. No source earlier than that manuscript family has been identified.

5. **What did the Sagittarius notation originally classify?**  
   It may be an iconographic gloss, an astrological rubric inherited from a lost table, or an editor’s association of archer with sign.

6. **Can Harley 6483’s Leraje seal be traced to an earlier Rudd witness?**  
   A detailed comparison of Harley 6482, Harley 6483, Sloane 3824, Wellcome 3203, and Peter Smart’s hands may clarify which graphical and angelic features predate 1712.

7. **Was Oray already present in de Plancy’s 1818 edition?**  
   The 1863 text is verified; a page-level examination of the first edition is still needed before assuming identical coverage or wording.

8. **When did the figure “33 legions” first appear?**  
   It is absent from the primary chain. Its earliest printed or online occurrence remains to be established.

9. **When did the love and “driving away mobs” offices arise?**  
   They appear to be mistranslation or conflation, but their precise publication history has not been reconstructed.

10. **Can Loray’s name be etymologized at all?**  
    A future discovery of an intermediate manuscript might show that Loray itself is a corruption of Berci/Barsy, Leyraiell, or an entirely different lost form. At present, no derivation satisfies both philology and textual history.

---

## Conclusions

**[Documented core]** Leraje begins securely as **Loray alias Oray**, Weyer’s thirteenth spirit in 1577: a great Marquis, appearing as a handsome archer with bow and quiver, author of battles, corrupter of wounds inflicted by archers, and ruler of thirty legions.

**[Seventeenth-century development]** The *Ars Goetia* makes him number 14, changes the name toward Leraje/Leraye, dresses the archer in green, supplies a seal, adds Sagittarius, and incorporates him into an operative ritual system.

**[Rudd development]** Harley 6483 sets spirit 14 against Shem angel 14, **Mebahel**, using angelic names and psalmody as instruments of constraint. The surviving manuscript is from 1712–13 and is not Thomas Rudd’s autograph.

**[Nineteenth-century angelology]** Lenain gives Mebahel the epithet “God the Preserver,” 5°–10° Gemini, Psalm 9:9/10, a 4:20–4:40 a.m. interval, justice and liberty, and opposition to calumny and false witness.

**[Golden Dawn/Crowley development]** A separate system places Mebahel at 5°–10° Libra, while *777* places Leraje at 10°–20° Leo with Jupiter and the Six of Wands. These are distinct tables and do not reconcile into one ancient astrology.

**[Negative historical finding]** No ancient god, classical figure, biblical demon, or securely attested pre-Weyer Loray has been identified. Berci/Barsy is a meaningful descriptive relative; Leyraiell is a possible name resemblance; neither proves identity.

**[Modern reception]** The green archer became an elf, huntress, sniper, game demon, vestige, and projectile-based enemy. These works preserve the strongest iconographic elements but freely alter sex, biography, cosmology, weapon, and powers.
