# Stolas: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes seven kinds of statement:

- **[Documented text]** — stated directly in a cited manuscript, printed grimoire, or identifiable literary/artistic work.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** — an inference made through manuscript comparison, palaeography, or historical analysis.
- **[Tradition]** — a teaching repeated in later occult literature, but not necessarily present in the earliest texts.
- **[Esoteric correspondence]** — a deliberate magical, astrological, kabbalistic, or tarot attribution rather than a historical fact about the name.
- **[Disputed]** — proposed identifications or datings for which significant contrary evidence or uncertainty exists.
- **[Legend]** — an assertion made within the mythic narrative of the grimoires.
- **[Modern invention]** — a feature originating in modern occultism, fiction, games, animation, or fan culture.
- **[Negative finding]** — something specifically sought but not located in the historical source chain.

“Demon,” “spirit,” “angel,” “prince,” and similar terms below normally report the vocabulary of the sources; they are not claims that the beings exist objectively.

Two astrological systems must therefore be kept separate:

1. **[Esoteric correspondence] Rudd/Lenain numerical pairing:** Stolas 36 ↔ Menadel 36 ↔ the five-degree Virgo sector ↔ Ten of Pentacles.
2. **[Esoteric correspondence] Crowley’s *777* demon-decan table:** Stolas ↔ daytime half of Pisces III ↔ Ten of Cups.

The second attribution is what *777* actually prints for Stolas. Assigning Stolas himself exclusively to 25°–30° Virgo is a later transfer from his paired angel and is not Crowley’s demon-decan attribution.

## The name and its origins

### The exact name in the source chain

Joseph Peterson’s comparative manuscript table provides the following readings:

| Witness | Date | Number | Reading |
|---|---:|---:|---|
| Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* | 1577 | 69 | **Stolas** |
| Scot, *Discoverie of Witchcraft* | 1584 | 68 in Scot’s translated enumeration; last item | **Stolas** |
| Sloane MS 3825 | seventeenth century | 36 | **Stolas** |
| Sloane MS 3648 | seventeenth century | 36 | **Stolas** |
| Sloane MS 2731 | 1686/87 | 36 | **Stolas** |
| Harley MS 6483 | copied c. 1712–1713 | 36 | **Stolus** |
| Northwestern University MS 65 | early eighteenth century | 36 | **Stolas** |
| Mathers–Crowley | 1904 | 36 | **Stolas, or Stolos** |
| A. E. Waite | later printed recension | 36 | **Solas or Stolas** |
| Idries Shah | twentieth century | 36 | **Solas** |
| Wellcome MS 3203 | 1828 copy, leaves dislocated | 17 in that copy | **Stolas** |

This comparison is documented in Peterson’s [“Goetia spirit variant spellings”](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia_spirits.pdf).

**[Documented text]** The stable historical core is therefore **Stolas**. **Stolus** is the Harley reading; **Stolos** appears as Mathers’s alternate; **Solas** is an identifiable later editorial variant.

**[Disputed/poorly attested]** *Stoppas* is widely copied by contemporary demonology websites, but it is absent from the principal source chain tabulated by Peterson. It looks more like a modern transcription or typographical mutation than an independent early reading. The same applies to *Stomas*. They should not be presented on an equal footing with *Stolas*, *Stolus*, or *Stolos*.

### Weyer’s numbering and the apparent 68/69 discrepancy

**[Documented text]** In Weyer's full catalogue, Stolas is the **69th and final spirit**. Peterson’s contents explicitly number Phoenix 68 and Stolas 69. Some modern editions or appendices reproduce Scot’s list as “68. Stolas,” because Scot omitted Weyer's fourth spirit, Pruflas/Bufas, and therefore has one fewer entry. This is a numbering artifact, not evidence for two different Stolas traditions.

The arithmetic is:

- Weyer: 69 spirits.
- Scot omits Pruflas: effectively 68.
- The *Goetia* retains Scot’s omission but adds four spirits: 68 + 4 = 72.

The four *Goetia* additions are:

1. **Vassago**, inserted as no. 3.
2. **Seere**, no. 70.
3. **Dantalion**, no. 71.
4. **Andromalius**, no. 72.

The one Weyer spirit absent from both Scot and the *Goetia* is **Pruflas**, alias **Bufas**. Peterson observes that the reordered *Goetia* resembles a catalogue whose individual spirit leaves had been shuffled; no securely documented doctrinal reason for the new order is known. [Peterson’s Weyer introduction and comparison](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

### Distolas in the *Livre des Esperitz*

The most important pre-Weyer lead is not “Stolas” exactly, but **Distolas**.

**[Documented text]** Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29 contains the French *Livre des Esperitz*. Jean-Patrice Boudet transcribes its entry from fol. 182:

> *Distolas est ung grant marquis qui appert en belle figure et donne voluntiers responce de ce que on luy demande et commande, et si apporte pierres si on luy commande, et donne au maistre ung cheval qui le porte en une heure cent ou deux ou trois centz lieues ou plus ; et a XX legions.*

Translation:

> Distolas is a great marquis who appears in a fair form and willingly gives an answer concerning what is asked or commanded of him; he brings stones if commanded, and gives the master a horse that carries him in one hour one, two, or three hundred leagues or more; and he has twenty legions.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Boudet aligns **Distolas, marquis, twenty legions** with **Stolas, prince, twenty-six legions**. The shared name-shape and the association with stones support the comparison, but the rank, legion count, appearance, and remaining offices differ. Distolas therefore is a plausible ancestral or collateral form—not proof that every detail of the later Stolas already existed.

The manuscript’s dating also requires care. It has sometimes been called late fifteenth- or early sixteenth-century; Boudet argues that the relevant hand is probably nearer the **middle of the sixteenth century**. Consequently:

- **[Negative finding]** The exact spelling **Stolas** has not been securely located before Weyer’s 1577 print.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A related **Distolas** probably existed in the manuscript tradition before, or independently of, Weyer.
- **[Disputed]** Calling Distolas unequivocally “the first Stolas” overstates the evidence.

See Boudet, [“Les *who’s who* démonologiques de la Renaissance et leurs ancêtres médiévaux”](https://doi.org/10.4000/medievales.1019), and the [digital *Livre des Esperitz*](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/livre-esperitz/read/).

### Mistalas in the Folger *Book of Oberon*

**[Documented text]** A related Offices-of-Spirits text in the Folger manuscript tradition calls a spirit **Mistalas** and associates it with Paimon. Peterson notes that this witness preserves substantially the same raven/human appearance and instructional offices as Stolas.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** *Mistalas* is likely a scribal or transmissional relative of *Stolas*, possibly produced through an added or misread initial syllable. It is stronger evidence than modern forms such as *Stoppas*, but it is not part of the main Weyer–Scot–Goetia spelling line. See Peterson’s [Folger transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/folger/v_b_26_transcription.pdf).

### Proposed etymologies

No demonstrated etymology exists.

#### Latin *stola / stolas*

Latin *stola* means a garment or robe; **stolās/stolas** can be an inflected plural form. Because *stola* derives through Greek στολή, the name has occasionally been interpreted as something like “robes” or “vestments.”

**[Disputed]** This is a formal resemblance only. No manuscript glosses the demon’s name with clothing, and nothing in Stolas’s offices or appearance suggests a robe etymology. See the lexical entry for [Latin *stolas*](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/stolas).

#### Greek *stolos*

Greek **στόλος**, *stolos*, has a broad historical semantic range including equipment, an expedition, a fleet, an armed force, or—in some contexts—dress or equipment.

**[Disputed]** Mathers’s spelling *Stolos* makes the comparison visually tempting, but no known compiler derives the spirit from this word. The offices of astronomy, herbs, and stones do not establish a connection.

#### The ancient settlement Stolus/Stolos

An ancient settlement called **Stolus/Stolos** is recorded in Chalcidice.

**[Disputed/negative finding]** No textual chain links that place to the demon. Similar spelling is insufficient evidence, and no antiquarian or grimoire source identifies the spirit as a genius loci of Chalcidice.

#### “Star,” *stella*, or an astronomical root

Because Stolas teaches astronomy, contemporary writers sometimes connect the name with Latin *stella*, “star,” or a supposed root meaning stellar knowledge.

**[Modern speculation]** The phonology is weak, and the explanation appears to have been inferred from the office after the fact. No Weyer, Scot, manuscript, or early lexicographical gloss supports it.

#### An owl-name

Modern sources occasionally claim that *stolas* is Greek for an owl or a particular night bird.

**[Negative finding]** No reliable classical Greek lexical evidence for that derivation was located. It appears to reverse-engineer the name from de Plancy’s owl illustration.

### Ancient god, hero, or place

**[Negative finding]** Unlike Astaroth/Astarte, Amon/Amun, Asmodai/Ashmedai, Belial, or Phoenix, Stolas has **no securely identified ancient god, biblical personality, hero, or mythological creature behind the name**.

There is likewise no Stolas in:

- the *Testament of Solomon*;
- the Hebrew Bible or New Testament;
- the *Book of Enoch*;
- securely identified classical Greek or Roman demon lists;
- the principal ancient Mesopotamian, Egyptian, or Canaanite pantheons.

**[Legend]** The *Goetia* presents Stolas as one of the spirits once constrained by Solomon in a brass vessel. That is part of the pseudo-Solomonic frame, not evidence of an ancient Solomonic text mentioning the name.

## The description across the sources

### Weyer, 1577: Latin text

**[Documented text]** Johann Weyer, *De praestigiis daemonum*, fifth edition, Basel, 1577, appendix *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*, final entry, p. 931 in the original pagination:

> *Stolas magnus Princeps: prodit forma nycticoracis: coram exorcista hominis simulachrum suscipit, docetque absolutè astronomiam. Herbarum & lapidum pretiosorum vires intelligit. Viginti sex legiones huic subiacent.*

Close translation:

> Stolas is a great prince. He comes forth in the form of a *nycticorax*. Before the exorcist he assumes the likeness of a man, and teaches astronomy completely. He understands the powers of herbs and precious stones. Twenty-six legions are subject to him.

Source: [Peterson’s Latin edition of Weyer](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm#stolas).

Several nuances matter:

- *Princeps* is “prince,” “chief,” or “principal person,” not necessarily a feudal title with a fixed planetary correspondence in Weyer.
- *Simulachrum* means an image, likeness, or representation.
- *Vires* means powers, efficacies, or operative properties.
- *Astronomia* in the sixteenth century did not always mark the modern astronomy/astrology boundary as sharply as present English does. The text nevertheless says *astronomia*, not *astrologia*.
- Weyer says Stolas **understands** the powers of herbs and gems; he does not explicitly say that he teaches them, although that becomes the natural reading in later English versions.

### What is a *nycticorax*?

This is the source of the raven–owl problem.

**[Documented philology]** Latin *nycticorax*, from Greek νυκτικόραξ, literally combines “night” and “raven/crow.” In zoological and biblical usage it normally denotes a **night-heron** or another nocturnal, crow-voiced bird—not simply a raven and not an owl.

**[Transmission]**

1. Weyer: *forma nycticoracis*.
2. Scot: “forme of a nightraven.”
3. Sloane MS 3825: “shape of a Night Raven.”
4. Sloane MS 2731 appears to have written or preserved a reading susceptible to correction.
5. Mathers–Crowley: “Shape of a Mighty Raven.”
6. De Plancy: *hibou*, “owl.”

Thus the three familiar birds represent different historical stages:

- **Night-heron:** closest zoological gloss of Weyer’s Latin.
- **Night-raven:** Scot’s literalizing English and the best *Goetia* manuscript reading.
- **Mighty raven:** manuscript or editorial corruption spread by Mathers–Crowley.
- **Owl:** de Plancy’s nineteenth-century reinterpretation, made visually canonical by Le Breton.

### Scot, 1584: English text

**[Documented text]** Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, London, 1584, Book XV, chapter II, final catalogue entry—number 68 in the shortened English list:

> Stolas is a great prince, appearing in the forme of a nightraven, before the exorcist, he taketh the image and shape of a man, and teacheth astronomie, absolutelie understanding the vertues of herbes and pretious stones; there are under him twentie six legions.

The text can be checked in Peterson’s [parallel Weyer–Scot edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm#stolas) and in the [Cornell facsimile of the 1584 *Discoverie*](https://digital.library.cornell.edu/catalog/witchcraft081). In Peterson’s 2001 *Lesser Key* appendix, the parallel Latin and Scot texts appear on p. 254.

The first-edition blackletter witness is most reliably cited by **book and chapter**, since digital facsimiles and later reprints paginate it differently. The Brinsley Nicholson facsimile reprint is [available through Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Index:The_discoverie_of_witchcraft_(Nicholson,_1886_).djvu).

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Peterson attributes the translation probably to **Abraham Fleming**, who translated other Latin material for Scot. The attribution is likely but not printed as a signature beneath the Stolas entry.

**[Context]** Scot included this material within a skeptical exposure of conjuring and witchcraft claims. His reproduction of the catalogue does not mean that he endorsed the spirits as literal beings in the way a practising ritual magician might.

Scot follows the catalogue with:

> *Legio 6666.*

and a note that a legion contains 6,666. If applied arithmetically, twenty-six legions would equal **173,316 spirits**.

**[Legend/textual arithmetic]** That total is a consequence of Scot’s appended convention, not a headcount independently supplied in the Stolas entry.

### Sloane MS 3825 and Peterson’s edition

**[Documented text]** Peterson’s diplomatic transcription, based principally on British Library Sloane MS 3825, reads:

> The 36th spirit is called Stolas, he is a great and powerfull Prince, appearing in ye shape of a Night Raven at first before ye Exorcist, but afterwards he taketh the image of a man &c; he teacheth the Art of Astronomy, & the vertuses [sic] of hearbs & precious stones, he governeth 26 legions of spirits his seal is thus to be made & worne as a Lamin.

Source: [Peterson, *Lemegeton, Part 1: Goetia*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm#stolas); printed in *The Lesser Key of Solomon*, 2001, approximately p. 24 in the catalogue.

This introduces or makes explicit three things not present in Weyer:

1. Stolas is no. **36**, rather than 69.
2. His understanding of herbs and stones is phrased as part of what “he teacheth.”
3. His seal must be made and worn as a **lamen**, meaning a ritual badge or pendant.

“*Vertuses*” is a scribal spelling or error for “virtues.”

### Sloane MS 2731

**[Documented text]** Sloane MS 2731 also calls the spirit **Stolas**, no. 36, with the same general appearance, offices, and twenty-six legions. It is an important witness because its catalogue includes the seal drawings and because its phrasing was among the materials available to later editors.

A diplomatic transcription with folio images is available in the [Sloane 2731 digital *Ars Goetia*](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/goetia-sloane2731/).

**[Textual issue]** The reading behind Mathers’s “mighty raven” probably arose in this portion of the manuscript tradition. Peterson reports evidence that a form resembling “night” was corrected or misread as “mighty.” Sloane 3825’s unambiguous “Night Raven,” supported by Weyer’s *nycticorax* and Scot’s “nightraven,” is textually superior.

### Harley MS 6483: the Rudd recension

**[Documented text]** Harley MS 6483 reads **Stolus**. In Skinner and Rankine’s edition, p. 137:

> The six and thirtieth spirit is Stolus he is a great and powerful Prince appearing in the shape of a mighty raven at first before the Exorcist, but after he taketh the Image of a man &c. He teacheth the art of Astronomy, and the virtue of herbs and precious stones, he governeth 26 Legions of spirits his Seal is this.

The manuscript was copied around **1712–1713**, apparently by Peter Smart, among a series of manuscripts labelled as material of “Dr Rudd.”

**[Disputed]** Stephen Skinner and David Rankine argue that the underlying recension may descend from work of Thomas Rudd around 1655–1656. The surviving codex is later, however, and the notices attributing the compilations to Rudd do not prove that he personally authored every component. “Rudd recension” is therefore safer than “Rudd’s autograph.”

Source: Stephen Skinner and David Rankine, *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (Golden Hoard, 2007), p. 137; [searchable copy](https://pdfcoffee.com/the-goetia-of-dr-rudd-by-dr-stephen-skinner-david-rankine-pdf-free.html).

### Mathers–Crowley, 1904

**[Documented text]** S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Aleister Crowley, *The Goetia: The Lesser Key of Solomon the King*, London, 1904, p. 34:

> (36.) STOLAS, OR STOLOS.—The Thirty-sixth Spirit is Stolas, or Stolos. He is a Great and Powerful Prince, appearing in the Shape of a Mighty Raven at first before the Exorcist; but after he taketh the image of a Man. He teacheth the Art of Astronomy, and the Virtues of Herbs and Precious Stones. He governeth 26 Legions of Spirits; and his Seal is this, which is, etc.

Source: [Sacred Texts, *Goetia*, p. 34](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks05.htm); see also the [Project Gutenberg edition](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/72679/72679-h/72679-h.htm).

The important Mathers–Crowley innovations or editorial choices are:

- the alternate form **Stolos**;
- capitalization and polished prose;
- retention and popularization of **“Mighty Raven”**;
- a redrawn, standardized seal.

**[Textual criticism]** “Mighty Raven” is almost certainly secondary to “Night Raven.” It is not a separate ancient tradition about an exceptionally large or powerful raven.

### Peterson, 2001

**[Documented edition]** Joseph H. Peterson’s *The Lesser Key of Solomon* (Weiser, 2001) returned to Sloane MS 3825 as the main witness and collated other manuscripts. Peterson therefore restores **Night Raven**, preserves the manuscript spelling *vertuses* with a marker, and notes both the Weyer/Scot ancestry and the variants.

Peterson’s edition is not simply a reprint of Mathers–Crowley. Its chief contribution is textual: it makes visible where the familiar 1904 wording is a correction, corruption, or modernization.

### Offices that are not in the early sources

**[Negative finding]** Stolas is not said in Weyer, Scot, or the principal *Goetia* manuscripts to:

- give love;
- make people invisible;
- transmute metals;
- provide familiars;
- teach languages;
- reveal treasure;
- answer past, present, and future questions;
- reconcile enemies;
- poison enemies;
- teach specifically “poisonous plants”;
- govern the night, dreams, moon, Saturn, or the element Earth.

Some of these are offices of other Goetic spirits. Others are modern deductions from Stolas’s bird form or knowledge of plants and stones.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Distolas does answer questions and provide rapid transportation, but those functions disappeared from the Stolas entry. Transferring every Distolas office to Stolas is possible only if one first accepts their identity and then assumes the omitted material remained doctrinally valid—two unproven steps.

## Rank, planet, metal, and time

### Prince: Jupiter and tin

**[Esoteric correspondence]** In the *Goetia*’s rank instructions as transmitted and systematized by Mathers, the metals are:

| Rank | Planet | Metal |
|---|---|---|
| King | Sun | Gold |
| Duke | Venus | Copper |
| Prince or prelate | Jupiter | Tin |
| Marquis | Moon | Silver |
| President | Mercury | Mercury |
| Earl/count | Mars | Iron |
| Knight | Saturn | Lead |

Stolas, as a prince, therefore receives **Jupiter and tin**. Source: [*Goetia*, classification and magical directions](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks07.htm).

This is a ritual correspondence applied to the rank. **[Negative finding]** Weyer does not personally say “Stolas is Jovial,” nor does his Stolas paragraph prescribe tin.

The contrary modern assignment of Stolas to **Saturn/lead** often comes from his nocturnal appearance, supposed gravity, or stone lore. It conflicts with the rank table and should be labelled a modern reinterpretation.

### Time of conjuration

The *Goetia*’s generic rule says:

> “Prelates may be bound in any hour of the day.”

Because princes are classed with prelates in the same directions, Stolas is not given a unique nighttime hour merely because his form is a night-raven.

Three different timings circulate:

- **[Documented ritual rule]** Prince/prelate: any hour of the day.
- **[Esoteric correspondence]** Crowley’s *777*: Stolas is the **day** demon of Pisces III.
- **[Esoteric correspondence]** Menadel’s Lenain hour: **11:40 a.m. to noon**.
- **[Modern invention]** Saturday at the hour of Saturn, midnight, or a “night-raven hour”: not the rank rule and not stated in the Stolas entry.

## The seal

### What the sources contain

**[Documented text]** Weyer and Scot provide **no seal for Stolas**. The seal belongs to the seventeenth-century *Ars Goetia* manuscript layer, not to the printed *Pseudomonarchia* entry.

The conventional Stolas character can be described without imposing symbolic meanings:

- it is wider than it is tall;
- its main construction is a sequence of angular, mostly right-angled or stepped strokes;
- a small loop or rounded terminal appears at the left in common recensions;
- short transverse strokes cross or branch from the central structure;
- the design extends toward round or looped terminals on the right and lower-right;
- some witnesses make the steps sharply rectangular, while others soften them into curves;
- line lengths, joins, terminal circles, and overall proportions differ from copy to copy.

The familiar clean version is the Mathers–Crowley redrawing, available as the [public-domain Stolas sigil](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:36-stolas-sigilo-ars-goetia.svg).

### Manuscript variants

**[Documented textual comparison]**

- Sloane MS 3825: the seal accompanies the “Night Raven” entry and is to be worn as a lamen.
- Sloane MS 2731: preserves the same recognizable skeleton, with scribal differences in stroke proportions and terminals.
- Harley MS 6483: gives the Goetic character within Rudd’s double-seal apparatus and spells the spirit **Stolus**.
- Mathers–Crowley: regularizes the drawing, as it does many seals.
- Peterson: reproduces manuscript-derived forms rather than treating Mathers’s redrawing as uniquely authoritative.

**[Negative finding]** The manuscripts do not say that a particular circle means cosmic unity, that a cross means the intersection of matter and spirit, or that the steps depict ascent through planetary spheres. Such explanations are modern symbolic exegesis.

### Material

The individual Stolas paragraph says the seal is to be made and worn as a lamen but does not name its material. The general rank table supplies **tin** for a prince.

**[Tradition]** Modern pendants made in silver, steel, brass, gold, or printed paper may reproduce the design, but their materials do not follow the original rank prescription unless they are tin.

## The angel: Menadel/Manadel

### Identification and spelling

**[Documented text]** Harley MS 6483 pairs Stolas/Stolus with the thirty-sixth Shem angel, spelled **Manadel** in Skinner and Rankine’s tabulation. The dominant later French spelling is **Menadel**.

The root is:

> מנד

Adding the common angelic suffix אל, *El*, produces:

> מנדאל

The vocalization is conventional because the Exodus-derived roots are consonantal. *Menadel*, *Menaḏel*, and *Manadel* represent later reading traditions, not variant words written out with full vowels in Exodus.

### How the seventy-two names are derived

**[Tradition with medieval documentation]** Exodus 14:19–21 contains three successive Hebrew verses traditionally counted at seventy-two letters each. The later method writes:

1. verse 19 in its ordinary direction;
2. verse 20 in reverse order;
3. verse 21 in its ordinary direction;

and reads the resulting columns as seventy-two three-letter names.

The thirty-sixth column yields **מנד**, M-N-D.

This arrangement is frequently described as “boustrophedon,” because the middle verse runs in the opposite direction.

The general history is more complicated than modern charts imply:

- **[Documented rabbinic usage]** *Shem ha-meforash* originally means the “explicit,” “distinguished,” or “fully expressed” divine Name and can denote the Tetragrammaton.
- **[Medieval tradition]** Jewish sources subsequently discuss divine names of forty-two and seventy-two letters.
- **[Medieval kabbalah]** The *Sefer ha-Bahir*, probably composed or redacted in Provence in the later twelfth century, is among the earliest surviving sources to describe a seventy-twofold divine-name construction.
- **[Tradition]** *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh*, a composite medieval work later printed in Amsterdam in 1701, helped transmit seventy-two-name and angelic materials.
- **[Christian Cabala]** Renaissance Christian writers transformed the three-letter divine roots into a list of angels, commonly by adding **-el** or **-yah**.

For the medieval construction see [*Sefer ha-Bahir* §§106–108](https://fringe.hekhal.org/texts/sefer-ha-bahir-106-108-targum). Ibn Ezra knew the numerical discussion but resisted simplistic claims that the sequence alone disclosed the ultimate divine Name: [Ibn Ezra on Exodus 14:19](https://www.sefaria.org/Ibn_Ezra_on_Exodus.14.19.2).

### From the kabbalists to Rudd

#### Reuchlin

**[Historical transmission]** Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517) was an important Christian-Cabalist vehicle for presenting Hebrew divine-name doctrines to Latin readers. Christian Cabala increasingly treated the seventy-two roots as angelic names through added suffixes.

This is transmission and reinterpretation: it is not evidence that the completed names and later offices all appeared in ancient Judaism.

#### Vigenère

**[Historical transmission]** Blaise de Vigenère’s *Traicté des chiffres* (1586) discussed cryptographic, Hebrew, and divine-name material and contributed to the early modern Christian reception of the seventy-twofold Name.

#### Kircher

**[Documented text]** Athanasius Kircher treated the “Cabala of the Hebrews” in *Oedipus Aegyptiacus*, vol. II, part I (1652). His tables are influential in later occult reconstructions, but his exposition is a seventeenth-century Catholic-Christian interpretation, often polemical toward Jewish Kabbalah—not an unmediated transcript of an ancient angel system. See Peterson’s [Kircher Cabala transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/kircher/cabala.htm).

#### Rudd

**[Esoteric correspondence]** The Harley recension makes a direct numerical pairing:

> Demon 36: Stolus/Stolas  
> Angel 36: Manadel/Menadel

Rudd’s innovation is not merely putting two lists side by side. The angelic name, seal, and Psalm verse are incorporated into the controlling apparatus placed over or against the demon’s character.

### How Rudd constrains Stolas

**[Documented ritual structure]** In Harley MS 6483, each Goetic spirit is associated with:

- the spirit’s seal;
- a Hebrew spelling or transliteration of the spirit’s name;
- the corresponding angelic name and character;
- a Psalm verse associated with that angel.

The theological logic is hierarchical: the divine Name and its angel are superior powers by which the lower spirit is compelled.

For Stolas, Skinner and Rankine print the verse:

> *Domini [read: Domine] dilexi decorum domus tuae et locum habitationis gloriae tuae.*

The standard Vulgate form is:

> *Domine, dilexi decorem domus tuae, et locum habitationis gloriae tuae.*

Translation:

> O Lord, I have loved the beauty of thy house, and the place where thy glory dwells.

That is **Psalm 25:8 in the Vulgate**, corresponding to **Psalm 26:8** in Hebrew and most Protestant English numbering.

**[Textual note]** *Domini* and *decorum* in transcriptions can be scribal or editorial errors for *Domine* and *decorem*. The underlying biblical identification is clear.

**[Legend/tradition]** Within the rite, Menadel does not negotiate with Stolas as an equal. The angel functions as a divine superior whose name and authority constrain the spirit.

### Lenain, 1823

**[Documented text]** Lazare Républicain Lenain, *La science cabalistique* (Amiens, 1823), pp. 68–69, gives the fullest early nineteenth-century French notice:

> *36e. Menadel. Son attribut (Dieu adorable), il correspond au saint nom Alla, suivant la langue des Maures. Son rayon commence depuis le 176e degré de la sphère jusqu’au 180e, inclusivement, correspondant à la 18e décade et au génie nommé Aphut. Il préside les cinq jours ci-après : 24 avril, 5 juillet, 15 septembre, 26 novembre, 6 février. L’on invoque ce génie pour se maintenir dans son emploi, et pour conserver les moyens d’existence que l’on possède : on prononce la demande avec les noms divins et le 8e verset du psaume 25. (Domine dilexi decorem domus tuae : et locum habitationis gloriae tuae.) Il sert contre les calomnies et pour délivrer les prisonniers. L’heure favorable commence depuis 11 heures 40 minutes du matin jusqu’à midi précis.*

Translation:

> 36. Menadel. His attribute is “Adorable God.” He corresponds to the holy name Alla, according to the language of the Moors. His ray begins at the 176th degree of the sphere and continues through the 180th degree inclusive, corresponding to the eighteenth decan and to the genius named Aphut. He presides over the following five days: 24 April, 5 July, 15 September, 26 November, and 6 February. This genius is invoked to remain in one’s employment and to preserve one’s existing means of livelihood; the request is pronounced with the divine names and the eighth verse of Psalm 25. He serves against calumnies and to deliver prisoners. The favorable hour begins at 11:40 in the morning and lasts until precisely noon.

Source: [Lenain, *La science cabalistique*](https://books.google.com/books?id=BqzyzEEX3MAC).

Lenain continues that Menadel gives information about distant persons from whom no news has been received and returns exiles.

Important distinctions:

- **“Dieu adorable”** is Lenain’s assigned divine attribute.
- The five scattered dates are not the same system as a single five-day “guardian angel birthday period.”
- The 176th–180th degree sector is conventionally rendered **25°–30° Virgo**.
- “Alla” reflects an early nineteenth-century French representation of Arabic *Allah*, embedded in Lenain’s theory of divine names among different peoples.

### Papus

**[Historical transmission]** Gérard Encausse, “Papus,” republished and synthesized French occult-kabbalistic materials, including texts associated with Lenain. His *La Cabbale* and the *Almanach du Magiste* helped carry these tables into fin-de-siècle occultism.

**[Negative finding]** Papus does not appear to be the origin of the Stolas–Menadel pairing, Menadel’s Psalm, or the 176°–180° sector. Those elements precede him in Rudd and Lenain. His role is primarily diffusion and systematization. See [Papus, *La Cabbale*, 1903 edition](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/pdf/abg405b2732165.pdf).

### Golden Dawn

**[Esoteric correspondence]** Golden Dawn materials integrate:

- the seventy-two Shem angels;
- five-degree zodiacal subdivisions;
- decans and tarot pips;
- Hebrew names and angelic hierarchies;
- the seven planetary forces.

In the resulting pattern, Menadel occupies the second five-degree half of the third Virgo decan, hence:

- **25°–30° Virgo**;
- **Ten of Pentacles**;
- **Mercury in Virgo** as the tarot decan dignity.

The Golden Dawn did not invent the three-verse Exodus derivation, but it embedded the names in a comprehensive tarot-astrological system.

### Ambelain

**[Tradition]** Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) reproduces much of Lenain’s Menadel material, including “Dieu adorable,” the 176th–180th degrees, the five dispersed dates, the Psalm, and the employment/liberation offices. It helped make the French “guardian angels of the Kabbalah” literature accessible to later occultists.

Ambelain’s presentation is an occult manual, not a critical history of medieval Jewish sources. Apparent hour or Psalm discrepancies in translations and online excerpts often arise from tables shifting between angels or from modern recalculation.

### Haziel

**[Modern tradition]** François Bernard-Termès, writing as **Haziel**, popularized the seventy-two angels as personal guardian angels in late twentieth-century French and translated editions. In that literature Menadel commonly governs a short September birth period, protects employment, helps prisoners or exiles, and opposes slander.

The commonly printed date ranges vary—often around **18–23 September**, but sometimes shifted by a day or several degrees.

**[Disputed/esoteric]** Those contiguous birthday periods are not the same as Lenain’s five scattered calendar days. They result from a later decision to convert consecutive five-degree zodiacal sectors into consecutive civil dates.

### Meaning of Menadel

The following should not be conflated:

- **[Documented letters]** מנד is the thirty-sixth three-letter sequence under the standard Exodus table.
- **[Linguistic fact]** אל means “God” or “El” and is a normal angelic suffix.
- **[Tradition]** “Adorable God,” “God worthy of adoration,” “Honorable God,” or “God who preserves” are later devotional attributes.
- **[Negative finding]** No secure Hebrew derivation was found by which מנד itself literally means “adorable.” Lenain’s gloss is theological, not a demonstrated dictionary translation.

### Crowley’s *777*: the important correction

**[Documented esoteric correspondence]** In *777*, Stolas is entered under the Goetic demons assigned to the decans **by day**. He appears in the Pisces row:

- **20°–30° Pisces**;
- third decan of Pisces;
- **Ten of Cups**;
- **Mars in Pisces**;
- day demon **Stolas**;
- night demon **Andromalius**.

Source: Aleister Crowley, [*777* (1909 PDF)](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1909__crowley___777.pdf).

This illustrates why each tarot pip may be said to “carry two spirits”: Crowley’s table divides each ten-degree decan between a daytime and a nighttime Goetic spirit. It does **not** give each demon a unique five-degree quinance.

**[Disputed modern synthesis]** Some contemporary charts place Stolas at 25°–30° Virgo because he is paired numerically with Menadel. That is internally coherent as a Rudd/Shem construction, but it should not be cited as Crowley’s *777* placement.

### Astrological timing in practice

Four historically distinct options exist:

1. **Goetic rank timing:** any hour of the day for a prince/prelate.
2. **Rank planet:** a planetary hour of Jupiter, preferably using tin.
3. **Rudd–Menadel timing:** the angel’s sector and Psalm, with Lenain’s 11:40–12:00 period.
4. ***777* decan timing:** Sun in 20°–30° Pisces, with daytime emphasis.

**[Tradition]** Modern practitioners often combine these—for example, a Jupiter hour during Pisces III while using Menadel’s Psalm. No early witness prescribes that exact composite operation.

## Iconography

### Before de Plancy

**[Documented text]** Weyer, Scot, and the *Goetia* describe a bird followed by a human form but provide no portrait.

The seals are abstract ritual characters, not pictures of the spirit. Calling the seal a schematic owl, constellation, or alchemical diagram is modern interpretation.

### Collin de Plancy

Jacques Collin de Plancy first published the *Dictionnaire infernal* in **1818**. It passed through major revisions as de Plancy’s religious position changed. The famous illustrated sixth edition appeared in **1863**, with engravings designed by **Louis Le Breton** and engraved by several hands.

**[Documented text]** The 1863 entry, pp. 635–636, reads:

> *Stolas, grand prince des enfers, qui apparaît sous la forme d’un hibou ; lorsqu’il prend celle d’un homme et qu’il se montre devant l’exorciste, il enseigne l’astronomie, ainsi que les propriétés des plantes et la valeur des pierres précieuses. Vingt-six légions le reconnaissent pour général.*

Translation:

> Stolas, great prince of Hell, who appears in the form of an owl; when he takes that of a man and shows himself before the exorcist, he teaches astronomy, as well as the properties of plants and the value of precious stones. Twenty-six legions recognize him as their general.

Source: [French Wikisource, 1863 Stolas entry](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.,_1863/Stolas).

De Plancy cites Wier/Weyer, but changes the bird:

- Weyer: *nycticorax*.
- Scot: night-raven.
- Mathers’s later line: mighty raven.
- De Plancy: **hibou**, owl.

This is the earliest securely checked source in the chain that explicitly makes Stolas an owl.

### Louis Le Breton’s portrait

**[Documented iconography]** Le Breton depicts:

- an owl-like head with enormous round, forward-facing eyes;
- a small pointed crown;
- a short hooked beak;
- a compact feathered body with folded wings;
- extraordinarily long, thin, nearly wader-like bare legs;
- taloned feet planted on the ground.

The pose turns a nocturnal scholar-prince into an instantly recognizable heraldic monster. The long legs may visually echo a heron or wading bird even though the head is unequivocally owl-like.

The plate is available through [Wikisource](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Fichier:Ill_dict_infernal_p0651_stolas.jpg) and the [Bibliothèque nationale de France/Gallica scan](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k5754923d/f645.image).

**[Historical correction]** The phrase “de Plancy’s portrait” is common shorthand, but Le Breton was the designer. De Plancy wrote the dictionary.

### The 1818 edition

**[Documented bibliographical fact]** The first edition was published in 1818 and was not the famous Le Breton illustrated edition. The checked portrait belongs to 1863.

**[Open textual point]** The exact wording of every early-edition Stolas notice was not established here page-by-page. The securely quoted text is from 1863; claims that the 1818 first edition already contained the identical owl wording should be checked against the relevant copy rather than inferred from the sixth edition.

### Modern occult art

**[Modern invention]** Twentieth- and twenty-first-century artists generally choose among four models:

- a naturalistic raven;
- the crowned, long-legged Le Breton owl;
- an owl-headed humanoid magician or prince;
- an elegant human or anthropomorphic noble with owl features.

Stars, astrolabes, herbs, crystals, blue or purple robes, Jupiter symbols, and the seal are common visual additions. These illustrate Stolas’s transmitted offices or later correspondences, but they are not described in Weyer.

## People and works

### Johann Weyer

Weyer, a physician and former student of Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa, published *De praestigiis daemonum* as a major attack on witch persecutions and demonological credulity. In its fifth edition of 1577 he appended the *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*.

**[Documented text]** He says his source was a *Liber officiorum spirituum*, also styled *Empto Salomonis*. He deliberately omitted ritual material so that the work would be less usable. His exact Stolas is the first securely dated printed occurrence of the name.

### Reginald Scot and Abraham Fleming

Scot printed an English version in *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584), another skeptical work directed against witch-hunting and fraudulent or deluded magical claims.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Abraham Fleming probably translated the demon catalogue. His “nightraven” is a close literal response to *nycticorax* and became the basis of the English manuscript tradition.

### Anonymous *Lemegeton* compilers

**[Documented manuscript history]** Seventeenth-century compilers reorganized the Weyer/Scot material into the first book of the *Lemegeton*, added seals, ritual directions, and four spirits, and made Stolas no. 36.

No single author of the *Ars Goetia* is known. “Solomon” is the work’s legendary authority, not its historically demonstrated author.

### Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

**[Documented manuscript attribution]** Harley MS 6483 belongs to a set copied in the early eighteenth century and labelled as Rudd material. Peter Smart is associated with the copying or organization of this corpus.

**[Disputed]** Thomas Rudd may have devised or transmitted the demon–Shem-angel system earlier in the seventeenth century, but the surviving manuscript is not his autograph and the full extent of his authorship remains uncertain.

### Collin de Plancy and Louis Le Breton

De Plancy transformed the terse grimoire notice into a demonological dictionary entry. Le Breton gave Stolas his most influential image: the crowned, long-legged owl.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers

Mathers collated British manuscript material and prepared the English text published in 1904. He supplied the polished wording, alternate *Stolos*, standardized seal, rank correspondences, and “Mighty Raven” reading familiar to twentieth-century readers.

### Aleister Crowley

Crowley published and framed Mathers’s text, adding material including the “Preliminary Invocation” and an “Initiated Interpretation.”

**[Esoteric interpretation]** Crowley encouraged psychological and initiatory readings of Goetic evocation. His later *777* tables assigned Stolas to Pisces III by day and to the Ten of Cups.

**[Historical distinction]** Crowley’s Enochian and Thelemic overlays are not part of Weyer’s Stolas entry and should not be retrojected into it.

### A. E. Waite

Waite printed **Solas or Stolas**, helping the shorter form circulate. His treatments were often critical and comparative but could impose his own judgments on manuscript material.

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 edition and continuing digital editions are central to modern textual study. His work:

- restores “Night Raven”;
- compares Weyer, Scot, and manuscripts;
- documents spellings;
- identifies Pruflas’s omission and the four additions;
- distinguishes the source layers;
- reproduces manuscript seals.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their *Goetia of Dr Rudd* (2007) made Harley MS 6483’s angel-constrained recension widely accessible and documented **Stolus–Manadel**, the Psalm, and the double-seal method.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent argued more generally for reading grimoire spirits within long traditions of spirit catalogues, underworld religion, and classical goēs rather than reducing them to Christian fallen angels.

**[Negative finding]** No compelling Stolas-specific identification with an ancient deity was found in this scholarship. General arguments for classical continuity cannot supply a missing Stolas etymology.

### Poke Runyon

Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* and his ritual-magic teaching contributed to the late twentieth-century revival of operant Goetia.

**[Tradition]** His work is significant to modern practice as a whole. No primary evidence was located that makes him the origin of a distinct historical claim about Stolas.

### Lon Milo DuQuette

DuQuette, with Christopher Hyatt, published *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia*, presenting the system in an accessible modern and often psychological framework.

**[Tradition]** This is part of Stolas’s modern reception, not an independent textual witness to his sixteenth-century description.

### Frater Achad

**[Negative finding]** A dedicated Stolas operation or Stolas-specific doctrine by Frater Achad was not located. His name is relevant to Crowley’s wider magical milieu but should not be listed as a documented Stolas authority without a work and passage.

## Grimoire ancestors

### *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

**[Documented reference]** Weyer identifies his source as a *Book of the Offices of Spirits*. Johannes Trithemius had already listed a book of this general title in his *Antipalus maleficiorum* of 1508.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The lost or dispersed Offices tradition is the immediate ancestor of Weyer’s catalogue. It likely circulated in multiple versions rather than as one stable archetype.

### *Livre des Esperitz*

This French catalogue is the strongest specific ancestral witness because of **Distolas**. Its overlap with Weyer establishes a common textual family, although it is not simply a French translation of Weyer.

### Munich handbook, Clm 849

**[Documented manuscript comparison]** The fifteenth-century Munich handbook Clm 849 contains spirit catalogues and ritual materials with several names and descriptions resembling later Offices spirits.

**[Negative finding]** Stolas itself is not securely present. Clm 849 demonstrates the antiquity of the catalogue genre and some members of the family, not the antiquity of this particular name.

### Folger Offices texts and the *Book of Oberon*

The Folger manuscripts preserve expanded Offices traditions, including **Mistalas**. These materials show that Weyer’s abbreviated publication was not the only surviving branch.

### *Book of Abramelin*

**[Negative finding]** No securely identifiable Stolas occurs in the principal Abramelin spirit lists. General similarity between large demon catalogues does not prove direct descent.

### *Testament of Solomon*

**[Documented background]** The late-antique *Testament of Solomon* supplies a powerful precedent for Solomon interrogating, binding, and assigning demons through superior angelic or divine powers.

**[Negative finding]** It contains no Stolas. The *Ars Goetia*’s Solomonic frame is traditional pseudepigraphy rather than evidence that its seventy-two names were already in the *Testament*.

## Enochian and other later overlays

### Rudd’s Enochian synthesis

The Rudd manuscript collection juxtaposes Goetic, angelic, and materials derived from John Dee’s angelic tables. Skinner and Rankine discuss this broader synthesis.

**[Esoteric correspondence]** This creates a ritual environment in which Goetic spirits, Shem angels, and Enochian or Dee-derived structures can be interpreted together.

**[Negative finding]** No securely documented, unique Enochian governor or Dee spirit identified specifically as Stolas was established. A collection-level synthesis should not be mistaken for an exact ancient equivalence.

### Crowley

Crowley’s use of the “Bornless Ritual,” divine names, pentagrams, hexagrams, and his later correspondences reframed the operation in Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn and Thelemic terms.

**[Historical distinction]** None of these additions explains the origin of the name Stolas.

### Demonolatry “enn”

The formula commonly printed for Stolas is:

> *Stolos ramec viasa on ca.*

**[Modern invention/tradition]** This belongs to twentieth-century demonolatry systems associated with modern “enns.” It is absent from Weyer, Scot, Sloane 2731, Sloane 3825, Harley 6483, and Mathers–Crowley.

These writers influenced the cultural imagination of Hell, but not every later named demon occurs in their works.

### *Bartimaeus*

**[Negative finding]** No attested Stolas character was located in Jonathan Stroud’s principal *Bartimaeus* novels or their standard character lists. The series contains many named spirits and draws on demonological language, but Stolas should not be claimed without a passage.

**[Negative finding]** No securely identified Stolas character was found in the principal *Hellboy* comics or films. General Goetic imagery and other named demons are not evidence for Stolas.

### *Castlevania*

**[Modern invention, attested]** Stolas is an enemy in *Castlevania: Aria of Sorrow* (2003) and *Dawn of Sorrow* (2005), represented as an owl-associated magical creature. This is a game adaptation of the demonological name and imagery, not part of the grimoire tradition.

### *Helluva Boss*

**[Modern invention, strongly attested]** Vivienne Medrano’s animated series *Helluva Boss* introduced its Stolas in the 2019 pilot and developed him into a major character.

The series retains several traditional cues:

- the name **Stolas**;
- princely or Goetic status;
- owl iconography derived ultimately from de Plancy/Le Breton;
- astral imagery and astronomical knowledge;
- a magical grimoire used for access to the human world.

It invents nearly everything biographical:

- his family relationships with Paimon, Stella, Octavia, and Andrealphus;
- his romance with Blitzø;
- his personality, sexuality, marriage, and domestic plot;
- the organization of the Ars Goetia as an aristocratic family;
- the details of his powers and political position.

The pilot was released on the creator’s official YouTube channel on 25 November 2019: [official *Helluva Boss* pilot](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=OlahNrlcgS4).

The modern character is consequently not “accurate” or “inaccurate” in a simple sense. It is an original dramatic character constructed around a handful of grimoire and de Plancy signifiers.

### Music

**[Modern reception]** The Masada-related album *Stolas: Book of Angels Volume 12*, performed by the Masada Quintet and composed by John Zorn, uses Stolas as part of the *Book of Angels* naming sequence. The title is an artistic appropriation of the demonological name, not a narrative treatment of his traditional offices.

A post-hardcore band named **Stolas** also adopted the name in the twenty-first century.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Does Stolas predate Weyer?

The most defensible answer is qualified.

- **Exact name:** no secure pre-1577 occurrence found.
- **Probable relative:** Distolas in the *Livre des Esperitz*.
- **Related branch:** Mistalas in the Folger Offices tradition.
- **General catalogue ancestry:** definitely older than Weyer.
- **Ancient Stolas:** no evidence.

Thus “Stolas first appears in 1577” is correct for the exact printed name, but “Weyer invented him from nothing” is not demonstrated.

### 2. Raven, night-heron, or owl?

- Weyer’s word is *nycticorax*.
- Its closest zoological gloss is night-heron.
- Scot translates its components as night-raven.
- the principal *Goetia* reading is Night Raven.
- “Mighty Raven” is a secondary corruption.
- De Plancy makes it an owl.
- Le Breton makes the owl visually canonical.

Calling all these forms equally ancient erases the transmission history.

### 3. Stolas or Stolos?

**Stolas** is the best-attested form. **Stolus** is a real Harley variant. **Stolos** is Mathers’s alternate and may reflect either manuscript variation or editorial regularization.

No evidence shows that *Stolos* is a more “correct Greek” original.

### 4. Solas

**Solas** appears in Waite, Shah, and modern games. It is a genuine reception-history variant, but it is later than Stolas.

It should not be conflated with unrelated names such as Irish *solas*, “light,” without evidence.

### 5. Stoppas

**[Disputed]** *Stoppas* has become common online, largely through copied encyclopedic lists. Peterson’s manuscript comparison does not record it in the requested main witnesses.

The most likely explanation is an editorial or digital typo that acquired the appearance of tradition through repetition.

### 6. Twenty-five or twenty-six legions?

**Twenty-six** is the unanimous number in the principal sources checked:

- Weyer;
- Scot;
- Sloane 3825;
- Sloane 2731;
- Harley 6483;
- Mathers–Crowley;
- de Plancy.

Claims of **twenty-five** legions lack support in this source chain and may be copy errors or confusion with another spirit.

### 7. Is he a prince of Jupiter or Saturn?

- **Jupiter/tin:** follows the *Goetia* rank table.
- **Saturn/lead:** later symbolic inference based on night, stones, solemnity, or occult knowledge.
- **Mars in Pisces:** the planet of the *777* tarot decan, not the planet of Stolas’s rank.
- **Mercury in Virgo:** the decan planet of Menadel’s Virgo quinance, not Stolas’s rank planet.

All four can appear in modern charts because they answer different questions. Presenting any one without naming its system creates false contradictions.

### 8. Virgo or Pisces?

- Menadel by number: 25°–30° Virgo.
- Stolas in Crowley’s demon-decan table: 20°–30° Pisces, by day.

The Virgo attribution is not a textual correction of *777*; it is a separate Rudd/Shem overlay.

### 9. Is Menadel an ancient guardian angel?

**[Disputed framing]** The trigram construction has medieval Jewish roots, and Menadel belongs to an early-modern Christian-Cabalist angel list. The modern personal guardian-angel profile—birthday window, personality, career guidance, life mission—is substantially later.

### 10. Does Menadel literally mean “Adorable God”?

No philological demonstration was found. “Dieu adorable” is Lenain’s divine attribute.

The suffix *El* is transparent. The meaning assigned to the MND root is traditional or contemplative.

### 11. Was Harley 6483 written by Thomas Rudd?

The manuscript carries Rudd attributions, and Skinner and Rankine reconstruct an earlier Rudd recension. The surviving manuscript was copied after Rudd’s lifetime.

Calling the whole text “by Rudd” is conventional but not palaeographically certain.

### 12. Was the *Goetia* written by Solomon?

**[Legend]** The text says or implies Solomonic authority.

**[Historical finding]** The surviving work is a seventeenth-century compilation using sixteenth-century printed material and older ritual traditions. No ancient Hebrew *Ars Goetia* containing Stolas is known.

### 13. Are astronomy and astrology interchangeable here?

Not exactly. Weyer says *astronomia*. In Renaissance usage that discipline could encompass areas later divided between observational astronomy, judicial astrology, cosmology, and calendrical science.

It is fair to note that semantic breadth. It is not fair to silently replace the word with “astrology” and claim the manuscript says so.

### 14. “Poisonous plants”

The early texts say **herbs**, not poisonous plants. Poison lore is a modern elaboration.

### 15. Is Distolas the same spirit?

Evidence supporting the identification:

- strong name resemblance;
- stones occur in both entries;
- same catalogue family;
- Boudet explicitly aligns them.

Evidence against treating it as certain:

- marquis versus prince;
- twenty versus twenty-six legions;
- fair form versus night bird;
- answers and magical horse versus astronomy and herbs;
- only the stone office clearly overlaps.

The safest formulation is **probable textual relative or ancestor**.

## Open questions

1. **What was the exact form in Weyer’s lost or unidentified *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*?**  
   Weyer’s source might resolve whether *Stolas* preceded *Distolas*, resulted from it, or derived from a third form.

2. **Does the rubric “De stolas” in the French tradition preserve the exact name despite the body’s “Distolas”?**  
   A full palaeographical comparison of heading, spacing, and scribal hands could determine whether initial *Di-* arose through fusion, abbreviation, or correction.

3. **What precisely produced *Mistalas*?**  
   It may preserve a fuller name, a copying error, or a contaminated reading associated with Paimon.

4. **Which manuscript first changed “night” to “mighty”?**  
   The likely path can be reconstructed, but an exact stemma of the correction requires direct comparison of all relevant hands.

5. **Did de Plancy independently reinterpret *nycticorax* as owl, or follow an intermediate French source?**  
   His citation points to Weyer, but the immediate origin of *hibou* remains uncertain.

6. **Did the 1818 *Dictionnaire infernal* already use the owl wording?**  
   The 1863 text is verified. A page-level collation across all six editions would date the verbal change precisely.

7. **How much of Harley 6483’s angelic apparatus is genuinely Rudd’s?**  
   The attribution requires continued codicological comparison across Harley MSS 6479–6486.

8. **What historical source supplied the individual “meanings” of the Shem roots?**  
   The Exodus letter-groups, angelic suffixes, Lenain’s attributes, Psalm verses, and modern personality profiles are different layers that are frequently collapsed.

9. **Why was the Weyer list reordered?**  
   Peterson’s shuffled-leaves explanation is plausible, but no compiler’s note survives.

10. **Was the Stolas seal invented for the *Goetia*, copied from an unidentified character-book, or adapted from an earlier sigil?**  
    No matching pre-Goetia seal has yet been securely identified. Peterson notes that earlier spirit catalogues contain seals, but not the same set.
