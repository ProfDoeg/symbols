# Zepar: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **[Documented]** directly stated or visibly present in a historical source.
- **[Reconstruction]** an inference from textual comparison or modern scholarship.
- **[Tradition]** an attribution made within an occult or religious lineage.
- **[Disputed]** a claim for which sources conflict or the evidence is insufficient.
- **[Legend]** a supernatural claim reported as part of the tradition, not independently verified.
- **[Modern invention]** an attribution first found in recent occultism, fiction, games, fan culture, or unsourced web compilations.

Because complete historical entries exceed the quotation allowance available here, I give exact short excerpts and then an exhaustive clause-by-clause account. The linked editions supply the complete passages.

# The name and its origins

## 1. Medieval background: the catalogue before Zepar

### The Testament of Solomon

**[Documented]** The late-antique *Testament of Solomon* supplies the foundational story of Solomon receiving authority over demons, interrogating them about their forms, activities, astrological relations, and thwarting angels. It is therefore a major ancestor of the *genre*.

**[Negative finding]** The exact name **Zepar** does not appear in the standard Greek/English textual tradition of the *Testament*. Nor is there a securely identifiable demon whose name, appearance, and entire office can be equated with Zepar. See Peterson’s [Testament of Solomon](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/testamen.htm).

The later claim that Zepar personally was sealed by the biblical Solomon is consequently **[Legend]**, inherited from the Solomonic framing of the *Lemegeton*, not historical evidence for an ancient Zepar cult.

### Medieval books of spirits’ offices

**[Documented]** Medieval authors mention works called *Liber officiorum spirituum*, *De officiis spirituum*, or similar titles—“Book of the Offices of Spirits.” Roger Bacon and Johannes Trithemius knew titles of this type, while surviving necromantic miscellanies contain abbreviated catalogues arranged by a spirit’s rank, appearance, office, and subordinates.

**[Reconstruction]** These works constitute the most plausible textual family behind Weyer’s catalogue. That does not mean that every medieval *Liber officiorum* contained Zepar; “book of offices” was partly a genre label, and the relevant complete exemplar has not survived.

### The Munich Handbook and Taob

Richard Kieckhefer’s *Forbidden Rites* edits the fifteenth-century Munich necromancer’s handbook, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek Clm 849. Its catalogue includes a spirit called **Taob**.

**[Documented]** Taob is a prince who:

- has a human or physician-like manifestation;
- is especially effective in matters concerning women;
- inflames women with love for men;
- changes women into another form until they reach or enjoy the beloved;
- makes them sterile;
- commands twenty-five legions.

This is extraordinarily close to Zepar’s later office. The source and scholarly edition are Richard Kieckhefer, *Forbidden Rites: A Necromancer’s Manual of the Fifteenth Century* (1997), especially its catalogue tables; see the [JSTOR edition record](https://www.jstor.org/stable/10.5325/jj.5233075).

**[Reconstruction]** Taob is therefore a strong functional or textual precursor to Zepar’s notice.

**[Disputed]** Taob is also close in name to Weyer’s **Tap**, an alias of Gaap. Catalogue comparisons have consequently associated Taob with Gaap/Tap rather than Zepar. The most responsible conclusion is:

> Taob’s *office* is a likely ancestor or close relative of Zepar’s office, but **Taob is not an early spelling of Zepar**.

Websites stating that “Zepar was first recorded as Taob” convert a textual comparison into a false exact-name attestation.

### The *Livre des esperitz* and Coap

The French *Livre des esperitz*, preserved in Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29, belongs to the same broad demon-catalogue tradition. Older literature often dated it to the fifteenth century; Jean-Patrice Boudet’s palaeographical assessment places the extant copy around the middle of the sixteenth century, while accepting that its material may be older.

Its spirit **Coap** is a prince associated with procuring women, bringing them where desired, and making them barren; he commands twenty-seven legions. The text is available in the [*Livre des esperitz* transcription](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre_des_esperitz/Texte_entier), and Boudet’s study is [“Les ‘who’s who’ démonologiques de la Renaissance et leurs ancêtres médiévaux”](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/30447646_Les_who%27s_who_demonologiques_de_la_Renaissance_et_leurs_ancetres_medievaux).

**[Reconstruction]** Coap is another functional parallel to Zepar.

**[Disputed]** Boudet does not establish **Coap = Zepar**. In fact, his comparative lists treat the names separately. The parallel may represent a shared notice that migrated between spirits as catalogues were recopied, abbreviated, or reordered.

Boudet finds that the *Livre des esperitz* and Weyer share many entries but also possess substantial independent material. His model is essentially a common textual trunk, not a simple claim that Weyer translated the surviving French manuscript.

### Abramelin

**[Negative finding]** No exact **Zepar** was found in the standard Abraham von Worms/Abramelin spirit lists in Peterson’s [Abramelin edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/abramelin/abramelin.htm). Similar functions and corrupt names are not enough to demonstrate identity.

## 2. Weyer’s exact Zepar, 1577

Johann Weyer—also Wier, Wierus, Weier or Weyer—published the *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* as an appendix to the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum* in 1577. The larger work had appeared in earlier forms beginning in 1563, but the standard printed *Pseudomonarchia* with its full spirit catalogue is dated 1577.

**[Documented]** This is the earliest securely located occurrence of the exact name **Zepar**.

Weyer says he obtained the catalogue from a *Liber officiorum spirituum*. He presented it polemically in a work arguing that alleged witches were commonly deluded or mentally afflicted rather than wielders of real supernatural power. He also says he withheld material so that the catalogue would not function as a complete operational manual. Peterson supplies the Latin and English tradition in [Weyer’s *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

**[Open possibility]** Weyer’s lost exemplar might have contained the name before 1577. Until such a manuscript is identified, that remains an unprovable possibility, not an earlier attestation.

## 3. Etymology

### What can be demonstrated

**[Documented]** The earliest exact spelling is Latin-script **Zepar**. It remains Zepar throughout the English manuscript transmission.

**[Negative finding]** No ancient god, hero, tribe, city, or ordinary Greek, Latin, Hebrew, German, or French word has been convincingly demonstrated as its source.

This makes Zepar unlike:

- **Bael**, transparently related to West Semitic *baʿal*, “lord”;
- **Astaroth**, related through demonological transmission to Astarte;
- **Asmoday**, related to Iranian/Jewish Asmodeus or Ashmedai;
- **Belial**, from Hebrew *bĕli-yaʿal*;
- **Phenex/Phoenix**, visibly related to Greek and Latin *phoinix/phoenix*;
- **Cerberus/Naberius**, where a classical name explicitly appears as an alias.

Weyer supplies no alias or etymological gloss for Zepar.

### Hebrew retro-transliteration

Harley 6483 and later occult tables render the name in Hebrew characters as **זאפר**, generally transliterated *ZAPhR*; simplified later tables give *ZPR*. Under conventional occult gematria this may be calculated as 7 + 1 + 80 + 200 = **288**, although Skinner’s tables also report **287**, evidently depending on transcription or arithmetic. The relevant comparative table is in Skinner’s [*Complete Magician’s Tables*](https://lyricaltokarev.com/public/dynamic/sync/stuff/pdf-documents-library/Complete%20Magician%27s%20Tables.pdf).

**[Tradition]** This Hebrew form is part of Christian Cabalistic occultism.

**[Disputed]** It is not evidence that *Zepar* originated as a Hebrew word. The consonants were selected to spell an already existing European demon-name.

### Unsupported proposals

- **Hebrew/Aramaic z-f-r or s-f-r, “dawn,” “shine,” “victory,” “book,” or “count.”**  
  **[Modern invention/disputed]** No historical source in the chain proposes these meanings, and the changes of consonant and meaning are uncontrolled.

- **Hebrew “he-goat” or “splitter.”**  
  **[Modern invention]** No convincing lexicographical derivation has been produced.

- **Zephyrus, Greek god of the west wind.**  
  **[Modern religious identification]** Some contemporary occult organizations identify Zepar with Zephyrus. The resemblance *Zepar–Zephyr* is insufficient: neither Weyer’s soldier, the erotic/sterility office, nor the textual genealogy has anything specifically western or wind-related. This identification appears in modern ritual literature such as the [Temple of Zeus Zephyrus ritual](https://tozrituals.org/rituals/god/zephyrus-power-ritual), not in Weyer, Scot, or the manuscripts.

- **Vepar/Separ.**  
  This is a conflation, not an etymology; see “Controversies.”

### Conclusion on the name

**[Best-supported finding]** *Zepar* is a stable but etymologically opaque early-modern spirit-name. Its exact form is first securely attested in 1577. Its office probably has medieval textual ancestors, but no ancient deity or pre-Christian cult lies demonstrably behind the name.

# The description across the sources

## 1. Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* (1577)

Weyer §19, approximately printed page 916 in the 1577 edition:

> “Zepar Dux magnus, apparens uti miles … Steriles quoque eas facit. Vigintisex huic parent legiones.”

A close gloss is: “Zepar, a great Duke, appearing as a soldier … He also makes them sterile. Twenty-six legions obey him.” The complete Latin is available in Peterson’s [Weyer transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

### Complete clause ledger

**[Documented]**

1. Name: Zepar.
2. Rank: great duke.
3. Appearance: like a soldier.
4. He inflames women with the love of men.
5. When commanded, he changes the women’s form into another.
6. The change lasts or serves until they enjoy their beloved.
7. He also makes those women sterile.
8. Twenty-six legions obey him.

### Important grammatical point

The Latin object of *transmutat* is *earum formam*, “their form,” referring to *mulieres*, the women. It does not say Zepar changes himself into their desired man.

Accordingly:

- “Zepar can change women’s appearance” is a fair reading.
- “Zepar assumes the form of a woman’s lover” is **[Modern misreading]**.
- The text gives no demanded second manifestation comparable to the explicit animal-to-human transformations of Botis, Sitri, or Furfur.

### Timing

Weyer’s general instructions, not Zepar’s individual notice, say that Dukes are constrained from the first hour until noon and in clear weather. See the rank instructions in the same [Weyer edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

**[Documented]** This is the earliest timing applicable to Zepar by rank.

**[Tradition]** Later Friday/Venus-hour timing comes from a different planetary-rank system.

### Legions

Weyer’s accompanying magical material defines a legion as 6,666 spirits. On that legendary arithmetic, twenty-six legions would equal 173,316 spirits.

**[Legend]** This is grimoire numerology, not Roman military history or a count independently attested for a supernatural hierarchy.

## 2. Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584)

Scot, Book XV, chapter 2, original page 219:

> “Zepar is a great duke, appearing as a souldier, inflaming women with the loove of men … he also maketh them barren.”

The complete paragraph is in Peterson’s [Scot transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/scot16.htm).

### Complete clause ledger

Scot reproduces every substantive element of Weyer:

1. great duke;
2. soldier;
3. inflames women with men’s love;
4. changes their shape when ordered;
5. this enables them to enjoy the beloved;
6. makes them barren;
7. commands twenty-six legions.

**[Documented]** Scot does not add red clothing, armor, a seal, a planet, or an angel.

The magical texts in Scot’s Book XV have sometimes been credited in part to Abraham Fleming’s editorial involvement, but the exact division of responsibility is not fully settled. What matters for the textual chain is that the *Discoverie* made Weyer’s catalogue available in English.

## 3. The *Ars Goetia* manuscripts

The extant *Lemegeton* manuscripts are seventeenth- and early-eighteenth-century witnesses. Peterson’s stemmatic observations indicate that the *Goetia* used the English Scot tradition—probably material connected with the expanded 1665 edition—rather than freshly translating Weyer’s Latin. The manuscript history is summarized in Peterson’s [Lemegeton introduction](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm).

### Sloane MS 3825

Sloane 3825, fol. 102v; Peterson’s 2001 printed edition, p. 15:

> “The 16th spirit is called Zepar … appeareth in red apparell & armed like a souldier.”

The complete entry is in Peterson’s [*Goetia* transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

### Complete clause ledger

**[Documented]**

1. Zepar is the sixteenth spirit.
2. He is a great duke.
3. He appears in red apparel.
4. He is armed like a soldier.
5. His office is to make women love men.
6. He brings the women and men together in love.
7. He makes the women barren.
8. He governs twenty-six legions of inferior spirits.
9. A seal follows.
10. The seal is the character to which he is obedient when he sees it.

### Changes from Weyer/Scot

| Feature | Weyer/Scot | *Goetia* |
|---|---|---|
| Number | 19 | 16 |
| Soldier | Yes | Yes |
| Red clothing | No | Added |
| Armor | Not explicit | Added |
| Inflaming love | Yes | Rephrased as causing love |
| Changing women’s form | Yes | Omitted |
| Bringing lovers together | Implicit in outcome | Made explicit |
| Barrenness | Yes | Retained |
| Legions | 26 | 26 |
| Individual seal | No | Added |

The *Goetia* thus makes the image more specific and the transformation less strange, while retaining the coercive love and sterility functions.

### Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, and Harley 6483

Peterson’s [variant table](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia_spirits.pdf) records **Zepar** in every one of these manuscripts.

- **Sloane 3825**: seventeenth century; Peterson judges it the most accurate against identifiable printed sources.
- **Sloane 3648**: circa 1665 or later; more heavily corrupted and later carelessly trimmed.
- **Sloane 2731**: dated or associated with 1686–87; compiled from more than one exemplar, including the Sloane 3648 family.
- **Harley 6483**: dated 1712–13; a late, expanded recension attributed to material of “Dr. Rudd” and copied by Peter Smart.
- **Northwestern MS 65**: completed in 1713 by Thomas Simpkins.

**[Documented]** None of these requested witnesses supplies a rival spelling for Zepar.

## 4. Mathers–Crowley, 1904

Mathers’s text, published by Aleister Crowley as *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King*, p. 27:

> “The Sixteenth Spirit is Zepar. He is a Great Duke, and appeareth in Red Apparel and Armour, like a Soldier.”

See the [1904 text at Sacred Texts](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks05.htm) and the [first-edition facsimile](https://www.100thmonkeypress.com/biblio/acrowley/books/goetia_1904/goetia_1904.htm).

The remainder states the same offices and twenty-six legions as Sloane 3825. Mathers modernizes spelling and punctuation but makes no major substantive alteration to Zepar’s entry.

**[Documented]** The famous modern image “soldier in red apparel and armor” is therefore not Crowley’s invention. It occurs in the manuscript *Goetia* that Mathers edited.

Crowley did, however, add unrelated framing materials and rendered the general conjurations into his version of Enochian. This did not create a Zepar-specific Enochian identity.

## 5. Peterson, 2001

Joseph H. Peterson, ed., *The Lesser Key of Solomon: Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis* (York Beach, Maine: Weiser, 2001), p. 15, follows Sloane 3825 at fol. 102v, noting variants from other witnesses.

**[Documented]** Peterson restores manuscript spelling and makes the source relationships explicit. His edition is not a new twentieth-century Zepar tradition; it is a critical presentation of the seventeenth-century text.

## 6. Offices Zepar does not possess

No principal Zepar notice says that he:

- teaches languages;
- teaches sciences;
- discovers treasure;
- gives familiars;
- grants invisibility;
- transmutes metals;
- answers past, present, or future questions;
- heals illnesses;
- governs the sea;
- raises storms;
- confers military strategy;
- grants titles or dignities.

Those are offices of other spirits. Assigning them to Zepar is normally the result of table misalignment, contamination from Vepar or Eligos, practitioner revelation, or modern creative expansion.

# Number and order

## Weyer’s 69 and the *Goetia*’s 72

**[Documented]** Weyer has sixty-nine entries; Zepar is number 19. The *Goetia* has seventy-two; Zepar is number 16.

The numerical change is not simply “three spirits were added.” The *Goetia* omits one Weyer spirit and adds four:

### Added to the *Goetia*

1. **Vassago**, number 3.
2. **Seere**, number 70.
3. **Dantalion**, number 71.
4. **Andromalius**, number 72.

### Omitted

- Weyer’s **Pruflas**, also called **Bufas**, number 4.

Thus:

\[
69 - 1 + 4 = 72
\]

The rest were extensively reordered. Zepar’s move from 19 to 16 is part of that general rearrangement.

**[Reconstruction]** Peterson observes that the sequence looks as if sheets or separate spirit entries were displaced and renumbered. He treats that as a possibility, not a demonstrated event. The seventy-two total may also have encouraged adjustment to a number already important in Solomonic and Cabalistic traditions. No compiler’s statement explains the process.

# Rank, planet, metal, and timing

## Duke = Venus = copper

The *Goetia* assigns metals to ranks for the manufacture of seals. Dukes’ seals are made on **copper**. Later Golden Dawn and Crowley tables interpret the rank correspondences planetarily:

| Rank | Conventional planet | Conventional metal |
|---|---:|---:|
| King | Sun | Gold |
| Duke | Venus | Copper |
| Prince/Prelate | Jupiter | Tin |
| Marquis | Moon | Silver |
| President | Mercury | Mercury |
| Earl/Count | Mars | Iron |
| Knight | Saturn | Lead |

**[Tradition]** Therefore Zepar, as a Duke, is Venusian and his proper seal-metal is copper.

**[Caution]** The Weyer and individual Zepar notices do not call him Venusian. The classification was standardized in the operational *Goetia* and later correspondence tables. It is rank-derived, not deduced from Zepar’s name.

The erotic office fits Venus symbolically, whereas his red armor looks Martian. The textual scheme resolves that tension in favor of rank: **Venus is the formal correspondence; martial coloring belongs to the manifestation**.

## Timing systems

| Source/system | Timing applicable to Zepar | Status |
|---|---|---|
| Weyer/Scot rank rule | First hour to noon; clear weather | **[Documented]** |
| *Goetia* planetary-rank practice | Venus periods; later commonly Friday and a Venus planetary hour | **[Tradition]** |
| Crowley’s *777* | First Virgo decan, “by day” | **[Esoteric correspondence]** |
| Modern tropical-calendar conversion | Roughly the first ten degrees of Virgo, usually late August into early September | **[Derived]**; changes slightly by year |
| Lenain’s Hakamiah operation | 5:00–5:20 a.m., facing east | **[Documented for the angel]**, not Zepar |
| Modern “dawn only” claims | Often extrapolated from angelic or planetary tables | **[Modern synthesis]** |

# The seal

## Manuscript origin

**[Documented]** Weyer and Scot do not give individual seals. The seal first enters the surviving Zepar tradition in the manuscript *Ars Goetia*.

The standard form consists of:

- an outer circle;
- the name ZEPAR distributed around or inside that boundary in printed versions;
- a central upright stroke;
- a high cross-member or spreading pair of arms;
- downward extensions terminating in curves, hooks, or loops;
- smaller differences in curvature, closure, joining, and line weight between copies.

This is a visual character rather than an alphabetic inscription, so a prose description cannot specify it as accurately as a facsimile. The familiar printed version can be viewed in the [Mathers–Crowley facsimile](https://www.100thmonkeypress.com/biblio/acrowley/books/goetia_1904/goetia_1904.htm) and in Peterson’s [Sloane-based *Goetia*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

## Variants

**[Documented]** The manuscripts reproduce the same recognizable core character, but freehand copying produces small stroke variants. Sloane 2731 is generally more careful graphically than Sloane 3648, which was also damaged by trimming.

**[Disputed/open]** A rigorous seal stemma would require direct high-resolution comparison of the Zepar folio in each manuscript. Peterson’s online name table establishes the spelling chain but does not provide a diplomatic plate comparing every Zepar seal side by side.

## Harley 6483

The Rudd recension augments the demonic character with:

- Zepar’s Hebrew name;
- the corresponding Shem angel Hakamiah;
- an angelic seal or divine-name material;
- a Psalm verse used as a protective or constraining divine text.

**[Tradition]** The idea is not that the angel is Zepar’s friendly “patron.” The angelic name is superior divine authority used to bind, counterbalance, or compel the corresponding spirit.

Modern double-sided Zepar/Hakamiah medallions are inspired by this system but are manufactured devotional or commercial objects, not surviving seventeenth-century artifacts.

## The seventy-two names from Exodus

### The biblical matrix

**[Tradition/documented technique]** Exodus 14:19–21 consists, in the traditional Hebrew consonantal text, of three verses of seventy-two letters each. The later construction writes:

1. verse 19 in normal Hebrew direction;
2. verse 20 in reverse direction;
3. verse 21 in normal direction.

Reading down the resulting seventy-two columns produces seventy-two three-letter combinations. Hakamiah’s is the sixteenth, **הקם**.

The Golden Dawn paper clearly describes this boustrophedon procedure in [*Shem ha-Mephoresch: The 72 Fold Name*](https://www.magicgatebg.com/Books/INDEXII/Shem%20Ha-Mephoresch-The72FoldName.pdf).

### “Shem ha-Mephorash”

**[Historical qualification]** Hebrew *shem ha-meforash* means the “explicit,” “distinctly pronounced,” or “fully expressed” divine name. In older Jewish sources it can refer to the Tetragrammaton rather than specifically to seventy-two angel names.

**[Tradition]** Medieval and Christian Cabalistic authors increasingly used the expression for the seventy-twofold name derived from Exodus.

### Jewish precedents

- **Rashi**, commenting on *Sukkah* 45a, describes the seventy-two-letter name and its derivation from three scriptural verses.
- The **Bahir**, in its medieval textual form, associates the divine name with seventy-two powers or names.
- Layers of **Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh** transmit divine-name and angelic material, including seventy-twofold arrangements.

**[Historical qualification]** “From the *Sefer Raziel*” is too simple if it implies that one fixed ancient book already contained the complete modern Hakamiah biography. *Sefer Raziel* is a composite medieval work with a complicated manuscript and printing history. The triads, the suffixed angel names, zodiac sectors, Psalm verses, moral qualities, and demon pairings accumulated in different stages.

## Reuchlin and Agrippa

Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517) helped transmit the seventy-two triads into Christian Cabala, adding **-el** or **-iah** to form pronounceable angel names.

Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia*, Book III, chapter 25, prints the sequence and credits Reuchlin. **Hakamiah** appears as number 16 in Peterson’s [Agrippa edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/agrippa/op3.htm).

**[Documented]** By 1533, therefore, Hakamiah’s name existed in print independently of Zepar.

**[Negative finding]** Agrippa does not pair Hakamiah with Zepar. That requires the later Rudd arrangement.

## Kircher

Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus*, especially the *Cabala Hebraeorum* material published in the 1650s, reproduced and systematized Christian Cabalistic divine-name tables. See Peterson’s [Kircher transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/kircher/cabala.htm).

**[Historical qualification]** Kircher was an encyclopedic Catholic author who frequently recorded esoteric systems while subjecting them to Christian theological interpretation. His tables should not automatically be read as personal endorsement of every magical use later attached to them.

## Rudd and Harley 6483

Harley MS 6483 is dated 1712–13 and internally presents itself as copied by Peter Smart from sheets belonging to “Dr. Rudd.”

**[Documented]** Its sequential scheme places angel 16, **Hakamiah**, over Goetic spirit 16, **Zepar**. The pairing is confirmed by Skinner’s comparative [*Complete Magician’s Tables*](https://lyricaltokarev.com/public/dynamic/sync/stuff/pdf-documents-library/Complete%20Magician%27s%20Tables.pdf).

**[Tradition]** The superior angelic name and its divine verse constrain the demon. The magical logic is hierarchical:

\[
\text{Divine name and Scripture} \rightarrow \text{Shem angel} \rightarrow \text{Goetic spirit}
\]

**[Disputed authorship]** The manuscript certainly associates its material with “Dr. Rudd,” traditionally identified as the mathematician and military engineer Thomas Rudd. What has not been demonstrated is that every sheet was personally authored by him.

Skinner and Rankine argue that portions may reflect material dating to approximately 1655–56. The secure physical witness remains Peter Smart’s 1712–13 manuscript.

Stephen Skinner and David Rankine published this recension as *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (Golden Hoard Press, 2007). See the [publisher’s description](https://www.llewellyn.com/product.php?ean=9780738723556) and [Open Library bibliographic record](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL11622811M/The_Goetia_of_Dr_Rudd).

## Lenain, 1823

Lazare Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* (Amiens, 1823), pp. 55–56, gives Hakamiah a developed operative biography.

Its epithet is:

> “Dieu qui érige l’univers.”

Its Psalm text is:

> “Domine Deus salutis meae, in die clamavi, et nocte coram te.”

See the digitized [1823 Lenain edition](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1823__lenain___science_cabalistique.pdf).

### Lenain’s data

**[Documented in Lenain]**

- Number: 16.
- Zodiacal ray: degrees 76–80 inclusive.
- Modern zodiac conversion: 15°–20° Gemini.
- Decadal genius: **Vérasua**.
- Planet attached to that ten-degree division: Mars.
- Nation: France.
- Sacred vernacular divine name: *Dieu*.
- Five recurring calendar days: 4 April, 15 June, 26 August, 6 November, 17 January.
- Invocation purpose: opposing traitors, gaining victory, and escaping oppression.
- Daily prayer: facing east, 5:00–5:20 a.m.
- Sphere: crowned heads, great captains, victory, prevention of sedition, iron, arsenals, and war.
- Favorable character: frank, loyal, brave, honor-conscious, oath-keeping, and “passionate for Venus.”
- Adverse genius: treason, sedition, and revolt.

### Meaning

The Hebrew root קום (*q-w-m*) means to rise or stand; its causative forms can mean to raise, establish, or set up.

**[Plausible philological interpretation]** This supports Lenain’s “establishes/raises” theme.

**[Tradition]** “God who establishes the universe” is nevertheless an occult epithet, not a word-for-word translation of the five-letter angel-name.

### Psalm numbering

Lenain’s “Psalm 87” is Vulgate numbering. English Protestant Bibles call it Psalm 88. Differences between verse 1 and verse 2 arise because some editions count the superscription as a verse.

These are therefore references to essentially the same opening prayer, not evidence for several unrelated Hakamiah Psalms.

## Papus

Papus, the pen name of Gérard Encausse, incorporated Lenain-derived material in the enlarged editions of *La Cabbale: tradition secrète de l’Occident*, especially the 1903 edition, which explicitly advertises new texts from Lenain. See the [1903 Papus edition](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1903__papus___cabbala_tradition_secrete_de_loccident.pdf).

**[Tradition]** Papus helped carry the French Hakamiah system into Martinist and twentieth-century popular Cabala.

**[Qualification]** He was a transmitter and reorganizer here, not the originator of the Exodus triads, the name Hakamiah, or Rudd’s demon pairing.

## Golden Dawn

The Golden Dawn paper gives:

- spelling **Hoqamiah**;
- number 16;
- **15°–20° Libra**;
- Saturn;
- meaning “Raise up, praying day and night”;
- Psalm 88:2 in its numbering.

These details are explicit in [*Shem ha-Mephoresch: The 72 Fold Name*](https://www.magicgatebg.com/Books/INDEXII/Shem%20Ha-Mephoresch-The72FoldName.pdf).

### Why Libra instead of Gemini?

Lenain begins the sequence at 0° Aries:

\[
16 \times 5^\circ = 80^\circ \Rightarrow 15^\circ\text{–}20^\circ \text{ Gemini}
\]

The Golden Dawn document begins angel 1 at 0° Leo. Advancing fifteen quinances places angel 16 at 15°–20° Libra.

**[Documented disagreement]** Neither value is a mere arithmetic error; they belong to different starting points.

## Ambelain

Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (Paris: Niclaus, 1951) substantially reproduces Lenain while reorganizing and sometimes correcting it. The English translation is available in [Ambelain, *Practical Kabbalah*](https://www.benpadiah.com/otherstuff/elib/magick/Shemhamphorash.pdf).

Ambelain retains:

- Hakamiah;
- 76°–80°;
- Vérasua;
- the five scattered calendar dates;
- protection against traitors;
- victory and command;
- the Psalm;
- 5:00–5:20 a.m.;
- the favorable and adverse moral portraits.

He places Hakamiah in the **Cherubim** group and later derivative tables give **Jophiel** as the governing archangel.

**[Tradition]** These choir and archangel assignments belong to Ambelain’s synthesis; they are not stated by Exodus, Weyer, or the *Goetia*.

## Haziel

“Haziel” was the publishing name of François Bernard-Termès, whose late-twentieth-century angel books popularized a guardian-angel version of the seventy-two-name system.

**[Modern esoteric tradition]** In this literature Hakamiah becomes a personal or birth guardian associated with leadership, loyalty, honor, protection from betrayal, and a continuous June birth-period—commonly June 5–9 or June 6–10 depending on the calendar convention.

This popular system descends mainly through Lenain and Ambelain but transforms a five-degree astrological sector and five scattered annual days into a modern personality-based “birth angel.” It should not be retrojected into Rudd, Agrippa, or medieval Jewish Kabbalah.

## Crowley’s *777*

In columns CLV–CLXVI, Crowley distributes the first thirty-six Goetic spirits among the thirty-six daytime decans and spirits 37–72 among the corresponding nighttime decans.

For Virgo’s first decan, row/key scale 20:

- day spirit: Zepar, number 16;
- magical image: “A soldier in red apparel and armour”;
- night spirit: Alloces, number 52.

See [Crowley’s *777* tables](https://publicism.info/magic/full/thelema/777/index.html).

The first decan of Virgo is ruled by the Sun in the Golden Dawn decan sequence and corresponds to the Eight of Disks, *Prudence*.

### The “quinance” problem

**[Documented]** A decan is ten degrees; a quinance is five degrees. Crowley’s printed Goetic columns say “by day” and “by night,” not “first five degrees” and “second five degrees.”

**[Later reconstruction]** Some authors place the day demon in the first five degrees and the night demon in the second five. That produces:

- Zepar: 0°–5° Virgo;
- Alloces: 5°–10° Virgo.

But this is a rationalized later scheme. The secure Crowley attribution is **the entire first Virgo decan by day**.

### Why Nine of Swords appears online

Hakamiah is angel number 16. In Lenain’s Aries-starting scheme, angel 16 falls at 15°–20° Gemini, within the second Gemini decan, Moon in Gemini, associated with the Nine of Swords.

Some modern tables then copy that Gemini/Nine-of-Swords sector directly onto demon 16, Zepar.

**[Contested-material finding]** This number-for-number splice is not Crowley’s *777* attribution. Crowley gives Zepar the first Virgo decan and therefore the Eight of Disks.

## Enochian and later overlays

Crowley included Enochian versions of the *Goetia*’s general conjurations and integrated the spirits into his broader tables.

**[Documented]** This is a ritual-language overlay.

**[Negative finding]** No early manuscript gives Zepar:

- an Enochian proper name;
- a unique Enochian tablet square;
- a Watchtower angle;
- a Dee/Kelley sigil.

Claims that Zepar is “an Enochian spirit” confuse Crowley’s translation of the calling formula with the historical origin of the entity.

Modern Qliphothic spheres, path assignments, incense, direction, candle color, enn, and “demonic tarot” correspondences vary by author and are not part of the early chain.

# Iconography

## The manuscript seal

The earliest securely attested Zepar image is not a bodily portrait but the *Goetia* seal. Its authoritative status is textual: the manuscript says Zepar obeys when shown the character.

**[Tradition]** The seal is both identifier and instrument of constraint.

**[Historical qualification]** Nothing establishes that it is an ancient sign used by Solomon. It appears in surviving late-seventeenth-century manuscripts, while Weyer’s earlier entry lacks it.

## Collin de Plancy

### First edition, 1818

Collin de Plancy first published the *Dictionnaire infernal* in 1818. The editions changed extensively over his lifetime.

**[Negative finding]** I could not locate a Zepar headword or portrait in the searchable 1818 two-volume edition. The 1818 edition should therefore not be cited as the source of Zepar’s famous engraved portrait. See the [1818 edition index](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/1re_%C3%A9dition,_1818).

### Sixth edition, 1863

The sixth edition, p. 712, contains a **Zépar** entry and a Louis Le Breton illustration.

A short exact excerpt reads:

> “Zépar, grand-duc de l’empire infernal … sous ce nom de Zépar, il a la forme d’un guerrier.”

See [Collin de Plancy’s Zépar entry](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.,_1863/Z%C3%A9par) and the [1863 facsimile](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Dictionnaire_infernal.pdf).

### De Plancy’s changes

**[Documented]**

- calls him a great duke of the infernal empire;
- suggests that Zépar might be identical with Vépar or Sépar;
- gives him a warrior’s form;
- says he impels men toward “infamous passions”;
- gives twenty-eight legions.

This differs from Weyer and the *Goetia*:

| Matter | Earlier sources | De Plancy |
|---|---|---|
| Target of erotic influence | Women made to love men | Men driven toward morally condemned passions |
| Barrenness | Explicit | Omitted |
| Legions | 26 | 28 |
| Vepar identity | Clearly separate spirit | Suggested possible identity |
| Tone | Catalogue of magical offices | Catholic moralizing vocabulary |

### Portrait

**[Documented visual description]** Le Breton represents Zepar as a conventionally human military figure rather than a zoological monster: a stern warrior’s head and upper body, helmeted and armored, emphasizing the martial form supplied by the text. The engraving does not independently depict the love or barrenness offices.

The sixth edition advertises portraits drawn by Louis Le Breton “according to formal documents.” That should be understood as nineteenth-century illustrative rhetoric: Le Breton was visualizing inherited descriptions, not copying an ancient portrait.

## Modern art

Modern Zepar art generally falls into four groups:

1. **Seal reproductions** based on Mathers–Crowley.
2. **Le Breton adaptations**, often digitally colored.
3. **Fantasy soldiers**, emphasizing red armor, swords, horns, or a ducal crown not specified in the entry.
4. **Erotic, feminine, and androgynous forms**, influenced by anime, games, demonolatry, or the love office.

**[Modern invention]** Wings, goat attributes, a red horse, a golden crown, forked tail, cat claws, and a specifically female body do not come from the Weyer–Scot–Goetia description. Some belong to later gaming or artistic systems.

The modern enn **“Lyan ramec catya Zepar”** belongs to recent demonolatry—widely propagated through S. Connolly’s publications—and is absent from Weyer, Scot, the *Goetia*, Rudd, and Crowley.

# People and works

## Principal transmitters

### Johann Weyer

**[Documented]** Weyer supplied the first exact printed Zepar, in 1577. His larger purpose was to undermine claims that alleged witches possessed the powers prosecutors attributed to them. His catalogue was not an uncomplicated endorsement of demon summoning.

### Reginald Scot

**[Documented]** Scot transmitted Weyer’s Zepar into English in 1584. The *Discoverie* likewise opposed witch persecution and exposed conjuring material partly to discredit it.

### The anonymous *Goetia* compiler

**[Reconstruction]** A late-seventeenth-century compiler drew heavily on Scot, rearranged the catalogue, supplied individual seals and elaborate conjurations, and integrated the work into the five-part *Lemegeton*.

The compiler is not known, and there is no evidence that Solomon, Weyer, Rudd, Mathers, or Crowley composed the original *Goetia*.

### “Dr. Rudd” and Peter Smart

**[Documented]** Harley 6483 says Peter Smart copied sheets of Dr. Rudd. The recension adds Cabalistic control structures, including the Shem-angel opposition.

**[Disputed]** Thomas Rudd’s personal authorship of every component remains unproved.

### Mathers and Crowley

Mathers produced the English edition published by Crowley in 1904. Crowley supplied an introduction, additional ritual material, Enochian renderings, and later the *777* tables.

**[Documented]** Neither created the name, number, red soldier, love office, barrenness, twenty-six legions, or basic seal.

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 edition collated the major manuscripts and identified dependencies on Weyer, Scot, Agrippa, Trithemius, pseudo-Peter of Abano, Paracelsian material, and other printed sources. His manuscript analysis is indispensable for separating early readings from Mathers’s alterations.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their 2007 *Goetia of Dr Rudd* foregrounded Harley 6483 and the angel-demon pairing. Their work is the principal modern source for the claim that Hakamiah is the spirit specifically set over Zepar.

## Practitioners and modern occult writers

### Poke Runyon

Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* integrates Goetic spirits, Shem angels, astrology, and dark-mirror practice. A later edition is described on [Runyon’s official book page](https://www.pokerunyon.com/books/the-book-of-solomons-magick-second-edition).

**[Modern practitioner synthesis]** His tabulation gives Zepar 15°–19° Gemini and Hakamiah, following the number-for-number angelic model rather than Crowley’s Virgo *777* decan.

**[Negative finding]** I found no adequately sourced public account of a specific Zepar operation by Runyon that could be treated as historical evidence rather than practice testimony.

### Lon Milo DuQuette and Christopher Hyatt

Their *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* (1992) is an influential practitioner-oriented presentation of Crowley’s edition.

**[Documented]** It reproduces the traditional Zepar description and discusses Goetic practice generally.

**[Negative finding]** It is not evidence for a distinct ancient Zepar tradition or a uniquely documented DuQuette–Zepar operation.

### Frater Achad

**[Negative finding]** No Zepar-specific teaching, ritual record, or correspondence securely attributable to Frater Achad was located. His Cabalistic work is relevant to Crowley’s intellectual environment but should not be inserted into Zepar’s history without a citation.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s *Geosophia*, *The True Grimoire*, and related writings argue for a broader spirit-working genealogy connecting late-antique, medieval, Renaissance, and popular magical traditions.

**[Scholarly-practitioner interpretation]** This is relevant to how modern occultists reconstruct Goetic ancestry.

**[Negative finding]** I did not locate a published Zepar-specific historical derivation by Stratton-Kent sufficient to replace the Taob/Coap/Weyer evidence.

### Owen Davies

Davies’s *Grimoires: A History of Magic Books* provides social history for magical manuscripts and print culture.

**[Contextual scholarship]** It helps explain transmission but does not establish a separate Zepar origin.

### Jan Veenstra and Julien Véronèse

Their research illuminates medieval ritual magic, *Almadel*, necromancy, and Solomonic textual traditions.

**[Contextual scholarship]** They are relevant to the larger ancestry of the *Lemegeton* but have not, in the material consulted, established a pre-Weyer demon named Zepar.

Véronèse’s observation that the medieval *Ars Notoria* reference to a “Lemogeton” may be fictitious is reported in Peterson’s [Lemegeton introduction](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm).

### Jean-Patrice Boudet

Boudet is directly relevant. He published and compared the *Livre des esperitz*, Weyer, and medieval demon catalogues, demonstrating a complex family of shared and independent notices. His work supports a medieval genealogy for Zepar’s *type of office*, but not the exact-name claim.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Does Zepar exist before Weyer?

**Finding:** not under that name.

- **[Documented]** Exact Zepar: Weyer, 1577.
- **[Reconstruction]** Taob and Coap preserve highly similar functions.
- **[Disputed]** They may be remote ancestors, displaced versions of the same notice, or independent members of a stock love-magic motif.
- **[Unsupported]** “Zepar is a fifteenth-century name for Taob” or “Coap is definitely Zepar.”

Absence of an exact pre-1577 attestation is presently the strongest defensible finding.

## 2. An ancient god behind Zepar

No ancient deity has been demonstrated.

- Zephyrus: modern resemblance-based identification.
- Astarte/Venus: symbolic connection through the ducal planet, not an etymology.
- Mars: suggested by armor, but not a name-origin.
- Baal, Amun, Asmodeus, Belial, Phoenix: no textual or linguistic connection.

**Verdict:** **[Unknown etymology]**, not a demonized ancient god presently recoverable from evidence.

## 3. Zepar versus Vepar/Separ

Weyer contains two distinct entries:

- **Zepar**, number 19: soldier, love, transformation, sterility, twenty-six legions.
- **Vepar**, also called **Separ**, number 33 in Weyer: mermaid, waters, armed ships, storms, putrefying wounds, twenty-nine legions.

The *Goetia* likewise separates Zepar 16 from Vepar 42.

**[Documented]** Collin de Plancy’s 1863 Zépar entry is the earliest located source explicitly suggesting they might be identical.

**[Disputed/error]** French and modern web lists that give “Zepar, Vepar, Separ” as simple aliases erase two demonstrably separate Weyer entries. **Separ belongs securely to Vepar**, not Zepar.

## 4. Twenty-six or twenty-eight legions?

- Weyer: 26.
- Scot: 26.
- *Goetia* manuscripts: 26.
- Mathers–Crowley: 26.
- Peterson: 26.
- De Plancy: 28.
- AD&D: 28 companies.

**Verdict:** twenty-six is the stable source-chain reading. Twenty-eight begins as de Plancy’s alteration or error and gains a second life in gaming.

## 5. Who changes shape?

Weyer’s Latin says Zepar changes **the women’s form**.

**[Modern misreading]** Some summaries say Zepar turns himself into the desired lover. That is grammatically unsupported.

**[Open interpretive question]** The text does not explain why changing the women’s appearance lets them enjoy their beloved. It may assume deception, magical beautification, disguise, or an omitted narrative step.

## 6. Is there a second manifestation?

No. Unlike several other Goetic spirits, Zepar is not said to arrive as one creature and then assume a human form on demand.

The “second form” often reported online is the same mistranslation of the women’s transformation clause.

## 7. Soldier versus red armored soldier

- Soldier: Weyer, 1577.
- Red apparel and explicit armor: surviving *Goetia* manuscript tradition, late seventeenth century.

**Verdict:** red is not part of the earliest notice, but it predates Mathers and Crowley.

## 8. Does Zepar make people fertile?

The early texts say the opposite: Zepar makes women sterile or barren.

**[Modern practitioner inversion]** Some occultists reason that a power to cause infertility entails a power to remove it, or reinterpret Zepar as governing reproductive choice. That may be a modern religious belief, but it is not stated in Weyer, Scot, or the *Goetia*.

## 9. Fallen angel and former choir

Weyer and the *Goetia* sometimes identify a spirit’s former angelic order. Zepar’s entry does not.

**[Modern generalization]** Calling Zepar “a fallen angel formerly of the Powers/Virtues” is not supported by his principal notice. It often results from copying data belonging to adjacent spirits or treating all Goetic spirits as biographically identical fallen angels.

## 10. Gemini/Nine of Swords versus Virgo/Eight of Disks

Both clusters exist, but they refer to different tables:

- Hakamiah number 16 in Lenain: 15°–20° Gemini; therefore within the Nine of Swords decan.
- Zepar in Crowley’s *777*: first Virgo decan; therefore Eight of Disks.

**Verdict:** “Zepar = Gemini/Nine of Swords according to Crowley’s *777*” is false. It conflates the Rudd–Lenain angelic sequence with Crowley’s daytime Goetic decans.

## 11. Hakamiah: Gemini or Libra?

- Lenain/Ambelain: 15°–20° Gemini.
- Golden Dawn angel paper: 15°–20° Libra.

**Verdict:** a genuine system difference caused by different zodiac starting points. Cite the system whenever giving the degrees.

## 12. Hakamiah’s Psalm: 87, 88:1, or 88:2?

These generally refer to the same opening line under:

- Vulgate numbering;
- Protestant numbering;
- different treatment of the Psalm title.

**Verdict:** numbering convention, not necessarily textual contradiction.

## 13. Hakamiah’s “meaning”

Possible answers include:

- “God who establishes the universe” — Lenain/Ambelain epithet.
- “Raise up, praying day and night” — Golden Dawn interpretive meaning.
- “Loyalty,” “honor,” “leadership” — later functional summaries.
- “God who raises” — plausible abbreviated modern translation.

None is simply the dictionary definition of the whole Hebrew form.

## 14. Rudd authorship

- Extant Harley 6483: securely 1712–13.
- Peter Smart copying “Dr. Rudd’s sheets”: manuscript claim.
- Thomas Rudd as ultimate author: traditional and plausible, but not fully proved.
- A 1655–56 original: Skinner and Rankine’s reconstruction.

**Verdict:** call it the “Rudd recension” or “material attributed to Dr. Rudd,” unless discussing the traditional authorship as such.

## 15. Modern enn, incense, color, direction, and offerings

The common Zepar enn, green or red candles, sandalwood incense, southern direction, wine, roses, copper jewelry, and specific offerings belong to modern demonolatry and eclectic ritual practice.

**[Modern tradition]** They may be meaningful within those religions.

**[Historical finding]** They are absent from Zepar’s Weyer, Scot, and manuscript descriptions. Copper and Venus have an older rank-based basis; the rest require author-specific citations.

## 16. “Red horse” and crown

Neither Weyer nor the *Goetia* puts Zepar on a horse or gives him a crown. Those features may be borrowed from nearby Sallos or Berith, or from fantasy art.

**[Modern invention/conflation]**

# Open questions

1. **What did the name mean to Weyer or his source?**  
   No gloss survives.

2. **Did Weyer’s lost *Liber officiorum spirituum* already spell Zepar exactly?**  
   Probable enough to consider, impossible presently to prove.

3. **How are Taob, Coap, Gaap/Tap, and Zepar related?**  
   The offices show textual movement, but the name relationships do not produce a single unambiguous stemma.

4. **Why does the transformation concern women’s form?**  
   The catalogue supplies no narrative explanation.

5. **Who added red apparel and armor?**  
   They occur before the surviving *Goetia* manuscripts’ final copies but not in Weyer or Scot; the intermediate source is lost.

6. **Where did Zepar’s seal originate?**  
   It is first visible in the *Goetia* manuscript family. No pre-*Goetia* exemplar has been identified.

7. **Can every seal variant be genealogically ordered?**  
   Only through a new high-resolution palaeographical comparison of Sloane 3825, 3648, 2731, Harley 6483, Northwestern 65, and related witnesses.

8. **Why did de Plancy change twenty-six legions to twenty-eight?**  
   No explanation is given. Misprint, memory, altered source, or deliberate revision are all possible.

9. **Why did de Plancy propose Zepar = Vepar/Separ?**  
   Probably name resemblance; the functions strongly contradict the identification.

10. **Was Thomas Rudd personally responsible for the Hakamiah pairing?**  
    The extant manuscript attributes sheets to Dr. Rudd, but the complete authorial and scribal history remains unsettled.

11. **Did the *Goetia* compiler deliberately aim for seventy-two spirits to match the Shem?**  
    The numerical fit is suggestive, but no compiler’s explanation survives.
