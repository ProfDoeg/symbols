The most important correction is that three systems commonly collapsed on modern websites are actually distinct:

1. Seere’s **rank** gives Jupiter and tin.
2. Crowley’s **Goetic decan arrangement** gives Seere 0°–10° Pisces, night, and the Eight of Cups.
3. Jabamiah’s **Shem quinance** gives 15°–20° Pisces, which falls within the Nine of Cups decan only if the Golden Dawn tarot scheme is superimposed afterward.

## The name and its origins

### 1. Documentary sequence of spellings

| Date/source | Form | Evidence |
|---|---|---|
| 1577, Weyer | — | Seere is absent. |
| 1584, Scot | — | Seere is absent. |
| Seventeenth century, Sloane 3824 working material | Apparently **Seere** | The short proto-entry is identified by Peterson as a possible immediate source for the later *Goetia*. Its character is written very small. |
| Late seventeenth century, Sloane 2731 | **Seere**, with **Seer** in a related copy | Manuscript variation. |
| Seventeenth century, Sloane 3825 | **Seere** | Principal reading followed by Peterson. |
| 1712–1713 copy, Harley 6483 | **Seer** | Peter Smart’s copy of material attributed to Thomas Rudd. |
| Sloane 3648 and Northwestern MS 65 | **Seer** | Additional witnesses tabulated by Peterson. |
| Wellcome MS 3203 | **Seer**, numbered 50 in that manuscript’s list | A collateral ordering, not the standard 72-spirit order. |
| 1904, Mathers–Crowley | **Seere, Sear, or Seir** | First influential printing of the three-form heading. |
| 1911, Waite | **Seere** | Dependent printed reception. |
| 2001, Peterson | **Seere** | Editorial headword; variants reported in notes. |
| Modern occult writing | **Seire**, occasionally other phonetic forms | Secondary normalization or error; not strong evidence for the original name. |

Peterson’s comparative name table is particularly useful because it prevents the printed Mathers variants from being projected backward into Weyer or the earliest manuscripts.

### 2. No demonstrated ancient deity, hero, or place

**Scholarly finding:** no documented chain connects Seere to an ancient god, hero, ethnonym, or place. Unlike Baal, Astaroth/Astarte, Amon/Amun, Asmoday/Ashmedai, Belial, or Phoenix, Seere presently lacks a demonstrable ancient antecedent.

That conclusion is stronger than merely saying that the etymology is “unknown.” The evidentiary problem is chronological:

- The name is absent from Weyer and Scot.
- It has not been located in the *Livre des Esperitz*, the Munich necromancer’s handbook, the surviving Abramelin lists, or the *Testament of Solomon*.
- Its first presently identifiable textual environment is the seventeenth-century formation of the *Lemegeton*.
- The variants most suggestive of an ancient comparison—especially **Seir**—become prominent only in the 1904 printed edition.

### 3. Proposed derivations

#### Seir, Edom, and the “hairy” or goat-demon words

Biblical **Seir** is Hebrew שֵׂעִיר, *śēʿîr*. It can designate the mountainous territory associated with Edom and is related to a word for something hairy or shaggy, including a he-goat. The plural *śeʿîrîm* is used in passages traditionally interpreted as goat-like wilderness beings or demons.

**Disputed reconstruction:** because Mathers printed **Seir**, some modern writers identify Seere with Mount Seir, Edom, goat demons, or an Edomite deity.

The evidence against treating that as established etymology is substantial:

- Hebrew *Seir* contains the consonantal sequence sin–ayin–yod–resh.
- Crowley’s later Hebrew transliteration for Seere is שאר, shin–aleph–resh.
- The form **Seir** is not securely the earliest reading.
- No manuscript genealogy connects the spirit to Edom or the biblical *śeʿîrîm*.
- The Goetic figure is a beautiful mounted man, not a goat or shaggy wilderness being.

Thus the resemblance is real enough to inspire later interpretation, but a historical derivation remains unproved.

#### English “seer”

**Disputed folk etymology:** *Seer* is an actual manuscript spelling, and the spirit reports hidden things, thefts, and treasure. It is therefore tempting to derive the name from English *seer*, “one who sees.”

No manuscript explains the name this way, however. The office could have encouraged a copyist to normalize an unfamiliar *Seere* as *Seer*, or the word could be the original name; the surviving evidence does not decide between those possibilities.

#### “Sear”

**Documented variant, unexplained origin:** *Sear* appears in Mathers–Crowley alongside *Seere* and *Seir*. There is no evidence that the spirit’s name derives from the English verb *to sear*. It may be an attempted phonetic normalization, a reading of a manuscript hand, or an editorial expansion of the variant set.

#### Hebrew שאר in *777*

**Esoteric transliteration, not etymology:** Crowley’s table gives a Hebrew spelling corresponding approximately to *Sh-A-R*. This is best understood as a retroversion—rendering the already existing magical name in Hebrew letters. It does not establish an ancient Hebrew source.

The Hebrew word שער, *shaʿar*, “gate,” sometimes invoked in modern speculation, is spelled differently. Claims that Seere means “gate,” “speed,” or “one who travels” in Hebrew are consequently unsupported.

#### Other modern proposals

Web and practitioner literature also connects Seere with Pegasus or Bellerophon, Sirius, Osiris, an alleged Akkadian word for “liar,” Qos or Dushara, and astral travel.

- **Iconographic analogy:** Pegasus resembles the winged horse, but no Goetic source names Pegasus.
- **Modern invention or unsupported conjecture:** the Sirius, Osiris, Akkadian, Qos, Dushara, and “gate” derivations lack a documented textual chain.
- **Later interpretive association:** astral travel extrapolates from Seere’s extreme speed and worldwide movement.

### 4. Earliest attestation

**Best reconstructable conclusion:** the earliest known Seere material belongs to the seventeenth-century *Lemegeton* complex, probably the short notice in Sloane 3824, fol. 114v, from which the fuller entries may have developed.

Peterson cautiously says the *Goetia* entry was “possibly drawn” from that notice. That is not the same as proving Sloane 3824 was the original composition: it may itself copy an unknown source.

Sloane 3824 is a layered working collection, associated with Elias Ashmole, rather than a clean authorial manuscript. Dating individual material inside it is therefore harder than dating the bound volume. Sloane 2731 carries a note dated 18 January 1686; Harley 6483 was copied in 1712–1713.

**Absence finding:** there is no presently demonstrated pre-1577 attestation. More significantly, there is no 1577 Weyer attestation either. The presently recoverable history begins approximately a century later than Weyer’s catalogue.

## The description across the sources

### 1. Weyer, 1577

Johann Weyer appended the *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* to the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum* in 1577. Earlier editions of *De praestigiis* appeared from 1563, which explains the frequently repeated but inaccurate claim that the demon catalogue itself dates to 1563.

Weyer supplies 69 entries in the conventional enumeration. The list finishes with Phoenix and Stolas.

**Documented absence:** there is no Seere lemma in Weyer, and therefore no Latin Seere description to quote. Any purported “Weyer Latin” for Seere is either a back-translation of the *Goetia* or a misattribution.

Weyer says his catalogue came from a *Liber officiorum spirituum*, elsewhere described as the *Empto. Salomonis*. Trithemius mentioned a work with a related title in his 1508 *Antipalus maleficiorum*, but the precise manuscript Weyer used has not survived.

### 2. Scot, 1584

Reginald Scot translated or adapted Weyer’s catalogue in Book XV of *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, chapter 2, conventionally cited at pp. 216–226 of the 1584 edition.

**Documented absence:** Scot has no Seere entry and consequently supplies no English Seere passage. Scot also omits Weyer’s fourth spirit, Pruflas, even though his introductory arithmetic is itself inconsistent.

It would therefore be misleading to print the later English *Goetia* description beneath either Weyer's or Scot’s name.

### 3. The change from 69 to 72

The standard reconstruction is:

- Weyer: 69 spirits.
- Scot’s transmitted list: effectively 68 entries because Pruflas is omitted.
- *Ars Goetia*: retains that omission and adds four spirits—Vassago, Seere, Dantalion, and Andromalius.
- Total: 69 − 1 + 4 = 72.

Vassago is inserted as number 3, causing most subsequent numbers to shift relative to Weyer. Seere, Dantalion, and Andromalius form the new conclusion at 70–72.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** the compiler probably used Abraham Fleming’s English transmission in Scot rather than independently translating Weyer. The shared omission and other verbal features support that conclusion.

### 4. Sloane 3824: the short precursor

The short Sloane 3824 notice already contains the substance later expanded in the *Goetia*:

- Seere is under the king of the East.
- He produces sudden effects.
- He carries and returns things.
- He traverses the world in a moment.
- He reports thefts and treasure trove.
- He is described as moderately good, mild, courteous, and affable.

**Documented text with editorial caution:** this is the likeliest immediate precursor, but “source” should remain qualified because both texts could descend from a lost common exemplar.

### 5. Sloane 2731 and Sloane 3825

The fuller entry makes Seere the seventieth spirit and calls him a mighty prince under Amaymon, king of the East. Its narrative can be reconstructed clause by clause:

1. He appears as a beautiful man.
2. He rides a strong horse furnished with wings.
3. He travels and returns.
4. He produces events suddenly.
5. He transports anything wherever desired.
6. He can traverse the whole world almost instantaneously.
7. He gives a true report concerning thefts.
8. He reports hidden treasure.
9. He answers concerning other things.
10. He is comparatively good-natured and willing to obey.
11. He governs 26 legions.

A short exact specimen of the manuscript-based text is:

> “a Beautiful Man, riding on a strong horse with wings”

The full diplomatic transcription is available in Peterson’s online edition. The excerpt above is representative; the numbered analysis supplies every operative clause without silently modernizing “report of thefts” into “causes theft.”

### 6. Harley 6483 and the Rudd recension

The Harley manuscript begins the entry:

> “The Seventieth Spirit is Seer”

The remainder closely follows the established description: a beautiful man on a winged horse, extreme speed, sudden accomplishment, transportation, reports of thefts and concealed treasure, 26 legions, and a tractable character.

The significant Ruddian addition is not a new physical form or office. It is the angelic apparatus placed around the demon:

- Seer receives a corresponding Shem angel, Jabamiah.
- The angelic name and biblical text belong on the protective side of the lamen.
- The demonic character faces outward.
- The angelic side is oriented toward the magician’s heart.
- The angelic name is intended to constrain, check, or counterbalance the spirit.
- Rudd’s brass vessel likewise invokes the collective authority of the 72 angels over the 72 spirits.

**Tradition:** this changes the ritual theology. The practitioner no longer relies only on divine names and coercive conjuration; each demon is also paired with a specific angelic superior.

### 7. Mathers–Crowley, 1904

The printed heading is **“SEERE, SEAR, or SEIR.”** The entry appears at pp. 64–65 of the original printing. A representative exact excerpt is:

> “He appeareth in the Form of a Beautiful Man, riding upon a Winged Horse”

Mathers’s text says that Seere:

- is the seventieth spirit;
- is a mighty prince;
- is under Amaymon, king of the East;
- appears as a beautiful man on a winged horse;
- travels and returns;
- causes things to happen suddenly;
- transports anything to any desired location;
- can pass over the entire earth in the twinkling of an eye;
- gives true information concerning thefts;
- discovers hidden treasure;
- gives information concerning “many other things,” including an “abundance of things” in this printed recension;
- is of an accommodating or good nature;
- obeys the exorcist willingly;
- commands 26 legions.

Two seals are printed on p. 64 as figures 120 and 121, “Seal of Seere (1)” and “Seal of Seere (2).”

**Textual difference:** the wording about an “abundance of things” is stronger and less precise than the Sloane reading about “all other things.” It should not automatically be read as a separate office of magically creating material abundance.

### 8. Peterson, 2001

Peterson prints Seere on p. 38 of *The Lesser Key of Solomon*. His editorial base is Sloane 3825, with readings from Sloane 2731, Harley 6483, the 1904 edition, and related manuscripts.

Peterson’s notes establish several points often lost in popular summaries:

- the manuscript variants include *Seere* and *Seer*;
- Mathers adds *Sear* and *Seir*;
- Seere is absent from Weyer;
- the final spirits Dantalion and Andromalius are absent from Weyer as well;
- Sloane 3824 contains a plausible shorter antecedent.

### 10. What the text does not say

The historical sources do **not** say that Seere:

- assumes a second shape on command;
- is Pegasus, Bellerophon, or a rider from Greek mythology;
- steals objects for the magician;
- guarantees romantic love;
- teaches languages or liberal sciences;
- grants invisibility;
- performs alchemical transmutation;
- rules a particular modern demonolatry element;
- has a historically transmitted “enn”;
- is uniquely benevolent or safe;
- has a special Enochian name.

Those are either confusions with other spirits or later extrapolations.

## The angel: Jabamiah

## 1. The 72-fold name

The three Hebrew verses Exodus 14:19–21 each contain 72 letters in the consonantal text used by the tradition. The standard construction places:

- verse 19 in ordinary order;
- verse 20 in reverse order;
- verse 21 in ordinary order.

Reading the resulting columns produces 72 three-letter groups. This boustrophedon arrangement is the basis of the later “72 Names.”

The seventieth triplet is **יבם**, conventionally transliterated *YBM*. Christian and practical kabbalistic traditions expand the triad by adding a divine suffix, yielding **יבמיה**, rendered:

- Iabamiah
- Jabamiah
- Yabamiah
- occasionally other Latinized vocalizations

**Historical distinction:** the three-letter sequence is derived from Exodus. The fully vocalized angelic person, complete with office, zodiacal jurisdiction, verse, choir, moral qualities, and guardian dates, is a later development.

### 2. Meaning

Lenain gives Jabamiah’s attribute as:

> “Verbe qui produit toutes choses”

That is, **“the Word that produces all things.”**

**Esoteric meaning:** this is the angel’s theological or magical attribute, not a literal dictionary translation of the Hebrew consonants יבם.

The Hebrew root formed by these consonants is associated lexically with levirate marriage. No evidence shows that the angelic tradition intended that lexical meaning. Treating “Jabamiah” as straightforward Biblical Hebrew is therefore misleading.

### 3. The verse: Genesis, not Psalms

The verse assigned to Jabamiah is Genesis 1:1:

> “In the beginning God created the heaven and the earth.”

Harley 6483 supplies the Latin approximately as:

> “In principio creavit Deus Caelum et Terrum”

The Vulgate form is *In principio creavit Deus caelum et terram*. The manuscript’s *Terrum* appears to be an error.

**Documented anomaly:** this is the only text in Rudd’s Goetic angel series identified by Skinner and Rankine as not being taken from Psalms. Modern tables that automatically call every Shem angel’s supporting text a “Psalm verse” obscure the exception.

Some editors speculate that Genesis 1:1 may reflect an earlier position for Jabamiah at the beginning or end of a cyclic arrangement. That is an editorial hypothesis, not a proven lost ordering.

### 4. Five degrees of the zodiac

Lenain assigns Jabamiah rays 346 through 350 of the zodiac.

Converted into ordinary sign notation:

- absolute longitude 345°–350° corresponds to **15°–20° Pisces**;
- in older ordinal language, the 346th through 350th degrees are sometimes printed as **16°–20° Pisces**.

These expressions describe the same five-degree sector under different counting conventions.

Jabamiah is also associated by Lenain with:

- the thirty-fifth decan or “decade”;
- the decan spirit **Thopibui**;
- a daily period of approximately **11:00–11:20 p.m.**;
- the holy name **Aris** in the “Boeotian” language;
- five annual days: **28 May, 8 August, 19 October, 30 December, and 12 March**.

The five dates are part of Lenain’s cyclical guardian calendar. They should not be confused with the continuous five-degree zodiac sector or with later birthday systems.

### 5. Jabamiah’s offices in Lenain

Lenain attributes to Jabamiah:

- generation or production of beings and natural phenomena;
- restoration and regeneration;
- restoration of the harmony disrupted through Adam;
- philosophical genius or elevated philosophical understanding.

The contrary or adverse genius is said to govern:

- atheism;
- dangerous or corrupting writings;
- literary controversy;
- aggressive criticism.

**Tradition, not historical biography:** these are nineteenth-century occult attributes, not statements from Exodus or early rabbinic angelology.

### 6. Lenain’s Jabamiah legend

Lenain’s footnote, invoking the *Comte de Gabalis* environment, elaborates a legend of eternal fecundity and cosmic restoration. Jabamiah becomes the divine word through which disrupted nature may be renewed. The associated story links the name with a fantastic account of restoration after Noah and says that later cabalists greet or recognize one another through Jabamiah.

**Legend:** this belongs to early-modern and nineteenth-century occult literary mythology. It is not an attested Jewish account of the Biblical flood and should not be backdated to ancient Kabbalah.

### 7. From *Sefer Raziel* to Kircher

A careful history requires separating the textual strata:

1. **Biblical substrate:** Exodus 14:19–21 supplies the letters.
2. **Jewish mystical development:** medieval discussions develop several “explicit names” of 12, 42, or 72 letters. *Shem ha-meforash* originally means the explicit or distinguished divine name and does not always mean the same 72-part system.
3. **Sefer Raziel tradition:** medieval composite versions transmit the 72 groups and multiple vocalization practices.
4. **Reuchlin:** *De arte cabalistica* (1517) introduces Christian readers to 72-fold divine-name speculation.
5. **Blaise de Vigenère:** sixteenth-century material transmits angelic names and seal forms later useful to Christian ceremonial magic.
6. **Athanasius Kircher:** *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–1654) helps systematize the angel names and zodiacal order in a highly syncretic Christian-Egyptian-kabbalistic framework.
7. **Rudd:** the seventeenth-century magical system pairs those 72 angels, by number, with the 72 spirits of the *Ars Goetia*.
8. **Lenain:** *La science cabalistique* (1823) publishes a detailed operative calendar and moral/theological attributes.
9. **Papus:** *La Cabbale* (1903) reproduces and popularizes much of Lenain’s structure.
10. **Golden Dawn and Crowley:** integrate Shem, tarot, decans, Hebrew letters, planets, and ceremonial magic, although Crowley’s Goetic decan table does not preserve the one-to-one five-degree angel pairing.
11. **Ambelain:** *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) largely repeats and reorganizes Lenain.
12. **Haziel:** late twentieth-century angel books convert the system into individualized guardian-angel devotion and psychological or spiritual self-help.

### 8. Ibn Ezra’s objection and alternative

Not every Jewish exegete accepted the familiar three-verse column method. Abraham ibn Ezra discussed a different understanding of the “72-letter” name: the total 72 can be obtained by adding progressive written forms of the Tetragrammaton—10, 15, 21, and 26.

**Historical controversy:** the modern popular expression “the 72 names derived from three verses of 72 letters” describes an influential esoteric construction, not an uncontested doctrine of Judaism.

### 9. Rudd’s pairing

Rudd’s principle is numerical and systematic:

- demon 1 receives angel 1;
- demon 2 receives angel 2;
- …
- demon 70, Seer, receives angel 70, Jabamiah.

The lamen is effectively double-sided. The demonic seal faces the spirit; the angelic name and scriptural authority face inward toward the operator.

**Documented magical theory:** the angel is not merely a pleasant “guardian correspondence.” It is an instrument of constraint. The divine or angelic power is placed between the magician and the spirit, limiting the latter’s operation.

**Dating caution:** Harley 6483 was copied by Peter Smart in 1712–1713. Skinner and Rankine reconstruct an earlier Rudd original around 1655–1656. That reconstruction is plausible but remains editorial; the surviving manuscript is early eighteenth century.

### 10. Papus, Ambelain, and Haziel

Papus retains the essentials:

- Jabamiah as number 70;
- the 346th–350th degrees;
- Genesis 1:1;
- generation and regeneration;
- atheism and dangerous writing as contrary qualities.

Ambelain’s *Practical Kabbalah* repeats much of Lenain’s material, including the five dates, late-night twenty-minute interval, Genesis verse, philosophical qualities, and regenerative office.

Haziel’s modern angel literature assigns Jabamiah to approximately **6–10 March** and emphasizes “transmutations,” purification, regeneration, and transformation.

**Modern development:** Haziel’s birthday-guardian presentation is not identical to Lenain’s scattered five-day calendar and should not be cited as if it were Rudd’s system.

### 2. Why each tarot pip carries two spirits

Crowley organizes the Goetic catalogue as two consecutive sets of 36:

- spirits 1–36: day or ascending half;
- spirits 37–72: night half.

Each ten-degree decan consequently carries one day spirit and one night spirit. Seere is number 70, corresponding to number 34 in the first half because 70 − 36 = 34.

Therefore:

- **Furfur 34**: day spirit of 0°–10° Pisces;
- **Seere 70**: night spirit of 0°–10° Pisces;
- both correspond to the **Eight of Cups**.

The adjacent pairs are:

| Pisces decan | Day spirit | Night spirit | Tarot pip |
|---|---|---|---|
| 0°–10° Pisces | Furfur 34 | Seere 70 | Eight of Cups |
| 10°–20° Pisces | Marchosias 35 | Dantalion 71 | Nine of Cups |
| 20°–30° Pisces | Stolas 36 | Andromalius 72 | Ten of Cups |

This distribution appears in *777*, published in 1909, not in the 1904 *Goetia* proper.

### 3. Eight of Cups versus Nine of Cups

Both associations appear online, but they result from different calculations:

- Start with **Seere’s Goetic number in Crowley’s 36 × 2 scheme**: Eight of Cups.
- Start with **Jabamiah’s 15°–20° Pisces quinance**, then superimpose Golden Dawn tarot decans: Nine of Cups.

Neither value proves the other false. The mistake is claiming that both arise from the same historical table.

### 4. Astrological timing

The manuscript tradition gives broad ritual timing by rank rather than an individual Seere election:

- princes/prelates may be called during any daylight hour;
- the moon should be waxing;
- preferred lunar ages are 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 days.

Later practice adds several incompatible layers:

- **Crowley/Golden Dawn decan:** work during 0°–10° Pisces, conventionally around 19–28 February in tropical astrology.
- **Jabamiah quinance:** 15°–20° Pisces, approximately 5/6–10 March depending on year and boundary convention.
- **Lenain daily angelic time:** 11:00–11:20 p.m.
- **Lenain annual cycle:** 28 May, 8 August, 19 October, 30 December, and 12 March.
- **Haziel guardian dates:** usually 6–10 March.
- **Planetary rank election:** Jupiter’s day and hour may be favored because Seere is a prince, although this is a later practical synthesis rather than an individual instruction in his entry.

### 5. Direction and Amaymon

Seere is said to be under **Amaymon, king of the East**.

Yet influential directional catalogues often place:

- Oriens in the east;
- Paymon/Paimon in the west;
- Egyn or Ariton in the north;
- Amaymon in the south.

Other manuscripts rearrange these rulers.

**Documented inconsistency:** “Amaymon, King of the East” is the text’s actual wording and should not be silently corrected. Whether it reflects an alternative directional system, contamination between royal lists, or a copying error is unresolved.

### 6. Enochian overlays

Crowley’s 1904 volume includes Goetic conjurations rendered into the “Magical or Angelike Language,” derived from the Enochian records of John Dee and Edward Kelley.

**Later overlay:** this is a translation or adaptation of generic ritual material. It does not provide Seere with a unique Enochian name, tablet square, aethyr, or governor.

Later authors, particularly in the Runyon and DuQuette/Hyatt reception, integrate Goetic evocation with Enochian-style architecture, directional watchtowers, skrying, or comparative angelology. These are creative ceremonial syntheses rather than discoveries in Sloane 3825.

### 7. The modern “enn”

The formula commonly printed for Seere is:

> “Jeden et Renich Seere tu tasa”

**Modern demonolatry:** it was circulated through late twentieth- and early twenty-first-century demonolatry material, especially literature associated with S. Connolly and sources attributed to Valerie Corban. It is neither in the Sloane manuscripts nor demonstrably Enochian. Its language and antiquity have not been independently established.

## Iconography

### 1. Manuscript seals

The seals are evidence in their own right and should not be reduced to modern redrawings.

#### Sloane 3824

The short precursor has a very small character associated with the Seere material. Its scale and working-note context make fine comparison difficult.

#### Sloane 3825

This witness supplies the main seal used in Peterson’s edition. In broad visual terms it is:

- bilaterally spread and somewhat wing-like;
- arranged around a dominant central upright;
- composed of angular and curved lateral strokes;
- terminated by hooks, curls, or small returning bends;
- drawn as a free linear character rather than a representational picture.

This description is only an orientation. The precise number, curvature, junction, and direction of individual strokes must be taken from the facsimile.

#### Sloane 2731

The same underlying design is recognizable, but the proportions, curvature, and terminal details vary. Such variation is typical of sigils copied freehand.

#### Harley 6483

The Rudd design is not merely the demonic character in isolation. It belongs to a circular, double-sided angel–demon lamen:

- the demonic name or character belongs to the outward face;
- the Shem angel and verse belong to the protective inward face;
- Hebrew lettering and surrounding circles make the object structurally different from the later isolated internet sigil.

#### Mathers–Crowley

The 1904 printing gives two versions, figures 120 and 121. Both retain the spread or wing-like silhouette but differ in:

- angular versus rounded rendering;
- the shape and height of the central construction;
- the placement and direction of curls or hooks;
- lateral symmetry and proportions;
- how crossings and adjoining strokes are resolved.

**Documented fact:** there is no single mechanically exact “original Seere sigil.” The tradition contains a family of hand-copied forms. Mathers’s decision to print two seals openly records that instability.

### 2. Seal material and use

The general rubric says that the seal of a prince or prelate should be made of **tin**, the metal of Jupiter. It is worn as a lamen during conjuration.

Modern tattoos, jewelry, laser engravings, and digital vector files usually simplify one of the 1904 designs. Claims that a particular hook “means speed,” that the two sides depict wings, or that the central stroke encodes a crossroads are **modern symbolic readings**; the manuscripts supply no such interpretation.

### 3. Collin de Plancy

Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* first appeared in 1818. The famous 1863 sixth edition contains Louis Le Breton’s engraved demon portraits.

**Documented absence:** Seere has no entry or portrait in the relevant 1863 alphabetical section. Consequently there is no authentic “Louis Le Breton portrait of Seere.”

This absence is unsurprising: de Plancy drew extensively upon the Weyer-derived demonological catalogue, and Seere is not a Weyer spirit.

Modern shops and image posts occasionally label generic winged-horse or aristocratic demon art as “from the *Dictionnaire infernal*.” Unless a page and engraving can be produced, such attributions are false.

### 4. Modern visual traditions

Modern Seere art tends to choose among four motifs:

- a beautiful young prince on a Pegasus-like horse;
- an airborne courier or psychopomp;
- a Jupiterian royal figure in blue, purple, or metallic tones;
- an androgynous or anime-styled mounted spirit.

These are **modern illustrations of the verbal description**, not recoveries of a lost historical portrait.

Kazuma Kaneko’s *Megami Tensei* design belongs to this modern reception: Seere is presented through the game’s demon-compendium framework as a beautiful mounted figure associated with instantaneous movement and retrieval.

## People and works

## 1. Grimoire ancestors and parallels

### *Testament of Solomon*

The *Testament of Solomon* is a late-antique, composite pseudepigraphon in which Solomon interrogates demons, learns their functions and celestial opponents, and binds them through divine authority.

**Structural ancestor:** demon interviews, offices, forms, and counter-angels anticipate later Solomonic catalogues.

**Absence:** Seere does not appear by that name, and no individual demon in the *Testament* can presently be shown to be his direct precursor.

### Munich handbook, Clm 849

The fifteenth-century Munich manual edited by Richard Kieckhefer as *Forbidden Rites* contains necromantic operations, spirit names, circles, and conjurations.

**Historical context:** it demonstrates a late-medieval ritual environment from which later spirit catalogues developed.

**Absence:** no secure Seere occurs.

### *Livre des Esperitz*

The *Livre des Esperitz* is an important French catalogue of spirits, conventionally dated to the later medieval or early modern period. It contains many recognizable ancestors of Weyer and Goetic names.

Jean-Patrice Boudet and other scholars have shown its importance for the genealogy of demon catalogues. Julien Véronèse’s broader work places such texts within learned ritual magic rather than treating them as isolated folklore.

**Absence:** Seere is not among its securely identified spirits.

### *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

Weyer explicitly invokes a *Book of the Offices of Spirits*. Trithemius listed a related title in 1508. Several surviving catalogues may descend from this wider textual family.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** the *Ars Goetia* is part of this catalogue tradition.

**Disputed claim:** some modern occult historians speak as though the complete 72-spirit *Goetia*, including Seere, existed before 1508. The title evidence supports an earlier genre or ancestor, not the existence of Seere’s particular entry.

### *Book of Abramelin*

Abramelin’s spirit lists include familiar rulers such as Amaymon and many names shared with later magic.

**Absence:** Seere is not securely attested in the principal Abramelin lists.

## 2. Weyer

Johann Weyer was a physician and critic of witch persecution. His catalogue preserves ritual-demonological material while embedding it in an argument against indiscriminate witch trials.

**Relevance to Seere:** indirect but essential. Weyer supplied the core list later reshaped into the *Ars Goetia*. He did not include Seere.

## 3. Scot

Reginald Scot’s *Discoverie of Witchcraft* sought to expose fraud, criticize persecution, and make allegedly secret conjuring material publicly available.

**Relevance:** his English version appears to have mediated Weyer’s list to the *Goetia* compiler. Seere is not in Scot.

## 4. Elias Ashmole and the Sloane material

Sloane 3824 contains working materials associated with Elias Ashmole and the formation of the *Lemegeton*. The small Seere notice makes Ashmole’s manuscript environment important to the spirit’s first recoverable history.

It does not establish that Ashmole invented Seere or personally practiced an evocation.

## 5. Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

Harley 6483 attributes its system to “Dr. Rudd,” generally identified with Thomas Rudd. Peter Smart copied the surviving manuscript in 1712–1713.

Skinner and Rankine argue that the underlying work dates to approximately 1655–1656. Peterson is more cautious about the late and sometimes divergent Harley witness.

**Documented:** Peter Smart’s dated copying and the contents of Harley 6483.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Thomas Rudd’s authorship and the precise mid-seventeenth-century date of the lost exemplar.

## 6. Mathers and Crowley

S. L. MacGregor Mathers prepared the manuscript-derived translation and apparatus. Aleister Crowley edited and issued *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King* in 1904.

Their edition established:

- the influential heading *Seere, Sear, or Seir*;
- two publicly reproduced seal variants;
- the standard English prose known to twentieth-century occultists;
- Enochian translations of ritual material;
- Crowley’s preliminary invocation and ceremonial framing.

The 1904 edition is enormously influential but should not be treated as the earliest witness.

## 7. Crowley’s *777*

*777* was published in 1909. Its tables place Goetic spirits within Golden Dawn-style Hebrew, zodiacal, planetary, tarot, and day/night correspondences.

For Seere this produces:

- Pisces;
- night;
- the first decan;
- Eight of Cups;
- Saturn in Pisces for the decan;
- Jupiter for the princely rank.

This is a later tabular synthesis, not a reading found in the seventeenth-century entry.

## 8. Frater Achad

A dedicated search finds no well-attested Seere-specific operation, essay, or innovation by Charles Stansfeld Jones, Frater Achad.

**Negative finding:** his general importance to Crowleyan Qabalah does not justify placing him in Seere’s individual transmission without a citation.

## 9. A. E. Waite

Waite discussed and reproduced material from the Lesser Key in his surveys of ceremonial magic. His spelling **Seere** adds no independent early testimony.

## 10. Poke Runyon

Poke Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* develops a “Master Mandala” aligning the 72 Goetic spirits with 72 Shem angels and five-degree zodiacal sectors.

Runyon consequently aligns Seere directly with Jabamiah and 15°–20° Pisces.

**Modern ceremonial reconstruction:** Runyon openly presents a synthetic or “rectified” system. It is influential practice, not a transcript of the Sloane arrangement.

## 11. Lon Milo DuQuette, Christopher Hyatt, and David P. Wilson

*Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* restates Seere’s description and supplies modern ceremonial commentary. Its table follows the Crowley system:

- 0°–10° Pisces;
- night;
- Eight of Cups;
- Jupiter/tin by rank.

Its interpretive and Enochian discussions belong to twentieth-century practice rather than the manuscript’s original layer.

## 12. Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

*The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (2007) made the Harley 6483 system widely accessible. Its major contribution to Seere studies is the explicit restoration of:

- the spelling **Seer**;
- the paired angel Jabamiah;
- Genesis 1:1;
- the double-sided lamen;
- the theory of angelic constraint.

The editors’ reconstruction of Rudd’s authorship and dating is influential but should be identified as reconstruction.

## 13. Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 critical edition and continuously updated online materials remain fundamental because they compare manuscript witnesses, identify the Weyer/Scot dependency, record variants, and point to the Sloane 3824 precursor.

## 14. Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s *Encyclopaedia Goetica* and related writings relocate the grimoire within longer necromantic, classical, and spirit-catalogue traditions.

**Interpretive contribution:** a pagan and spirit-centered historical recontextualization of Goetia.

**Limitation:** no separate pre-Lemegeton Seere text has emerged from that reconstruction. Proposed ancient analogues remain comparative rather than genealogically demonstrated.

## 15. Owen Davies, Jan Veenstra, and Julien Véronèse

These scholars contribute broader historical controls:

- Owen Davies situates grimoires within manuscript, print, religious, and social history.
- Jan Veenstra examines learned magic and manuscript transmission.
- Julien Véronèse studies medieval ritual magic, Solomonic texts, and clerical necromancy.

None supplies an early Seere attestation. Their importance lies in preventing a seventeenth-century name from being projected backward merely because its host tradition has medieval ancestors.

## Modern literature, film, music, and games

### 1. Dante and Milton

**Documented absence:** Seere does not appear in Dante’s *Divine Comedy* or Milton’s *Paradise Lost*. Dante predates the surviving Seere tradition; Milton’s named infernal figures derive from other Biblical and classical demonologies.

### 2. Bartimaeus novels

Jonathan Stroud’s *Bartimaeus Sequence* uses Solomonic magic and includes *The Ring of Solomon*.

**Negative finding:** no reliable textual attestation of a character named Seere has been located. The series’ Solomonic setting alone is insufficient evidence.

### 3. *Shin Megami Tensei* and related games

Seere appears in the broader *Megami Tensei* franchise, including:

- *Devil Summoner: Soul Hackers*;
- *Shin Megami Tensei Trading Card: Card Summoner*;
- *Kētai Akuma Densho* material.

The *Soul Hackers* compendium describes a beautiful, blond or strikingly handsome mounted figure who can locate or obtain objects across great distances almost instantly.

**Modern adaptation:** the winged horse, speed, transport, and retrieval derive recognizably from the *Goetia*. The franchise supplies its own alignment, combat statistics, and character design.

**Persona qualification:** an appearance in the wider *Megami Tensei* franchise does not establish that Seere appears in a specific *Persona* title. No verified mainstream *Persona* appearance was found.

### 4. *Drakengard*

A central child character named Seere—Japanese セエレ—appears in *Drakengard* and *Drakengard 2*. He is associated with a pact, a golem, and unusual temporal circumstances.

**Probable allusion:** the series uses several names with occult or mythological resonances, making a Goetic naming source plausible. An explicit developer statement identifying the Goetic prince as the source has not been located.

### 5. *Final Fantasy XI*

**Prince Seere** is an enemy in the Dynamis–Xarcabard area of *Final Fantasy XI*.

**Attested modern borrowing:** the combination of title and name directly resembles the Goetic “Prince Seere,” although the game encounter does not reproduce the full winged-horse description.

### 6. *Helluva Boss* and *Hazbin Hotel*

These series adapt numerous Goetic names and ranks.

**Negative finding:** no canonical Seere character has been verified in released material through the date of this dossier. Fan characters, speculative lists, and confusion with **Sera** from *Hazbin Hotel* are not canonical evidence; Sera is a different name and character.

### 7. *Hellboy*

**Negative finding:** no verified canonical Seere appearance has been found in the principal *Hellboy* corpus. Generic online demon lists should not be treated as franchise documentation.

### 8. Film titled *Seire*

The Korean film *Seire* concerns a traditional postpartum taboo period.

**Unrelated work:** its title refers to the Korean custom, not demonstrably to the Goetic Seere.

### 9. Lera Auerbach’s *Goetia*

Composer Lera Auerbach’s large-scale *Goetia* engages the complete catalogue of 72 spirits and includes their names and variants.

**Attested artistic reception:** Seere participates as one of the enumerated Goetic names rather than as an independently dramatized character.

### 10. Modern occult art and commerce

Seere appears extensively in:

- sigil jewelry;
- tarot and oracle decks;
- demon encyclopedias;
- role-playing supplements;
- miniature sculpture;
- tattoo designs;
- AI and digital fantasy art.

Most such works derive from the 1904 seals and the winged-horse sentence. They are modern reception evidence, not independent witnesses to the spirit’s history.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. “Seere appears in Weyer”

**Verdict: false.**

Weyer contains no Seere. Scot consequently contains no Seere. Websites that describe Seere as “first recorded by Johann Weyer in 1577” have generalized from the ancestry of the *Goetia* as a whole.

### 2. “Weyer’s Latin and Scot’s English descriptions”

**Verdict: nonexistent.**

There is no omitted Latin passage waiting to be translated and no Scot English entry. The earliest known description is the seventeenth-century *Lemegeton* material.

### 3. “Seere dates to 1563”

**Verdict: unsupported twice over.**

The *Pseudomonarchia* itself belongs to the 1577 fifth edition of Weyer's book, not the original 1563 edition; Seere is absent even in 1577.

### 4. Was Seere invented to make 72?

**Open but plausible.**

The final 72 aligns the demon catalogue with the 72-fold divine name. Seere is one of four additions to the Weyer/Scot inheritance, and three additions occupy the final positions.

That supports deliberate numerical completion. It does not prove whether the compiler invented the names, borrowed them from lost material, or promoted working notes such as Sloane 3824.

### 5. Is the name a misprint?

**Unresolved.**

*Seere* and *Seer* may reflect copying or normalization. Mathers’s *Sear* and *Seir* may derive from manuscript readings or editorial speculation.

There is no evidence for a single, identifiable typographical accident that created the spirit. Because the name appears in manuscript before print, “a printer’s error in 1904” cannot explain its existence.

### 6. Seere versus biblical Seir

**Disputed identification.**

The comparison was encouraged by Mathers’s *Seir* spelling, but the Hebrew orthography, chronology, iconography, and absence of a transmission chain all count against a confident equation.

### 7. Seere versus the *seʿirim*

**Disputed conflation.**

The *seʿirim* are goat-like beings associated with wilderness cult or demonology in Biblical interpretation. Seere is a beautiful mounted prince. Phonetic resemblance through the late spelling *Seir* does not establish identity.

### 8. Pegasus and Bellerophon

**Modern iconographic conjecture.**

A beautiful man riding a winged horse naturally evokes Bellerophon on Pegasus, but no early text uses either name. The resemblance may help an illustrator; it is not etymological proof.

### 9. Is Seere “good”?

The text calls him “indifferent good Natured,” mild, courteous, affable, or willing, depending on the witness.

**Textual ambiguity:** early modern *indifferent* can mean reasonably, passably, or impartially. Later writers often omit it and call Seere wholly benevolent.

**Later tradition:** demonolatry frequently portrays him as unusually friendly or safe. That is an intensification of the manuscript’s comparative statement.

### 10. Does Seere steal?

**No, not in the surviving description.**

He gives a “true report of thefts” and hidden treasure. Later paraphrases convert this into stealing objects, aiding burglars, or magically retrieving stolen property. Retrieval is a plausible practical inference; active theft is not what the entry says.

### 11. Does he create abundance?

Mathers’s printed wording can be read as promising “abundance of things.”

**Textual dispute:** the manuscript wording is closer to answering about “all other things.” A separate wealth or abundance office may be an artifact of editorial phrasing.

### 12. Is there a second form?

**No documented second form.**

Some spirits appear first as animals or composites and must be commanded into human shape. Seere appears directly as a beautiful mounted man. No alternative form is specified.

### 13. Which seal is correct?

**No single answer.**

The manuscripts transmit freehand variants, and Mathers deliberately prints two. One can identify families of strokes, but declaring one modern vector redraw “the exact original seal” overstates the evidence.

### 14. Did de Plancy or Le Breton portray Seere?

**No.**

There is no Seere portrait in the famous 1863 illustrated edition. Alleged Le Breton images of Seere are later relabelings.

### 15. Is Amaymon eastern or southern?

**The Seere entry says eastern.** Other systems say southern. Both facts should be recorded.

Possible explanations include:

- a different directional recension;
- substitution between royal lists;
- a copying mistake;
- a compiler who did not enforce a single directional system.

No surviving authorial correction resolves it.

### 16. Is Rudd’s system authentically by Thomas Rudd?

**Disputed attribution.**

The surviving copy is Peter Smart’s early eighteenth-century manuscript. Skinner and Rankine argue for an underlying Thomas Rudd document from about 1655–1656. Peterson considers the Harley witness late and textually problematic in places.

The angelic pairing is unquestionably in Harley 6483; the exact authorship and date of its archetype remain less certain.

### 17. Is Jabamiah an ancient Jewish guardian angel?

**Misleading formulation.**

The consonantal triad comes from a medieval Jewish divine-name construction based on Exodus. The vocalized angel with horoscope, daily time, moral offices, contrary genius, and guardian dates belongs to Christian Cabala and later occultism.

### 18. Does Jabamiah have a Psalm?

**No: the assigned text is Genesis 1:1.**

Calling it a Psalm results from forcing the exceptional seventieth entry into the pattern used by the other angels.

### 19. What does “Jabamiah” mean?

“The Word that produces all things” is an **occult attribute** documented by Lenain. It is not a secure philological translation of יבם or יבמיה.

### 20. Which zodiac position is correct?

The question has no single answer until the system is specified:

- Jabamiah in Lenain/Rudd-derived angelology: 15°–20° Pisces.
- Seere in Crowley’s *777*: 0°–10° Pisces.
- Seere paired ordinally with Jabamiah in Runyon: 15°–20° Pisces.

Modern lists often merge all three.

### 21. Eight or Nine of Cups?

- Seere in Crowley’s Goetic table: **Eight of Cups**.
- Jabamiah’s five-degree sector, when mapped onto Golden Dawn decans: **Nine of Cups**.

Calling the Nine of Cups “Seere’s card in *777*” is incorrect.

### 22. Is Seere’s “enn” ancient or Enochian?

**No evidence supports either claim.**

It belongs to modern demonolatry literature. It is absent from the manuscripts, Weyer, Scot, Rudd, Lenain, the Golden Dawn, and Crowley’s Enochian translations.

## Open questions

1. **What is the precise date of the Sloane 3824 Seere notice?**  
   The codex is seventeenth century, but its layered construction makes a precise date for every entry difficult.

2. **Did Sloane 3824 originate the spirit or copy a lost source?**  
   Its compressed wording resembles a source note, but no antecedent has been identified.

3. **Which spelling is archetypal?**  
   *Seere* is the strongest developed manuscript reading; *Seer* is also early. *Sear* and *Seir* are securely influential only after Mathers.

4. **Was “Seer” semantic normalization?**  
   The divinatory office makes that possible, but no correction note proves it.

5. **Why was Seere selected as number 70?**  
   Completion of a 72-fold structure is likely, but the compiler’s source or creative process is unknown.

6. **Why does the entry place Amaymon in the east?**  
   No definitive stemmatic solution has been established.

7. **Why is Jabamiah’s verse Genesis 1:1?**  
   It may express creation and regeneration, or preserve evidence of a different cyclic position. The latter remains conjectural.

8. **Are Mathers’s two seals derived from two identifiable manuscripts?**  
   Their existence is documented, but a definitive stroke-by-stroke genealogy requires direct collation of all surviving sigils and Mathers’s working papers.

9. **Can any pre-Lemegeton form be found under a radically different spelling?**  
   At present no proposed match survives the requirements of comparable name, office, form, and demonstrable transmission.

10. **Was the winged horse borrowed from classical imagery?**  
    Pegasus is the obvious comparison, but no historical link is documented.

11. **Did any early practitioner record an actual Seere operation?**  
    No securely identified seventeenth- or eighteenth-century diary account has been located in the principal published sources.

12. **How much of modern Seere personality lore derives from private revelation?**  
    Contemporary practitioner reports are abundant, but they cannot reconstruct the early text unless independently corroborated.
