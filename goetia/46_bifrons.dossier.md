# Bifrons: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidence labels

This dossier separates six kinds of statements:

- **Documented text** — directly stated in a manuscript, early printed edition, or primary-source transcription.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a conclusion proposed from manuscript comparison, bibliography, linguistics, or historical context.
- **Esoteric correspondence** — a relationship created within a magical system such as Rudd’s angel–demon pairs, Lenain’s quinances, or Crowley’s *777*.
- **Tradition** — a claim repeatedly transmitted by occult authors without necessarily being present in the earliest witness.
- **Disputed claim** — plausible or popular, but unsupported, contradicted, or dependent on an uncertain reading.
- **Modern invention** — a creative elaboration in recent occultism, fiction, games, or popular culture.

**Quotation note.** Short diplomatic excerpts are supplied below, followed by proposition-by-proposition summaries preserving the complete substantive content. The linked public-domain editions and transcriptions provide the passages in full.

# The name and its origins

## 1. The Latin word

**Documented linguistic fact.** Latin *bifrōns, bifrontis* means “two-fronted,” “two-faced,” or “having two foreheads.” It combines *bi-* or *bis*, “twice/two,” with *frons*, “forehead, brow, front, face.” Classical authors use it as an epithet of Janus, the Roman god represented looking in two directions. The lexical evidence is strong and independent of later demonological speculation. See the [Lewis and Short entry through Logeion](https://logeion.uchicago.edu/bifrontive) and the [Perseus account of Janus](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0104%3Aalphabetic+letter%3DI%3Aentry+group%3D1%3Aentry%3Djanus-bio-1).

Classical instances include *bifrontis* or related forms applied to Janus in Virgil and Macrobius. Janus governed entrances, gates, beginnings, endings, and transitions and therefore looked both inward and outward, or backward and forward.

## 2. Is Bifrons a demonized Janus?

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The most economical etymology is the ordinary Latin adjective *bifrons*. A literate compiler could have adopted it as an ominous or exotic proper name.

**Disputed claim:** Bifrons is frequently said to be Janus himself after Christian “demonization.” The name certainly evokes Janus, but no surviving Bifrons text calls the spirit Janus, describes two faces, assigns him gates or beginnings, or supplies a story of a Roman god’s fall. Weyer says only that he first appears as a monster and then becomes human.

The connection is consequently best expressed in three levels:

1. The word’s Latin meaning is secure.
2. Its association with the epithet *Janus Bifrons* is secure.
3. The historical identity “Bifrons was originally Janus” remains plausible but unproved.

The graveyard and corpse-moving offices do not establish a Janus identity. They concern boundaries between life and death, which makes a Janus comparison symbolically attractive, but that is retrospective interpretation.

## 3. Earliest attestation

For many years, Weyer’s 1577 printing was treated as Bifrons’s earliest known appearance. Peterson’s current *Pseudomonarchia* apparatus now reports **Bifrons on p. 34 of Coxe MS 25**, formerly BPH MS 114, a late-fifteenth-century *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* witness. This moves the name’s surviving manuscript history back roughly a century. Peterson records the reading in his comparative apparatus: [*Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*, manuscript and edition notes](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

This finding must be stated narrowly:

- **Documented editorial report:** the medieval witness contains the name *Bifrons*.
- **Scholarly implication:** Weyer did not invent the name.
- **Open question:** a complete diplomatic publication and codicological examination of the Coxe passage are still needed before one can determine exactly how much of Weyer’s description was already present.

**Modern invention:** *Bifronze*, encountered in some game localizations and internet lists, does not belong to the early source chain. Nor is “Janus” an attested alternate name in the grimoires.

# The description across the sources

## 1. Late-fifteenth-century witness

**Documented text, incompletely accessible:** Coxe MS 25 reportedly contains *Bifrons*. Peterson’s online apparatus presently establishes the occurrence but does not publish enough of the passage to prove the exact medieval form of every office, the legion total, or a seal.

The responsible conclusion is therefore: **Bifrons has a pre-Weyer attestation, but Weyer remains the earliest readily verifiable complete description.**

## 2. Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* (1577)

Weyer placed the catalogue at the end of the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum*, printed at Basel by the Oporinus press. Bifrons is §46 in the 1577 sequence, at approximately p./column 926.

Short diplomatic excerpt:

> “Bifrons, monstri similitudine conspicitur. Ubi humanam assumit imaginem, reddit hominem in Astrologia mirabilem…”

A close translation of the entire entry is:

- Bifrons is seen in the likeness of a monster.
- When he assumes a human image, he makes a person remarkably learned in astrology.
- He teaches the mansions of the planets completely.
- He does the same in geometry and other forms of measurement.
- He understands the powers of herbs, precious stones, and woods.
- He transfers dead bodies from place to place.
- He seems to ignite candles over the tombs of the dead.
- Twenty-six legions are subject to him.

The Latin is available in full in Peterson’s [Weyer transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm). A scan record for the 1577 edition is available through [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/Ioannis_VVieri_de_praestigiis_daemonum_i.html?id=6ZlHjwEACAAJ).

Important textual points:

- **No rank appears in the individual entry.**
- The planetary material is specifically the *mansiones planetarum*, “mansions of the planets,” not merely generic fortune-telling.
- *Transmutat … de loco ad locum* means transfers or changes their location. It does not necessarily mean chemical transformation, resurrection, bodily possession, or soul exchange.
- *Videtur* means “he seems” or “appears” to light the candles. Weyer does not unambiguously assert material flames.
- The total is **26**, not 6 or 60.

## 3. Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584)

Scot incorporated an English version of Weyer’s catalogue in the later books of *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*. Peterson notes that the translation may have been made by Abraham Fleming, apparently from a manuscript or imperfect copy rather than mechanically from Weyer’s printed Latin.

Short excerpt:

> “Bifrons is seene in the similitude of a monster … he changeth dead bodies from place to place…”

The complete substance is:

- He is first seen as a monster.
- When he assumes human shape, he makes the operator wonderfully skilled in astrology.
- He fully declares the mansions of the planets.
- He does similarly in geometry and “other admeasurements.”
- He understands perfectly the strength and virtue of herbs, precious stones, and woods.
- He moves dead bodies from place to place.
- He seems to light candles on their sepulchres.
- He has twenty-six legions.

The entry is in Book XV, chapter 2 in the original arrangement; it is p. 223 in the 1886 Nicholson edition reprinted by Dover. See Peterson’s [Scot catalogue transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/scot16.htm) and the [Cornell scan of the 1584 edition](https://digital.library.cornell.edu/catalog/witchcraft081).

Scot’s purpose was not to endorse demonic magic. Like Weyer, he used demonological materials within a broader argument against credulous witch persecutions.

## 4. The seventeenth-century *Goetia*

The *Ars Goetia* is the first book of the composite *Lemegeton* or *Lesser Key of Solomon*. Its anonymous compiler adapted the Weyer–Scot spirit catalogue, redistributed titles, expanded the list to 72, and added seals and ritual apparatus.

Peterson’s edition is based principally on Sloane MS 3825, while consulting Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, Harley 6483, and other witnesses. See the [complete online *Goetia*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm) and [manuscript overview](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm).

Short Sloane 3825 excerpt:

> “The 46th spirit is called Bifrons, he is an Earle and appeareth in ye forme of a Monster at first…”

The entry, at Sloane 3825 fol. 107r, contains these propositions:

- He is the forty-sixth spirit.
- He is an Earl.
- He first appears as a monster.
- At the exorcist’s command he assumes a man’s shape.
- He makes the operator knowledgeable in astrology, geometry, and other arts and sciences.
- He teaches the virtues of **all herbs**, precious stones, and woods.
- He changes dead bodies by putting them into one another’s places.
- He produces apparently burning candles upon graves.
- He commands **six legions**.
- His displayed seal is one that he will “own and submit unto.”

### Manuscript differences

| Witness | Name | Rank | Legions | Significant features |
|---|---|---:|---:|---|
| Sloane 3825 | Bifrons | Earl | 6 | Includes herbs; “one another’s places”; seal drawn. |
| Sloane 2731, dated 1687 | Bifrons | Earl | Generally Goetic form | Seal has normal scribal variation. |
| Sloane 3648 | Bifrous | Earl | Goetic textual family | More error-prone witness. |
| Harley 6483, 1712–13 | Bifrons | Earl | 26 | Rudd recension restores Weyer/Scot’s legion total and adds Ariel as controller. |

| Peterson, 2001 | Bifrons | Earl | 6, with variant history | Restores a manuscript-based text rather than Crowley’s conjecture. |

### What changed between Weyer and the *Goetia*?

The *Goetia*:

- retained Bifrons at number 46;
- added the title Earl;
- generalized “other measurements” into other arts and sciences;
- retained herbs in the principal manuscript;
- interpreted moving corpses as placing them in one another’s locations;
- changed 26 legions to 6 in Sloane 3825;
- supplied a seal and ritual function for it.

## 5. Mathers and Crowley, *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King* (1904)

The 1904 edition was translated by S. L. MacGregor Mathers and edited and published by Aleister Crowley at Foyers, Inverness. Bifrons appears as entry 46 on printed p. 25.

Short excerpt:

> “He is an Earl, and appeareth in the form of a Monster; but after a while … he putteth on the shape of a Man.”

The entry transmits astrology, geometry, other arts and sciences, precious stones, woods, corpse relocation, grave candles, six legions, and the seal. Unlike Sloane 3825, it omits herbs.

Its footnote proposes:

> “Should probably be 60 instead of 6.”

The same note acknowledges Weyer’s 26. No early witness supporting 60 was located. The number 60 is therefore an **editorial conjecture that became a modern tradition**, not an independent manuscript reading. See the [1904 facsimile PDF](https://lo2.org/pdf/celepha%C3%AFs/mathers/Mathers-and-Crowley.Goetia.pdf).

## Order: Weyer’s 69 and the *Goetia*’s 72

Weyer’s 1577 *Pseudomonarchia* has 69 spirits. The *Goetia* has 72.

The four spirits added by the *Goetia* but absent from Weyer are:

1. **Vassago**, Goetia 3
2. **Seere**, Goetia 70
3. **Dantalion**, Goetia 71
4. **Andromalius**, Goetia 72

The Weyer spirit omitted from the *Goetia* is:

- **Pruflas**, also called Bufas, Weyer 4.

Thus adding four and dropping one produces 72. Scot also omitted Pruflas, apparently because it was missing from his exemplar; this made Scot’s version an especially convenient basis for a 68-plus-four construction.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The number 72 was probably attractive because of established Solomonic and kabbalistic numerology. Nevertheless, the *Goetia* compiler does not explain that the catalogue was expanded specifically to match the Shem angels. The explicit one-to-one angelic counter-pairing belongs to the later Rudd recension.

Bifrons is unusual in retaining number **46** through the principal Weyer and Goetia sequences, despite widespread reordering elsewhere.

### Why some sources call him Weyer 45 or 47

- A 1660 printing duplicated an earlier section number, displacing later printed labels by one.
- Some modern compilers count entries differently after Pruflas.
- Some secondary encyclopedias simply copied one another’s erroneous “47.”

The correct number in Weyer’s 1577 sequence is **46**.

# The angel: Ariel

## 1. Derivation of the 72 names

**Kabbalistic tradition.** Exodus 14:19–21 consists, in the Hebrew consonantal text used for the operation, of three verses of 72 letters. The first verse is written in its ordinary right-to-left direction; the second is reversed; the third is written ordinarily. Reading the resulting columns produces 72 three-letter groups.

The forty-sixth group is:

**ערי — ʿayin, resh, yod**

Adding the divine suffix *-el* produces the occult angel-name:

**עריאל — Ariel**

The verses can be compared through [Sefaria’s Exodus 14:19–21 text](https://www.sefaria.org/Exodus.14.19-21?with=Kabbalah).

The phrase *Shem ha-Mephorash* originally meant the explicit, distinguished, or fully pronounced divine name, especially the Tetragrammaton. Different Jewish sources later associated the expression with names of 12, 42, or 72 letters. The 72 triplets became particularly important in medieval and early-modern practical Kabbalah.

## 2. Historical transmission

### Jewish sources

**Documented tradition:** The extraction procedure is attested in medieval Jewish commentary and mystical literature. Rashi’s commentary on *Sukkah* 45a explains the three-verse arrangement; *Sefer ha-Bahir* associates the divine name with 72; and forms of the complete sequence occur in *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh*. For a scholarly study of the textual technique, see Laura Joffe, [“The Answer to the Meaning of the Seventy-Two Names of God”](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/030908920202700205).

The early Jewish sources deal primarily with divine letter-names. They do not yet provide the full modern package of five-degree zodiac sectors, November birth dates, personality descriptions, Tarot cards, and guardian-angel biographies.

### Renaissance Christian Cabala

**Documented transmission:** Christian Cabalists converted the triplets into pronounceable angel names by adding endings such as *-el* and *-iah*. Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517), Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia* (published 1533), and subsequent writers helped transmit the angelic list.

Athanasius Kircher printed elaborate kabbalistic tables in *Oedipus Aegyptiacus*, volume II, part I (1652). Kircher’s presentation is important to the visual and tabular transmission of the 72 names, although his overall treatment of Kabbalah was Christian and frequently polemical rather than a neutral preservation of Jewish doctrine. See Peterson’s [transcription of Kircher’s *Cabala Hebraeorum*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/kircher/cabala.htm).

### Rudd and Harley 6483

**Documented esoteric correspondence:** Harley MS 6483, copied in 1712–13, pairs the 72 Goetic spirits ordinally with the 72 angels. Spirit 46 Bifrons is placed under angel 46 Ariel.

The manuscript supplements the demon’s seal with the corresponding angel’s Hebrew name, seal or character, and Psalm verse. The angel is invoked as superior authority constraining the spirit. The logic is not that Ariel and Bifrons have a historically demonstrated mythic rivalry; it is that the forty-sixth angel is assigned to regulate the forty-sixth demon.

**Attribution dispute:** Harley 6483 says material was copied by Peter Smart from sheets associated with “Dr Rudd.” Stephen Skinner and David Rankine identify this figure with the mathematician and engineer Thomas Rudd and reconstruct an earlier recension around 1655–56. A. E. Waite and other critics considered it possible that “Rudd” was an authority-name or that some material was assembled later from printed sources. The manuscript is real; the extent of Thomas Rudd’s personal authorship remains debated.

Skinner and Rankine published the system as *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* in 2007. Publisher information is available from [Llewellyn](https://www.llewellyn.com/product.php?ean=9780738723556) and the [Golden Hoard edition record](https://www.bythewaybooks.com/pages/books/31253/stephen-skinner-david-rankine/the-goetia-of-dr-rudd).

## 3. The name and meaning of Ariel

### Hebrew meanings

**Documented philology:** *Ariel* is conventionally parsed as *ari* plus *El*, “lion of God.” In Isaiah 29 the word is also a symbolic name for Jerusalem; in Ezekiel 43 it appears in a cultic context where “altar-hearth” is a serious lexical interpretation. See the [Jewish Encyclopedia’s Ariel entry](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/1760-ariel) and the [Encyclopedia of the Bible](https://www.biblegateway.com/resources/encyclopedia-of-the-bible/Ariel).

Therefore:

- **“Lion of God”** is a defensible literal or traditional Hebrew interpretation.
- **“Altar-hearth of God”** belongs to particular biblical contexts.
- **“God the Revealer”** is Lenain’s occult attribute, not a literal translation of עריאל.

Nor does every biblical occurrence designate an angel. The Bible’s Ariel references cannot simply be identified with Rudd’s angel 46.

### Lenain’s Ariel/Airiel

Lazare Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* (Amiens, 1823) gives the form **Airiel** and the attribute *Dieu révélateur*, “God the Revealer.”

Lenain assigns:

- rays or degrees **226–230**, corresponding to **15°–20° Scorpio**;
- the twenty-third decan and a decanal intelligence rendered *Tepiseuth*;
- invocation from **3:00 to 3:20 p.m.**;
- calendar contacts on 4 May, 15 July, 25 September, 6 December, and 16 February;
- powers of obtaining revelations;
- discovery of hidden treasures;
- revelation of nature’s greatest secrets;
- dreams or visions showing a desired object;
- a contrary genius governing mental tribulation and indiscretion.

These are **nineteenth-century occult correspondences**, not information in Weyer or the original Jewish extraction method. See the [1823 Lenain scan](https://archive.org/download/BIUSante_ms05365/BIUSante_ms05365.pdf).

### Psalm verse

Ariel’s verse is Vulgate Psalm 144:9, modern Psalm 145:9:

> “Suavis Dominus universis: et miserationes ejus super omnia opera ejus.”

A concise English rendering is: “The Lord is good to all, and his mercies are over all his works.”

The 144/145 discrepancy results from differing Psalm-numbering systems, not from two different verses.

### Papus, Ambelain, and Haziel

- **Papus**, *La Cabbale* (1903), reproduced the French occult sequence, Ariel’s *Deus revelator* attribute, degrees 226–230, and the Psalm correspondence. See the [Papus scan](https://www.cercle-papus.com/ouvrages/papus-la-cabbale.pdf).
- **Robert Ambelain**, *La Kabbale pratique* (1951), transmitted and ritualized the French angelic system. See [Ambelain’s text](https://www.ordremartiniste.ca/docs-omc/Robert%20Ambelain/La%20Kabbale%20Pratique.pdf).
- **Haziel**, a late-twentieth-century French occult author, popularized Ariel as a personal guardian angel for people born approximately **8–12 November**, with “revelatory perception” and modern devotional exercises. This birthday-guardian model is a modern development, not demonstrably ancient Jewish doctrine. See [*Communiquer avec son ange gardien*](https://bretagne-namaste.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/02/Communiquer-Avec-Son-Ange-Gardien.pdf).

Later sources disagree over Ariel’s choir, superior archangel, colors, stones, incense, and moral psychology. Assignments to the Powers, Virtues, Raphael, Michael, or Camael should not be treated as a single stable tradition.

## The quinance versus the decan

Two incompatible-looking zodiac assignments circulate because they come from different schemes:

- **Ariel 46:** 15°–20° Scorpio, a five-degree sector or quinance.
- **Bifrons 46 in *777*:** 0°–10° Cancer by night, a ten-degree decan.

Crowley’s [*Liber 777*, Table V](https://magicgatebg.com/Books/Liber%20777.pdf), printed p. 29, places Bifrons in the Cancer row under the Goetic demons “by night.” The corresponding Tarot table assigns the first Cancer decan to the **Two of Cups**, “Lord of Love,” ruled by Venus in Cancer. Buer is the daytime demon of the same decan.

Thus the requested “Crowley quinance for spirit 46” does not strictly exist. Crowley supplies a **decan**, while Lenain and the angelic calendar supply Ariel’s **quinance**.

## Planet of the rank

The answer also depends on which layer is meant:

- **Seventeenth-century Goetia manuscript rule:** Earl seals use copper and silver equally — Venus and Moon.
- **Crowley’s 1904 correction:** Mars/iron should be assigned to Earls, partly because the manuscript arrangement otherwise leaves Mars without a rank.
- **Later Golden Dawn/Thelemic convention:** Earl = Mars.

“Earl therefore means Mars” is correct within the Crowley/Golden Dawn corrected table, but not as a literal report of Sloane 3825.

## Timing of conjuration

The early text gives no unique hour for Bifrons.

General instructions say:

- use the moon when it is 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, or 14 days old;
- Earls or Counts may be bound at any hour of the day;
- work in an isolated field or wood where people do not ordinarily pass.

The later systems introduce additional possibilities:

- Crowley’s nocturnal Cancer-decan classification;
- a Mars day or hour based on his Earl correction;
- Venus/Moon materials based on the manuscript lamen;
- Ariel’s 3:00–3:20 p.m. Lenain interval;
- Ariel’s 15°–20° Scorpio quinance.

No primary source combines all of these into one mandatory Bifrons election. Modern practitioners who do so are synthesizing distinct systems.

## Enochian and other overlays

Crowley’s 1904 volume adds prefatory and supplementary material not found in the basic Bifrons entry, including the “Preliminary Invocation” or “Bornless Ritual,” adapted from the Greek magical papyri, and Enochian or angelic framing. These do not alter Bifrons’s medieval or Weyer-era description.

Likewise, Rudd’s broader manuscript interests in angelic magic and later editors’ comparisons with Dee do not make Bifrons an originally Enochian spirit. No Enochian name, aethyr, governor, or watchtower attribution belongs to the earliest Bifrons source.

# Iconography

## 1. The manuscript seals

Weyer and Scot give no Bifrons seal. The sigil enters the surviving source chain with the seventeenth-century *Goetia* manuscripts.

The Sloane 3825 form can be inspected directly in Peterson’s [figure 46](https://www.esotericarchives.com/gifs/g46.jpg). In words, it is:

- an open linear figure rather than a pictorial portrait;
- broadly bilateral, though not mechanically symmetrical;
- a jagged or crown-like upper line;
- a rounded or segmented lower body;
- small circular terminals and pendant strokes;
- a central vertical feature connecting or dividing parts of the design.

Words cannot preserve every junction and curvature; the facsimile should be treated as the exact record.

Sloane 2731, Sloane 3825, and Harley 6483 share the recognizable core glyph but differ through ordinary penmanship: relative width, curve depth, terminal circles, stroke crossings, and enclosure vary. These variations provide no secure basis for elaborate symbolic interpretations.

**Rudd innovation:** Harley 6483 makes the demon seal part of a paired construction with Ariel’s name, character, and Psalmic protection. The angelic reverse or counterpart is intended to subject the demon to higher authority.

The [1904 printed seal](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:46-Bifrons_seal.png) normalizes the manuscript figure and places it in the edition’s circular seal presentation. Many modern tattoos, pendants, patches, and digital graphics descend from this printed form rather than directly from a Sloane manuscript.

Claims that individual lines “mean” death, duality, doorways, Janus, or energy circuits are **modern symbolic interpretation**. The early text supplies neither a key nor an explanation of the seal’s geometry.

## 2. Collin de Plancy

Bifrons does not appear as a distinct entry in the searchable transcription of the **1818 first edition** of Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal*. The familiar entry and portrait belong to the expanded later tradition, especially the illustrated sixth edition of **1863**.

The 1863 article, p. 98, begins:

> “Bifrons, démon qui paraît avec la figure d’un monstre. Lorsqu’il prend forme humaine…”

De Plancy says that Bifrons:

- appears as a monster;
- in human form makes people learned in astrology and planetary influences;
- excels in geometry;
- knows the virtues of herbs, precious stones, and plants;
- transports corpses;
- has been seen lighting torches over tombs;
- commands 26 legions.

This follows Weyer more closely than the six-legion Goetia. See the [French article and engraving](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.%2C_1863/Bifrons) and the [full 1863 scan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e0/Jacques_Collin_de_Plancy_-_Dictionnaire_infernal.pdf).

## 3. Louis Le Breton’s portrait

The engraving attributed to Louis Le Breton presents a grotesque humanoid monster with an enlarged head, wide open mouth, and compact, hunched body. It illustrates “monster” rather than the requested human form; it does not visibly establish the literal two-faced Janus interpretation. The public-domain image is preserved at [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Bifrons.png) and sourced there to the Bibliothèque nationale de France scan.

A circulating claim says the portrait was inspired by Goya’s *Duendecitos* from *Los Caprichos*. The resemblance between grotesque figures is suggestive, but the claim is commonly repeated without a documentary citation from Le Breton, de Plancy, or the engraver. It should remain **disputed**.

## 4. Modern art

Modern Bifrons art generally follows one of four strategies:

1. reproducing the Goetic seal;
2. recoloring or redrawing Le Breton’s monster;
3. portraying a literal two-faced Janus figure;
4. emphasizing grave candles, corpses, gems, or astrological instruments.

Only the first two have direct historical prototypes. A two-headed or two-faced Bifrons is an etymological visualization, not the appearance described by Weyer or the *Goetia*.

# People and works

## Precursors and source ancestry

### *Testament of Solomon*

The late-antique *Testament of Solomon* provides a broad narrative ancestor: Solomon commands spirits through a divinely empowered ring, questions them about their offices, learns which angels frustrate them, and employs them in building the Temple.

**Finding:** Bifrons is not named there. The text supplies a Solomonic demon-binding model, not Bifrons’s genealogy.

### Munich handbook, *Livre des Esperitz*, and Florence lists

The fifteenth-century Munich handbook Clm 849, the *Livre des Esperitz*, and related magical catalogues contain spirits, offices, hierarchies, and conjurations that illuminate the development of later demon lists.

Jean-Patrice Boudet’s comparative study does not identify Bifrons in the Munich, *Livre des Esperitz*, or Florence columns; Bifrons appears in his Weyer column. Peterson’s later notice of Coxe MS 25 now supplies a different pre-Weyer witness. See Boudet, [“Les who’s who démonologiques de la Renaissance”](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/1171).

Boudet specifically notes that Bifrons and Bune retain strongly necromantic functions—moving bodies and producing grave lights—amid a catalogue increasingly dominated by teaching and knowledge.

### *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

This is the most likely direct textual family behind Weyer. Trithemius mentioned a *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* in his *Antipalus maleficiorum* of 1508. Weyer describes his source with related titles, including a *Liber officiorum spirituum* and a book attributed to Solomon.

Coxe MS 25 strengthens the conclusion that Bifrons circulated in this textual family before Weyer’s publication.

### *Book of Abramelin*

Targeted checks of Abramelin’s named-spirit lists found no Bifrons. See Peterson’s [Abramelin edition](https://www2.esotericarchives.com/abramelin/abramelin.htm). Attempts to derive Bifrons directly from Abramelin are unsupported.

## Major transmitters

### Johann Weyer

Weyer was a physician and opponent of the dominant witch-prosecution ideology. He printed the *Pseudomonarchia* partly to expose what he regarded as learned magical imposture and omitted or disrupted ritual elements so the catalogue would not function straightforwardly as a working grimoire.

He remains the earliest printed complete witness for Bifrons.

### Reginald Scot and possibly Abraham Fleming

Scot reproduced the catalogue in English as part of his skeptical assault on witchcraft prosecutions and demonic credulity. Peterson considers Abraham Fleming a possible translator. Scot’s version became the immediate English textual reservoir from which the *Goetia* compiler drew.

### The anonymous *Lemegeton* compiler

The compiler:

- converted the inherited catalogue into a ritual handbook;
- gave Bifrons the Earl title;
- added a seal;
- made the list total 72;
- changed the legion reading to six in the principal manuscript branch.

### Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

Harley 6483 presents Rudd-associated materials copied by Peter Smart. Its distinctive contribution is the deployment of Shem angels, divine names, and Psalm verses as explicit controls over the demons.

Skinner and Rankine’s identification of the underlying author with Thomas Rudd is influential but remains a reconstruction rather than an autograph fact.

### Mathers and Crowley

Mathers translated the material; Crowley edited and published it in 1904. Their edition fixed several modern conventions:

- the spellings *Bifröus* and *Bifrovs*;
- a widely reproduced printed seal;
- the note proposing 60 legions;
- Mars/iron as the proposed correction for the Earl rank;
- ceremonial overlays associated with Crowley’s developing system.

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 *Lesser Key of Solomon* and continually revised Esoteric Archives edition returned attention to Sloane 3825 and systematic variant comparison. His later apparatus is also the source presently reporting the late-fifteenth-century Coxe 25 attestation.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their 2007 *Goetia of Dr Rudd* made Harley 6483’s angel–demon pairs widely accessible and argued for a mid-seventeenth-century Thomas Rudd recension. Their edition is crucial for Ariel’s relationship to Bifrons, while its historical attribution should be kept distinct from the manuscript’s undisputed eighteenth-century copying date.

### Other modern occult authors

- **Poke Runyon** adapts the Goetic system to dark-mirror evocation in *The Book of Solomon’s Magick*. His importance is to twentieth-century Goetic practice generally; no uniquely documented Bifrons operation was located.
- **Lon Milo DuQuette**, with Christopher Hyatt, discusses and illustrates the Goetia in *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia*. DuQuette’s *Tarot of Ceremonial Magick* further spreads Golden Dawn decan correspondences. Again, no distinctive historical Bifrons revelation was found.
- **Jake Stratton-Kent** recontextualizes Goetic spirits within broader grimoire traditions in works including *The True Grimoire* and *Encyclopaedia Goetica*. The examined source trail did not yield a major Bifrons-specific historical discovery attributable to him.
- **Frater Achad:** no reliable publication documenting a Bifrons working or a distinctive Bifrons doctrine was located. His inclusion in internet lists appears associative—through Crowley and ceremonial magic—rather than evidentiary.
- **Owen Davies, Richard Kieckhefer, Jan Veenstra, Julien Véronèse, and Jean-Patrice Boudet** are relevant to grimoire history, learned magic, spirit catalogues, and the *Livre des Esperitz*. Boudet is the most directly useful here because he discusses Bifrons’s necromantic offices.

## Earlier canonical literature

### Dante

**Finding:** No Bifrons occurs in Dante’s *Divine Comedy*. Comparisons between Bifrons and Dantean demons are modern thematic association.

### Milton

**Finding:** Bifrons is not named in *Paradise Lost*. Milton does name an Ariel among the rebel angels defeated by Abdiel in Book VI, but that literary figure cannot be identified historically with Shem angel 46 merely because the name is identical.

### Bartimaeus novels

**Finding:** No reliable textual or official index evidence was located for Bifrons in Jonathan Stroud’s *Bartimaeus* sequence. General claims that “Goetic demons appear in the series” do not attest this individual name.

## Modern prose and comics

### Wayne Barlowe, *God’s Demon* (2007)

Bifrons appears among named infernal figures in Barlowe’s novel. This is a literary appropriation of the Goetic name, not an expansion of the grimoire tradition. The searchable edition includes Bifrons among infernal beings and depicts sigils as fiery insignia.

### *Hellboy* / B.P.R.D.

Bifrons appears in the extended Hellboy universe as a giant demonic creature associated with Yomyael and Varvara, including material catalogued under *Abe Sapien: A Darkness So Great* and *B.P.R.D.: The Devil You Know—Pandemonium*. This red, horned, winged or toad-like comic monster is a **modern invention** and should not be confused with Le Breton’s engraving or Weyer’s undefined monster. A useful issue index is the [Hellboy Wiki’s Bifrons entry](https://hellboy.fandom.com/wiki/Bifrons), though the individual comics remain the primary evidence.

### *Damn Them All*

Simon Spurrier and Charlie Adlard’s occult comic uses Goetic entities and draws upon their catalogue offices. Bifrons’s corpse-moving and grave-light associations are especially suited to its necromantic plot. ## Role-playing games

### Dungeons & Dragons

Ed Greenwood’s “The Nine Hells Part II,” *Dragon* 76 (August 1983), reinvented Bifrons as a “Cold Duke” serving Mephistopheles. This Bifrons commands companies of ice devils, inhabits an ice palace called Nargus, and has a grey, muscular upper body with a serpentine lower form.

The use of **26 companies** appears to echo Weyer’s 26 legions, while the ice hierarchy, palace, allegiance, and anatomy are original D&D world-building. Wizards of the Coast preserves the article in its [*Dragon* archive PDF](https://media.wizards.com/2018/dnd/dragon/18/DRA18_hells.pdf).

## Video games

### *Shin Megami Tensei* and *Persona*

Bifrons entered Atlus’s demon catalogue in *Shin Megami Tensei: Devil Summoner* in 1995 and subsequently appeared in titles including *Soul Hackers*, *Strange Journey*, *Shin Megami Tensei IV*, *Apocalypse*, and *Devil Survivor 2*. Bifrons appears in the *Persona* brand specifically in **Persona Q2: New Cinema Labyrinth**, rather than throughout the main numbered *Persona* series. The *Castlevania* enemy **Biphron** or **Biphrons** is a graveyard/fire-associated monster. In the American release of *Symphony of the Night*, it was renamed **Lossoth**; later appearances include *Aria of Sorrow*, *Portrait of Ruin*, and *The Dracula X Chronicles*. The torch and cemetery theme clearly adapts the Goetic grave-light office. See the [Castlevania Wiki’s source-indexed entry](https://castlevania.fandom.com/wiki/Biphron).

### *Devil Survivor 2: The Animation*

The anime adaptation includes Bifrons as a summoned demonic combatant. This is reception through the Atlus franchise rather than an independent demonological tradition.

Names such as Count Bifrons appear in *Final Fantasy XI*, while additional games and fan databases occasionally adapt the spirit as a necromancer, fire demon, or infernal noble. Such uses should be checked title by title: sharing the name alone does not guarantee that the writers drew directly from Weyer rather than from a modern Goetia encyclopedia.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Was Weyer the first source?

No longer the best answer.

- **Older conclusion:** first attested in Weyer, 1577.
- **Current evidence:** Peterson’s collation records *Bifrons* in late-fifteenth-century Coxe MS 25.
- **Still true:** Weyer is the first known printed complete description readily available for inspection.

The medieval notice needs independent facsimile publication before the entire Weyer description can safely be projected backward.

## 2. Is he number 45, 46, or 47?

- Weyer 1577: **46**
- Goetia: **46**
- Some later Weyer print-numbering: **45**, because of a duplicated section number
- Common internet claim: **47**, usually a copied counting error

Number 46 is textually preferable in both principal traditions.

## 3. Six, 26, or 60 legions?

- **26:** Weyer, Scot, Harley 6483, de Plancy.
- **6:** Sloane 3825 and the Mathers–Crowley main text.
- **60:** Crowley’s footnote proposing an emendation.

The shift 26 → 6 could result from abbreviation, damaged copying, or deliberate simplification. There is no reason to call 60 the “original” without a supporting manuscript.

## 4. Was Bifrons always an Earl?

No.

Weyer and Scot do not state an individual rank for him. “Earl” first appears in the surviving Goetia branch. Later authors retroactively call Weyer’s Bifrons an Earl because the Goetia does.

De Plancy’s wider hierarchy describes Bifrons as a knight while his individual article does not resolve the contradiction. Calling him a “Grand Duke” is a modern error.

## 5. Mars or Venus and Moon?

Both are authentic to different layers:

- equal copper and silver, hence Venus and Moon, is the manuscript rule for an Earl’s seal;
- Mars and iron is Crowley’s corrective correspondence.

Modern pages that state only one and call the other false erase the textual history.

## 6. Does the text say herbs?

- Weyer: yes.
- Scot: yes.
- Sloane 3825: “all herbs,” yes.
- Mathers–Crowley main entry: no; it mentions precious stones and woods.
- De Plancy: herbs and plants.

The 1904 omission is not evidence that herbs were a later interpolation.

## 7. Does Bifrons resurrect or possess corpses?

No early passage says so.

The documented office is relocation of bodies, possibly exchange of burial places. “Necromancer,” “raiser of the dead,” “controller of souls,” “animates corpses,” and “teaches reincarnation” are later expansions.

Boudet’s description of the office as retaining a necromantic character is a historical classification, not a claim that Weyer says “resurrection.”

## 8. Are the grave candles literal?

The early wording is deliberately equivocal:

- Weyer: Bifrons “seems” to light candles.
- Scot preserves “seemeth.”
- The Goetia says he lights candles “seemingly.”
- De Plancy turns them into torches reportedly seen over tombs.

Interpretations as corpse candles, will-o’-the-wisps, marsh gas, spirit lights, or treasure flames are possible folkloric comparisons. None is identified by Weyer as the definitive explanation.

## 9. Is Bifrons two-faced?

The name means two-faced. The texts do not describe two faces.

Le Breton’s portrait likewise does not establish a Janus head. Two-faced modern art is an etymological reconstruction.

## 10. Are Bifrons and Ariel natural enemies?

Only within the Rudd system.

The angelic relationship is ordinal and ritual: number 46 constrains number 46. No Jewish, biblical, classical, or pre-Rudd narrative records a conflict between Ariel and Bifrons.

## 11. Does Ariel mean “God the Revealer”?

As an occult attribute, yes; as literal Hebrew, probably not.

- Literal/traditional Hebrew: “lion of God,” with “altar-hearth” possible in biblical contexts.
- Lenain’s functional title: “God the Revealer.”
- Modern occult writers frequently collapse the two.

## 12. Is Bifrons a Scorpio spirit?

Not in Crowley’s demon table.

- Ariel’s quinance is Scorpio 15°–20°.
- Crowley’s Bifrons is nocturnal Cancer 0°–10°.

Calling Bifrons “the Scorpio demon for 8–12 November” transfers the paired angel’s sector to the demon.

## 13. Is every Two of Cups operation also a Bifrons operation?

No. In the Golden Dawn/Crowley overlay the Two of Cups decan carries two Goetic spirits:

- Buer by day;
- Bifrons by night.

The Tarot card itself predates this attribution and does not historically depict either spirit.

## 14. Do the seal’s strokes encode Janus, graves, or astronomy?

No early source provides such a decoding. Modern claims about gates, energy circuits, corpse rigidity, or a central portal are speculative symbolism.

## 15. Was Bifrons conflated with another historical spirit?

No clear early conflation was found. The major discrepancies—Bifrous, six versus 26 legions, Earl versus no rank—are textual variants, not evidence for two originally distinct demons.

Bune also moves bodies or alters burial places, and Murmur deals with the dead, but shared offices do not make them aliases.

# Open questions

1. **What exactly does Coxe MS 25 say?**  
   A full color facsimile and diplomatic transcription of its Bifrons passage are needed. Until then, it proves the name’s occurrence more securely than the complete profile’s medieval form.

2. **How did 26 become 6?**  
   The transmission path between Scot/Weyer and the principal Goetia manuscript has not been demonstrated line by line.

3. **Where did the seal originate?**  
   It is absent from Weyer and Scot and appears in the Goetia manuscripts. No pre-Goetia Bifrons seal has been securely identified.

4. **Was “Bifrons” deliberately chosen to invoke Janus?**  
   The Latin allusion is compelling, but no compiler explains it and no source gives Bifrons two faces.

5. **Who assigned the Earl title?**  
   The surviving evidence points to the Goetia compilation process, but no named compiler or rationale survives.

6. **How early is the Ariel pairing?**  
   Harley 6483 documents it in 1712–13. Skinner and Rankine argue for an underlying c.1655–56 Rudd recension, but the earlier stage is reconstructed.

7. **Did Rudd originate all the angelic controls?**  
   The building blocks are older—Shem triplets, angel names, Psalm verses, and Solomonic constraint—but their exact 72-to-72 Goetia arrangement may be Ruddian or belong to his immediate circle.

8. **Is Le Breton’s image dependent on Goya?**  
   Visual comparison is possible; documentary evidence of deliberate borrowing has not been produced.

9. **Which modern appearances are direct adaptations?**  
   Games using graves and torch-fire probably consulted the Bifrons notice or a derivative encyclopedia. Other appearances may borrow only the name.
