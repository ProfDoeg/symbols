# Bathin: Research Dossier

*Research cut-off: 14 September 2026.*

## Evidentiary labels

- **Documented text** — stated or depicted in a surviving primary source.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a relationship proposed from textual, codicological, or historical evidence.
- **Tradition** — an attribution repeated within a religious or magical transmission but not independently demonstrated historically.
- **Esoteric correspondence** — an intentional symbolic assignment, not a historical or scientific fact.
- **Disputed** — a reading, date, attribution, or identification for which the evidence permits competing conclusions.
- **Legend** — a traditional narrative presented as lore.
- **Modern invention** — a recent expansion, reinterpretation, or fictional use absent from the historical sources.

Because reproduction limits apply to online editions, the three long entries requested below are represented by short exact excerpts followed by complete close translations or paraphrases; links and page references are supplied for the full public-domain texts.

## The name and its origins

### The fifteenth-century Bathin-like figure: Baltim/Balthym

**Documented text.** The earliest presently identifiable Bathin-like name occurs in the fifteenth-century Munich necromancer’s handbook, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek Clm 849. Richard Kieckhefer’s edition transcribes uncertain forms including **Baltim**, **Balthym**, **Balthim**, and **Baltym**. The figure is a *dux*, a duke, invoked in a transportation operation associated with winds, cloud, speed, and protection.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** This is a plausible ancestor or collateral form of Bathin because it combines:

1. A closely resembling name.
2. Ducal rank.
3. Rapid supernatural conveyance.
4. An intermediary tradition of spirit-office catalogues.

It is not conclusive identity. The Munich text does not provide Bathin’s later serpent-tailed horseman appearance or its herb-and-stone knowledge. “Baltim is Bathin” should therefore be phrased as a strong textual hypothesis, not as a statement made by the manuscript. See Kieckhefer’s *Forbidden Rites* and the searchable transcription of the Munich material.

### Machin in the *Livre des esperitz*

**Documented text.** The French *Livre des esperitz* describes **Machin** as a great duke resembling a strong man. He teaches the virtues of herbs and precious stones, transports the master “from region to region” wherever desired, and commands **thirty-seven legions**. In the surviving catalogue he is number 16.

A short exact portion reads: “**Machin eſt ung grant duc … en ſemblance dun home fort**”—“Machin is a great duke … in the likeness of a strong man.” The complete entry is available in the [Wikisource transcription](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre_des_esperitz/Texte_entier).

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Jean-Patrice Boudet identifies *Machin/Bathym* as one of numerous cognate pairs linking the French catalogue with Weyer’s source. The nearly identical offices are much stronger evidence than the names alone. Boudet does not treat the *Livre* as merely a French translation of Weyer: the catalogues have shared material, omissions on both sides, reordered names, and distortions suggesting a common textual family or intermediary witnesses.

**Disputed dating.** The surviving *Livre des esperitz* manuscript has been dated variously to the later fifteenth, early sixteenth, or mid-sixteenth century. Elizabeth Butler and Kieckhefer favored relatively early datings; Boudet cautioned against assigning it too early. Consequently:

- It is reasonable to call *Machin* an apparently pre-Weyer or at least Weyer-independent witness.
- It is not safe to give a single uncontested composition date.
- The physical manuscript does not prove that the exact spelling *Bathym* existed in the fifteenth century.

Machin has neither a serpent’s tail nor a pale horse in its brief description. Its 37 legions became 30 somewhere in the later textual branch.

### Barton in Folger MS V.b.26

**Documented text.** An English *Book of Magic, with Instructions for Invoking Spirits*, Folger MS V.b.26, compiled around 1577–1583, lists **Barton** as a great duke. Barton appears as a great bear with a dragon’s tail, knows the virtues of herbs and precious stones, carries someone swiftly and safely from region to region, and rules 30 legions.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The offices, rank, transport formula, tail, and legion count make Barton a persuasive sibling of Bathin. The bear is probably a copying or interpretive divergence from Weyer’s strong man, while “dragon’s tail” parallels the serpent’s tail.

**Disputed chronology.** Because dates inside the Folger manuscript straddle Weyer’s 1577 publication, it cannot automatically be cited as earlier than Weyer. It may preserve independent material, may depend on a related English source, or may reflect circulation shortly after Weyer.

### Weyer’s Bathym/Marthim

**Documented text.** Johann Weyer’s *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*, printed with the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum* in 1577, is the first secure printed attestation. Weyer’s number 10 is headed:

> “**Bathym, alibi Marthim Dux magnus & fortis**”

That is, “Bathym, elsewhere Marthim, a great and strong duke.” Weyer continues that he appears as a very strong man with a serpent’s tail, mounted on a pale horse; understands the virtues of herbs and precious stones; transports a man at extreme speed from region to region; and has 30 legions under him. The Latin is on printed p. 915 in the [1577 text reproduced by Joseph Peterson](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

The crucial terms are:

- *constitutione viri fortissimi* — in the form, constitution, or appearance of a very strong man.
- *cauda serpentina* — with a serpent’s tail.
- *equo pallido insidens* — seated upon a pale horse.
- *virtutes herbarum & lapidum pretiosorum* — the powers or virtues of herbs and precious stones.
- *de regione in regionem transfert* — transports from region to region.

**Documented historical context.** Weyer did not present this catalogue as a religion he endorsed. He was a physician and critic of witch persecution. The *Pseudomonarchia* exposed what he considered the fraudulent and delusive apparatus of conjurors, and he withheld or condemned some operational material.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Weyer named his source as a *Liber officiorum spirituum*, or book of the offices of spirits, associated with a pseudo-Solomonic *Empto. Salomonis*. The precise exemplar has not survived. The *Livre des esperitz*, Folger office-book, and other catalogue fragments establish the larger textual genre, but none can yet be proven to be Weyer’s exact copy.

### Scot’s Bathin/Mathim

**Documented text.** Reginald Scot’s *Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584), Book XV, Chapter 2, converts the Latin forms into **Bathin** and **Mathim**:

> “**Bathin, sometimes called Mathim, a great duke and a strong**”

Scot says Bathin appears as a very strong man with a serpent’s tail, sits on a pale horse, understands herbs and precious stones, suddenly transfers men from country to country, and rules 30 legions. See the [complete 1584-derived text](https://esotericarchives.com/solomon/scot16.htm), original printed p. 379; the Nicholson 1886 reprint places the passage on approximately p. 218.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Peterson notes that Scot’s translation may have been made by Abraham Fleming and perhaps from a manuscript rather than directly from the printed 1577 edition. The precise responsibility remains uncertain. The name changes can be explained by ordinary transmission:

- **Bathym → Bathin:** final *-ym* regularized as *-in*.
- **Marthim → Mathim:** loss of *r*, whether scribal, typographic, or present in Scot’s exemplar.

There is no reason to assume that Bathym and Marthim were originally two separate demons. Weyer explicitly treats Marthim as an alternative name.

### Is Bathin named after an ancient god?

**Finding: no demonstrated ancient divine, heroic, geographic, Greek, Latin, or Hebrew etymology exists.**

Unlike names traceable with varying confidence to Baal, Astarte, Amun, Asmodeus/Ashmedai, Belial, or Phoenix, **Bathin has no accepted ancient referent**. Neither Weyer nor Scot supplies an etymology. The name is not transparently derived from a classical Greek or Latin common noun.

**Disputed and unsupported proposals include:**

- Derivation from an Ethiopian or African salutation resembling *Batha*.
- Association with a purported word *mathios* and a serpent-renewing herb.
- Identification with the Egyptian goddess Nephthys.
- An origin in a Hebrew root reconstructed from the later spelling **BAThIN**.
- An association with Venusian deities simply because a duke is assigned to Venus.

These proposals appear in modern occult dictionaries or demonolatry literature without a demonstrable philological chain. Skinner and Rankine appropriately caution that turning already corrupted European spirit names back into Hebrew or Greek letters does not prove their origin.

The responsible conclusion is:

- **Secure exact name:** Bathym, 1577.
- **Secure English form:** Bathin, 1584.
- **Probable earlier relatives:** Baltim/Balthym and Machin.
- **Ancient divine origin:** unknown and presently unsupported.

### Weyer’s Latin

**Documented text.** Weyer’s full sense, closely translated from the 1577 Latin, is:

Bathym, elsewhere Marthim, is a great and strong duke. He is seen in the constitution of a very strong man, having a serpent’s tail and sitting on a pale horse. He understands the powers of herbs and precious stones. With the swiftest course he transfers a man from one region to another. Thirty legions are subject to him.

The source gives **no**:

- change into a courteous human form;
- command to abandon an animal or monstrous shape;
- knowledge of languages or liberal sciences;
- discovery of treasure;
- love magic;
- familiar spirits;
- invisibility;
- alchemical transmutation;
- truthful answers to all questions.

Those belong to other Goetic entries or later generalized ritual claims.

### Scot’s English

**Documented text.** Scot’s 1584 entry begins:

> “**Bathin, sometimes called Mathim … is seene in the shape of a verie strong man**”

It continues with the serpent’s tail, pale horse, understanding of herbs and precious stones, sudden transfer of men between countries, and 30 legions. Scot changes Weyer’s singular *hominem* into the more general “men” and *regione* into “countrie,” but does not materially enlarge the office.

The full passage should be read in the [Peterson transcription of Scot](https://esotericarchives.com/solomon/scot16.htm) or the [Library of Congress scan](https://www.loc.gov/item/59059328/).

### The seventeenth-century *Ars Goetia*

**Documented text.** Sloane MS 3825, fol. 103r, begins:

> “**The 18th spirit is called Bathin, he is a mighty [and] strong duke**”

It describes the strong man, serpent’s tail, pale-colored horse, knowledge of herbs and precious stones, sudden transportation between countries, and 30 legions. It concludes by directing that his seal be made and worn before the operator. Peterson’s [critical online transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm) is based principally on Sloane 3825, while his 2001 printed edition places Bathin on p. 14.

The principal manuscript spellings tabulated by Peterson are:

- Weyer: **Bathym; Marthim**
- Scot: **Bathin; Mathim**
- Sloane 3825: **Bathin**
- Sloane 3648: **Bathin**
- Sloane 2731: **Bathin**
- Harley 6483: **Bathin**
- Northwestern University MS 65: **Bathin**
- Mathers–Crowley: **Bathin**
- A. E. Waite: **Bathin**, with Bathym/Marthim noted as alternatives
- Idries Shah: **Bathin/Bathym/Marthim**
- Wellcome MS 3203: Bathin’s entry is absent from the incomplete witness

The comparison is available in Peterson’s [parallel spirit-name table](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia_spirits.pdf).

### Mathers–Crowley, 1904

**Documented text.** The printed entry on p. 27 begins:

> “**The Eighteenth Spirit is Bathin. He is a Mighty and Strong Duke**”

It otherwise follows the manuscript description. The printed word **[Horse?]** is bracketed because Mathers regarded the older handwriting as uncertain. Crowley’s editorial note observes that some quarto manuscripts seemed to read “ass,” while Weyer unquestionably wrote *equo pallido insidens*—mounted on a pale horse. See the [1904 text at Sacred Texts](https://www.sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks05.htm) and the [facsimile PDF](https://lo2.org/pdf/celepha%C3%AFs/mathers/Mathers-and-Crowley.Goetia.pdf).

**Textual conclusion.** “Horse” has the best early evidence:

- It is explicit in Weyer’s Latin.
- It is explicit in Scot.
- It suits the transportation office.
- “Ass” is a manuscript/editorial reading reported only in the later apparatus.

Crowley’s comparison with the pale horse of Revelation 6:8 is an editorial analogy, not proof that Revelation generated Bathin’s image.

### The 1665 additions to Scot

**Documented but secondary text.** A 1665 edition of Scot contains material absent from the original 1584 *Discoverie*, including instructions for raising “Paymon, Bathin, Barma.” It recommends a waxing moon, Mercury’s rule, and approximately eleven at night, and calls them “three Hunters of the North.”

The supplementary text adds broad benefits such as riches, treasure, invisibility, flight, knowledge, and the obtaining of a familiar.

**Critical qualification.** These are **not** Bathin’s original catalogue offices and should not be retrojected into 1577 or 1584. Nor is the 11 p.m. Mercury operation Bathin’s individual hour in the *Goetia*. It belongs to a later composite conjuration for three spirits.

### Number of legions

The textual pattern is straightforward:

- **Machin:** 37.
- **Barton:** 30.
- **Bathym/Bathin from Weyer onward:** 30.

No early source explains the change. It could result from a numeral-copying error, a deliberate standardization, or a separate textual branch.

### Rank, Venus, copper, and timing

**Documented ritual system.** Bathin’s individual entry says only “duke.” The *Goetia*’s general instructions associate ranks with metals and planetary timing:

- Kings — Sun, gold.
- Dukes — Venus, copper.
- Princes and prelates — Jupiter, tin.
- Marquises — Moon, silver.
- Presidents — Mercury.
- Earls/counts — a mixture of Venus/copper and Moon/silver.
- Knights — Saturn, lead.

Accordingly, Bathin’s seal is properly made in **copper** under the rank rule.

The same instructions place the constraining of dukes between **sunrise and noon**, in fair or clear weather. Friday and a planetary hour of Venus are logical later applications of planetary magic, but the Bathin paragraph itself does not prescribe Friday.

### The seal and its variants

**Documented text and image.** No Bathin seal appears in the surviving Weyer or Scot catalogue text. The seal is securely present in the seventeenth-century *Goetia* manuscript tradition.

The source does not verbally encode the lines. “Exact description” therefore requires acknowledging that it is a drawing, not a sequence of textual directions:

- It is an enclosed, approximately circular lineal character.
- Letter-like strokes or name-derived forms occur around its perimeter.
- Its center contains an asymmetrical monogrammatic construction.
- Mathers–Crowley prints **two alternative seals**, separated by “or.”
- The alternatives share the circular format but have visibly different central strokes, joins, and crossbars.
- Manuscript copies vary in closure, line weight, angle, and whether adjacent strokes touch.
- Harley 6483’s Bathin sigil is unusually over-inked; Skinner and Rankine describe it as “closed up.”

The two commonly reproduced 1904 forms can be inspected directly as [seal 1](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:18-Bathin_seal01.png) and [seal 2](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:18-Bathin_seal02.png). A modern vector is available [here](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:18-bathin-sigilo-ars-goetia.svg), but a cleaned vector should not be mistaken for manuscript evidence.

**Tradition.** The seal is to be made and worn before the operator. In the Rudd arrangement, the reverse or corresponding protective portion bears the constraining angel and biblical verse.

## Why Bathin is number 10 in Weyer and 18 in the *Goetia*

**Documented text.** Weyer’s extant catalogue contains **69** spirits. The *Ars Goetia* contains **72**.

The *Goetia* adds four figures not found in Weyer’s surviving list:

1. Vassago, number 3.
2. Seere, number 70.
3. Dantalion, number 71.
4. Andromalius, number 72.

It omits Weyer’s **Pruflas/Bufas**, Weyer number 4. Thus:

> 69 − 1 + 4 = 72.

Bathym is number 10 in Weyer; Bathin becomes number 18 after the *Goetia*’s additions and extensive rearrangement. For comparison:

- Weyer: Botis 9, Bathym 10, Pursan 11.
- *Goetia*: Botis 17, Bathin 18, Sallos 19, Purson 20.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Reordering may reflect displaced leaves, compilation from multiple exemplars, or deliberate reshaping into a 72-member Solomonic system. No surviving compiler’s note tells us which occurred.

The often repeated statement that Weyer “listed 72 demons” is misleading. His prefatory language participates in the symbolic 72 tradition, but the surviving catalogue itself has 69 entries.

## The angel: Caliel

### Identification

| Field | Caliel |
|---|---|
| Number | 18 of 72 |
| Hebrew | **כליאל** |
| Usual transliterations | Caliel, Kali’el, Caliyel; occasionally Kaliel |
| Consonantal triad | K-L-Y or K-L-I, depending transliteration |
| Traditional meaning | “God prompt to hear/answer,” “God quick to grant,” or “the invocable God” |
| Zodiacal segment | 25°–30° Gemini |
| Five traditional calendar days | 6 April, 17 June, 28 August, 8 November, 18 January |
| Lenain invocation period | 5:40–6:00 a.m. |
| Order | Thrones in Lenain’s hierarchy |
| Office in Lenain | Help in adversity; revelation of truth in judicial proceedings; vindication of innocence; confounding the guilty and false witnesses |
| Lenain psalm | Vulgate Psalm 7:9; approximately Psalm 7:8 in common English numbering |
| Rudd Bathin verse | A variant centered on Vulgate Psalm 34:24, corresponding to Psalm 35:24 in common English numbering |
| Tarot correspondence | Ten of Swords through the third decan of Gemini |

### Derivation from Exodus 14:19–21

**Tradition with documented textual method.** The 72 triads are extracted from three Hebrew verses, Exodus 14:19, 14:20, and 14:21. Each verse was traditionally counted as 72 letters. The method writes:

1. Verse 19 in its ordinary direction.
2. Verse 20 in reverse direction.
3. Verse 21 in its ordinary direction.

Reading the resulting columns yields 72 three-letter combinations. Later Christian kabbalists add endings such as **-El** or **-Yah** to turn the triads into angelic names. The eighteenth produces the basis for **Caliel**. Joshua Trachtenberg summarizes the construction in [*Jewish Magic and Superstition*](https://sacred-texts.com/jud/jms/jms09.htm); the biblical verses are available together at [Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Exodus.14.19-21?with=Kabbalah).

**Historical qualification.** *Shem ha-Mephorash* has not always meant “the 72 guardian angels.” In rabbinic literature it can mean an explicitly pronounced or expanded divine name, including the Tetragrammaton. The fully personalized angel system is a later development.

### Sefer Raziel and the medieval tradition

**Documented historical context.** *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* is a medieval compilation, not a demonstrably ancient book dictated intact to Adam by an angel. Its layers include cosmology, angelology, divine names, amulets, and magical procedures. The printed Amsterdam edition appeared in 1701, but constituent materials circulated earlier.

**Legend.** The book was traditionally said to have been given by the angel Raziel to Adam and transmitted through biblical patriarchs. This explains its authority within later lore but is not its critical date of composition.

**Historical qualification.** The modern package—one angel per five zodiacal degrees, personality descriptions, birthdays, moral qualities, and compact invocations—should not be attributed wholesale to medieval *Sefer Raziel*. Much of that format belongs to early-modern Christian Kabbalah and nineteenth- or twentieth-century occult synthesis. See the [Jewish Encyclopedia overview](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/12606-raziel-book-of) and recent scholarship on the text’s layers at [De Gruyter](https://www.degruyter.com/document/doi/10.1515/asch-2024-2017/html).

### Reuchlin, Agrippa, Vigenère, and Kircher

**Documented historical transmission.**

- Renaissance Christian kabbalists such as Johannes Reuchlin helped circulate permutations of Hebrew divine names in a Christian framework.
- Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia*, Book III, Chapter 25, lists the 72 names, explains the addition of divine suffixes, and associates them with celestial quinaries. See [Agrippa, Book III](https://esotericarchives.com/agrippa/op3.htm).
- Blaise de Vigenère transmitted related name-mysticism and tables in the later sixteenth century.
- Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–54) reproduced and systematized Christian-kabbalistic diagrams, divine-name equivalences, and 72-fold structures. A relevant Kircher transcription is available at [Esoteric Archives](https://www.esotericarchives.com/juratus/kircher.htm).

These authors document stages in the framework’s development. They do not independently establish that Caliel was paired with Bathin before Rudd.

### Rudd’s pairing

**Documented manuscript tradition.** Harley MS 6483, copied in 1712–13, places the 72 Goetic spirits in numerical opposition to the 72 angels. Spirit 18 Bathin is therefore constrained by angel 18 Caliel.

Skinner and Rankine’s edition prints Bathin’s catalogue description and then the protective verse:

> “**Iudica me Domine secundum misericordiam et iustitiam tuam**”

The continuation asks that enemies not rejoice over the speaker. See Skinner and Rankine, *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (2007), p. 120, in the [searchable copy](https://pdfcoffee.com/the-goetia-of-dr-rudd-by-dr-stephen-skinner-david-rankine-pdf-free.html).

**Tradition.** The angel’s name and divine verse are placed opposite or behind the demonic character so that the angelic power constrains, balances, or compels the spirit. This is Rudd’s defining innovation or preserved system: the operator does not rely on the demon’s character alone.

**Disputed authorship.** The surviving manuscript postdates the historical Thomas Rudd, who died around 1656. Skinner and Rankine argue that it copies a lost Rudd original from approximately 1655–56. That is possible, but no autograph Bathin page by Rudd survives. The safest language is:

- “The Rudd tradition preserved in Harley 6483.”
- Not “Thomas Rudd certainly wrote this surviving manuscript.”

Dan Harms discusses the attribution and manuscript date in his [review of the 2007 edition](https://danharms.wordpress.com/2007/10/27/on-the-shelf-the-goetia-of-dr-rudd-part-1/).

### Caliel in Lenain

**Documented text.** Lazare Républicain Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* (Amiens, 1823) gives Caliel’s attribute as “God prompt to answer.” It assigns:

- Degrees 86–90 of the zodiacal sphere: 25°–30° Gemini.
- The five dates 6 April, 17 June, 28 August, 8 November, and 18 January.
- Invocation from 5:40 to 6:00 a.m.
- Aid in adversity.
- Truth in legal proceedings.
- Victory for innocence.
- Confusion of guilty parties and false witnesses.
- A native character marked by justice, integrity, truthfulness, and distinction in the magistracy.

Its contrary spirit governs scandalous proceedings, corrupt or servile conduct, and enrichment at clients’ expense.

Lenain prescribes:

> “**Judica me Domine secundum justitiam meam**”

This is Vulgate Psalm 7:9, corresponding approximately to Psalm 7:8 in widespread modern English numbering. Consult the [1823 scan](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_ms05365). A searchable reproduction appears in Ambelain’s [*La Kabbale pratique*](https://djvu.online/file/O6LjS6ZXfyHrE).

### The Psalm discrepancy

There are at least two Caliel/Bathin verse traditions:

1. **Lenain:** Psalm 7:9 in Vulgate numbering—judgment according to righteousness and innocence.
2. **Harley 6483/Rudd edition:** a mixed or expanded line ending with the language of Vulgate Psalm 34:24, common English Psalm 35:24—judgment according to divine justice and enemies not rejoicing.

The Skinner–Rankine transcription labels the latter “[Psalm 9:9]” in the searchable edition, but its wording does not match that reference. This appears to be a source, scribal, or editorial miscitation.

**Finding.** Modern lists that give Caliel simply “Psalm 35:24” are usually following or normalizing the Rudd-side verse. Lists giving Psalm 7:8/7:9 follow Lenain. The difference is historical, not merely a modern Bible-numbering problem.

### Papus, Ambelain, and Haziel

**Documented transmission.**

- **Papus**, in *La Cabbale* and related occult compilations, republished or summarized the Lenain angelic notices. The corrected 1909 edition helped keep the 72-genius system in circulation. See the [Bibliothèque nationale de France record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb30789329z).
- **Robert Ambelain**, especially in *La Kabbale pratique* (1951), reproduced Lenain’s angel notices and combined them with seals and operational material drawn from French manuscript traditions.
- **Haziel**, the pen name of François Bernad-Termes, popularized the angels in late-twentieth-century French and translated “guardian angel” books. His school emphasizes birth periods, moral missions, prayers, and several levels of guardian influence.

**Historical qualification.** Haziel’s elaborate personality system is a modern devotional-occult interpretation. It should not be cited as evidence for what medieval Jewish kabbalists believed about Caliel.

### Meaning of Caliel

“God prompt to answer” is well documented as **Lenain’s interpretation**. Alternative renderings such as “God ready to hear,” “the invocable God,” or “God who grants swiftly” are traditional paraphrases.

They are not uncomplicated dictionary translations. The first three consonants were obtained through a letter-extraction technique; they were not necessarily a normal Hebrew word. The **-El** suffix supplies the transparent element “God.”

### Why each tarot pip carries two Shem angels

The 360-degree zodiac divided by 72 produces segments of five degrees. A tarot decan spans ten degrees. Therefore each decan—and each numbered tarot pip from Two through Ten—contains two successive five-degree angels.

For 20°–30° Gemini:

- The first angel occupies 20°–25°.
- **Caliel occupies 25°–30°.**
- Both consequently fall under the **Ten of Swords**.

This is a mathematical consequence of combining a 72-part angelic zodiac with a 36-decan tarot zodiac.

### Why Bathin also receives the Ten of Disks

Crowley’s demonic table follows a different operation:

- 72 demons are divided into 36 “day” and 36 “night” rulers.
- Each zodiacal decan receives one day demon and one night demon.
- Bathin, number 18, falls in the final day-demon decan: 20°–30° Virgo.
- The Golden Dawn tarot card for that decan is the **Ten of Pentacles**, called the **Ten of Disks** in Crowley’s Thoth terminology.
- Spirit 54, Murmur, supplies the corresponding night ruler.

This allocation appears in the *777* tradition, not in the seventeenth-century Bathin entry. See [*Liber 777 Revised*](https://hermetic.com/_media/93beast.fea.st/files/section1/777/liber_777_revised.pdf).

### Astrological timing in practice

Three historically distinct timings must be kept separate:

1. **Goetic rank timing:** duke, sunrise to noon, fair weather.
2. **Planetary elaboration:** Venus, therefore Friday and an hour of Venus.
3. **Lenain’s Caliel timing:** 5:40–6:00 a.m.
4. **Scot 1665 supplement:** waxing moon, Mercury, around 11 p.m., for the collective Paymon–Bathin–Barma operation.
5. **Modern zodiacal election:** Sun or another elected point in 25°–30° Gemini for Caliel, or 20°–30° Virgo for Crowley’s Bathin decan.

These timings arise from different systems. There is no single historically universal “correct hour of Bathin.”

### Enochian overlays

**Negative historical finding.** Weyer, Scot, Sloane 3825, Sloane 2731, and Harley 6483 do not give Bathin an Enochian call, tablet square, aethyr, or John Dee spirit.

**Modern invention/synthesis.** Lon Milo DuQuette’s *Tarot of Ceremonial Magick* combines Enochian, Goetic, astrological, and tarot systems on shared cards. In such a system Bathin can acquire Enochian associations indirectly through the Ten of Pentacles/Disks. That is a twentieth-century cross-indexing device, not a recovered Solomonic attribution.

## Iconography

### The core image

**Documented text.** Bathin’s historically stable iconographic elements are:

- Masculine human or humanoid body.
- Exceptional strength.
- Serpent’s tail.
- Pale horse.
- Usually mounted rather than standing beside it.

The pale horse is not described as skeletal, winged, demonic, or armored. Bathin’s clothing, age, hair, weapons, and facial features are unspecified.

**Interpretive possibility.** The horse visually reinforces rapid transportation; the serpent’s tail conveys hybridity and otherness. Neither interpretation is given by Weyer.

### Collin de Plancy

**Documented text.** Jacques Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* first appeared in 1818. Its sixth edition of 1863 famously included Louis Le Breton engravings. Under **Martym ou Batym**, de Plancy calls the figure a great and strong duke of Hell, a robust man with a serpent’s tail, mounted on a horse of livid whiteness. He knows the virtues of herbs and precious stones, transports men between countries at incredible speed, and commands 30 legions.

The French opens:

> “**Martym ou Batym, duc aux enfers, grand et fort**”

See [*Dictionnaire infernal*, 1863, “Marthym,” p. 449](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.,_1863/Marthym).

**Negative finding: no dedicated Le Breton Bathin portrait was located.** The 1863 entry is textual. The famous Le Breton portfolio includes many Goetic figures, but the direct scan and indexed Bathin/Marthym entry do not show a dedicated engraving for this spirit.

Therefore the claim that “de Plancy’s 1863 edition contains Louis Le Breton’s portrait of Bathin” is unsupported and probably arises from websites treating the presence of any 1863 entry as proof that it received an engraving.

### The seals as iconography

The Goetic seal, rather than a de Plancy portrait, became Bathin’s most frequently reproduced visual identifier. Modern line art often cleans up ambiguous manuscript strokes into a symmetrical logo. Such normalization can conceal real variants:

- opened versus closed loops;
- touching versus separated strokes;
- alternative inner monograms;
- different peripheral letter forms;
- over-inking in Harley 6483.

A modern tattoo, pendant, or vector should therefore be identified by which source drawing it copies.

### Modern visual art

**Modern invention.** Contemporary depictions commonly amplify the sparse description into:

- a mounted serpent-knight;
- an armored courier;
- a necromantic horseman;
- a female or androgynous spirit;
- a red or black horse rather than a pale one;
- wings, horns, skulls, capoeira costume, or science-fiction teleportation effects.

Only the strong human form, serpent tail, pale horse, herbs/stones, and rapid conveyance belong to the early textual image. Gender changes, armor, wings, Egyptian dress, and specific colors are modern artistic choices.

## People and works

### Johann Weyer

**Documented history.** Weyer printed the first secure Bathym entry in 1577. He was a physician and critic of witch trials, and his catalogue was embedded in an argument against the credulity and cruelty of witch prosecution. His role was preservative but hostile: he transmitted the demon list while presenting it as deceptive magical literature.

### Reginald Scot and Abraham Fleming

**Documented history.** Scot published the first English form **Bathin** in 1584 as part of *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, another skeptical work opposing witch persecution and exposing conjuring techniques. Abraham Fleming is a plausible translator or collaborator for the catalogue, but the division of labor is not finally established.

### The anonymous *Lemegeton* compiler

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The *Ars Goetia* compiler borrowed heavily from Scot, then reorganized the list, added seals, ritual instructions, four spirits, and a 72-member Solomonic structure. The compiler’s identity is unknown.

### Thomas Rudd

**Tradition and disputed attribution.** The protective angel system is associated with Dr Thomas Rudd. The extant Harley manuscript was copied decades after his death. The historical Rudd may have devised or transmitted the system, but the surviving evidence does not establish that every page is his composition.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Aleister Crowley

**Documented history.** Mathers prepared a translation or edition from British Museum manuscripts. Crowley published and edited it in 1904 at Boleskine. Their edition:

- standardized the 72 entries for a modern occult readership;
- printed Bathin’s two seals;
- preserved the horse/ass textual uncertainty;
- added editorial cross-references;
- placed the *Goetia* within a Golden Dawn/Thelemic setting.

Crowley’s introductory claim that the spirits can be understood as “portions of the human brain” is a modern psychological interpretation. It does not erase the grimoire’s literal ritual language, nor does it prove that earlier magicians thought of Bathin psychologically.

### A. E. Waite

**Documented history.** Waite summarized Bathin in *The Book of Black Magic and of Pacts* and later *The Book of Ceremonial Magic*. He retained Bathin as principal spelling and noted Bathym/Marthim. His treatment was critical and comparative rather than evidence of personal evocation. See [Waite’s catalogue](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/bcm/bcm55.htm).

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s *The Lesser Key of Solomon* (Weiser, 2001) remains an important manuscript-based edition. It uses Sloane 3825 as its principal text and compares other witnesses. His online editions also allow Bathin’s entry to be traced through Weyer, Scot, and the *Goetia* rather than through modern summaries.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (Golden Hoard, 2007) made Harley 6483’s angel-constrained system widely available, including the Bathin–Caliel pairing, protective verse, and note about the over-inked seal. Their attribution of an earlier lost manuscript to Thomas Rudd is influential but remains a reconstruction.

### Poke Runyon

**Modern esoteric synthesis.** Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* integrates Goetic spirits, Shem angels, astrology, and dark-mirror ritual. His Bathin tables associate the spirit with Caliel and use modern ceremonial correspondences. This is important to late-twentieth-century practice but cannot be read back into Weyer.

### Lon Milo DuQuette

DuQuette co-produced *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* and later combined Goetic, Enochian, astrological, and tarot symbolism in the *Tarot of Ceremonial Magick*. His work helped popularize a readable, psychologically informed, syncretic Goetia.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s *Geosophia* and related work place Goetic magic in a longer continuum of ancient, medieval, and early-modern spirit traditions. He is relevant to interpreting the *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* family and criticizing purely psychological readings.

**Negative finding.** No uniquely influential Bathin-specific operation or etymology by Stratton-Kent was located. Modern pages sometimes attach his name to general “chthonic courier” or psychopomp interpretations without an exact citation.

### Frater Achad

**Negative finding.** No Bathin-specific discussion of clear historical influence by Frater Achad was located. His importance to Crowleyan qabalah does not by itself make him a source for Bathin.

## Grimoire ancestors and parallels

### *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Bathin belongs most directly to the family of “books of the offices of spirits”: catalogues naming spirits, ranks, forms, powers, and subordinates. Medieval references by Roger Bacon and later Trithemius show that works with titles resembling *Liber de officiis spirituum* circulated before Weyer.

That establishes the genre’s antiquity, not Bathin’s personal antiquity. Weyer’s precise exemplar remains lost.

### Munich Clm 849

The Munich handbook gives the strongest early Bathin-like combination of name, rank, and transport. It should be treated as a probable precursor, not an exact Bathin entry.

### *Livre des esperitz*

Machin provides the closest pre-*Goetia* office parallel: strong duke, herbs, stones, travel, legions. The text is essential to reconstructing Bathin’s genealogy.

### The *Book of Abramelin*

**Negative finding.** Abramelin literature contains extensive subordinate-spirit lists, but no Bathin/Bathym/Marthim entry with this description has been securely identified. Similarities between broad demon catalogues do not establish direct descent.

### *Testament of Solomon*

**Documented analogue.** The late-antique Greek *Testament of Solomon* depicts Solomon interrogating demons about their names, appearances, functions, and thwarting angels. It is a major ancestor or analogue for Solomonic demonology as a form.

**Negative finding.** Bathin does not appear under that name, and no particular *Testament* demon has been demonstrated to be Bathin. The Rudd practice of opposing a demon with an angel resembles the *Testament*’s thwarting-angel structure, but direct textual descent has not been shown. See the [primary translation](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Testament_of_Solomon) and the [NASSCAL scholarly overview](https://www.nasscal.com/e-clavis-christian-apocrypha/testament-of-solomon/).

### Later grimoires

Names resembling **Bathsin/Bathim** occur in later French grimoire traditions, sometimes among infernal subordinates. These witnesses are later than Weyer and often textually unstable. They cannot supply an ancient origin merely because their narratives claim venerable authorship.

**Modern invention, securely attested.** *Dragon* magazine no. 75 (July 1983) presents **Bathym**, the “Black Duke,” as a duke of Hell serving Belial, with black armor and command over companies of barbed devils. Later Dungeons & Dragons reference works retained or adapted the figure.

This Bathym takes the historical name and rank but replaces the pale-horse herbal transporter with the cosmology and military hierarchy of the game. ## Controversies and disputes

### 1. What is the earliest attestation?

Three answers are possible, depending on evidentiary strictness:

- **Earliest plausible cognate:** Baltim/Balthym in fifteenth-century Clm 849.
- **Earliest near-complete office analogue:** Machin in the *Livre des esperitz*, whose manuscript dating is disputed.
- **Earliest secure exact printed member of the Bathym/Bathin chain:** Weyer’s **Bathym**, 1577.
- **Earliest secure spelling Bathin:** Scot, 1584.

Saying simply “Bathin first appears in 1577” is defensible for the exact demon entry but overlooks earlier related evidence. Saying “Bathin is a fifteenth-century demon” overstates the identity of Baltim or Machin.

### 2. Is the name a corruption or a deliberate alias?

**Documented:** Weyer says “Bathym, elsewhere Marthim,” so he inherited at least two readings.

**Likely reconstruction:** transmission produced a family including Baltim/Balthym, Machin, Bathym, Marthim, Bathin, Mathim, and Barton.

**Unknown:** the archetypal form. No surviving manuscript lets us confidently declare whether the oldest name began with B-, M-, or contained *-r-*.

### 3. Was Machin originally Bathin?

The office correspondence is extremely strong, and Boudet explicitly links them. But “Machin” and “Bathym” could be divergent descendants of a lost form rather than one being copied directly from the other.

Modern French *machin*, “thingummy” or “whatsit,” is not a serious demonstrated etymology for the spirit’s name.

### 4. Is Barton a different spirit?

Folger’s Barton differs in appearance—bear and dragon’s tail—but agrees in rank, herbs, stones, transport, and 30 legions. It is best treated as a probable textual relative. The evidence does not compel either complete identity or complete independence.

### 5. Pathin is not Bathin

A separate Folger spirit called **Pathin** has three heads, a serpent-related body, and burning-iron imagery. Its description is closer to Aim/Haborym than to Bathin. Similar spelling should not override the incompatible office and form.

### 6. Horse or ass?

- Weyer: unquestionably horse, *equus*.
- Scot: horse.
- Some *Goetia* codices: uncertain reading.
- Mathers–Crowley: “[Horse?]”; editorial report that quartos read “ass.”

The horse is textually prior. An ass is a legitimate later manuscript variant but not the best archetypal reading.

### 7. Is the pale horse a reference to Revelation?

Crowley’s editor noticed the resemblance to Revelation 6:8. No earlier Bathin source cites Revelation. The allusion is possible but unproved.

### 8. Thirty or thirty-seven legions?

The *Livre*’s Machin has 37. Weyer and all the main Bathin witnesses have 30. No source explains the change. Numeral corruption is plausible but cannot be demonstrated.

### 9. Does Bathin teach magic, herbal medicine, or natural philosophy?

The early phrase “virtues of herbs and precious stones” belongs to medieval and Renaissance natural-magic language. A “virtue” is the hidden power, efficacy, or property of a substance.

Modern claims that Bathin specifically teaches:

- pharmacology;
- crystal healing;
- homeopathy;
- toxicology;
- cannabis magic;
- chemistry;
- modern geology

are interpretations. They may extrapolate from the source, but none is its wording.

### 10. Does Bathin grant astral projection?

The early texts describe transportation of a person between physical regions or countries. “Astral projection,” “remote viewing,” “pathworking,” and “interdimensional portals” are modern reinterpretations. The source neither confirms nor explicitly excludes a visionary reading, but it does not use those concepts.

### 11. Does Bathin have a female “true form”?

No early witness assigns a female form or identifies Bathin with Nephthys. This is a modern demonolatry development, prominently circulated in the Joy of Satan milieu. It is religious lore within that movement, not a historical recovery.

### 12. Is Bathin an ancient Egyptian deity?

No Egyptological evidence supports the equation with Nephthys or another Egyptian deity. The association appears to be constructed from later gender and planetary correspondences rather than names, inscriptions, cult titles, or ancient texts.

### 13. Is the sigil ancient?

No. Its earliest secure witnesses are early-modern *Goetia* manuscripts. Weyer and Scot provide no Bathin seal. The modern vector is later still.

### 14. Are the two printed seals two spirits?

No source says so. Mathers–Crowley presents them as alternatives for the same Bathin. They probably represent divergent manuscript copies or separate received forms.

### 15. Does Caliel belong to the original Bathin tradition?

No.

- Weyer does not pair Bathym with Caliel.
- Scot does not.
- The basic Sloane catalogue does not derive the pairing from Jewish Kabbalah.
- Rudd’s scheme pairs them because both are number 18 in two 72-member systems.

It is a sophisticated early-modern overlay, not evidence that Bathin and Caliel were paired in antiquity.

### 16. Which is Caliel’s correct psalm?

Both Psalm traditions are documented in different occult sources:

- Lenain: Psalm 7.
- Rudd-side material: Psalm 35, with an apparent erroneous reference in the modern edition.

There is no neutral historical basis for erasing one. A practitioner may follow a chosen lineage, but textual scholarship should report both.

### 17. What is Bathin’s zodiac sign?

This question is underdetermined:

- **Gemini 25°–30°** belongs to Caliel in the numbered Shem scheme.
- **Virgo 20°–30°** belongs to Bathin in Crowley’s demon-decan table.
- **Venus** belongs to Bathin by rank, not zodiac sign.

Web tables frequently combine all three without explaining their different derivations.

### 18. Does de Plancy portray Bathin?

The 1863 dictionary describes Martym/Batym but no dedicated Louis Le Breton engraving was found on the entry page or in the indexed plate set. Claims of a famous “de Plancy portrait of Bathin” lack direct support.

### 19. Is Bathin in *Persona 5* or *Helluva Boss*?

No reliable canonical evidence was located. These claims appear to originate in franchise-wide lists, fan art, or generated summaries.

### 20. Misprints that became names

The family illustrates how copying creates durable demon names:

- Bathym/Bathin through final-letter reinterpretation.
- Marthim/Mathim through loss of *r*.
- Possible Bathin/Barton through minims and letter-shape confusion.
- Machin/Bathym through a less transparent corruption.
- “Horse/ass” through ambiguous manuscript handwriting.
- Psalm 9:9 as an apparent erroneous citation attached to a Psalm 35-like verse.

Once printed, such readings become legitimate branches of occult tradition even when they began as mistakes.

8. Why did two alternative seals enter the Mathers–Crowley edition?
9. Can multispectral examination of Harley 6483 recover strokes obscured by over-inking?
10. Is the Rudd verse a deliberate combination of biblical phrases or an accidental contamination?
11. Does an earlier witness prove the Rudd angel–demon counter-pairing before Harley 6483?

## Conclusions

Bathin is best understood as the early-modern culmination of an older, unstable office-spirit tradition. Its genealogy can be reconstructed backward from the secure printed form **Bathym** in Weyer’s 1577 *Pseudomonarchia* to the nearly identical **Machin** of the *Livre des esperitz* and the transportation duke **Baltim/Balthym** of the Munich necromancer’s handbook. The reconstruction is strong, but the exact archetypal name remains unknown.

The stable core established by Weyer and Scot is narrow:

- a great and strong duke;
- a strong man with a serpent’s tail;
- mounted upon a pale horse;
- knowledge of the virtues of herbs and precious stones;
- extremely rapid transportation;
- 30 legions.

The *Ars Goetia* renumbered him from 10 to 18, supplied seals, and placed him within a 72-spirit ritual structure. The Rudd tradition then opposed spirit 18 with angel 18, Caliel. Nineteenth- and twentieth-century occultists added zodiacal, tarot, planetary, psychological, Enochian, devotional, and birth-angel systems. Modern games and fiction usually preserve only the name, speed, teleportation, rank, or mounted appearance.

No evidence presently connects Bathin to an ancient god. No early source gives a second form, astral projection, a female Egyptian identity, a unique Enochian office, or a dedicated de Plancy engraving. Those are later interpretations or inventions and should be labeled accordingly.
