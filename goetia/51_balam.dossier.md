> **Method and labels.** “Documented text” means that the named primary source actually says it. “Scholarly reconstruction” identifies an argument from textual history or manuscript comparison. “Tradition” marks inherited religious or magical teaching. “Esoteric correspondence” marks a deliberately constructed occult classification. “Disputed” identifies claims for which the evidence is inconclusive or contradictory. “Legend” and “modern invention” identify later narrative additions.

> **Rudd’s Balam–Hahasiah relationship is an ordinal pairing: spirit 51 with angel 51. It is not a shared zodiacal position.**

Crowley’s Goetic table puts Balam in the third decan of Leo, whereas the normal five-degree allocation of angel 51 puts Hahasiah in Sagittarius. Tables that put “Balam–Hahasiah” together in one Sagittarius quinance are hybrid modern constructions.

## The name and its origins

### 1. The ancient Balaam

**[Documented text: ancient literary tradition]** The Hebrew Bible’s Balaam is בִּלְעָם, *Bilʿām*, son of Beor. In Numbers 22–24, Balak of Moab hires him to curse Israel. Balaam receives divine messages and repeatedly blesses Israel instead. The famous ass perceives the threatening angel before Balaam does. Numbers 31:16 and later Jewish and Christian texts recast him more negatively, associating him with the affair of Peor, mercenary divination, corrupt teaching, or idolatry. The textual portrait is therefore internally ambivalent rather than uniformly demonic. See [Numbers 22–24](https://www.sefaria.org/Numbers.22) and the scholarly discussion of the narrative’s divine terminology and composition in [Pyschny, “Balaam’s God(s)”](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/14/8/967).

**[Documented text: archaeology]** Plaster inscriptions discovered at Tell Deir ʿAlla in Jordan preserve the name *blʿm br bʿr*, “Balaam son of Beor,” and call him a seer of the gods. The inscription is usually assigned to the eighth century BCE, with proposed dates varying somewhat according to palaeography and archaeological context. It demonstrates that an independent Balaam tradition circulated in Iron Age Transjordan. It does not mention the Goetic Balam’s three heads, bear, hawk, invisibility, forty legions, or seal. See [André Lemaire’s discussion](https://www.persee.fr/doc/crai_0065-0536_1985_num_129_2_14268) and [Barré, “The Portrait of Balaam in Numbers 22–24”](https://wp.ces.org.tw/wp-content/uploads/2022/12/Barre-Michael-L-Interpretation-51-1997-254-266-The-Portrait-of-Balaam-in-Numbers-22-24.pdf).

**[Disputed etymology]** No consensus etymology of *Bilʿām* explains the Goetic form. Proposals include:

- a derivation involving Hebrew *b-l-ʿ*, “to swallow/devour,” and *ʿam*, “people”;
- a punning interpretation “without/not of the people”;
- combinations involving an ancient divine element such as Bel or Baal and *ʿAm*;
- later moralizing folk etymologies, including Philo’s interpretation as a “vain people.”

These proposals concern the biblical name and do not demonstrate how *Balam* entered a sixteenth-century spirit catalogue.

### 2. Is the demon named after Balaam?

**[Disputed scholarly reconstruction]** The identification is plausible but unproved.

Evidence in favor:

1. *Balam* and Latin *Balaam* are close graphically and phonetically.
2. Both figures give supernatural information concerning what is or will be.
3. Mathers’s 1904 text explicitly prints “Balam or Balaam.”

Evidence against treating the identification as established:

1. Weyer, Scot, Sloane 3825, Sloane 2731 and Harley 6483 call the spirit **Balam**, without mentioning the biblical diviner.
2. The demon’s iconography and offices contain no Balak, Moab, ass, angel, curse, or Peor motif.
3. Mathers’s “or Balaam” is first securely visible in the requested source chain in 1904—more than three centuries after Weyer.
4. A very close sixteenth-century spirit profile exists under the quite different name **Bason**.

The safest conclusion is that Mathers either recognized, guessed, or regularized the name as the familiar biblical *Balaam*. It is possible that the sixteenth-century form had already been corrupted from *Balaam*, but no surviving witness demonstrates that transmission.

### 3. The name Balam is not Baal

**[Documented textual distinction]** Bael or Baal is Goetia spirit 1; Balam is spirit 51. *Baal* is a Northwest Semitic title meaning “lord” or “owner,” used both of gods and human possessors. No principal Balam entry calls Balam “Baal,” gives him Bael’s forms, or connects him with a specific Baal cult.

**[Disputed claim]** Modern demonographies sometimes derive almost every name beginning *Bal-* from Baal. Phonetic resemblance alone is insufficient. Balam’s strongest ancient-name candidate is Balaam, not the generic divine title Baal, but even the Balaam identification remains unproved.

### 4. The Maya word *balam*

**[Documented linguistic coincidence]** In Yucatec Maya, *balam* means “jaguar” and appears in names such as the *Books of Chilam Balam*. See the [Encyclopaedia Britannica account of the Chilam Balam books](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Chilam-Balam).

**[Modern invention/conflation]** No evidence connects this Maya word with Weyer’s European spirit catalogue. Modern encyclopedias that combine “Maya jaguar,” biblical Balaam, Abramelin’s demons and Goetic Balam into one biography are joining unrelated traditions on the strength of spelling.

## The description across the sources

## 1. Weyer, 1577

### Context and place in the list

Johann Weyer appended the *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* to the 1577 fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum*. The catalogue contains 69 numbered spirits, although its introductory material refers to a Solomonic total of 72. Weyer says the material was drawn from a work associated with the offices of spirits—often discussed under titles such as *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* or *Empto. Salomonis*—but his exact exemplar has not survived.

Balam is Weyer’s **63rd** spirit.

Weyer presented the catalogue in a polemical work opposing witch persecutions and criticizing supposed magical powers. Printing a spirit catalogue in that context does not prove that he personally practiced its conjurations.

### Latin

**[Documented text]** Weyer begins:

> “Balã Rex magnus & terribilis: prodit tribus capitibus … cauda … serpentina, oculis flammeis, equitans in urso fortissimo.”

The complete entry, at §63, says:

- Balam is a great and terrible king.
- He comes forth with three heads: first a bull, second a man, third a ram.
- He additionally has a serpent’s tail and flaming eyes.
- He rides an extremely powerful bear.
- He carries a hawk on his hand.
- His voice is hoarse.
- He answers perfectly concerning past, present and future.
- He renders a person both invisible and prudent.
- He commands forty legions.
- He was formerly of the order of Dominations.

Full Latin: [Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*, §63](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm). The 1660 *Opera omnia* printing places the passage on p. 662; the online edition also exposes variant readings and Scot’s translation.

### Textual points

- **“Accipitrem”** is a hawk or bird of prey; later English witnesses prefer goshawk.
- **“Prudentem”** means prudent, discerning, judicious or wise. The later “witty” reflects an older English sense of intelligent or mentally capable.
- **“Fortissimo”** means extremely strong or powerful. Scot’s “furious” and later Goetic “furious Bear” shift the nuance.
- Weyer contains no seal.
- Weyer gives no second form that the conjurer may demand.
- No individual time is included in Balam’s entry.

## 2. Reginald Scot, 1584

Scot incorporated an English version of Weyer’s catalogue into Book XV of *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*. Balam appears as entry 63 on **p. 326 of the 1584 first edition**; in the Brinsley Nicholson 1886 reprint it appears on p. 392.

**[Documented text]** Scot begins:

> “Balam is a great and a terrible king … he hath a serpents taile, and flaming eies.”

Scot’s complete content is:

- a great and terrible king;
- three heads, respectively bull, man and ram;
- serpent’s tail and flaming eyes;
- riding a furious bear;
- carrying a hawk on his fist;
- speaking with a hoarse voice;
- answering perfectly about present, past and future;
- making a person invisible and wise;
- governing forty legions;
- formerly of the order of Dominations.

Full entry: [Scot, *Discoverie of Witchcraft*, 1584 pagination p. 326](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/60766/60766-h/60766-h.htm).

**[Textual comparison]** Scot follows Weyer very closely. His “furious bear” is less literal than Weyer’s “most powerful bear.” “Wise” is a reasonable translation of *prudentem*. The principal conceptual change comes later when the manuscripts or their English source use “witty.”

## 3. Sloane 3825

Sloane 3825 is the most internally consistent of the principal Lemegeton manuscripts and is the base text preferred by Joseph Peterson. The British Library catalogue records it as [Sloane MS 3825](https://searcharchives.bl.uk/catalog/040-002116215).

**[Documented manuscript text]** Its Balam entry says, with original spelling and punctuation normalized here only for readability:

- Balam is “the 51 spirit in order.”
- He is a terrible, great and powerful king.
- He appears with three heads resembling a bull’s, man’s and ram’s.
- He has a serpent’s tail and flaming eyes.
- He rides a furious bear and carries a goshawk on his fist.
- He speaks with a hoarse voice.
- He gives true answers about past, present and future.
- He makes people “go invisible” and witty.
- He governs forty legions.
- His seal is to be made and worn as a lamen.

The diplomatic transcription is reproduced in [*The Testament, Key and Lemegeton of Solomon* scan/transcription](https://www.yankeeclassic.com/miskatonic/library/stacks/magic/solomon/Solomon_-_The_Testament_Key_and_Legematon_of_Solomon_%28unabridged%29.pdf).

Changes from Weyer/Scot include:

- Number 63 becomes 51.
- “Powerful” is added to the rank formula.
- “Answers perfectly” becomes “giving true answers.”
- “Wise/prudent” becomes **witty**.
- “Goshawk” replaces generic hawk.
- The instruction to manufacture and wear the seal as a lamen is added.
- Former membership in the Dominations disappears from the ordinary Goetia entry.

## 4. Sloane 2731, dated 1686

Sloane 2731 is explicitly dated 18 January 1686. It is incomplete and contains rearrangements, omissions and copying errors, but its independent readings are valuable.

Its Balam entry appears on manuscript p. 11 in the [facsimile and diplomatic transcription](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/goetia-sloane2731/read/).

**[Documented manuscript text]** It has:

- **Balam**, spirit 51;
- terrible, great and powerful king;
- bull, man and ram heads;
- serpent’s tail and flaming eyes;
- a **strong bear**, rather than Sloane 3825’s furious bear;
- goshawk on the fist;
- hoarse voice;
- true answers concerning past, present and future;
- invisibility and wit;
- forty legions;
- a seal to be made and worn as a lamen.

“Strong bear” is closer to Weyer’s *ursus fortissimus* than “furious bear.” This suggests either access to a reading closer to the Latin or preservation of an alternative English translation.

## 5. Harley 6483 and the Rudd recension

Harley 6483 is a manuscript copy made by **Peter Smart**, internally dated 1712–1713/14. Stephen Skinner and David Rankine argue that it preserves material from an earlier Thomas Rudd recension, perhaps composed around 1655–1656. The earlier date is a reconstruction; it is not the physical date of Harley 6483.

**[Documented manuscript tradition]** Balam remains spirit 51, a king with forty legions and the recognizable inherited description and seal.

**[Distinctive Rudd feature]** The spirit’s seal is paired with the seal or name of **Hahasiah**, the corresponding angel numbered 51. In the reconstructed Rudd procedure, the angelic device is placed on the reverse side of the spirit’s lamen, facing the magician’s heart. The protective angel is intended to restrain, counterbalance or defend against the associated spirit.

**[Disputed attribution]** Skinner and Rankine identify Thomas Rudd as the principal earlier compiler. Critics note that “Rudd” is an inferred attribution and that Harley 6483 is a later Smart copy containing composite material. The cautious formulation is “Rudd-associated recension preserved in Harley 6483,” not “Rudd’s autograph.”

See Skinner and Rankine, *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (Golden Hoard, 2007), and the publisher’s [catalogue description](https://www.sskinner.com/pdf/GHP-Catalogue-2022-2023.pdf).

## 6. Mathers–Crowley, 1904

S. L. MacGregor Mathers prepared the transcription or translation; Aleister Crowley edited and published it in 1904. The Balam entry occupies **pp. 38–39**.

**[Documented text]** A diagnostic passage reads:

> “He rideth upon a furious Bear, and carrieth a Boshawk upon his Fist. He speaketh with a hoarse Voice.”

The full entry identifies:

- “Balam or Balaam” as spirit 51;
- a terrible, great and powerful King;
- three heads—bull, man and ram;
- serpent’s tail and flaming eyes;
- furious bear and “Boshawk” or goshawk;
- hoarse voice;
- true answers concerning past, present and future;
- power to make people invisible and witty;
- forty legions;
- the accompanying seal.

Full entry: [Mathers–Crowley, *The Goetia*, pp. 38–39](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks05.htm). A scan of the printed page and seal is available at [From Old Books](https://www.fromoldbooks.org/Mathers-Goetia/pages/051-Seal-of-Balam/).

“Boshawk” in searchable versions is a print or transcriptional anomaly for **goshawk**. It did not generate a new spirit name.

The 1904 edition does not restore Weyer’s “formerly of the order of Dominations.”

## 7. Peterson, 2001

Joseph H. Peterson’s *The Lesser Key of Solomon: Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis* was published by Weiser in 2001. Peterson gives priority to Sloane 3825 while recording variants from other witnesses.

**[Documented editorial finding]** Peterson prints **Balam**, not Mathers’s “Balam or Balaam.” His corrigenda corrects a Latin transcriptional *responet* to *respondet*. See [Peterson’s corrigenda](https://ftp.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lkey_cor.htm) and [online Goetia](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

### Offices Balam does not have

**[Negative textual finding]** No principal Balam entry grants languages, liberal sciences, treasure-finding, love, familiars, transmutation, titles, reconciliation, flight, healing or necromancy. His stable offices are:

1. knowledge of past, present and future;
2. invisibility;
3. prudence, wisdom or wit.

Scientific and medicinal offices sometimes found beside Balam in modern tables belong to **Hahasiah**, not Balam.

### No demanded second form

**[Negative textual finding]** Weyer, Scot and the Goetia do not say that Balam must assume a second form at the magician’s command. De Plancy later describes a horned, nude rider as his more usual appearance, but this is not an instruction that the conjurer demand a transformation.

## The order: 69 spirits versus 72

Weyer’s catalogue has 69 numbered entries. The Goetia has 72.

The usual synopsis—“the Goetia merely added four”—is incomplete:

- **Pruflas**, Weyer’s spirit 4, is omitted from the Goetia.
- **Vassago**, Goetia 3, is absent from Weyer.
- **Seere**, **Dantalion** and **Andromalius**, Goetia 70–72, are absent from Weyer.

Thus the numerical change is:

> 69 Weyer spirits − 1 omitted Pruflas + 4 additions = 72 Goetic spirits.

The order is not a simple one-position shift caused by Vassago. Substantial rearrangement occurred in the middle and later parts of the catalogue. Balam moves from Weyer **63** to Goetia **51**. Peterson’s [Weyer–Goetia concordance](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm) displays the complete rearrangement.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The final total of 72 probably encouraged alignment with existing seventy-twofold religious and magical structures: the seventy-two angelic names, five-degree zodiacal divisions, and the conventional total of Solomonic spirits. That does not prove that the Goetia’s compiler derived its individual spirits from those angels.

## Possible grimoire ancestors

## 1. *Livre des Esperitz*

The *Livre des Esperitz*, preserved in Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29, is a French spirit catalogue probably compiled in the mid-sixteenth century.

It contains **Vaal**, a great king commanding thirty-nine legions, who answers questions and grants dignities or favor. It does not contain Balam’s name or his distinctive three-headed bear-rider description.

**[Scholarly finding]** Jean-Patrice Boudet’s concordance treats the *Livre*’s Vaal and Weyer’s Balam as separate entries. Vaal may contribute to the broad king-and-legions catalogue tradition, but it is not a secure Balam antecedent. See Boudet, [“Les who’s who démonologiques de la Renaissance”](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/pdf/1171), and the [French transcription of Vaal](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre_des_esperitz/Vaal).

## 2. *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* / *Book of Oberon*

Folger MS V.b.26, compiled approximately 1577–1583 and commonly called the *Book of Oberon*, preserves material headed *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*.

Its spirit **Bason**:

- makes a person invisible and wise;
- answers questions;
- appears with three heads, described as dog, man and raven;
- rides a wild bear;
- carries a goshawk;
- speaks hoarsely or emits fire, depending on the reading.

This is the closest surviving cognate profile to Balam.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Bason and Balam probably descend from related catalogue material, or one profile was remodeled from the other. The changed name and animal heads prevent certainty that they are the same spirit. Since the Folger manuscript overlaps or postdates Weyer’s 1577 printing, it cannot by itself prove pre-Weyer attestation. See the [Folger manuscript transcription](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/liber-officiorum/read/) and Harms, Clark and Peterson, *The Book of Oberon* (2015), [publisher description](https://www.llewellyn.com/product.php?ean=9780738743349).

## 3. Munich Handbook

Clm 849, the fifteenth-century Munich necromancer’s handbook edited by Richard Kieckhefer as *Forbidden Rites*, documents earlier ritual techniques, spirit catalogues and experiments. No secure **Balam** entry has been identified in it.

It is a contextual ancestor of the ritual milieu, not presently an attestation of Balam.

## 4. *Book of Abramelin*

**[Negative textual finding]** The Abramelin spirit lists include names such as Balaken and Balalos, but not the complete Goetic Balam profile. See the [Abramelin transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/abramelin/abramelin.htm).

**[Modern conflation]** Some demon encyclopedias join:

- Goetic Balam;
- a “Balaam” associated with avarice, idolatry or immorality;
- an Abramelin demon;
- the Maya word for jaguar;
- a figure commanding thirty rather than forty legions.

That composite has no single primary source behind it.

## 5. *Testament of Solomon*

The ancient or late-antique *Testament of Solomon* supplies the archetype of Solomon questioning and binding demons and assigning their thwarting angels. It includes Ornias, Beelzeboul, Asmodeus, Ephippas and the demons of the thirty-six decans, but not Balam. See the [complete *Testament of Solomon*](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Testament_of_Solomon).

**Conclusion on earliest attestation:** The earliest securely dated printed attestation of **Balam by name and profile is Weyer, 1577**. Bason supplies a nearly contemporary cognate. No securely pre-1577 Balam witness has yet been established.

## The angel: Hahasiah

## 1. The seventy-two names

Exodus 14:19–21 consists, in the traditional Hebrew consonantal count, of three verses of seventy-two letters each. The later Kabbalistic method writes:

1. verse 19 forward;
2. verse 20 backward;
3. verse 21 forward;

then reads vertical groups of three letters. This produces seventy-two triplets, collectively associated with the seventy-twofold Name.

Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa gives the procedure in *De occulta philosophia* III.25 and explains the addition of divine endings such as **-el** and **-iah** to create angelic names. See [Agrippa, Book III, chapter 25](https://www2.esotericarchives.com/agrippa/op3.htm).

**[Historical qualification]** *Shem ha-Mephorash* has not always meant precisely this 216-letter arrangement. Jewish sources also apply “explicit/distinctive Name” to the Tetragrammaton and discuss twelve-, forty-two- and seventy-two-letter names. The popular equation “Shem ha-Mephorash = these seventy-two angels” is a later narrowing of a more complicated terminology.

## 2. Name and spelling

Angel 51’s triplet is:

> **החש — He–Chet–Shin**

With the conventional *-iah* suffix it becomes forms such as:

- **Hahasiah**
- **Hahashiah**
- **Hechashiah**
- **Hechashiyah**
- **Hahasiyah**

The exact Roman spelling depends on whether the compiler transliterates ח as *h*, *ch* or *kh* and how the vowels are supplied.

**[Traditional meaning]** “The hidden God,” “God who is hidden,” or “God of concealed mysteries.”

This is a devotional interpretation of the suffixed angelic name, not a literal one-to-one translation of the three-letter root.

## 3. Medieval and Renaissance transmission

### *Sefer Raziel*

The *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* is a composite medieval magical and angelological compilation, later printed at Amsterdam in 1701. It transmits seventy-two-name materials, divine names, seals and angelic lore. It should not be treated as a single book dictated in antiquity to Adam, except when reporting its own legend. See the [YIVO account of *Sefer Raziel*](https://encyclopedia.yivo.org/article/2073).

**[Legend]** The book presents itself as angelic revelation given to Adam and transmitted through figures including Noah and Solomon.

**[Historical finding]** Its surviving textual strata are medieval and composite.

### Reuchlin, 1517

Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* helped circulate Latin Christian-Kabbalistic forms of the seventy-two angelic names, including Hahasiah-type spellings. A digital copy is catalogued by the [Badische Landesbibliothek](https://digital.blb-karlsruhe.de/blbihd/content/titleinfo/7174105).

### Agrippa, 1533

Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia* III.25 provides a clear Latin explanation of the three-verse construction and angelic suffixes. This is a demonstrable textual ancestor of later Western ceremonial-magic tables.

### Kircher, 1652–1654

Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* reproduces and elaborates the seventy-twofold scheme in large synoptic tables, associating the names with verses, languages and cosmological diagrams. See the scan of [*Oedipus Aegyptiacus*, volume II](https://www.esonet.org/wp-content/uploads/2010/06/Oedipus_Aegyptiacus02.pdf).

Kircher is significant for the visual and tabular transmission of the names, but he is not evidence that Hahasiah was already paired with Balam.

## 4. Rudd’s constraining angel

**[Documented esoteric construction]** In Harley 6483’s Rudd-associated system, the seventy-two Goetic spirits are opposed by the correspondingly numbered angels. Thus:

> Goetia 51, Balam → Shem angel 51, Hahasiah.

The angelic name or seal is put on the reverse of the demon’s lamen, toward the operator’s heart. The theory is that the divine and angelic hierarchy constrains the infernal spirit and protects the operator against deception or malice.

This pairing is structurally reminiscent of the *Testament of Solomon*, in which demons disclose which angels frustrate them, but the individual Balam–Hahasiah equation is an early modern construction, not found in the ancient testament.

Skinner and Rankine made this system broadly accessible in *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (2007). Their argument that the recension originated with Thomas Rudd is plausible but not universally accepted.

## 5. Lenain, 1823

Lazare Républicain Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* presents the angel as number 51.

**[Documented text]** Lenain gives:

- Name: **Hahasiah**
- Divine attribute: **Dieu caché**, “Hidden God”
- Degrees: **251st through 255th degrees** of the zodiac
- Zodiacal conversion: **10°–15° Sagittarius**
- Decan: the 26th decan of the zodiac, under the Moon
- Contrary or opposing genius: **Sesmè/Sesmé**
- Invocation period: **4:40–5:00 p.m.**
- Five presidency days: **9 May, 20 July, 30 September, 11 December and 21 February**
- Psalm: Lenain prints “the 32nd verse of Psalm 103”
- Latin verse: the sentence meaning, “May the glory of the Lord endure forever; may the Lord rejoice in his works.”

Lenain’s quotation corresponds to **Vulgate Psalm 103:31**, or **Psalm 104:31** in Hebrew- and English-numbered Bibles. His “32nd verse” is probably a counting discrepancy, possibly involving the superscription.

Hahasiah’s favorable offices in Lenain are:

- elevation of the soul;
- contemplation of divine mysteries;
- chemistry and physics;
- knowledge of animal, vegetable and mineral properties;
- medicine, cures and useful discoveries;
- the philosopher’s stone and universal medicine.

The contrary genius governs charlatanry and the abuse of credulity.

See Lenain, [*La science cabalistique*, 1823 scan](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1823__lenain___science_cabalistique.pdf).

**Important distinction:** Lenain’s five dates are discontinuous days distributed through the year. He does **not** give the later continuous “3–7 December birth angel” system.

## 6. Papus, Ambelain and Haziel

### Papus

Papus—Gérard Encausse—reproduced and popularized Christian-Kabbalistic angel tables and Lenain-derived material in works including *La Cabbale*. See the [Warburg Institute scan](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/pdf/abg405b2732165.pdf).

### Robert Ambelain

Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (Paris: Niclaus, 1951) systematized angelic names, seals, invocations, psalms and ritual practice for a twentieth-century esoteric readership. See the [1951 bibliographical record](https://edition-originale.com/fr/oeuvres/religions-and-spiritualites-8/esoterisme-and-spiritisme-38/ambelain-la-kabbale-pratique-1951-44828).

### Kabaleb and Haziel

François Brousse/Kabaleb-style angelology and the French writer-translator **Haziel** popularized a guardian-angel calendar in which Hahasiah is commonly assigned **3–7 December**, together with secondary days and a daily twenty-minute interval around 4:40–5:00.

The Bibliothèque nationale de France records Kabaleb’s *Les Anges*, French version by Haziel, Paris: Bussière, 1989: [BnF catalogue record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb35062108t).

**[Modern esoteric development]** These continuous five-day “birth angel” blocks are later calendar rationalizations. They should not be backdated to Lenain.

**[Potential confusion]** Haziel the twentieth-century author or translator is not the same as **Haziel**, angel 9 in the seventy-two-name list.

## Correspondences

### Balam and Hahasiah compared

| System | Balam | Hahasiah | Evidentiary status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weyer, 1577 | Spirit 63, King, 40 legions | No pairing | Documented |
| Goetia manuscripts | Spirit 51, King, 40 legions | No pairing in ordinary Goetia text | Documented |
| Harley 6483/Rudd | Spirit 51 | Angel 51, protective counterforce | Documented esoteric pairing |
| Rank correspondence | Sun; gold | Not derived from Balam’s rank | Goetic ritual correspondence |
| Continuous 5° zodiac | No original Balam quinance | 10°–15° Sagittarius | Later angelic correspondence |
| Golden Dawn decan | — | 10°–20° Sagittarius, Nine of Wands, Moon | Esoteric correspondence |
| Crowley’s *777* Goetic table | Night ruler of 20°–30° Leo | Listed separately in the Shem columns | Esoteric correspondence |
| Balam tarot pip | Seven of Wands | Nine of Wands for Hahasiah’s containing decan | They do not coincide |
| Balam decan planet | Mars in Leo | Moon in Sagittarius | Separate from Balam’s rank planet |
| Later guardian dates | — | Usually 3–7 December | Modern angelological calendar |
| Lenain’s actual presidency dates | — | 9 May; 20 July; 30 September; 11 December; 21 February | Documented 1823 table |

### Crowley’s *777*

Crowley’s *Liber 777* was first issued in 1909; the expanded *777 Revised* appeared posthumously in 1955.

Its Goetic columns divide the seventy-two spirits into thirty-six day rulers and thirty-six night rulers:

- spirits 1–36 are day spirits;
- spirits 37–72 are night spirits;
- each ten-degree decan therefore carries one day and one night Goetic spirit.

Balam, number 51, is the night member corresponding to decan number 15:

> 51 − 36 = 15.

Counting from 0° Aries, the fifteenth decan is **20°–30° Leo**. Its Golden Dawn tarot pip is the **Seven of Wands**, conventionally Mars in Leo. Balam’s day counterpart is spirit 15, **Eligor**.

See Crowley, [*Liber 777*](https://hermetic.com/_media/93beast.fea.st/files/section1/777/liber_777.pdf) and [*777 Revised*](https://hermetic.com/_media/93beast.fea.st/files/section1/777/liber_777_revised.pdf).

### Why some tables give Sagittarius

The Shem angels are commonly allocated in five-degree units beginning at 0° Aries. Angel 51 therefore begins at:

> (51 − 1) × 5° = 250° = 10° Sagittarius.

Hahasiah governs 10°–15° Sagittarius. Angel 52 governs 15°–20° Sagittarius. Together they occupy the second Sagittarius decan, associated with the **Nine of Wands** and the Moon.

Thus a tarot pip can carry:

- two Shem angels, one for each five-degree half;
- two Goetic spirits in *777*, one day and one night.

But these are separate enumerative overlays. Rudd’s angel–demon partners do not necessarily occupy the same zodiacal sector.

### Rank metals and planets

The Goetia’s general observations assign seals as follows:

| Rank | Metal | Planet |
|---|---|---|
| Kings | Gold | Sun |
| Dukes | Copper | Venus |
| Princes or Prelates | Tin | Jupiter |
| Marquises | Silver | Moon |
| Presidents | Mercury | Mercury |
| Earls or Counts | Copper and silver in equal parts | Venus and Moon |
| Knights | Lead | Saturn |

Balam is a **King**, so his proper seal-metal is **gold**, corresponding to the Sun. See [*Goetia*, “Observations”](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks06.htm).

This solar rank correspondence is independent of Balam’s Mars-ruled Leo decan in *777*.

### Timing of conjuration

**[Documented Goetic rule]** The chief kings may be bound from:

- the ninth hour of the morning until noon;
- the third hour after noon until sunset.

In ordinary clock language, this is customarily rendered **9 a.m.–noon and 3 p.m.–sunset**, although premodern magical hours can also be interpreted as unequal planetary hours.

**[Documented lunar rule]** The 1904 observations prefer the 2nd, 4th, 6th, 8th, 10th, 12th and 14th days of the Moon. Sloane 2731 gives the 9th where the printed edition gives the 8th.

**[Later extrapolation]** Conjuring Balam on Sunday or in a solar planetary hour follows the King–Sun correspondence, but Balam’s entry does not itself prescribe Sunday.

**[Later astrological overlay]** A practitioner following *777* might time work to the Sun’s transit through 20°–30° Leo, approximately mid-to-late August. That is a Crowley/Golden Dawn synthesis, not a Weyer or seventeenth-century manuscript instruction.

### Enochian overlays

**[Negative historical finding]** Balam’s entry contains no Enochian language, Dee–Kelley angel, Watchtower, Aethyr or Enochian tablet.

The Golden Dawn used Goetic, Kabbalistic, tarot, astrological and Enochian materials in a larger synthetic curriculum. Crowley likewise placed Goetic and Enochian data in adjacent tabular systems. That institutional proximity made later cross-system rituals easy to construct, but it does not make Balam originally Enochian.

Crowley’s “Preliminary Invocation” or “Bornless Ritual” is ultimately adapted through Charles Wycliffe Goodwin from a Greek magical text; it is not an Enochian invocation.

## The seal

### Function

**[Documented manuscript practice]** The manuscripts direct that Balam’s seal be made and worn as a **lamen**. The Goetia’s rank rules specify gold for a King.

Weyer and Scot do not print a Balam seal. Seals enter the surviving Balam chain with the Lemegeton manuscripts.

### Form

The familiar printed seal is an elongated monoline figure organized around a vertical stem. Its conspicuous features are:

- a small hooked or bent terminal at the top;
- paired, opposing curved strokes around the upper or central stem;
- an enclosed or nearly enclosed central figure produced by overlapping curves;
- a descending vertical;
- a divided lower structure with two outward and upward hooks;
- short transverse or hooked terminal strokes.

The 1904 form can be examined directly at [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:51-Balam_seal.png) and in the [scan of pp. 38–39](https://www.fromoldbooks.org/Mathers-Goetia/pages/051-Seal-of-Balam/).

### Variants

**[Documented material variation]** The Sloane and Harley drawings remain recognizably homologous but differ in:

- whether central curves touch, cross or remain slightly open;
- the tightness of the upper curls;
- the length and angle of the central staff;
- whether lower hooks are rounded or angular;
- small terminal strokes;
- general symmetry and penmanship.

No manuscript explains these differences as different “versions” with distinct powers. They are best treated as scribal and engraving variants unless further evidence emerges.

In the Rudd recension, the significant innovation is not principally a new Balam glyph but the **angelic counterseal on the reverse**.

## Iconography

## 1. Weyer through the Goetia

The textual image is a composite sovereignty-and-divination monster:

- bull’s head;
- human head;
- ram’s head;
- serpent’s tail;
- flaming eyes;
- powerful bear mount;
- goshawk carried like a noble falconer;
- hoarse voice.

The bear and hawk evoke aristocratic hunting and command, while the multiple heads visually express multiplied sight. Interpreting the three heads as symbols of past, present and future is an attractive modern reading, but the early texts never make that equation explicitly.

Balam’s three heads partially duplicate **Asmoday’s** Goetic anatomy: bull, man and ram, serpent’s tail and fiery emission or breath. Asmoday, however, rides a dragon, has goose-like feet and carries a lance and banner.

**[Disputed reconstruction]** The duplication may result from copying, recombination of catalogue motifs or shared ancestry. It is insufficient to prove that Balam and Asmoday were once one spirit.

## 2. Collin de Plancy

Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* first appeared in 1818 and passed through multiple revisions. The securely verified illustrated **sixth edition of 1863** calls the spirit **Balan**, on pp. 76–77.

A short diagnostic phrase calls him:

> “roi grand et terrible dans les enfers.”

The full entry preserves the bull, human and ram heads, serpent’s tail, fiery eyes, bear, hawk, hoarse voice, temporal knowledge, forty legions and former membership in the Dominations.

De Plancy adds or emphasizes:

- that Balan only “sometimes” has three heads;
- that he more ordinarily appears nude and horned, astride a bear;
- that he teaches tricks, cunning or finesse;
- that he supplies the convenient means of seeing without being seen.

Full French entry: [Collin de Plancy, “Balan,” 1863](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.%2C_1863/Balan).

“À cheval … sur un ours” means astride or mounted on a bear; it does not require a separate horse.

### Louis Le Breton’s engraving

Louis Le Breton’s portrait shows a muscular nude, horned, broadly human-bodied figure riding a shaggy bear and carrying a hawk on his raised hand. The engraving simplifies the literal three-headed description into a forceful composite horned figure. It does not plainly show three fully separate heads.

The public-domain image is available as [*Balan (Demon)*](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Balan_(Demon).png).

**[Historical qualification]** The famous Le Breton portraits belong to the illustrated 1863 edition, not the original 1818 edition. The 1818 work established the dictionary, but one should not project the 1863 engraving backward into it.

## 3. Modern art

Modern Balam art generally follows one of four models:

1. literal three-headed bear rider;
2. Le Breton’s nude horned falconer;
3. anthropomorphic king with bull or ram attributes;
4. abstract sigil-centered devotional or ritual art.

These are contemporary interpretations. Even when marketed as “traditional,” their color schemes, costumes, gender presentation, wings, crowns and weaponry often have no textual basis in Weyer or the manuscripts.

## People and works

### Johann Weyer

Weyer provides the earliest secure printed Balam entry in 1577. As a physician and critic of witch persecutions, he used demonological material partly to argue that alleged witches were deceived, deluded or ill rather than wielders of the powers attributed to them.

### Reginald Scot

Scot translated or adapted Weyer’s catalogue in 1584 within another skeptical work. His English vocabulary—especially “furious bear” and “wise”—shaped the later vernacular transmission.

### The Lemegeton compilers and scribes

Anonymous seventeenth-century compilers reorganized Weyer’s catalogue into 72 spirits, added seals and lamen instructions, revised descriptions and incorporated the catalogue into the five-part *Lesser Key of Solomon*.

### Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

The Rudd-associated tradition superimposed the seventy-two Shem angels on the seventy-two spirits. Peter Smart copied Harley 6483 in the early eighteenth century. The degree of Thomas Rudd’s personal authorship remains debated.

### Mathers and Crowley

Mathers prepared the text that Crowley issued in 1904. The edition added Mathers’s annotations and Crowley’s prefatory material, including Crowley’s psychological interpretation of spirits as portions or faculties of the human brain.

**[Negative finding]** Publication and interpretation do not establish that Crowley personally evoked Balam. No well-documented Crowley Balam operation has been identified in the materials consulted.

### Frater Achad

Charles Stansfeld Jones, Frater Achad, wrote extensively on Qabalah and Crowleyan number symbolism. No Balam-specific working or historically influential Balam interpretation was located. His inclusion in lists of “people associated with Balam” normally reflects his general Thelemic and Qabalistic work, not a documented connection.

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 edition collated the manuscript witnesses and restored the dominant reading **Balam**. His online archives also make Weyer, Scot, the Goetia and Abramelin available for direct comparison.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their 2007 *Goetia of Dr Rudd* brought Harley 6483’s angelic counterseal system into modern circulation. They are the principal modern source for the explicit Balam–Hahasiah pairing.

### Poke Runyon

Runyon’s *Book of Solomon’s Magick* and Ordo Templi Astartes practice promote a ritual system involving the “Master Mandala” and dark-mirror methods. His books reproduce and operationalize Goetic catalogues, including Balam. No independently documented Balam-specific operation or historical discovery was found. See [Poke Runyon’s author biography](https://www.pokerunyon.com/about).

### Lon Milo DuQuette

DuQuette and Christopher Hyatt’s *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* reframed Crowley’s edition for a modern audience. DuQuette’s *Tarot of Ceremonial Magick* visually implements *777*-style allocations, including the day/night Goetic decan scheme. Its Balam association is therefore the Seven of Wands/third Leo decan, not Hahasiah’s Sagittarius quinance.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s *Pandemonium* and broader *Encyclopaedia Goetica* project compare multiple spirit catalogues and advocate reading the grimoire spirits as an inherited spirit tradition rather than merely psychological symbols. His work encourages comparison of Balam with figures such as Bason, but does not supply an undisputed pre-Weyer Balam. See the [publisher’s author page](https://scarletimprint.com/jake-stratton-kent).

### Modern scholarship

- **Owen Davies**, *Grimoires: A History of Magic Books*, provides the broad history of grimoire production, transmission and reception.
- **Richard Kieckhefer**, *Forbidden Rites*, edits and contextualizes the Munich handbook.
- **Jean-Patrice Boudet** supplies the particularly relevant scholarly concordance for the *Livre des Esperitz* and Weyer.
- **Jan Veenstra** has worked on the *Holy Almandal*, relevant to the wider Lemegeton but not a direct Balam source.
- **Julien Véronèse** studies medieval Solomonic and *Ars notoria* traditions; he is relevant to the larger manuscript environment. The specific *Livre des Esperitz* concordance used here is Boudet’s, not Véronèse’s.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Balam versus Balaam

**Finding:** possible but unproved identity.

- First explicit equation in the requested chain: Mathers–Crowley, 1904.
- Earlier witnesses: consistently Balam.
- Supporting resemblance: name and divinatory office.
- Missing evidence: no biblical narrative motifs or intermediary manuscript explaining the transformation.

Label: **disputed reconstruction**, not documented historical fact.

### 2. Balam versus Bason

Bason has the strongest matching profile: three heads, bear, goshawk, hoarse or fiery manifestation, invisibility, wisdom and answers.

Possible explanations include:

- name corruption;
- parallel versions of one catalogue item;
- transfer of one spirit’s description to another;
- both deriving from a lost *Liber Officiorum* exemplar.

Because the manuscripts are nearly contemporary and the heads differ, none can presently be proved.

Label: **strong textual relationship; exact identity disputed**.

### 3. Balam versus Asmoday

Both have bull, human and ram heads and serpentine or fiery features. Their mounts, emblems, ranks and offices differ. The repeated anatomy may be a compiler’s duplicated formula.

Label: **documented similarity; conflation unproved**.

### 4. Balam versus Abalam

Abalam or Abalim appears as one of the kings accompanying Paimon in the ninth Goetic entry. Balam is separately numbered 51 with an independent seal and profile.

Label: **distinct in the surviving Goetia**. A corruption from one name to the other is possible only as speculation.

### 5. “Thirty legions” or “fifty-one legions”

The main chain uniformly gives **forty legions**.

- Thirty is found in composite modern demonographies that merge Balam with another Balaam tradition.
- Fifty-one is apparently an error produced by copying the spirit’s ordinal number into the legion field.

Label: **modern error**.

### 6. “Formerly a Dominion”

Weyer and Scot explicitly carry the tradition that Balam was formerly of the order of Dominations. The ordinary Goetia entry omits it.

Label: **documented in 1577 and 1584; absent from later principal entry**, not disproved.

### 7. Wisdom versus wit

- Weyer: *prudentem*.
- Scot: wise.
- Manuscript Goetia and 1904: witty.

In early modern English, “witty” can mean intelligent, ingenious or possessed of understanding. It need not mean humorous.

Label: **translation and semantic development**, not a change from wisdom to comedy.

### 8. Hawk, goshawk and “boshawk”

- Weyer: *accipiter*, hawk.
- Scot: hawk.
- Manuscripts: goshawk.
- Some 1904 transcriptions: boshawk.

“Boshawk” is a typographic or transcriptional mistake, not a special occult bird.

### 9. Powerful bear versus furious bear

Weyer’s *fortissimus* and Sloane 2731’s “strong” concern strength. Scot, Sloane 3825 and Mathers–Crowley say “furious.” Both readings belong to the transmission, but “most powerful” is closer to the Latin.

### 10. De Plancy’s “second form”

De Plancy’s nude, horned bear rider is frequently presented online as a second form the magician may demand. De Plancy actually says that Balan “more ordinarily” appears that way.

Label: **documented nineteenth-century alternative depiction; not a conjuration command**.

### 11. The Hahasiah pairing

The Balam–Hahasiah pairing is real in the Rudd-associated ordinal system. It is not part of Weyer, Scot or the ordinary Goetia description.

Label: **documented early modern esoteric correspondence, not ancient identity**.

### 12. Zodiacal contradiction

There is no contradiction once the systems are separated:

- Hahasiah #51 → 10°–15° Sagittarius by continuous five-degree angelic enumeration.
- Balam #51 → night spirit of the fifteenth decan, 20°–30° Leo, by *777*’s separate 36 day/36 night Goetic arrangement.

Label: **two independent correspondential algorithms**.

### 13. The “birth angel” calendar

Hahasiah’s popular 3–7 December interval is not Lenain’s original five-date table. It belongs to later twentieth-century angelological systems spread through Kabaleb, Haziel and related publications.

Label: **modern esoteric development**.

### 14. No demonstrated pre-Weyer Balam

The biblical Balaam is ancient; the demon Balam’s profile is not. Neither the *Testament of Solomon*, Munich handbook, *Livre des Esperitz* nor Abramelin supplies a secure earlier occurrence of the complete name and description.

Label: **absence of evidence and current documentary conclusion**.

## Open questions

1. **What was Weyer’s immediate manuscript exemplar?**  
   His references imply an office-of-spirits text, but no extant witness can be identified as his exact copy.

2. **Were Bason and Balam originally one catalogue item?**  
   The matching bear, bird, voice, invisibility and wisdom are striking. The changed name and heads remain unexplained.

3. **Did “Balam” originate as a scribal contraction of Balaam?**  
   Mathers believed or suspected so, but no pre-1904 source in the requested chain states it.

4. **Why does Balam share his three principal heads with Asmoday?**  
   Common symbolism, formulaic copying and description-transfer are all possible.

5. **When exactly was the catalogue rearranged from Weyer’s 69 to the Goetia’s 72?**  
   The surviving seventeenth-century manuscripts show the result, not the compiler’s working notes.

6. **How early is the Rudd angelic counterseal system?**  
   Harley 6483 is early eighteenth century. Skinner and Rankine’s mid-seventeenth-century Thomas Rudd archetype is an informed reconstruction that would benefit from an earlier physical witness.

7. **What is the complete stemma of Balam’s seal?**  
   A definitive answer requires direct, high-resolution comparison of every relevant folio, including line order, corrections and ink layers—not merely modern redrawn tables.

8. **Does the biblical name supply more than the spelling?**  
   No textual evidence yet connects the biblical Balaam’s ass, Balak, curses or oracles directly to the demon’s bear, hawk and invisibility.

9. **Does De Plancy’s nude horned form come from an unidentified source?**  
   It may be editorial elaboration, inherited folklore or an undocumented source. The early Balam entries do not contain it.
