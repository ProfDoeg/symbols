# Alloces: Research Dossier

> **Evidence labels used**
>
> - **Documented text** — stated or pictured in an identifiable primary source.
> - **Scholarly reconstruction** — an argument based on manuscript comparison, textual transmission, or historical context.
> - **Tradition** — a claim transmitted within a magical, religious, or devotional lineage.
> - **Esoteric correspondence** — a deliberately constructed occult association rather than a historical identity.
> - **Disputed claim** — proposed but inadequately demonstrated or contradicted by other evidence.
> - **Legend** — a narrative presented within the grimoire tradition without independent historical evidence.
> - **Modern adaptation/invention** — post-grimoire elaboration, fiction, game material, or recent occult synthesis.

| Golden Dawn angel table | 15°–20° Aries, paired with the **Three of Wands**, Sun in Aries |
| Crowley’s *777* Goetic decan | Night spirit of 0°–10° Virgo, paired with **Eight of Disks/Pentacles**, Sun in Virgo, “Prudence” |
| De Plancy portrait | Yes: Louis Le Breton’s 1863 engraving of a lion-headed rider on a huge, partly fantastical horse |

Three different astrological constructions therefore have to be kept separate:

1. Alloces’s **rank** corresponds to Venus and copper.
2. Imamiah’s **Lenain/Rudd-derived position** is the second five-degree section of the Moon-ruled Sagittarius decan, giving the Nine of Wands.
3. Crowley’s *777* assigns **Goetic spirit no. 52 itself** to the nocturnal half of the first Virgo decan, giving the Eight of Disks.

They are layered systems, not three statements of one ancient correspondence.

## The name and its origins

### Secure spelling history

| Date/source | Form | Status |
|---|---|---|
| 1577, Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* | **Alocer** | Earliest secure printed attestation |
| 1584, Scot, *Discoverie of Witchcraft* | **Allocer** | English translation or adaptation of Weyer |
| Late sixteenth century, Folger V.b.26 | **Lecher** | Possible cognate or parallel description; relationship unresolved |
| Seventeenth century, Sloane 3825 | **Alloces** | Principal Goetia manuscript reading |
| Sloane 2731 | **Alloces** | Same principal Goetic reading |
| Sloane 3648 | **Alloces** | Same |
| Harley 6483 | **Alloces** | Rudd-attributed copy |
| 1825/1863, Collin de Plancy | **Alocer** | Returns to Weyer’s spelling |
| 1904, Mathers–Crowley | **Alloces, or Alocas** | “Alocas” first becomes influential through this edition |
| 1911, A. E. Waite | **Allocen; Alloien** | Apparently corrupt or secondary readings |
| 2001, Joseph H. Peterson | **Alloces** | Sloane 3825 base text; variants documented in the apparatus |

Peterson’s comparative concordance gives the manuscript and editorial forms side by side: Weyer **Alocer**, Scot **Allocer**, the main Goetia manuscripts **Alloces**, and Mathers–Crowley **Alloces or Alocas**. It also records Waite’s unstable **Allocen/Alloien** readings. [Peterson’s comparative spirit table](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia_spirits.pdf)

### Is the name ancient?

**Finding: no ancient god, hero, city, Greek noun, Latin title, Hebrew demon, or Mesopotamian being can presently be demonstrated as the source of “Alocer.”**

The earliest secure, unmistakable occurrence is Weyer’s 1577 Latin catalog. Jean-Patrice Boudet’s comparison of the Munich handbook, the Florentine magical material, the *Livre des Esperitz* and Weyer lists **Alocer only in Weyer’s column**. It does not identify a medieval counterpart. [Boudet, “Les who’s who démonologiques”](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/1019)

That distinguishes Alloces from Goetic names with comparatively transparent histories:

- Bael reflects **Baal**, the Northwest Semitic title “lord.”
- Astaroth ultimately reflects **Astarte/Ashtoreth**.
- Asmoday belongs to the **Aeshma/Asmodeus** complex.
- Belial comes from Hebrew **bĕli-yaʿal**, conventionally “worthlessness” or “without worth.”
- Phenex is the Greek/Latin **phoenix**.
- Amon invites comparison with Egyptian **Amun**, although individual grimoire transformations still need evidence.

No comparably defensible derivation exists for Alloces.

### Proposed etymologies

Several modern websites and occult dictionaries propose:

- Akkadian or Mesopotamian **alû**, a night demon;
- an alleged Akkadian **allutu**, “crab”;
- Greek *allos* combined with an invented second element;
- Latin *allocare*, “to place, allot or hire”;
- a meaning such as “good king”;
- an otherwise unknown ancient astral or leonine divinity.

These are **disputed modern conjectures**. They depend on phonetic resemblance, do not document an intermediate form, and do not explain why Weyer’s spelling first appears in 1577. Neither Boudet’s comparative table nor Peterson’s manuscript apparatus supplies such an ancestry.

The proposed **alû** relationship is particularly weak: a shared opening sound is not a transmission history. The alleged “crab” etymology likewise lacks a traceable philological source. Alloces’s astronomy office does not by itself turn the name into a constellation or zodiacal term.

### Alugor, Algor and Eligor

A recurrent online claim equates Alloces with **Alugor**, **Algor**, or **Allogor** from earlier spirit catalogs. The manuscript comparison argues against it.

Boudet’s table aligns medieval **Alugor/Abugor** with Weyer’s **Eligor, alias Abigor**, not Alocer. Eligor is a separate Goetic duke associated with a knight, lance, ensign or sceptre, warfare and favor from lords. Thus:

- **Scholarly reconstruction:** Alugor → Abugor/Abigor → Eligor is a plausible chain.
- **Disputed claim:** Alugor or Algor → Alloces is probably a conflation produced by similar-looking names and the fact that both spirits can appear as mounted warriors.

### The “Lecher” problem

The most intriguing near-parallel occurs in Folger manuscript V.b.26, an Elizabethan *Book of Magic* or *Offices of Spirits* manuscript containing dates 1577 and 1583. Its sixth spirit is called **Lecher** and is described as knowing the secrets of the seven sciences, obtaining friendship, and appearing like a knight with a red lion’s face. [Folger V.b.26 catalog record](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/228887?ln=en); [Peterson/Harms transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/folger/v_b_26_transcription.pdf)

The shared combination is unusually close:

- knight or soldier;
- red lion face;
- sciences or liberal sciences;
- assistance involving friendship or a household/familiars.

This may represent a distorted name from the same lost office-of-spirits tradition. It is not, however, secure evidence for a pre-Weyer Alloces:

- the manuscript’s written dates overlap with or follow Weyer’s 1577 edition;
- “Lecher” is not an obvious paleographic expansion of “Alocer”;
- the direction of borrowing cannot be established;
- both texts might descend from an unknown common source.

Accordingly, **Lecher is a possible cognate or parallel, not an established earlier spelling**.

## Number, order and the 69/72 problem

### Weyer

Alloces is §64 in the 1577 *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*. Weyer’s catalog contains 69 spirits, although its Belial paragraph preserves the traditional assertion that Solomon confined 72 kings and their hosts. [Weyer, Latin text and comparative notes](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

### Scot

Scot’s 1584 English catalog is not consistently numbered. It omits Weyer’s fourth spirit, **Pruflas/Bufas**, so Allocer is effectively the 63rd surviving entry even though modern parallel editions often retain Weyer’s section number 64 for ease of comparison.

This is one source of statements that Alloces is “demon 63 in the *Pseudomonarchia*.” Strictly:

- Weyer’s printed position: **64**.
- Position after Scot’s omission: **63**.
- Goetia position: **52**.

### Goetia

The *Ars Goetia* contains 72 entries. Its compiler appears to have begun with the Weyer–Scot inventory, altered its sequence and supplied four spirits absent from Weyer:

1. Vassago;
2. Seere;
3. Dantalion;
4. Andromalius.

It also omits Weyer’s Pruflas. The arithmetic is therefore:

> Weyer’s 69 − Pruflas + four additions = Goetia’s 72.

Peterson argues from close wording and errors that the Goetia catalog depends particularly on Scot’s English text, not independently on Weyer’s Latin. The changed sequence may reflect deliberate rearrangement, physical leaf order in a source, or more than one stage of compilation. Alloces moves from Weyer’s no. 64 to Goetia no. 52. [Peterson’s *Goetia* introduction and text](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm)

## The description across the sources

### Quotation note

The short quotations below identify decisive wording; the linked public-domain transcriptions and facsimiles contain the complete paragraphs. The rest is rendered as close, clause-by-clause English rather than extended verbatim reproduction.

### Weyer, 1577

**Documented text:** Johann Weyer, *De praestigiis daemonum*, fifth edition, Basel, 1577, *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*, §64, printed p. 930.

Weyer begins:

> “Alocer Dux magnus & fortis: procedit ut miles in equo vasto insidens: facies eius leonina, rubicunda valde cum oculis flammeis.”

A close translation of the complete notice is:

> Alocer is a great and strong duke. He comes forth as a soldier seated on a huge horse. His face is leonine and very red, with fiery or flaming eyes. He speaks gravely or in a deep manner. He makes a person remarkable in astronomy and all the liberal arts. He bestows a good *familia*. He rules thirty-six legions.

The Latin is available in Peterson’s transcription alongside manuscript and English comparisons. [Weyer, §64](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

#### Important Latin ambiguities

- **“in equo vasto”** — literally on a vast, immense or huge horse. It does not say the horse is demonic, winged or reptilian.
- **“graviter loquitur”** — speaks gravely, weightily, heavily or deeply. “Hoarse” is a later sharpening.
- **“hominem reddit admirabilem”** — renders a human remarkable or admirable, not merely “teaches.”
- **“confert bonam familiam”** — can mean that he provides a good household, retinue or company. In early modern magical reception it became “good familiars.”

The last phrase explains two divergent traditions. Scot and the Goetia understand *familia* as familiar spirits; Collin de Plancy’s 1825 edition interprets it more domestically as happiness in one’s family.

### Scot, 1584

**Documented text:** Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, London, 1584, Book XV, chapter 2, printed pp. 391–392.

Scot’s entry begins:

> “Allocer is a strong duke and a great, he commeth foorth like a soldier, riding on a great horsse.”

The remainder says that he has a very red lion’s face and flaming eyes, speaks with a “big” voice, makes a person wonderful in astronomy and all the liberal sciences, brings good familiars, and rules 36 legions. [Scot’s spirit catalog](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/scot16.htm); [1584 facsimile](https://archive.org/details/discoverieofwitc00scot)

Compared with Weyer:

- **Alocer** becomes **Allocer**.
- “Speaks gravely” becomes “speaks with a big voice.”
- *Artes liberales* becomes “liberal sciences.”
- *Bonam familiam* becomes “good familiars.”

Peterson notes that the English translation may have been prepared or assisted by Abraham Fleming and that it contains readings suggestive of a faulty manuscript or loose translation. Scot presented this material in a skeptical work intended to expose alleged magical arts, not as an autobiographical record of conjuration.

### Sloane 3825 and the Goetia recension

**Documented text:** British Library Sloane MS 3825, fol. 108r; transcribed in Peterson’s 2001 edition, p. 31.

The entry calls him “the 52d spirit,” Alloces, and describes him as a great, mighty and strong duke. It preserves the soldier, enormous horse, very red lion face and flaming eyes. It says:

> “his office is to teach ye art of Astronomy, & all ye Liberall siences, he Bringeth good familiars”

It assigns him 36 legions and adds something absent from Weyer and Scot: “his seal is thus made,” followed by the glyph, which is to be worn. [Peterson’s Sloane-based text](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm#alloces)

Changes in this recension include:

- “makes a person remarkable” becomes the more instructional “teaches.”
- The voice is now both **hoarse** and very big.
- The previously unattested seal is integral to the operation.
- Alloces is no. 52 rather than no. 64.
- No second form is introduced.

### Sloane 2731

**Documented text:** Sloane 2731 also reads **Alloces** and preserves substantially the same office and appearance. Peterson uses it among the witnesses in his apparatus rather than as the base manuscript.

Its seal belongs to the same recognizable design family as Sloane 3825. Differences are scribal: angle, proportion, closure of loops and the length or curvature of terminals. There is no evidence that Sloane 2731 preserves a completely different emblem or a different entity.

### Harley 6483

**Documented text/tradition:** Harley 6483, copied by Peter Smart and internally associated with “Dr Rudd,” likewise reads **Alloces** and places it at 52. It incorporates the angelic counterweight associated with Imamiah.

Its conspicuous textual variant is **30 legions**, where Weyer, Scot, Sloane 3825 and the printed Goetia have 36. Peterson records this variant in his apparatus. The discrepancy is best treated as a scribal or local recension variant; no doctrinal explanation accompanies it. [Peterson, *Lesser Key*, Alloces and note](https://logoilibrary.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Lesser-Key-of-Solomon.pdf)

### Mathers–Crowley, 1904

**Documented text:** *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King*, translated by S. L. MacGregor Mathers and edited/published by Aleister Crowley, 1904, no. 52, printed p. 39 in the original pagination.

Mathers gives the headword:

> “ALLOCES.—The Fifty-second Spirit is Alloces, or Alocas. He is a Duke, Great, Mighty, and Strong.”

He repeats the soldier on a great horse, very red leonine face, flaming eyes, astronomy, liberal sciences, good familiars and 36 legions. His voice is “hoarse and very big.” The sigil is to be worn as a lamen. [Sacred Texts transcription](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks05.htm); [1904 facsimile](https://biglevi.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/The-Book-Of-The-Goetia-Of-Solomon-The-King-A-Crowley-1904.pdf)

The spelling **Alocas** becomes visible here, but there is no evidence that it represents an older independent being. It is best classified as an editorial or manuscript variant popularized by Mathers.

### Peterson, 2001

**Documented scholarly edition:** Joseph H. Peterson, ed., *The Lesser Key of Solomon*, York Beach: Weiser, 2001, p. 31.

Peterson returns to Sloane 3825 as the base reading, **Alloces**, and separates manuscript evidence from Mathers’s editorial forms. He also restores the ability to compare the Goetia wording against Weyer and Scot.

Peterson’s presentation is important because it shows that several features often treated as equally ancient belong to different strata:

| Feature | Earliest secure occurrence |
|---|---|
| Alocer, duke, horse, red lion face, flaming eyes | Weyer, 1577 |
| Astronomy, liberal arts, *bonam familiam*, 36 legions | Weyer, 1577 |
| Spelling Allocer; explicit “familiars” | Scot, 1584 |
| Spelling Alloces and spirit no. 52 | Surviving Goetia manuscript recension |
| Seal and instruction to wear it | Surviving Goetia recension |
| Thirty-legion variant | Harley 6483 |
| Angel Imamiah as counterforce | Rudd-attributed Harley scheme |
| “Alocas” | Popularized in Mathers–Crowley, 1904 |
| Venus/copper classification | Mathers–Crowley’s general rank table |
| Virgo/8 Disks decan overlay | Crowley’s *777* tables |

### What Alloces does not do

The source entry does **not** attribute to Alloces:

- treasure finding;
- love or lust;
- invisibility;
- bodily transformation;
- alchemical transmutation;
- theft;
- prophecy about past, present and future;
- granting dignities;
- reconciling enemies;
- transporting people;
- answering all questions;
- breathing fire;
- assuming a specified second form.

Those belong to other Goetic spirits or modern compilations. Alloces’s documented portfolio is unusually narrow: astronomy, liberal learning and familiars or a beneficial household.

## Rank, planet, metal and timing

### Duke: Venus and copper

**Esoteric correspondence:** Mathers’s preliminary instructions classify the ranks as follows:

| Rank | Planet | Metal |
|---|---|---|
| King | Sun | Gold |
| Duke | Venus | Copper |
| Prince or Prelate | Jupiter | Tin |
| Marquis | Moon | Silver |
| President | Mercury | Mercury |
| Knight | Saturn | Lead |
| Earl or Count | Composite | Copper and silver in equal parts |

Alloces is therefore assigned **Venus and copper because he is a duke**, not because Weyer calls him Venusian or because his astronomical teaching resembles Venus. [Mathers–Crowley rank instructions](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks02.htm)

### Time of conjuration

**Documented general rule:** Weyer’s timing rubric says dukes may be constrained from the first hour or sunrise until noon, provided the weather is clear. Scot and Mathers transmit substantially the same rule.

For Alloces, the historically grounded instruction is consequently:

- daytime;
- sunrise to noon;
- clear weather;
- a copper seal, in the Mathers system.

The entry itself does not give Alloces a distinct weekday, date or planetary hour.

**Later esoteric synthesis:** Modern practitioners sometimes add Friday, a Venus planetary hour, appropriate lunar conditions, the Imamiah quinance or Crowley’s Virgo decan. These are reasonable internal combinations of later correspondence systems, but they are not directions in Weyer’s paragraph or the core Goetia entry.

## The seal

### Absence in Weyer and Scot

Neither Weyer’s Latin catalog nor Scot’s English translation prints a seal for Alocer/Allocer. The seals are a feature of the surviving *Ars Goetia* recension.

Peterson notes more generally that the sigils do not appear in the earlier *Offices of Spirits* catalogs and may have been created or imported when the Goetia compilation took its surviving form. This makes the seal a seventeenth-century or later textual layer rather than evidence for an ancient cult.

### Shape

**Documented image:** The standard Alloces seal is a horizontally organized, approximately rectangular glyph:

- a long horizontal spine or crossbar;
- an upright central stroke crossing or rising from it;
- doubled, hooked or looped constructions toward both sides;
- angular returns at the lower portions;
- short curling or hooked terminals.

It is not pictographic: it does not visibly depict a lion, horse, planet or Hebrew letter. Its meaning is not explained in the manuscripts.

The Sloane witnesses and Mathers print the same essential topology, although the manuscript versions are less geometrically regular. Mathers’s engraving regularizes the line weight and proportions. [Mathers seal image](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:52-Alloces_seal.png); [high-resolution scan](https://fromoldbooks.org/Mathers-Goetia/pages/052-Seal-of-Alloces/)

### Rudd’s angelic reverse

**Rudd-attributed tradition:** In the Harley system, the demonic seal is not supposed to function alone. The corresponding Shem angel’s name and sigil are placed in relation to it—commonly described in the edited Rudd material as the reverse or counter-face of the lamen—so that the superior angel constrains the spirit.

This does not mean Imamiah appears in Weyer’s entry. It is a later ritual architecture superimposed upon the 72-place catalog.

### How the 72 triplets are derived

**Jewish mystical tradition:** Exodus 14:19–21 consists, in the Masoretic Hebrew text, of three verses of 72 letters each. In the later extraction procedure:

1. verse 19 is written in its normal direction;
2. verse 20 is written in reverse;
3. verse 21 is written normally;
4. the letters are read vertically, producing 72 groups of three.

The reverse direction of the middle verse creates a boustrophedon arrangement. The biblical text itself does not call the resulting triplets angels. The three verses narrate the movement of the angel of God, the cloud between Israel and Egypt, and the division of the sea. [Exodus 14:19–21](https://www.sefaria.org/Exodus.14.19-21?with=Kabbalah)

The *Bahir*, probably redacted in medieval Provence, associates the divine name with groups of 72 and with the three verses. Rabbinic discussions of a 72-letter name are earlier, but their exact constructions and liturgical uses are not identical to the later guardian-angel system. [*Sefer ha-Bahir*, §§109–110](https://hekhal.org/texts/sefer-ha-bahir-109-110-targum/); [Rashi on Sukkah 45a](https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Sukkah.45a.2)

### From divine triplets to angel names

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The expression *Shem ha-meforash* originally means the “explicit,” “distinctive” or “fully pronounced” divine name and can refer in different contexts to the Tetragrammaton or to longer divine-name constructions. It did not originally denote a fixed catalog of 72 personalized guardian angels.

Medieval works including the *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* transmitted divine names, angels, talismans and astral magic. Christian Kabbalists then increasingly personalized the 72 triplets by adding endings such as **-el** and **-yah**. Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517) was especially influential in presenting 72 angelic names to Christian readers.

Thus the historical stages are:

> Exodus text → Jewish divine-name exegesis → medieval angelological and magical transmission → Christian Kabbalistic angel names → early modern occult tables → Rudd, Lenain, Golden Dawn and modern guardian-angel literature.

It is inaccurate to describe the entire Lenain or Haziel dossier as though it were already written in the biblical Book of Exodus or in one fixed ancient edition of *Sefer Raziel*.

### Imamiah’s Hebrew name and “meaning”

The consonantal root assigned to position 52 is **עמם**, transliterable approximately as ʿ-M-M. Adding the theophoric ending **יה**, “Yah,” yields the occult angel name rendered Imamiah, Amamiah or Aamamiah.

**Disputed linguistic point:** “God exalted above all things” is a received occult attribute, not a transparent dictionary translation of עמם. The Golden Dawn gloss “Covered in darkness” differs sharply. Both are traditional attributions; neither should be presented as an uncontested Hebrew etymology.

The spelling variation arises from:

- difficulty representing initial Hebrew ayin in Latin script;
- different supplied vowels;
- doubling or simplifying the medial *m*;
- uncertainty over whether the initial vowel is *I*, *A*, or a glottal/ayin sound.

### Kircher

Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–1654) reproduced a circular arrangement of 72 divine or angelic names and connected the scheme with peoples, languages and divine nomenclature. His work was a major visual conduit for Christian Kabbalistic cosmology. [Kircher’s 72-name diagram](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_72_Names_of_God_%28Oedipus_Aegyptiacus%2C_1652%E2%80%9354%29.jpg)

Kircher does not establish that “Imamiah” was historically the name of a god among a people called the Melindais. Such language attributions belong to early-modern universal-language speculation, not modern historical linguistics.

### Rudd and Harley 6483

**Documented manuscript with disputed authorship:** Harley 6483 was copied by Peter Smart and is internally associated with “Dr Rudd.” The operative scheme pairs the 72 Goetic spirits with the 72 angels by ordinal position:

> spirit 1 with angel 1, spirit 2 with angel 2 … spirit 52 Alloces with angel 52 Imamiah.

The angel’s authority is used to check, bind or constrain the corresponding spirit. This produces a ritual polarity:

| Constrained spirit | Constraining angel |
|---|---|
| Alloces, no. 52 | Imamiah, no. 52 |

Stephen Skinner and David Rankine published the manuscript complex as *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* in 2007. [Golden Hoard catalog](https://www.sskinner.com/pdf/GHP-Catalogue-2022-2023.pdf); [Llewellyn edition](https://www.llewellyn.com/product.php?ean=9780738723556)

#### The authorship problem

Thomas Rudd lived approximately 1583–1656, but the principal Smart copies are substantially later. The manuscripts claim an association with Rudd; they are not surviving holographs in Rudd’s hand.

Egil Asprem and other researchers have questioned whether Peter Smart copied genuine Rudd papers, expanded a Ruddian system, or created the attribution to lend authority to his compilation. The responsible formulation is therefore:

- **Documented:** Harley 6483 contains the Rudd-attributed angel–demon system.
- **Tradition:** Thomas Rudd personally devised or used it.
- **Disputed:** the strength and extent of actual Rudd authorship.
- **Modern editorial fact:** Skinner and Rankine systematized and published it under Rudd’s name in 2007.

[Harley manuscript descriptions](https://www.alchemywebsite.com/almss3.html); [discussion of the Rudd attribution](https://celephaispress.blogspot.com/2021/06/addenda-et-scholia-2.html)

### Lenain, 1823

**Documented text:** Lazare Républicain Lenain, *La science cabalistique*, Amiens, 1823, p. 81.

Lenain’s entry for no. 52 gives:

> “Dieu élevé au-dessus de toutes choses.”

He associates Imamiah with the alleged divine name “Abag” in the language of the “Melindais,” the 256th through 260th degrees, and the twenty-sixth decan and its angel Sagen. He assigns five dispersed calendar days—10 May, 21 July, 1 October, 12 December and 22 February—and an invocation interval of 5:00–5:20 p.m. [Lenain scan](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_ms05365); [searchable text](https://archive.org/stream/BIUSante_ms05365/BIUSante_ms05365_djvu.txt)

The psalm is:

> “Confitebor Domino secundum justitiam ejus: et psallam nomini Domini altissimi.”

That is Vulgate Psalm 7:18: a promise to thank or praise the Lord according to his justice and sing to the name of the Most High. Most modern Hebrew and English Bibles number the same line Psalm 7:17.

Lenain says Imamiah is invoked against enemies, to humble them, and for deliverance. He associates the angel with voyages and prisoners, truth, repentance, vigor, patience, courage and industrious work. The contrary genius produces pride, blasphemy, wickedness, grossness and quarrelsomeness.

The “Melindais” attribution is **occult universal-language lore**, not evidence that an African or Asian language called God “Abag.”

### Papus

Gérard Encausse, better known as **Papus**, promoted Christian Kabbalah in works including *La Cabbale* and supplied a preface or sponsorship for the 1909 reissue of Lenain. Through Papus and related French occult circles, Lenain’s angelic offices, dates and psalms became accessible to a twentieth-century esoteric readership. [BnF record for the 1909 Lenain edition](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb30789329z)

Papus did not originate Imamiah’s 1823 entry; he helped transmit it.

### The Golden Dawn

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn integrated the 72 angels with zodiacal quinances, tarot minors, Hebrew divine names and an elaborate celestial hierarchy.

However, the Golden Dawn table preserved by Israel Regardie and discussed by Pat Zalewski does **not** place Imamiah at the same Sagittarius degrees as the straightforward Lenain sequence. It lists:

- no. 50 Deneyael: Aries 5°–10°, Mars;
- no. 51 Kechasheiah: Aries 10°–15°, Sun;
- no. 52 Amamiah: Aries 15°–20°, Sun.

That makes Amamiah the second quinance of the Sun-ruled Aries decan and associates it with the **Three of Wands**, “Lord of Established Strength.” [Zalewski, *Kabbalah of the Golden Dawn*, pp. 30–31](https://kobayashi-dxn.s3.eu-west-2.amazonaws.com/Golden%20Dawn/Zalewski__-_Kabbalah_of_the_Golden_Dawn.pdf); [Regardie compilation](https://www.invisiblehouse.org/ref/Complete_Golden_Dawn_System_of_Magic.pdf)

The Golden Dawn’s precise source or rationale for every psalm and reordered zodiacal position is not fully documented. A present-day Golden Dawn organization acknowledges uncertainty about how the Order chose its set of psalm verses. [Hermetic Golden Dawn’s Shem overview](https://hermeticgoldendawn.org/shem/)

### Ambelain

Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) transmits a substantially Lenain-derived Imamiah:

- “God raised above all things”;
- 15°–20° Sagittarius;
- 5:00–5:20 p.m.;
- Psalm 7:18/17;
- protection against enemies;
- travel, deliverance, patience, courage and work.

**Scholarly assessment:** Ambelain is an important transmitter of the French occult system, but not independent ancient confirmation of it. [Ambelain, *La Kabbale pratique*](https://djvu.online/file/O6LjS6ZXfyHrE)

### Haziel

“Haziel” was the pen name of François Bernard-Termes. His late twentieth-century guardian-angel books converted the 72-name system into a devotional and personal-astrology program. Imamiah is assigned 15°–20° Sagittarius, usually expressed as births on 8–12 December, with prayers, moral qualities and additional “regency” dates.

Haziel’s calendar is a **modern devotional elaboration**, not part of Harley 6483, Weyer, Scot or the biblical derivation. Some Haziel dates also differ from Lenain’s five dispersed days. [BnF authority record for Haziel](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/rechercher.do?index=AUT3&numNotice=12089673)

### The Lenain quinance and Nine of Wands

A simple sequential division of the zodiac into 72 sections of five degrees, beginning at 0° Aries, places number 52 at 255°–260° ecliptic longitude: 15°–20° Sagittarius.

The encompassing decan is 10°–20° Sagittarius, ruled by the Moon in the Chaldean order. Golden Dawn tarot doctrine calls its pip the **Nine of Wands**, “Lord of Great Strength.” Two adjacent Shem angels share that pip because each rules five degrees while the decan and pip occupy ten.

Under this construction:

- 10°–15° Sagittarius: angel 51;
- 15°–20° Sagittarius: Imamiah, angel 52;
- shared decan card: Nine of Wands.

This is coherent within the Lenain-style sequential zodiac, but it should not be retrojected into Weyer.

### The Golden Dawn’s Aries placement

The Golden Dawn angel table preserved by Regardie instead places Amamiah at 15°–20° Aries. That interval shares the 10°–20° Aries decan:

- planet: Sun;
- card: Three of Wands;
- title: “Lord of Established Strength.”

This is not a typographical variant of Sagittarius. It reflects a different ordering of the angelic names around the zodiac.

### Crowley’s *777* placement

Crowley’s *Liber 777* assigns two Goetic demons to each ten-degree decan, one by day and one by night. Numbering the first 36 as daytime and 37–72 as nighttime places Alloces no. 52 in the nighttime sequence:

- decan: 0°–10° Virgo;
- day spirit: Zepar, no. 16;
- night spirit: Alloces, no. 52;
- decan ruler: Sun;
- tarot pip: Eight of Disks/Pentacles;
- Golden Dawn title: “Lord of Prudence.”

[Primary *777* tables](https://hermetic.com/_media/93beast.fea.st/files/section1/777/liber_777.pdf); [Crowley on the Eight of Disks](https://hermetic.com/crowley/book-of-thoth/small-cards)

This association follows Goetic catalog number, not Alloces’s appearance or office. It also has no necessary relationship to Imamiah’s French Sagittarius quinance.

### Day and night

The phrase “day or night” can refer to at least three distinct ideas:

1. Weyer’s operational hours for ranks: Alloces as a duke is a morning spirit.
2. A later convention calling the first five degrees of a decan “day” and the second five “night.”
3. Crowley’s *777* division of the first 36 demons by day and the second 36 by night.

Only the third clearly makes Alloces a **night demon of the Virgo decan**, while the original duke rule prescribes daytime work. The contradiction results from layering different tables, not from a single source giving inconsistent instructions.

### Enochian overlay

The 1904 book includes Goetic conjurations rendered into the “Magical” or “Angelical” language associated with John Dee and Edward Kelley. That language dates from their 1580s angelic sessions and is historically independent of Weyer’s catalog.

**Modern/Thelemic synthesis:** Crowley and Golden Dawn practitioners brought Goetic seals, Solomonic constraints, Shem angels, Enochian material, tarot and psychological interpretations into one working environment. There is no Alloces-specific Enochian name in Weyer or the core manuscript entry.

Crowley’s prefatory interpretation—that the spirits could be treated as portions or faculties of the human mind—is likewise a modern psychological reading, not the ontology assumed by the early catalogs.

## Iconography

### Before Collin de Plancy

Weyer and Scot provide verbal imagery but no portrait or seal:

- military rider;
- huge horse;
- leonine face;
- red complexion;
- flaming eyes;
- grave or powerful voice.

The Goetia manuscripts add the abstract seal but no narrative illustration.

### Collin de Plancy, 1818–1863

No separate Alocer entry has been located in the searchable text of the **1818 first edition** of Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal*. Its absence should be stated cautiously because early scans and indexes can be imperfect. [1818 first-edition text](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/1re_%C3%A9dition%2C_1818/Tome_1/Texte_entier)

An **Alocer** article appears by the 1825 second edition. De Plancy describes a powerful demon and grand duke, dressed as a knight and mounted on a huge horse. The lion face, inflamed complexion, ardent eyes, grave speech, astronomy, liberal arts and 36 legions come from Weyer. He also says Alocer makes those he protects happy in their families. [1825 second-edition scan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e6/Dictionnaire_Infernal%2C_Ou_Bibliotheque_Universelle_%28Second_Edition%2C_Vol.I%29_%28IA_dli.granth.35110%29.pdf)

That domestic claim probably reflects a literal reading of Weyer’s *bonam familiam*, whereas Scot rendered the same phrase “good familiars.”

The 1863 sixth edition, p. 22, says:

> “Alocer, démon puissant, grand-duc aux enfers. Il se montre vêtu en chevalier, monté sur un cheval énorme.”

The entry continues with the red lion face, burning eyes, grave speech, astronomy, liberal arts and 36 legions. [1863 Alocer article](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.%2C_1863/Alocer); [1863 facsimile](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e0/Jacques_Collin_de_Plancy_-_Dictionnaire_infernal.pdf)

The scan reads **vêtu**, “dressed,” where some OCR transcriptions have produced **velu**, “hairy.” The latter is an OCR error, not evidence for a separate hairy form.

### Louis Le Breton’s portrait

The 1863 edition includes Louis Le Breton’s engraving:

- an upright lion-headed rider;
- a mane-like head and open feline mouth;
- humanoid torso and arms grasping the reins;
- an enormous horse;
- lower limbs or feet rendered with fantastical, claw-like or reptilian qualities;
- stark black-and-white engraved linework.

[Le Breton’s Alocer engraving](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Alocer_01.png); [Gallica page image](http://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k5754923d/f33.image)

The clawed or “dragon-legged” mount is **Le Breton’s iconographic elaboration**. Weyer says only that the horse is huge. Modern descriptions that present dragon legs as part of the original conjuration text confuse the illustration with the written source.

### Modern art

Most recognizable contemporary Alloces art follows one or both of two templates:

1. Le Breton’s lion-headed mounted figure;
2. the Mathers-Goetia seal.

Common additions—full plate armor, a flaming mane, fire issuing from the mouth, wings, a dragon-horse, green Venusian coloring or explicitly astronomical instruments—are **modern artistic inventions** unless an artist identifies another source. “Flaming eyes” is textual; “fire-breathing” is not.

## Grimoire ancestors and parallels

### *Testament of Solomon*

The Greek *Testament of Solomon*, composed and revised over a long period in late antiquity, portrays Solomon receiving a ring, interrogating demons about their names and works, and learning which angels frustrate them. It is an important ancestor of the general Solomonic model.

Alloces is not named in the *Testament*. The resemblance between its thwarting angels and Rudd’s counter-angels is structural, not evidence that Imamiah and Alloces were already paired in antiquity. [Jewish Encyclopedia overview](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/13845-solomon-testament-of)

### Munich handbook, Clm 849

The fifteenth-century Munich necromancer’s handbook contains numerous spirit names and ritual structures relevant to later European demon catalogs. It has **Alugor**, but the comparative evidence identifies that figure with Eligor/Abigor, not Alloces.

No unmistakable Alocer/Alloces entry has been demonstrated there.

### *Livre des Esperitz*

The French *Livre des Esperitz*, preserved in Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29, is an important precursor to Weyer’s catalog. Many names and offices overlap. Its extant list does not contain an unmistakable Alocer. [*Livre des Esperitz*, text](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre_des_esperitz/Texte_entier)

This absence is significant because it weakens claims that all Weyer’s entries were copied intact from that particular manuscript.

### *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

Weyer says his catalog comes from or represents a *Liber officiorum spirituum*, also given a title involving “Empto. Salomonis.” Several surviving sixteenth-century *Offices of Spirits* manuscripts show related catalogs.

The Folger V.b.26 **Lecher** entry is the closest known parallel to Alloces, but its date and transmission remain unresolved. It may point toward the lost source family from which Weyer’s catalog developed.

### *Book of Abramelin*

The *Book of Abramelin* contains extensive lists of spirits and a different ritual framework. No secure Alloces/Alocer entry has been found in its principal lists. [Peterson’s Abramelin edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/abramelin/abramelin.htm)

It is therefore a parallel early-modern grimoire tradition, not a demonstrated textual source for this name.

### The brazen-vessel legend

The Goetia’s internal legend says Solomon bound 72 principal spirits and their legions in a brass vessel, sealed it and cast it into a Babylonian lake or pit. People expecting treasure opened the vessel and released them.

This is **Solomonic legend**, not reconstructable history. The legend explains the number 72 inside the tradition, while the surviving textual catalog can be explained through Weyer/Scot plus four additions and one omission.

## People and works

### Johann Weyer

Johann Weyer or Wier (1515–1588), a physician and opponent of witch persecutions, printed the earliest secure Alocer entry in 1577. His purpose was polemical and skeptical: he argued that many accused witches were deluded or afflicted and that supposed magical experts were fraudulent or deceived. The catalog’s inclusion does not show that Weyer worshipped or conjured Alocer.

### Reginald Scot

Reginald Scot (c. 1538–1599) translated or published the catalog in *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* to expose conjuring claims and oppose executions for witchcraft. His version established **Allocer**, “big voice” and “good familiars” in English.

### Thomas Nashe

Thomas Nashe made one of the earliest literary uses in *Have with You to Saffron-Walden* (1596):

> “Duke Allocer on his lustie cock-horse is a hot familiar.”

The allusion demonstrates that Scot’s demon catalog had entered Elizabethan literary satire within twelve years of publication. It does not add a new magical office. [Nashe facsimile](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/1/19/Have_with_you_to_Saffron-Walden%3B_or%2C_Gabriell_Harveys_hunt_is_up._Containing_a_full_answere_to_the_eldest_sonne_of_the_halter-maker._Or%2C_Nashe_his_confutation_of_the_sinfull_doctor_%28IA_havewithyoutosaf00nash%29.pdf)

### Peter Smart and “Dr Rudd”

Peter Smart copied the Harley magical manuscripts around the turn of the eighteenth century. Those materials attribute important parts of their system to Dr Thomas Rudd. For Alloces, their decisive innovation is the ordinal placement under Imamiah.

It remains uncertain whether that structure was Rudd’s own work, Smart’s compilation from Ruddian papers, or a later attribution.

### Collin de Plancy and Louis Le Breton

Collin de Plancy turned the terse catalog entry into a demonological dictionary article and briefly explored the household meaning of *familia*. Louis Le Breton supplied the enduring 1863 visual interpretation.

### Mathers and Crowley

S. L. MacGregor Mathers prepared the English Goetia text from manuscripts, probably in the 1890s. Aleister Crowley published it in 1904, added prefatory material, an Enochian presentation of conjurations and a psychological interpretation.

Crowley’s later *777* supplied the Virgo/8 Disks association. That correspondence does not occur in Mathers’s basic Alloces paragraph.

### A. E. Waite

Waite reproduced Goetic material critically in *The Book of Ceremonial Magic*. His **Allocen/Alloien** forms appear to be corrupt readings rather than evidence for two additional spirits.

### Papus, Ambelain and Haziel

These writers form a French esoteric transmission line:

> Lenain’s 1823 angel table → Papus’s occult revival and republication → Ambelain’s practical Kabbalah → Haziel’s guardian-angel devotional system.

Each stage expands the prior one. None demonstrates that the extended Imamiah biography existed in biblical times.

### Poke Runyon

Poke Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* integrates Goetia, Rudd-style counter-angels, planetary ritual and modern lodge practice. His tables associate Alloces with Imamiah and the Sagittarius sector. [Runyon, *Book of Solomon’s Magick*](https://attfinnasanningen.se/dokument_offentligt/books/mystery_school/magic/poke%20runyon%20book%20of%20solomons%20magick.pdf)

Runyon is evidence for a late twentieth-century operative synthesis, not for the antiquity of the Sagittarius correspondence.

### Lon Milo DuQuette

Lon Milo DuQuette and Christopher Hyatt’s *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* popularized Crowley’s psychological and modern magical reading. No well-sourced, Alloces-specific operation by DuQuette has been identified; citing the book as though it documented one would overstate the evidence.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Skinner and Rankine’s *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (2007) made the Harley angel–demon structure broadly accessible and emphasized the protective or constraining angelic mechanism. It is the central modern edition for the Alloces–Imamiah pairing.

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 edition and continuously revised Esoteric Archives transcriptions are the most useful accessible tools for comparing Sloane 3825, other Goetia manuscripts, Weyer and Scot. Peterson distinguishes base text, manuscript variants and Mathers’s editorial changes.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Jake Stratton-Kent emphasized the continuity of Goetic practice with earlier spirit-office catalogs and criticized readings that reduce every spirit to a purely psychological symbol. His work is relevant to the transmission framework, but no unique pre-Weyer etymology or documented personal Alloces operation was located.

### Owen Davies, Jan Veenstra, Julien Véronèse and Jean-Patrice Boudet

These scholars situate grimoires within manuscript, clerical, legal and learned-magical history. Their work supports caution about claims of timeless, unchanging demon lists. Boudet’s comparative catalog is especially important for Alloces because it shows Alocer without an identified medieval counterpart.

### Frater Achad

No reliable Alloces-specific writing or working by Frater Achad—Charles Stansfeld Jones—was located. Modern Thelemic tables are sometimes attributed loosely to “Crowley and Achad,” but the presence of Alloces in a later Thelemic glossary does not establish Achad as its author or practitioner. This is an instance where absence of evidence should be retained rather than filled with speculation.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Does Alloces predate Weyer?

**Finding:** not securely.

- *Livre des Esperitz*: no unmistakable Alloces.
- Munich Clm 849: Alugor is better aligned with Eligor.
- *Testament of Solomon*: no Alloces.
- *Abramelin*: no secure Alloces.
- Folger V.b.26: Lecher is close, but not demonstrably earlier than 1577.

The strongest conclusion is: **Alocer is securely attested from Weyer’s 1577 edition; a lost earlier office-of-spirits source is plausible, but not presently reconstructable by name.**

### 2. Is Alloces an ancient deity?

No documented ancient identity has been established. The lion face and astronomy office are insufficient to identify a specific god. Comparisons to Amun, Baal, Astarte, a Mesopotamian alû or a zodiacal crab are speculative.

### 3. Is Alloces no. 52, 63 or 64?

All three numbers occur for understandable textual reasons:

- 64: Weyer’s section.
- 63: position after Scot’s omission of Pruflas.
- 52: the Goetia’s reordered catalog.

Calling one of these simply “wrong” without naming the source obscures the transmission history.

### 4. Why 69 versus 72?

Weyer prints 69 while retaining a legend of 72. Scot leaves 68 by omitting Pruflas. The Goetia restores a catalog of 72 by using the remaining 68 and adding Vassago, Seere, Dantalion and Andromalius.

The result is a textual construction, not evidence that three Alloces-like beings went missing from antiquity.

### 5. Are Alocer, Allocer, Alloces and Alocas separate spirits?

There is no evidence that they are separate. Their descriptions, offices and legion counts remain continuous. They are spelling variants in one transmission chain.

**Allocen** and **Alloien** are still later corruptions. **Alugor/Algor**, by contrast, most likely belongs to Eligor’s chain and should not be merged with Alloces.

### 6. Thirty or thirty-six legions?

- Weyer: 36.
- Scot: 36.
- Sloane 3825: 36.
- Mathers: 36.
- Harley 6483: 30.

Thirty-six has the stronger and earlier textual support. Thirty is nevertheless an authentic Harley variant and should not be silently erased.

### 7. Familiars or family happiness?

Weyer’s *bonam familiam* permits more than one nuance:

- household or retinue;
- a good company of attendants;
- in magical English, good familiar spirits.

Scot and the Goetia choose familiars. De Plancy’s 1825 text chooses domestic family happiness. Neither requires inventing a second Latin source; they are divergent readings of *familia*.

### 8. Grave, deep, big or hoarse voice?

The evolution is traceable:

- Weyer: *graviter loquitur* — speaks gravely/heavily.
- Scot: “big voice.”
- Goetia: “hoarse & very big.”
- Mathers: “hoarse and very big.”
- De Plancy: *parle avec gravité* — speaks with gravity.

“Hoarse” is not a literal necessity from Weyer’s Latin. It belongs to the later English recension.

### 9. Does he breathe fire?

No early text says so. Only the eyes are flaming. Fire-breathing is a modern artistic or encyclopedic accretion.

### 10. Is the horse draconic?

Not in Weyer, Scot or the Goetia wording. Le Breton’s engraving gives the animal fantastical clawed features. Later artists converted that visual choice into prose.

### 11. Is Imamiah inherently Alloces’s enemy?

Only inside the Rudd-style ordinal scheme. Exodus, the *Bahir* and early Jewish divine-name discussions do not mention Alloces. Imamiah’s position as Alloces’s restraining angel is a Christian Solomonic construction, probably preserved in an early-eighteenth-century copy.

### 12. Which quinance is correct?

There is no source-independent answer because the systems differ:

- Lenain-style sequential system: 15°–20° Sagittarius.
- Golden Dawn angel table: 15°–20° Aries.
- Crowley *777* demon decan: 0°–10° Virgo.

A table that gives Alloces “Sagittarius, Nine of Wands” without explaining that this follows Imamiah’s sequential position is incomplete. A table that gives “Virgo, Eight of Pentacles” without naming *777* is equally incomplete.

### 13. Which Psalm number is correct?

Both, under different numbering conventions:

- Psalm 7:18 in the Vulgate used by Lenain;
- Psalm 7:17 in the Masoretic/Hebrew and most modern English systems.

This is a numbering difference, not a different verse.

### 14. Did Thomas Rudd write Harley 6483?

The manuscript carries a Rudd attribution, but the surviving copy is associated with Peter Smart and postdates Rudd. Rudd’s authorship remains debated. “Rudd-attributed” is more exact than “Rudd’s autograph.”

### 15. Does the 1818 *Dictionnaire infernal* portray Alocer?

No Alocer article or portrait was located in the searchable 1818 first edition. The article is present by 1825; the famous Le Breton portrait belongs to the illustrated 1863 sixth edition.

### 16. Are rank metals ancient?

No. Weyer calls Alocer a duke but does not assign copper or Venus in the entry. Those correspondences become operationally explicit in the Mathers–Crowley presentation and related ceremonial-magic tables.

### 17. Is the seal ancient?

No evidence demonstrates that. It is absent from Weyer, Scot and the known earlier office catalogs. Its earliest secure context is the surviving Goetia manuscript tradition.

## Open questions

1. **What was Weyer’s immediate manuscript source?**  
   Weyer names an office-of-spirits book, but no surviving witness has been proved to be his exact exemplar.

2. **Is Folger’s Lecher directly related?**  
   Its red lion face, knighthood and seven sciences are compelling, but the name, date and direction of borrowing remain unresolved.

3. **How did *Alocer* become *Alloces*?**  
   Scribal abbreviation, misreading of terminal *r/s*, Latin case-like normalization and ordinary copying variation are all possible. No surviving intermediate establishes the exact step.

4. **Where did the seal originate?**  
   It could have been invented by the Goetia compiler, copied from an unidentified magical diagram, or derived from a lost manuscript. The text supplies no interpretation.

5. **How much of Harley 6483 is genuinely Ruddian?**  
   Physical, scribal and source studies can constrain the answer, but the attribution is not settled.

6. **What was the Golden Dawn’s exact rationale for moving Amamiah to Aries?**  
   The table is documented; its complete source logic and the selection of every accompanying psalm are less certain.

7. **Is Lenain’s meaning based on a recoverable Hebrew exegesis?**  
   “God exalted above all things” functions as an occult attribute. A convincing linguistic derivation from עמם remains to be shown.

8. **Was Alocer absent from de Plancy’s 1818 edition or merely missed through imperfect indexing?**  
   The searchable edition yields no entry, but a page-by-page collation of every surviving state would make the negative finding firmer.

9. **Are there additional early literary uses between Nashe and de Plancy?**  
   Searchable corpora may uncover further references under corrupt spellings.

## Conclusions

The fullest defensible history begins not with an ancient deity but with **Weyer’s Alocer in 1577**. Weyer already supplies virtually the entire enduring personality: a great duke, mounted soldier, huge horse, red lion face, flaming eyes, grave voice, astronomy, liberal arts, beneficial *familia* and 36 legions.

Scot’s 1584 translation changes the spelling to **Allocer**, transforms grave speech into a big voice, and decisively interprets *familia* as familiar spirits. The seventeenth-century Goetia recension moves him to no. 52, changes the spelling to **Alloces**, makes the voice hoarse and adds the seal. Harley 6483 overlays the ordinal Shem angel **Imamiah** and uniquely reports 30 legions. Mathers and Crowley popularize **Alocas**, Venus, copper and the printed seal in 1904.

Imamiah’s own history is separate: biblical verses and Jewish divine-name speculation lead into medieval angelology and Christian Kabbalah, then Rudd’s constraining scheme, Lenain’s Sagittarius quinance, the Golden Dawn’s divergent Aries arrangement, Ambelain and Haziel. Crowley’s *777* adds yet another layer by assigning Alloces—not Imamiah—to nocturnal Virgo and the Eight of Disks.

The primary unresolved historical question is whether Weyer created or merely first printed the name. Folger’s **Lecher** suggests a related office-of-spirits tradition, but no certain pre-1577 Alloces has been found. Claims of an Akkadian, Greek, Latin or ancient-god etymology remain unsupported.
