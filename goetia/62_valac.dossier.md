# Valac: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary labels

This dossier separates several historically distinct objects that modern occult writing often merges:

1. **Volach/Volac/Valac**, the spirit in the medieval–early-modern “Offices of Spirits” catalogue.
2. **Valac no. 62**, as reordered and ritualized in the seventeenth-century *Ars Goetia*.
3. **Valu constrained by Iahhel**, Thomas Rudd’s attributed angel–demon synthesis.
4. **Iah-hel no. 62**, the Christian-kabbalistic angel whose five-degree astrology was systematized separately.
5. Later Golden Dawn, Crowleyan, demonolatry, artistic and fictional reinterpretations.

Labels used throughout:

- **[Documented text]** Directly present in a manuscript, printed primary source or identifiable edition.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A conclusion argued from comparison of sources.
- **[Tradition]** A received religious or magical teaching, without implying historical or supernatural truth.
- **[Esoteric correspondence]** A symbolic attribution belonging to a particular occult system.
- **[Disputed]** A claim for which sources conflict or evidence is inadequate.
- **[Legend]** Traditional narrative not independently demonstrated.
- **[Modern invention]** A demonstrably late addition, adaptation or reinterpretation.

Because the primary passages are available in linked public-domain facsimiles and transcriptions, the exact quotations below are kept short and followed by complete clause-by-clause accounts of their contents.

## The name and its origins

### Earliest presently identified attestations

#### Fifteenth-century Munich: *Volach*

**[Documented text]** The earliest securely identified witness is not Weyer. It is Munich, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Clm 849, the fifteenth-century necromantic manual edited by Richard Kieckhefer as *Forbidden Rites*. The manuscript gives the name **Volach** at fol. 66v.

Its opening is:

> “Volach magnus preses est. Apparet … in forma pueri, et habet alas ad modum angeli.”

The entry continues with the dragon, treasures, serpents and **twenty-seven** legions. Kieckhefer edits the passage at pp. 166 and 292 of *Forbidden Rites*. The [BSB manuscript record and facsimile](https://www.digitale-sammlungen.de/en/details/bsb00037155) permit direct inspection.

This overturns the frequently repeated assertion that the name first appeared in Weyer in 1577.

#### Late fifteenth-century Florence: *Volach*

**[Documented text]** Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Plut. 89 sup. 38, contains another *Volach* entry. Material in this compilation bears a January 1494 date, although the codex is composite and that date should not automatically be assigned to every leaf.

The Florence version closely resembles Munich but assigns **thirty**, not twenty-seven, legions. Brian Johnson published the relevant material in *Necromancy in the Medici Library*. A [digital edition](https://dokumen.pub/necromancy-in-the-medici-library-an-edition-and-translation-of-excerpts-from-biblioteca-medicea-laurenziana-ms-plut-89-sup-38-9781907881985-9781907881992.html) and the [manuscript’s IIIF record](https://iiif.biblissima.fr/collections/manifest/adc2821dbba8914226b503c3b2e09144f75ea2ee) are available.

### What the spelling changes mean

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The alteration of initial **V** and **U** is ordinary Latin and early-modern scribal/typographic variation. In many manuscripts and typefaces they were graphic variants rather than consistently distinct letters. Thus *Valu* and *Ualu* need not represent different pronunciations.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Final *-c*, *-ch* and *-k* likewise look like copying or normalization variants:

- *Volach* → *Volac*
- *Valac* → *Valak*
- truncation or misreading → *Valu*

This explains the transmission of the written form; it does **not** establish an etymology.

### Is there an ancient god, hero or place behind Valac?

**[Finding]** No credible primary or scholarly source consulted identifies Valac with an ancient deity, hero or place.

Unlike:

- Baal, whose title is Northwest Semitic “lord”;
- Astaroth, historically related to Astarte;
- Amon, often compared with Egyptian Amun;
- Asmodeus, connected with Avestan *Aēšma daēva*;
- Belial, from Hebrew *bĕliyyaʿal*;
- Phenex, transparently the Greek and Latin phoenix;

*Volach/Volac* has no demonstrated ancient-language derivation.

### Proposed etymologies

Several internet-era suggestions compare the name with:

- **Vlach/Wallachia**;
- Slavic **Veles/Volos**;
- an alleged Hebrew *valak*;
- Arabic *falaq*;
- miscellaneous Latin words.

**[Disputed]** These are phonetic resemblances without a documented chain of borrowing. None explains the fifteenth-century form, the functions involving treasure and snakes, or a known route into Latin ritual-magic manuscripts. They should be treated as conjectures, not recovered etymologies.

### Coolor and Doolas

Some modern catalogues list **Coolor** and **Doolas** as Valac variants.

**[Disputed scholarly reconstruction]** These names appear in the wider *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* or “Offices of Spirits” family and possess powers involving serpents or household spirits. The functional resemblance has encouraged identification with Volach. But their names, ranks and legion totals do not establish that they are merely corrupt spellings of Valac.

They may be:

1. textual doublets descended from a remote common entry;
2. independent spirits with overlapping functions;
3. later cataloguers’ conflation.

They should not be printed as unquestioned Valac spellings.

### Valac is not Vual

**[Documented distinction]** **Vual/Uvall/Voval**, Goetia no. 47, is a separate duke. Vual appears first as a dromedary and then as a man, procures the love of women, tells past, present and future, and rules thirty-seven legions.

Valac is a president, appears as a winged child on a two-headed dragon, concerns treasures and serpents, and normally rules thirty legions. Similar-looking names do not make them the same spirit.

## The description across the sources

## Medieval witnesses

### Munich Clm 849

**[Documented text]** The Munich entry contains every essential element later associated with Valac:

- name **Volach**;
- rank **great president**;
- appearance as a **boy**;
- wings “after the manner of an angel”;
- mounted on a dragon;
- the dragon has two heads;
- full or true answers about concealed treasures;
- knowledge of treasure locations;
- serpents delivered to the exorcist without effective power;
- **twenty-seven legions**.

The Latin concerning the serpent action is syntactically awkward, probably through corruption in transmission. Its general sense is nevertheless preserved by the later Florence and Weyer witnesses.

No seal is supplied.

### Florence Plut. 89 sup. 38

**[Documented text]** The Florence *Volach* preserves substantially the same description but gives **thirty legions**. This suggests that the 30-legion reading existed before Weyer and was not invented by him.

**[Open textual question]** It is not yet clear whether Munich’s *xxvij* was changed to *xxx*, whether Florence preserves the earlier count, or whether both descend from a third source already carrying variant numerals.

## Johann Weyer, 1577

### Date and context

**[Documented text]** Weyer’s *De praestigiis daemonum* first appeared in 1563, but the appended *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* was added in the fifth edition of **1577**. Thus “Weyer 1563” is correct for the parent book but not for Valac’s appearance in the printed spirit catalogue.

Weyer was a physician and critic of witch prosecutions. He did not simply endorse the catalogue as a manual: he presented it in a polemical discussion of demonic deception and deliberately withheld ritual elements. Peterson provides the [Latin catalogue and Scot’s parallel English](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

### Latin

Weyer’s opening reads:

> “Volac magnus Præses: progreditur uti puer alis angeli, super dracone equitans duobus capitibus.”

Complete contents:

1. **Volac** is a great President.
2. He proceeds or appears as a boy with an angel’s wings.
3. He rides upon a dragon with two heads.
4. He answers perfectly concerning hidden treasures.
5. He tells where serpents may be seen.
6. He hands those serpents into the exorcist’s hands deprived of their powers.
7. He governs thirty legions.

In the complete Weyer sequence Valac is the **fiftieth** spirit. Peterson’s parallel webpage labels the passage §49 because its presentation is aligned with Scot’s sequence, from which Pruflas is missing.

The entry occurs near p. 927 of the 1577 fifth edition’s catalogue sequence; numbering can differ in bound digitizations.

### Limits of Weyer's description

Weyer does **not** say that Valac:

- teaches languages or sciences;
- grants invisibility;
- causes love;
- changes metals;
- provides familiars;
- answers all past and future questions;
- commands planets;
- assumes a requested human form;
- is a fallen angel;
- bears a title such as “Marquis of Serpents.”

Those are either attributes of other spirits or later inventions.

## Reginald Scot, 1584

**[Documented text]** Book XV, chapter 2 of *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* carries the English catalogue. The translation was printed under Scot’s name; Peterson considers Abraham Fleming a plausible actual translator because Fleming is identified elsewhere in the volume.

Scot’s exact opening is:

> “Valac is a great president, and commeth abroad with angels wings like a boie, riding on a twoheaded dragon.”

The remainder says that Valac:

- answers perfectly about hidden treasure;
- says where serpents may be seen;
- puts them into the conjurer’s hands without force or strength;
- rules thirty legions of devils.

The passage can be inspected in the [1584 Cornell facsimile](https://digital.library.cornell.edu/catalog/witchcraft081) and in a [Book XV extraction](https://www.magicgatebg.com/Books/Discoverie%20of%20Witchcraft%2C%201584%20by%20Reginald%20Scot%20Booke%20XV.pdf). In the commonly used 1886 Elliot Stock reprint, the demon catalogue occupies approximately pp. 217–227 and Valac falls near pp. 224–25; original-1584 scans differ in image and signature numbering.

Scot’s role is significant because his spelling **Valac** became the dominant English form.

## From Weyer’s 69 spirits to the Goetia’s 72

**[Documented textual comparison]** The *Ars Goetia* is not simply Weyer plus three entries.

Its compiler:

- omitted **Pruflas/Bufas**, Weyer’s no. 4;
- added **Vassago**, eventually Goetia no. 3;
- added **Seere**, no. 70;
- added **Dantalion**, no. 71;
- added **Andromalius**, no. 72.

The arithmetic is:

> 69 Weyer spirits − 1 omitted + 4 added = 72 Goetic spirits.

The internal order was also rearranged. Valac moved from Weyer’s complete-list position **50** to Goetia **62**.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Pruflas may have disappeared before the Goetia was compiled because it was already accidentally absent from Scot’s English translation—or from the particular Weyer edition used by Scot. The Goetia compiler appears to have drawn heavily on Scot rather than directly and exclusively on Weyer.

## The seventeenth-century Goetia manuscripts

### Sloane 3825

Sloane 3825 contains dates no earlier than 1640/41 and is among the principal *Lemegeton* witnesses. Its description of **Valac**, no. 62, retains:

- mighty/great President;
- little boy or child;
- angel’s wings;
- two-headed dragon;
- hidden treasure;
- serpents delivered harmlessly;
- thirty legions.

The broader manuscript history is summarized in Peterson’s [Lemegeton introduction](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm).

### Sloane 2731

**[Documented text]** Sloane 2731 is dated 18 January 1686/87. Its Valac passage gives thirty legions and instructs that the spirit’s seal be made and worn as a lamen.

A [diplomatic transcription with manuscript images](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/goetia-sloane2731/read/) permits comparison of the seal and handwriting.

### Sloane 3648

This witness also reads **Valac**. Its importance is chiefly comparative: it confirms that *Valac*, rather than Rudd’s *Valu*, was the ordinary seventeenth-century Goetic reading.

### Harley 6483

Harley 6483, copied by Peter Smart in 1712–13 from material attributed to Thomas Rudd, calls the spirit **Valu**. It gives:

- rank: President;
- number: 62;
- appearance: child with angelic wings on a two-headed dragon;
- offices: treasure and serpents;
- thirty legions;
- Psalm 118:159 as part of the angelic constraint.

The entry is reproduced at p. 164 of Stephen Skinner and David Rankine’s *The Goetia of Dr Rudd*.

## Mathers–Crowley, 1904

The first edition of *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King* was issued in 1904 by S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Aleister Crowley. The Valac entry is on printed p. 30.

Its opening reads:

> “He is a President Mighty and Great, and appeareth like a Child with Angel’s Wings, riding on a Two-headed Dragon.”

The edition’s heading assembles **Volac, Valak, Valu and Ualac**; some electronic transcriptions read **Ualuc**, which is probably typographic or OCR corruption.

It preserves the treasure-and-serpent functions but gives **thirty-eight legions**.

**[Disputed textual reading]** Thirty-eight is poorly supported:

- Munich: 27;
- Florence: 30;
- Weyer: 30;
- Scot: 30;
- Sloane manuscripts: 30;
- Harley 6483: 30;
- Mathers–Crowley: 38.

The simplest explanation is a copying, editorial or typesetting error—possibly misreading a numeral or abbreviation—rather than an independent ancient tradition.

The entry can be checked in the [Hermetic Library scan](https://hermetic.com/_media/93beast.fea.st/files/section1/crowley_-_goetia.pdf), [Sacred Texts edition](https://www.sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/index.htm), and another [1904 scan](https://biglevi.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/The-Book-Of-The-Goetia-Of-Solomon-The-King-A-Crowley-1904.pdf).

## Appearance and the requested “second form”

Valac’s first and only individual form is the winged boy on the dragon.

**[Negative finding]** No early Valac entry specifies a second form that the conjurer may demand. This distinguishes Valac from spirits whose descriptions explicitly provide animal and human forms.

The Goetia’s general ritual instructions can require a spirit to take a fair, human or otherwise acceptable shape. Applying that provision to Valac is legitimate within the Goetia’s ritual grammar, but it is not a documented Valac-specific transformation.

---

## Offices in exact scope

Valac’s attested offices are unusually narrow:

1. **Hidden treasures:** gives truthful or complete answers concerning them.
2. **Serpent locations:** tells where serpents may be seen or found.
3. **Serpent handling:** delivers those serpents into the operator’s hands without effective power or strength.

**[Interpretive caution]** The text says that the serpents are deprived of force. It does not explicitly say Valac grants permanent kingship over all snakes, heals venom, teaches herpetology or turns into a serpent.

**[Negative finding]** The historical entries give Valac no attested power over:

- languages;
- liberal arts or sciences;
- astronomy;
- love;
- friendship;
- invisibility;
- transmutation;
- dignities;
- familiars;
- past and future generally.

De Plancy’s later planetary statement is discussed under Iconography and is probably contamination from the adjacent Orias entry.

## Rank, planet and metal

In the Goetia’s “Observations” the spirit ranks receive planetary metals:

| Rank | Planet | Metal |
|---|---|---|
| King | Sun | Gold |
| Marquis | Moon | Silver |
| Duke | Venus | Copper |
| Prince or Prelate | Jupiter | Tin |
| Knight | Saturn | Lead |
| President | Mercury | Mercury/quicksilver |
| Earl/Count | Mixed Venus–Moon | Copper and silver equally |

Accordingly:

- **[Esoteric correspondence] Valac’s planetary rank is Mercury.**
- **[Esoteric correspondence] Valac’s rank-metal is quicksilver.**

Peterson suggests electrum as a modern safer substitute. That is an editorial safety accommodation, not the manuscript’s stated metal.

## Hours and astrological timing

### Goetia rank-timing

The Goetia says Presidents may be bound:

- at any hour of the day except twilight;
- at night only under an additional condition involving invocation of the king to whom the President is subject.

The surviving English syntax is awkward, and no individual ruling king is named in Valac’s entry.

The “Observations” also commend operations when the Moon is 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 or 14 days old. These are generic Goetic rules, not Valac-specific dates.

### Crowleyan timing

Crowley’s later correspondence system makes Valac:

- a **night** ruler;
- of the second decan of Sagittarius;
- approximately **10°–20° Sagittarius**;
- paired with the **Nine of Wands**.

This is a separate astrological overlay. It does not occur in Weyer or the seventeenth-century Valac entry.

## The seal

### What it looks like

The principal Goetic seal is asymmetrical. In words:

- At the left or center is a small circular hub or ring.
- Several short spokes radiate from it.
- Some branches terminate in dots or tiny circles.
- Other terminals resemble crosses, forks or short transverse strokes.
- A horizontal bar extends to the right.
- It meets a much larger bowed, hooked or backward-D-like enclosure.
- Small ticks or short interior marks appear within or beside that enclosure.

A clean Mathers-style rendering can be viewed at [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:62-Valac_seal.png); a scalable redraw is [also available](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:62-valac-sigilo-ars-goetia.svg).

### Manuscript variations

**[Documented textual-artifact comparison]**

- The Sloane versions have the same recognizable underlying structure but irregular hand-drawn curves.
- Terminal dots and crossbars vary in number and placement.
- The right-hand enclosure may be narrower, broader or more angular.
- Harley 6483 places spirit seals within Rudd’s larger angelic-lamen arrangement.
- Mathers–Crowley regularizes the lines into a clean printable emblem.

These are normal scribal and engraving differences; they do not necessarily represent distinct seals.

### Antiquity of the seal

**[Negative finding]** No seal accompanies Volach in Munich, Florence, Weyer or Scot.

The seal enters the currently traceable evidence with the Goetia manuscript phase. Calling it “Valac’s ancient seal” therefore overstates the evidence.

**[Modern invention]** Claims that its geometry depicts a dragon, an angel, a serpent’s eye or planetary circuitry are modern symbolic readings. The manuscripts do not explain its lines that way.

## The angel: Iah-hel

## Identity

| Field | Finding |
|---|---|
| Number | 62 of 72 |
| Common spellings | Iahhel, Iah-hel, Iahael, Iahahel, Iahel, Yahel, Yahhel |
| Basic Hebrew triplet | יהה — Yod, He, He |
| Angelic expansion | יההאל |
| Traditional attribute in Lenain | *Être suprême* — “Supreme Being” |
| Zodiacal ray | 306°–310° |
| Conventional sign position | 5°–10° Aquarius |
| Psalm | Vulgate 118:159 / modern 119:159 |
| Office in Lenain | Acquiring wisdom; philosophers, illumined people and those withdrawing from the world |
| Rudd function | Angelic name and verse employed to constrain the corresponding demon, Valu |

### Meaning of the name

**[Documented text]** Lenain gives Iah-hel the attribute *Être suprême*.

**[Tradition]** Later angel manuals therefore translate the name as “Supreme God,” “Supreme Being” or “God the Highest.”

**[Philological caution]** This is not a straightforward lexical translation. The first three letters are mechanically extracted from Exodus; they do not necessarily constitute a normal Hebrew word. The suffix **אל**, *El*, means “God,” but “Supreme Being” is best described as Lenain’s occult attribute or gloss.

Readings such as “Light of God” and “Desire for God” are later interpretive expansions, not secure translations of יההאל.

## How the seventy-two names are derived

### Exodus 14:19–21

**[Tradition]** The seventy-twofold Name is constructed from three consecutive Hebrew verses, traditionally counted as seventy-two letters each.

The common procedure is:

1. Write Exodus 14:19 in its ordinary right-to-left order.
2. Write Exodus 14:20 beneath it in reverse order.
3. Write Exodus 14:21 beneath that in ordinary order.
4. Read the resulting columns as seventy-two three-letter groups.

This produces **72 triplets**, or **216 letters altogether**.

Calling this simply “a seventy-two-letter name” is traditional shorthand but technically ambiguous. Some Jewish discussions of a seventy-two-letter Name concern the full construction; the later angelic system treats each triplet as a separate root.

### Jewish and Christian-kabbalistic history

**[Tradition]** Medieval Jewish sources, including commentary associated with Rashi and material in the *Sefer ha-Bahir*, discuss a seventy-twofold divine Name derived from the Exodus verses.

**[Historical caution]** *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* is a composite medieval work, not a single text written at one identifiable moment by the biblical angel Raziel. Its textual traditions circulated in manuscript before the Amsterdam printed edition of 1701.

**[Documented history]** Johannes Reuchlin’s *De Arte Cabalistica* (1517) was important in Christian Cabala for circulating pronounceable angelic forms made by adding divine suffixes such as **-el** and **-iah**. Reuchlin did not invent the Exodus construction, but he helped move it into Latin Christian occultism. A scholarly study is Agata Paluch’s [“72 in His Name”](https://dokumen.pub/72-in-his-name-reuchlin-luther-thenaud-wolff-and-the-names-of-seventy-two-angels-9781644692455.html).

**[Documented history]** Athanasius Kircher treated the seventy-two divine and angelic names in *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–55), drawing upon Jewish sources such as the *Bahir* and Moses Cordovero while embedding them in his Christian universalist scheme. Kircher simultaneously rejected what he considered superstitious amuletic applications. Daniel Stolzenberg discusses this in [*Athanasius Kircher: The Last Man Who Knew Everything*](https://kirchernetwork.org/wp-content/uploads/2020/05/findlen-p-athanasius-kircher-the-last-man-who-knew-everything-2004.pdf).

### Rudd’s innovation

**[Esoteric correspondence]** Rudd’s attributed system sets the seventy-two Exodus-derived angelic names against the seventy-two Goetic spirits by shared ordinal number:

> Spirit 1 ↔ angel 1, spirit 2 ↔ angel 2 … spirit 62 ↔ angel 62.

Thus:

> **Valu/Valac no. 62 ↔ Iahhel no. 62.**

This opposition is not present in the medieval Volach entries, Weyer, Scot, or the principal Sloane description. It is a seventeenth-/early-eighteenth-century synthesis preserved in Rudd-associated material.

### The Rudd lamen

In *The Goetia of Dr Rudd*, the operator uses a double or paired talismanic construction:

- the demon’s seal belongs to one face or component;
- the corresponding angelic name, seal or psalmic authority belongs to the other;
- the angel is invoked as a superior constraining force;
- the lamen is worn at the breast or heart.

**[Tradition]** The theological premise is that a rebellious or dangerous spirit is safely compelled only under the authority of its opposing angel and divine name.

**[Historical caution]** Harley 6483 was copied by Peter Smart in 1712–13. The underlying material is attributed to Thomas Rudd and may descend from a source of about 1655/56, but Rudd’s personal authorship of every component is not proven.

Skinner and Rankine’s edition, originally published by Golden Hoard in 2007 and later by Llewellyn, is the main modern presentation of this system.

## Iah-hel in Lenain, 1823

Lazare Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* gives the following data for no. 62:

- name: **Iah-hel**;
- attribute: **Être suprême**;
- corresponding divine name among the alleged ancient Carmanians: **Suna**;
- ray: degrees **306 through 310**, inclusive;
- corresponding Egyptian-style decan genius: **Oroasoer**;
- days: 20 May, 31 July, 11 October, 22 December and 4 March;
- invocation: 8:20–8:40 p.m.;
- verse: Psalm 118:159;
- office: acquisition of wisdom;
- governance over philosophers, illuminated persons and those wishing to withdraw from the world;
- disposition: tranquility, solitude, exact performance of duties, modesty and virtue;
- contrary genius: scandal, luxury, inconstancy, divorce and marital disunion.

These data can be checked in the [1823 scan](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_ms05365) and [searchable PDF](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1823__lenain___science_cabalistique.pdf).

### Psalm verse

Lenain and Rudd use Vulgate numbering:

> Psalm **118:159**

Modern Hebrew and most English Bibles number it:

> Psalm **119:159**

The King James wording begins:

> “Consider how I love thy precepts…”

The full verse is available in the [parallel Septuagint/KJV presentation](https://www.die-bibel.de/en/bible/LXX%2CKJV/PSA.118).

The Latin tradition reads substantially:

> *Vide quoniam mandata tua dilexi; Domine, secundum misericordiam tuam vivifica me.*

Harley 6483 contains spelling or grammatical irregularities in its copied Latin. Those do not indicate a different psalm.

### Five degrees and calendar dates

Degrees 306°–310° correspond, under the ordinary tropical mapping beginning at 0° Aries, to **5°–10° Aquarius**.

Modern angel calendars commonly convert this to approximately **26–30 January**. Other authors print 19–23, 24–28 or 25–29 January.

**[Disputed conversion]** These date differences arise from:

- whether the count begins with degree 0 or degree 1;
- historical solar ingress tables;
- inclusive versus exclusive endpoints;
- tropical versus sidereal assumptions;
- later editors’ attempts to force five full civil days into each sector.

The historically safest citation is Lenain’s **306th–310th degrees**, not any one modern date range.

## From Lenain to Papus, Ambelain and Haziel

### Papus

**[Documented transmission]** Gérard Encausse, “Papus,” promoted Christian-kabbalistic occultism in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries and helped return Lenain’s work to occult circulation, including through prefatory and reprint activity. His writings transmitted the concept of seventy-two divine geniuses to a broad French occult readership.

Papus did not originate Iah-hel’s Exodus triplet or Rudd’s demon-pairing.

### Robert Ambelain

**[Tradition]** Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) reorganized and ritualized the seventy-two angels for twentieth-century practitioners. Later authors frequently derive their choirs, regency periods, incense, colors and practical petitions from Ambelain or from systems attributed to him.

Specific claims should be checked against the edition used: modern charts often silently combine Ambelain, Lenain, Golden Dawn color scales and astrology.

### Haziel and Kabaleb

**[Modern occult tradition]** François Bernard-Termès, writing as Haziel, and the Kabaleb school popularized the seventy-two as personal “guardian angels” governing five-day birth periods. They expanded Lenain’s short notices into psychological profiles, prayers, virtues, shadow qualities and life missions.

The popular presentation of Iah-hel as guardian of contemplatives born around 26–30 January belongs mainly to this modern current. It is not found in the medieval Jewish derivation itself.

### Choir, archangel and sephirah

Modern charts commonly assign Iah-hel to:

- the choir of **Archangels**;
- the sephirah **Hod**;
- a supervising archangel variously given as **Raphael** or **Michael**;
- the element **Air**.

**[Disputed esoteric correspondence]** These assignments vary by school. They are later Christian-Hermetic arrangements, not facts inherent in Exodus or consistently stated by Lenain.

### Why each pip card carries two Goetic spirits

Crowley’s Goetic decan scheme divides the seventy-two spirits into two sets of thirty-six:

- spirits 1–36 are day rulers;
- spirits 37–72 are night rulers.

The zodiac contains thirty-six decans of ten degrees. Therefore each decan—and consequently each minor-arcana pip from Two through Ten—receives:

- one day spirit;
- one night spirit.

Valac, no. 62, falls in the second Sagittarius decan, governed by the **Nine of Wands**. The Golden Dawn card title is “Lord of Great Strength”; its astrological force is the Moon in Sagittarius. Crowley’s tables are available in [*777*](https://www.astrumargenteum.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/777-by-Aleister-Crowley.pdf), with the card attribution also preserved in the [Hermetic Library’s Golden Dawn material](https://hermetic.com/crowley/equinox/i/viii/eqi08016).

### The Aquarius/Five of Swords attribution

Dividing the zodiac into seventy-two consecutive five-degree sectors makes no. 62 equal to degrees 306–310, or 5°–10° Aquarius. That lies inside the first ten-degree decan of Aquarius, whose Golden Dawn pip is the **Five of Swords**, “Lord of Defeat,” Venus in Aquarius.

**[Important distinction]** This is a rational later combination of the 72-angel quinance system with tarot decans. It is not Crowley’s published Goetic attribution for Valac.

## Enochian and other later overlays

### Rudd and the *Tabula Sancta*

Harley 6482–6483 preserves a synthesis of Goetic, angelic and material derived from John Dee’s angelic tables. Skinner and Rankine interpret this as evidence that Rudd or his circle attempted to connect letters in Dee’s *Tabula Sancta* with abbreviated Goetic names.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** This is an early-modern synthesis. Dee’s surviving diaries do not independently identify Valac or pair Valac with Iahhel.

### Crowley’s Enochian conjurations

The 1904 Mathers–Crowley book appends Enochian versions of Goetic conjurations.

**[Modern esoteric adaptation]** These translations are not part of the medieval Volach entry or the earliest Goetia manuscripts. They express Crowley’s attempt to combine Solomonic ritual with the Dee–Kelly angelic language.

### The “Bornless Ritual”

Crowley also connected Goetic work with the “Preliminary Invocation,” derived through nineteenth-century translation from a Greek magical papyrus and later called the Bornless Ritual.

That rite is historically separate from Valac. Its presence in Crowleyan practice should not be mistaken for an instruction in the seventeenth-century Valac entry. A history is given in the [Journal of the Western Mystery Tradition](https://hermetic.com/jwmt/v1n7/bornless).

## Iconography

## Before de Plancy

The medieval and early printed descriptions provide prose, not portraits:

- Munich: no picture;
- Florence: no known individualized portrait;
- Weyer: no seal or portrait;
- Scot: no portrait;
- Goetia manuscripts: sigil, but no narrative picture of the child and dragon.

Thus the now-famous image was not transmitted as a medieval illustration.

## Collin de Plancy

### Text

The *Dictionnaire infernal* first appeared in 1818 and went through substantially revised editions. The sixth edition of 1863 gives an entry under **Volac**, p. 696:

> “Volac, grand président aux enfers; il apparaît sous la forme d’un enfant avec des ailes d’ange…”

It continues with the two-headed dragon and thirty legions.

The [1863 French entry](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.,_1863/Volac) can be read directly.

### The “abode of planets” anomaly

De Plancy says Volac knows the “abode of the planets” and the retreat of serpents.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The planets clause is probably contamination from **Orias**, the spirit immediately preceding Volac in Weyer. Weyer says of Orias:

> *Callet planetarum mansiones* — he knows the mansions of the planets.

Weyer’s Volac instead answers about **hidden treasures**. De Plancy appears to have transferred Orias’s planetary phrase into Volac’s notice while dropping the treasure office.

This is strong evidence of a line-slip or conflation, not an independent ancient planetary power of Valac.

### Did the entry exist in 1818?

**[Partially verified]** The 1818 first edition is available in two volumes, but the 1863 text was heavily revised and expanded. The research completed here verifies the Volac entry and portrait in **1863**; it does not securely establish that the same wording already appeared in the 1818 first edition. The [1818 volume facsimiles](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre:Jacques_Collin_de_Plancy_-_Dictionnaire_infernal-1818-T1.djvu) should be searched leaf by leaf before asserting continuity.

### Louis Le Breton’s portrait

**[Documented image]** The 1863 edition includes Louis Le Breton’s engraving:

- a nude or nearly nude young child;
- feathered angel wings;
- sitting astride the long body of a reptilian dragon;
- two reptile heads projecting in opposite directions;
- both heads with open mouths;
- clawed legs and a curling tail;
- a minimal landscape beneath;
- captioned **VOLAC**.

The image can be viewed separately as [*Volac.png*](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Fichier:Volac.png) or in the [complete 1863 scan](https://archive.org/details/dictionnaireinfe00coll_1).

Le Breton did not invent the child, wings or two-headed dragon; those descend from the medieval prose. He did establish the visual composition most subsequent illustrators copied.

## The seal versus the portrait

The Goetic seal and Le Breton portrait are independent forms of iconography:

- the seal is a seventeenth-century ritual sign;
- the portrait is a nineteenth-century narrative illustration.

No primary source says that the seal visually encodes the child or dragon.

## Modern occult art

Twentieth- and twenty-first-century images typically follow one of four patterns:

1. literal redrawings of Le Breton’s child and dragon;
2. more ominous adolescent or adult riders;
3. serpentine dragons surrounding the Goetic seal;
4. images of a demonic nun derived from *The Conjuring 2*.

The fourth type is wholly cinematic and bears no visual relationship to the grimoire description.

## People and works

## Medieval and Renaissance transmission

### Anonymous Munich compiler

The compiler or copyist of Clm 849 preserves the earliest presently identified *Volach*. The manuscript shows that the figure belonged to practical Latin necromantic literature before print demonology.

### Anonymous Florence compiler

Plut. 89 sup. 38 independently confirms the pre-Weyer tradition and the thirty-legion reading.

### Johannes Trithemius

Trithemius’s *Antipalus maleficiorum* catalogued a work titled *Liber officiorum spirituum* by 1508.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** This is evidence that an “Offices of Spirits” textual family existed before Weyer, but Trithemius does not identify Valac by name in the bibliographic notice.

### Johann Weyer

Weyer printed *Volac* in 1577. His catalogue is the decisive bridge between manuscript necromancy and later demonological/Goetic literature.

His purpose was polemical and skeptical toward magical claims, especially in the broader context of witch prosecution, even though his worldview still admitted demons.

### Reginald Scot

Scot disseminated **Valac** in English in 1584. Like Weyer, he sought to expose fraud, superstition and cruelty rather than promote demon worship. Ironically, later magicians used his reproductions as source material.

## The Goetia and Rudd

### Anonymous *Lemegeton* compiler or compilers

The *Lemegeton* was assembled from pre-existing material during the seventeenth century. Its first book reorganized the spirit catalogue, added seals and placed the spirits inside an operative Solomonic ritual structure.

### Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

Material attributed to Thomas Rudd added corresponding Shem angels as controlling powers. Peter Smart copied the surviving Harley manuscripts in 1712–13.

The distinction is essential:

- “Rudd’s system” is a useful shorthand;
- the surviving physical manuscript is Smart’s copy;
- Rudd’s exact authorial responsibility remains uncertain.

## Nineteenth-century occultism

### Collin de Plancy

De Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* popularized Valac in French and introduced the probable Orias/planets contamination.

### Louis Le Breton

Le Breton’s 1863 portrait fixed Valac’s standard pre-cinematic visual identity.

### Lazare Lenain

Lenain did not write about Valac’s demon description. His significance is the structured presentation of **Iah-hel no. 62**, its degrees, hours, psalm and moral office.

### Papus

Papus helped transmit Lenain’s Christian-kabbalistic angelology into modern French occultism.

## Golden Dawn and Thelema

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers

Mathers translated and edited the Goetia material later issued with Crowley. His edition regularized spellings and seals but introduced or perpetuated readings not equally supported by the manuscripts, including the 38-legion figure.

### Aleister Crowley

Crowley published the 1904 edition and later supplied:

- a Thelemic framing;
- Enochian conjurations;
- decanic and tarot correspondences;
- material in *777*;
- the night attribution for Valac.

### Frater Achad

**[Negative finding]** No securely sourced Valac-specific working or doctrine by Charles Stansfeld Jones, Frater Achad, was found. He belongs to the wider Crowleyan milieu, but should not be presented as a documented Valac practitioner without a text or diary citation.

## Modern scholarship and practice

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 edition restores manuscript readings, records variants and makes Weyer, Scot and the Goetia manuscripts comparatively accessible. His work is fundamental for distinguishing “what the Goetia manuscripts say” from Crowley-era editorial convention.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

*The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (2007) published and reconstructed the Rudd material, including the angelic constraints and paired lamens. Their edition gives Valu at p. 164.

### Richard Kieckhefer

Kieckhefer’s edition of Clm 849 provides the crucial evidence that *Volach* predates Weyer.

### Brian Johnson

Johnson’s edition of the Florence material supplies the second major pre-Weyer witness.

### Jean-Patrice Boudet, Julien Véronèse and Jan Veenstra

These scholars have studied medieval ritual magic, spirit catalogues and texts such as the *Livre des Esperitz*. Boudet’s comparative work is available in [“Les who's who démonologiques de la Renaissance”](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/1171).

### Owen Davies

Davies provides broad historical context for grimoires and their readership. No distinctive Valac etymology or Valac-specific theory was identified in the consulted material.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent argued for reading the Goetia inside the broader necromantic, spirit-office and Mediterranean magical traditions rather than as an isolated Crowleyan text. No independently verified Valac-specific operation or etymology was located.

### Poke Runyon

Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* presents a modern ceremonial system using dark-mirror or “facial reflection” methods and the seventy-two spirits. A publisher description is available at [Poke Runyon’s site](https://www.pokerunyon.com/books/the-book-of-solomons-magick-second-edition). No published Valac-specific result was found in the material consulted.

### Lon Milo DuQuette

DuQuette, Christopher Hyatt and David P. Wilson’s *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* retransmits the Crowleyan system. Its Valac data include:

- night ruler;
- 10°–20° Sagittarius;
- Nine of Wands;
- Mercury by rank;
- Crowley’s thirty-eight legions.

The book’s bibliographic record is available through [Open Library](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL8638564M/Aleister_Crowley%27s_Illustrated_Goetia).

## Grimoire ancestors and comparanda

## *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The strongest ancestry lies in the “Offices of Spirits” family. Weyer names a source resembling *Liber officiorum spirituum* and “Empto. Salomonis.” Munich and Florence demonstrate that Volach material already circulated in that environment.

The precise stemma remains unresolved: the Munich text is not simply “Weyer in manuscript,” and Weyer need not have copied either surviving codex directly.

## *Livre des Esperitz*

The French *Livre des Esperitz*, preserved in Cambridge, Trinity College O.8.29, belongs to a related spirit-catalogue tradition. A [digital transcription](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/livre-esperitz/read/) is available.

**[Negative finding]** No secure *Valac/Volach* entry has been identified there. Its relevance is genealogical and generic, not a direct name attestation.

## Munich handbook

Munich Clm 849 is not merely a vague ancestor: it contains **Volach himself** and is therefore the most important pre-Weyer source presently known.

## *Book of Oberon*

The sixteenth-century *Book of Oberon* preserves another large spirit-office collection. Spirits such as Coolor or Doolas have been compared with Valac because of serpent-related powers.

**[Disputed]** Functional resemblance does not prove identity.

## *Book of Abramelin*

**[Negative finding]** No verified Valac/Volach entry was found in the principal Abramelin name lists. Similar spellings in modern indexes should be checked against the German and French manuscript forms before identification.

## *Testament of Solomon*

The late-antique *Testament of Solomon* supplies the foundational legend of Solomon interrogating and binding demons.

**[Documented distinction]** It does not contain Valac by that name or the winged-child-on-dragon description. It is an ancestor of the Solomonic literary framework, not a direct Valac witness.

## Literature, film and games

## Dante and Milton

**[Negative finding]** Valac does not appear in Dante’s *Commedia* or Milton’s *Paradise Lost*. Attempts to place him there confuse the broad subject of demons with the specific Goetic catalogue.

## Hellboy

**[Modern adaptation]** Mike Mignola’s *Hellboy: Box Full of Evil* nos. 1–2, published in August and September 1999, includes a demon named **Ualac**. The character seeks apocalyptic power connected with Hellboy and is recognizably named from the Goetic variant.

Publication data are recorded by the [Grand Comics Database](https://www.comics.org/issue/62889/).

## Megami Tensei

**[Modern adaptation]** **Valac** appears in *Megami Tensei II* (1990), later *Kyūyaku Megami Tensei*, and *Giten Megami Tensei*. The Japanese form is ヴォラク, *Voraku*. It is classified as a Wilder or Fallen demon depending on the game.

A series-specific catalogue is available at the [Megami Tensei Wiki](https://megamitensei.fandom.com/wiki/Valac).

**[Negative finding]** No Valac appearance in the *Persona* subseries was verified. General inclusion in the wider Megami Tensei franchise should not be turned into a Persona credit.

## *Chilling Adventures of Sabrina*

**[Modern adaptation]** A demon named **Volac** appears in the comic’s Edward Spellman backstory, particularly issue no. 7, published in 2017. The spelling points directly toward the Weyer/Goetia source tradition. Archie Comics’ [issue preview](https://archiecomics.com/sabrina7preview/) documents the publication.

## *Welcome to Demon School! Iruma-kun*

**[Modern adaptation]** Clara Valac bears the Goetic surname. The series uses numerous demonological names, making the connection highly probable.

**[Caution]** Unless an author interview explicitly confirms each name source, the derivation remains an informed inference rather than a documented statement of intent. The character appears in the [official NHK character guide](https://www.nhk-character.com/chara/iruma/en/character).

## *Bloodstained*

**[Modern adaptation]** *Bloodstained: Curse of the Moon* (2018) uses **Valac** for a large two-headed draconic boss. This is one of the clearest visual adaptations of the grimoire’s mount: the game turns the two-headed dragon into the named monster itself. The game’s official site is [curseofthemoon.com](https://curseofthemoon.com/en/1/). The design was also reused in *Bloodstained: Ritual of the Night*.

## Bartimaeus

**[Negative finding]** No Valac character was verified in Jonathan Stroud’s Bartimaeus novels. The books employ Solomonic magic and many spirit names, but thematic similarity is not attestation.

## *Helluva Boss*

**[Negative finding]** No named Valac character has been verified in *Helluva Boss* through September 2026. The program’s “Ars Goetia” aristocracy draws broadly from Goetic nomenclature, but Valac should not be added to its cast without an episode or official credit.

## *The Conjuring 2* and *The Nun*

### The cinematic character

**[Modern invention]** *The Conjuring 2* (2016) names its antagonist **Valak** and depicts it primarily as a corpse-like demonic nun, played by Bonnie Aarons. The character continued in:

- *The Nun* (2018);
- *The Conjuring: The Devil Made Me Do It* in associated franchise material;
- *The Nun II* (2023).

The official *Nun* site is [here](https://www.thenun-movie.com/).

### Relationship to the grimoire

The rare spelling **Valak** appears in Mathers–Crowley’s variant list, making a Goetic borrowing likely. But the film character’s:

- nun form;
- Catholic-abbey mythology;
- inverted crosses;
- possession narrative;
- status as a singular franchise supervillain;

are cinematic creations.

James Wan has explained that the nun design arose during production as a more personally unsettling replacement for an earlier horned-demon concept. His comments appear in a [Warner-associated interview concerning *The Nun II*](https://www.samdb.co.za/blogs/blog/2023/09/05/qa-james-wan-producer-writer-based-on-characters-the-nun-ii/).

**[Open question]** The consulted production material does not document precisely who selected the name from which occult book.

### Name revival

**[Cultural finding]** The film franchise transformed Valak from a relatively minor catalogue spirit into a globally recognizable horror name. It also caused a major iconographic inversion:

> before 2016: winged child and two-headed dragon;  
> after 2016: demonic nun.

The nun is now frequently treated online as “the traditional demon Valak,” but no pre-film grimoire gives Valac a female monastic appearance.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. “Valac first appeared in Weyer”

**Verdict: false in light of current evidence.**

- Munich Clm 849: *Volach*, fifteenth century.
- Florence Plut. 89 sup. 38: *Volach*, around the end of the fifteenth century.
- Weyer: first known **printed** appearance, 1577.

## 2. Weyer 1563 or 1577?

- 1563: first edition of *De praestigiis daemonum*.
- 1577: fifth edition carrying the *Pseudomonarchia* appendix.

Valac’s printed attestation belongs to **1577**.

## 3. Is Valac Weyer no. 49 or no. 50?

- In Weyer’s complete 69-spirit sequence, **50**.
- In Scot’s shortened sequence, after omission of Pruflas, **49**.
- Peterson’s parallel numbering can display §49 because it aligns the Latin with Scot.

The disagreement is editorial/list-based, not evidence of two Valacs.

## 4. Twenty-seven, thirty or thirty-eight legions?

- 27: Munich.
- 30: Florence and the dominant tradition.
- 38: Mathers–Crowley.

Thirty is the best-supported reading. Twenty-seven is a genuine early variant. Thirty-eight is probably a late error.

Internet claims of thirteen or twenty legions were not traced to a primary witness consulted here.

## 5. Does Valac know planetary mansions?

Weyer assigns planetary mansions to **Orias**, not Valac. De Plancy’s Volac notice appears to import the phrase from its neighboring entry.

The planets office is therefore likely a nineteenth-century textual conflation.

## 6. Is the seal ancient?

No seal accompanies the earliest four witnesses. Its currently demonstrable history begins in the seventeenth-century Goetia manuscripts.

## 7. Is Valac an ancient god?

No supporting evidence was found. Resemblances to Veles, Vlach, Wallachia or supposed Semitic words are speculative.

## 8. Is Valac the same as Vual?

No. Their ranks, forms, offices and legion totals are distinct throughout the catalogue.

## 9. Are Coolor and Doolas spellings of Valac?

Possibly related at the level of textual ancestry or function, but not demonstrated as mere orthographic forms. Printing them unqualified in a “names of Valac” list conceals the uncertainty.

## 10. Is Iah-hel intrinsically Valac’s opponent?

Not in the medieval or Weyer tradition. The opposition arises by ordinal pairing in the Rudd system.

## 11. Is Iah-hel’s meaning “Supreme Being”?

That is Lenain’s documented attribute. It is not a secure word-for-word Hebrew translation.

## 12. Is Iah-hel’s period 19–23 or 26–30 January?

The stable datum is degrees 306–310. Civil-date conversions differ between modern systems.

## 13. Is Valac Five of Swords or Nine of Wands?

Both occur, but in different systems:

- Iah-hel’s five-degree Aquarius sector → Five of Swords by later decan overlay.
- Crowley’s night-Goetia system → Nine of Wands.

Only the latter is Crowley’s Goetic correspondence for spirit 62.

## 14. Is Mercury or Saturn Valac’s planet?

The Goetia’s rank system makes every President Mercurial. Saturn, Uranus or other individualized planetary claims are later systems unless separately sourced.

## 15. Are modern “enns” traditional?

Phrases such as “Avage Secore on ca Valac” belong to modern demonolatry currents.

**[Modern invention]** They do not occur in Munich, Florence, Weyer, Scot, the Sloane manuscripts, Harley 6483 or Mathers–Crowley.

## 16. Is Valac a “fallen angel” or “Marquis of Serpents”?

These are modern categorical or literary titles. The historical rank is President. The older text does not narrate a fall from heaven.

## 17. Did Thomas Rudd personally write Harley 6483?

The manuscript is Peter Smart’s 1712–13 copy. “Rudd” describes the attributed source or system. Exact authorship and compilation remain open.

## 18. Is *The Conjuring* nun traditional?

No. The name probably draws from the grimoire variant **Valak**, but the nun design and franchise story are modern fiction.

## Open questions

1. **What is the etymology of *Volach*?**  
   No satisfactory linguistic derivation has been established.

2. **Which surviving or lost “Offices of Spirits” witness stands behind Munich, Florence and Weyer?**  
   Shared content demonstrates relationship, but not a complete stemma.

3. **Why does Munich give twenty-seven legions?**  
   It may preserve an earlier reading, a local revision or a numeral error.

4. **Who designed Valac’s Goetic seal?**  
   Its source before the seventeenth-century manuscripts is unknown.

5. **How exactly did thirty become thirty-eight in Mathers–Crowley?**  
   A full collation of Mathers’s exemplars and drafts would be required.

6. **Was de Plancy’s planets clause a deliberate synthesis or an accidental line-slip from Orias?**  
   The adjacency makes error highly probable, but authorial intent is unrecorded.

7. **How much of the angelic constraint system was authored by Rudd?**  
   The gap between attributed composition and Smart’s surviving copy remains significant.

8. **Is Coolor or Doolas genuinely a Volach doublet?**  
   Functional comparison alone cannot resolve this.

9. **Did the 1818 first edition of de Plancy already include Volac?**  
   The 1863 entry is secure; the first-edition occurrence requires direct volume-by-volume verification.

10. **Who selected “Valak” for *The Conjuring 2*, and from which edition?**  
    The spelling strongly resembles Mathers–Crowley, but the consulted production interviews do not document the bibliographic source.
