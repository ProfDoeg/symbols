# Astaroth: Research Dossier

## Editorial method and evidence labels

This dossier separates six kinds of claim:

- **[Documented text]** — the cited source actually says this.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** — a historical or philological inference supported by comparison, but not stated by the source itself.
- **[Tradition]** — an assertion transmitted within a religious, demonological, or magical system.
- **[Esoteric correspondence]** — a deliberately constructed magical association, not a historical identity or scientific property.
- **[Disputed]** — the evidence admits materially different readings.
- **[Legend]** — a supernatural or narrative claim reported without treating it as independently verified fact.
- **[Modern invention]** — a later artistic, occult, fictional, or gaming development.

Dates attached to manuscripts usually date the surviving witness, not necessarily the composition of every passage it contains.

# The name and its origins

## 1. Before the demon: ʿAshtart, Astarte, Ashtoreth, and Ashtaroth

**[Documented ancient usage]** The name behind Astaroth belongs to a much older West Semitic complex:

- Ugaritic and Northwest Semitic forms include forms conventionally transliterated **ʿAṯtart / ʿAštart**.
- Phoenician **ʿštrt** becomes Greek **Ἀστάρτη, Astartē**, and Latin **Astarte**.
- Biblical Hebrew has singular **עַשְׁתֹּרֶת, ʿAštōret**, conventionally *Ashtoreth*, and plural **עַשְׁתָּרוֹת, ʿAštārôt**, conventionally *Ashtaroth*.
- The Latin Vulgate used **Astaroth** in biblical passages referring to the foreign cult or its multiple manifestations. Thus the exact Latin spelling existed centuries before Weyer used it as a demon’s personal name.

In Judges 2:13, Israel is said to have served Baal and the Ashtaroth/Astartes. Milton later drew directly on this biblical vocabulary, not on the *Goetia*. The relevant ancient and biblical name history is summarized in the [Jewish Encyclopedia’s “Ashtoreth”](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/2005-ashtoreth) and can be checked against the biblical texts themselves.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Astarte was a major Levantine deity whose functions differed by period and place. Royal power, warfare, sexuality, hunting, maritime associations, and astral identifications all occur. Calling her simply “a fertility goddess” is an older simplification. She was identified in some Greek and Roman settings with Aphrodite or Venus, but that interpretatio does not make every Astarte cult identical with Greek Aphrodite worship.

**[Disputed]** Astarte, Akkadian Ishtar, Ugaritic ʿAthtart, and South Arabian ʿAthtar are linguistically and historically related, but “Astarte was merely Ishtar under another name” is too absolute. Their cults, genders, functions, and local histories diverged.

**[Disputed philology]** A longstanding explanation says that biblical **Ashtoreth** combines the deity’s consonants with vowels associated with Hebrew **bōšet**, “shame,” as a polemical deformation. That remains influential, but some philologists argue that the vocalization can be explained through ordinary linguistic development. The consonantal relationship to ʿAštart is considerably firmer than any single explanation of the Masoretic vowels.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The demon’s form **Astaroth** most plausibly arose from the biblical/Vulgate name—especially the Latinized plural—being treated as a masculine singular proper name in Christian demonology. No surviving text records a single historical moment when “the goddess Astarte became the male Duke Astaroth.” It was a gradual reinterpretation of a foreign god-name within Christian polemic and spirit catalogues.

**[Disputed alternative]** *Ashtaroth* also occurs in ancient place names, including Ashtaroth and Ashtaroth-Karnaim. A place-name influence is theoretically possible, but the biblical deity and cult supply the clearer route into demonology.

**[Correction of a frequent conflation]** Astarte and **Asherah** are distinct West Semitic goddesses. Older dictionaries and modern popular summaries sometimes merge them.

## 2. Earliest attestation of Astaroth as a demonic being

Astaroth does **not** first appear in Weyer.

### The *Passion of Bartholomew*

**[Documented text]** Latin manuscripts of the apocryphal *Passion of Bartholomew*, some datable to approximately the eighth or ninth century, contain **Astaroth/Astaruth** as an idol or god in an Indian temple whose voice is revealed to be demonic. The demon is credited within the story with making people sick so that they sacrifice, and then appearing to heal them. Bartholomew silences and expels him.

A later scene describes the exposed demon as an Ethiopian or black figure darker than soot, with fiery eyes, sparks issuing from the mouth, thorn-like wings, and hands constrained by fire. These details belong to the apostolic legend, not to the later dragon-riding Duke. The manuscript evidence and synopsis are catalogued by [NASSCAL’s e-Clavis Christian Apocrypha](https://www.nasscal.com/e-clavis-christian-apocrypha/passion-of-bartholomew/).

**[Finding]** This is the earliest secure manuscript attestation located here of Astaroth as an individual demon or false god. It proves that the demon-name predates Weyer by many centuries. It does **not** supply the later rank, seal, forty legions, viper, stinking breath, or offices.

### The *Golden Legend*

**[Documented medieval reception]** Jacobus de Voragine’s thirteenth-century *Golden Legend* retells the Bartholomew episode. Astaroth is the temple demon silenced by the apostle; another demon, Berith, explains what has happened. This carried the name into one of medieval Europe’s most widely circulated hagiographical collections. An English transcription is available in the [Christian Iconography edition](https://www.christianiconography.info/goldenLegend/bartholomew.htm).

## 3. Fifteenth-century ritual evidence

### Munich Handbook, Clm 849

**[Documented text]** The fifteenth-century necromancer’s handbook now Munich, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, Clm 849 contains Astaroth among demonic names used in ritual lists and conjurations. Richard Kieckhefer edited and studied it as *Forbidden Rites*. It establishes that the name had entered practical Latin necromantic literature before Weyer.

**[Finding]** The Munich material does not yet give Astaroth the complete Weyer profile. Its importance is continuity of name and ritual context, not identity of description.

### Florence, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Plut. 89 sup. 38

**[Documented text]** A manuscript dated January 1494 contains an office-of-spirits catalogue with **Ascarot/Astarot**, corresponding closely to Weyer's later Astaroth entry. Jean-Patrice Boudet’s comparison labels Astaroth a great and strong Duke with forty legions in both the Florentine witness and Weyer. See Boudet’s [study and concordance](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/pdf/1019) and Peterson’s discussion in the [*Pseudomonarchia* introduction](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** This Florentine witness is the earliest presently identified source for the recognizable *office* profile behind Weyer’s Duke. It is more directly ancestral to Weyer than the Bartholomew legend.

### *Livre des Esperitz*

**[Negative finding]** The surviving French *Livre des Esperitz* is an important ancestor or relative of the spirit-office tradition, but **Astaroth is absent from its surviving catalogue**. It should not be cited as though it contained his entry. The text can be searched in the [Wikisource edition](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre_des_esperitz).

### *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

**[Documented source claim]** Weyer said that his catalogue came from a *Liber officiorum spirituum*, also styled a book of “Empto. Solomonis.” This appears to designate a fluid family of spirit-office catalogues rather than one stable book with a single recoverable text.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The Florence manuscript, *Livre des Esperitz*, Weyer, and related English materials are witnesses to this larger family. Their overlapping names and offices indicate textual transmission, but their omissions, reorderings, and variant descriptions show that no surviving manuscript can simply be treated as the archetype of all the others.

# The description across the sources

## 1. Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* (1577)

Weyer’s catalogue is an appendix to *De praestigiis daemonum*. Astaroth is entry 28, printed on approximately p. 921 of the 1577 edition.

**[Documented Latin, exact opening]**

> “Astaroth Dux magnus & fortis, prodiens angelica specie turpissima, insidensque in dracone infernali, & viperam portans manu dextra.”

A literal gloss is: Astaroth is a great and strong Duke, coming forth in an exceedingly hideous angelic form, seated upon an infernal dragon and bearing a viper in his right hand.

The remainder of Weyer’s entry states, clause by clause:

- He answers truthfully about past, present, future, and hidden matters.
- He willingly speaks about the Creator of spirits and about their fall.
- He explains how the spirits sinned and fell.
- He claims he did not fall voluntarily.
- He makes a person extraordinarily learned in the liberal arts.
- He commands forty legions.
- The exorcist must not permit him to come too near because of the intolerable stench he breathes out.
- The operator is to hold a **silver magical ring** in the hand near the face, which protects against injury.

The Latin text is in Peterson’s [complete *Pseudomonarchia* transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm); a [1577 facsimile](https://booksofmagick.com/wp-content/uploads/2020/10/Pseudomonarchia-Daemonum-1577.pdf) permits the typography and pagination to be checked.

**[Context]** Weyer was a physician arguing against indiscriminate witch persecution. Publishing the catalogue did not amount to endorsing its spirits as reliable teachers or recommending their invocation. He said he withheld portions to frustrate would-be practitioners.

## 2. Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584)

Scot translated much of Weyer into English in Book XVI, chapter 2. In the first edition, Astaroth’s entry begins around p. 221.

**[Documented English, exact opening]**

> “Astaroth is a great and a strong duke, comming foorth in the shape of a fowle angell, sitting upon an infernall dragon”

Scot then reports:

- A viper is carried in the right hand.
- Astaroth answers truly concerning present, past, future, and secrets.
- He willingly discusses the Creator, the spirits’ sin, and their fall.
- He says his own fall was not voluntary.
- He makes a person wonderfully learned in the liberal sciences.
- He rules forty legions.
- His stinking breath makes close approach dangerous.
- A magical ring held near the conjurer’s face supplies protection.

Scot drops Weyer’s explicit adjective **silver** before “magical ring.” Peterson marks the omission editorially in his parallel text; it should not be silently restored as Scot’s own wording. The entry is in the [complete Book XVI transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/scot16.htm), with first-edition scans at [Cornell](https://digital.library.cornell.edu/catalog/witchcraft081) and [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/discouerieofwitc00scot/).

**[Context]** Scot’s book was skeptical and polemical. Like Weyer, he reproduced magical material substantially to expose or discredit contemporary beliefs. Later ritual magicians extracted the catalogue from that polemical frame.

## 3. Sloane 2731

Sloane 2731, whose *Goetia* portion is usually dated 1686/87, numbers Astaroth twenty-nine.

**[Documented manuscript reading]** Its distinctive appearance phrase is **“an unhealthfull Angel.”** The spirit rides an infernal dragon-like creature, carries a viper in the right hand, and has dangerous breath. It promises past-present-future knowledge, secrets, an account of the fall, and proficiency in liberal sciences. It rules forty legions.

The concluding lamen instruction is stronger than the simple Weyer entry: the seal is to be worn before the operator, otherwise Astaroth will neither appear nor obey. The manuscript page and seal may be inspected in the [Sloane 2731 diplomatic edition](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/goetia-sloane2731/read/#p011).

## 4. Sloane 3825

Peterson’s 2001 main text follows Sloane 3825 closely. The manuscript location is fol. 104v.

**[Documented text, exact opening]**

> “The 29th spirit in order is Named Astaroth, he is a Mighty & strong duke”

Its important reading is **“an unbeautifull angel.”** The rest matches the established profile:

- riding an infernal dragon-like creature;
- carrying a viper in the right hand;
- dangerous “stinking Breath”;
- protection by the magical ring;
- true answers about present, past, and future;
- discovery of all secrets;
- willing declaration of how the spirits fell and the reason for his fall;
- extraordinary knowledge in the liberal sciences;
- forty legions;
- the seal worn as a lamen, without which he will not obey.

Peterson’s annotated transcription is at [Esoteric Archives](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm). The 2001 edition is *The Lesser Key of Solomon: Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis*, ed. Joseph H. Peterson, Part I, spirit 29; its online manuscript apparatus identifies Sloane 3825 fol. 104v. A searchable [PDF of the 2001 edition](https://jamesparsonslakebucket.s3-us-west-1.amazonaws.com/text/Peterson_Joseph_H_The_Lesser_Key_of_Solomon.pdf) is available.

## 5. Harley 6483 and the “Rudd” material

Harley 6483 was copied by Peter Smart in 1712–13. It transmits the Goetic catalogue with angelic counterforces and is the principal manuscript behind Skinner and Rankine’s *The Goetia of Dr Rudd*.

**[Documented manuscript tradition]** Its Astaroth entry retains the Duke, dragon, viper, breath, knowledge, and forty legions. Published transcriptions report the appearance as a **“beautiful Angel,”** almost certainly a significant textual deviation from *turpissima*, “foul,” “unbeautiful,” and “unhealthful.”

**[Disputed]** “Beautiful” may be a copying error, omission of a negative element, deliberate correction, or evidence that Smart’s exemplar already differed. Mathers’ later “hurtful” is another attempt to make sense of the unstable adjective.

**[Distinctive Rudd material]** Astaroth is paired with the twenty-ninth Shem angel, Reiiel. The operative lamen places the demon’s seal on one face and the constraining angelic name, seal, or verse on the reverse. The angelic face is directed toward the magician or heart. This turns the lamen from a mere badge of authority into a double-sided structure: the spirit is confronted by its celestial counterforce while the operator stands behind the protecting angelic face.

## 6. Mathers-Crowley, *The Book of the Goetia* (1904)

The 1904 edition prints Astaroth on p. 31.

**[Documented text, exact opening]**

> “The Twenty-ninth Spirit is Astaroth. He is a Mighty, Strong Duke, and appeareth in the Form of an hurtful Angel”

The entry then gives the dragon-like infernal beast, right-hand viper, noisome breath, protective magical ring, knowledge of past-present-future, all secrets, the spirits’ fall and his own, liberal sciences, forty legions, and obligatory lamen.

The complete public-domain entry and plate are in the [Sacred Texts edition](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks05.htm), p. 31; the [1904 volume index and page images](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Index:The_Book_of_the_Goetia_(Mathers-Crowley,_1904).djvu) confirm the edition.

**[Textual finding]** “Hurtful” is not an exact translation of Weyer’s **turpissima**, which means extremely ugly, foul, or base. It may reflect a damaged English exemplar, a semantic guess, or a deliberate effort to connect the adjective with the subsequent warning about the breath.

## 8. What Astaroth does not do in the core entry

**[Negative textual finding]** Unlike some other Goetic spirits, Astaroth’s core entry does **not** promise:

- treasure;
- love or sexual favors;
- reconciliation of friends;
- languages;
- familiars;
- invisibility;
- transmutation;
- flight or transport;
- public office;
- theft;
- necromantic resurrection.

Later demonologies sometimes give him political friendship, treasury, or Wednesday rulership, but those are later accretions.

## 9. Timing, ring, and compelled appearance

**[Documented general Goetic rule]** The timing assigned to Dukes is from sunrise until noon in clear weather. This is a class rule, not repeated in Astaroth’s paragraph.

**[Documented general Goetic rule]** Operations are to be timed with regard to the Moon. Sloane witnesses differ slightly in their sequence of preferred lunar ages: Sloane 3825 gives 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 days, while Sloane 2731 appears to read 2, 4, 6, 9, 10, 12, and 14. These should be reported as manuscript variants, not harmonized.

**[Documented text]** Weyer specifies a silver magical ring. In the *Goetia*’s general equipment instructions, the magical ring may be gold or silver and is held before the face as protection against the spirit’s fumes or breath.

**[Documented general ritual]** If a spirit appears in an unsuitable form, the operator may use the Hexagram of Solomon and command it to assume a human or more pleasing form.

**[Finding]** That general remedy is sometimes misreported as “Astaroth’s second form.” The individual entry never describes what Astaroth’s alternate form would look like.

# Number, order, and the 69/72 problem

**[Documented text]** Weyer’s printed *Pseudomonarchia* contains sixty-nine spirits, although its Belial material alludes to a group of seventy-two kings or spirits.

The *Goetia* reaches seventy-two by:

- adding **Vassago**, **Seere**, **Dantalion**, and **Andromalius**, none of whom appears in Weyer’s printed sixty-nine;
- omitting Weyer’s **Pruflas/Bufas**.

Thus:

\[
69 - 1 + 4 = 72
\]

**[Documented textual history]** Scot had already omitted Pruflas, probably because the Weyer copy or derivative available to him lacked the entry. The seventeenth-century compiler did much more than insert a single new name: numerous spirits were reordered.

**[Finding]** Astaroth’s movement from Weyer 28 to *Goetia* 29 cannot be explained merely by “Vassago was inserted before him.” The intervening catalogue was extensively rearranged. A comparative list is available in Peterson’s [spirit concordance](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia_spirits.pdf).

**[Tradition]** Seventy-two was an attractive target because it could mirror the seventy-two names or angels of the Shem HaMephorash. That formal symmetry becomes explicit in the Rudd material. It remains unclear whether theological symmetry was the original reason the anonymous *Goetia* compiler expanded the list or a subsequent rationale.

## 2. The seventy-twofold name from Exodus

**[Documented Jewish tradition]** Exodus 14:19–21 consists, in the consonantal Hebrew tradition used for the operation, of three verses of seventy-two letters each.

The standard extraction is:

1. Write Exodus 14:19 in its normal direction.
2. Write 14:20 in reverse direction.
3. Write 14:21 in the normal direction.
4. Read the resulting seventy-two vertical columns as three-letter names.

This boustrophedon arrangement yields seventy-two triads. The twenty-ninth is **ריי, RYY**. The relevant biblical verses can be consulted together at [Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Exodus.14.19-21?with=Kabbalah).

**[Historical distinction]** In Jewish sources this is fundamentally a seventy-twofold **divine name**, not originally a cast of seventy-two Goetic guardian angels. Rabbinic discussion of the seventy-two-letter name and medieval magical elaboration precede the Christian-Hermetic angel lists.

**[Tradition]** Adding **-el** or **-yah** to the triads produces pronounceable angel names. This procedure became especially visible in Christian Kabbalah. It is not equivalent to the earlier Jewish claim that the triads themselves are individual guardian angels with dates, personality types, and tarot cards.

## 3. From *Sefer Raziel* to Christian Kabbalah

### *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh*

**[Tradition/history]** *Sefer Raziel* is a medieval compilation that preserves older Jewish magical and cosmological materials, including forms of the seventy-twofold name. Its legendary frame attributes the book to the angel Raziel and ultimately to Adam, but the surviving work is medieval; the famous printed edition appeared in Amsterdam in 1701.

**[Legend]** The book’s supposed angelic delivery to Adam is a sacred-magical origin story, not a historical account of authorship.

### Reuchlin and Christian Kabbalah

**[Documented reception]** Johannes Reuchlin’s *De Arte Cabalistica* (1517) helped circulate expanded names ending in *-el* or *-yah* among Christian Kabbalists. In this environment the Hebrew triads increasingly became a hierarchy of named angels.

### Athanasius Kircher

**[Documented reception]** Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–54) published and systematized Kabbalistic divine and angelic tables for a Christian learned readership. His presentation became an important intermediary for later occultists. Peterson reproduces relevant Kircher material in his [Kircher/Juratus notes](https://www.esotericarchives.com/juratus/kircher.htm).

**[Caution]** Kircher did not originate the Exodus procedure, and his antiquarian claims frequently mixed Jewish sources, Christian theology, speculative linguistics, and prisca-theologia assumptions.

## 4. Rudd’s angelic constraint

**[Documented manuscript system]** Harley 6483 assigns the angels to the Goetic spirits by ordinal position:

\[
\text{Spirit 29 Astaroth} \longleftrightarrow \text{Angel 29 Reiiel}
\]

This is the direct basis of the requested pairing.

The practical logic is apotropaic:

- the demon’s seal faces outward;
- the angelic counterseal, name, and/or scriptural verse occupies the reverse;
- the angelic face is oriented toward the operator;
- the demon is commanded through a divine authority represented by its numerically corresponding angel.

**[Tradition]** The arrangement assumes that the Shem angel can bind, thwart, or neutralize the corresponding Goetic spirit. It does not say that Reiiel is Astaroth’s creator, former identity, personal guardian, or moral “opposite” in a psychological sense.

**[Open historical point]** No theological argument in Harley 6483 explains why Reiiel in particular defeats Astaroth beyond the shared ordinal number twenty-nine.

### Was it really Dr Thomas Rudd?

**[Disputed]** Skinner and Rankine reconstruct the system as the work of the English mathematician and military engineer Thomas Rudd, possibly dating an antecedent to 1655–56. The surviving manuscript, however, is Peter Smart’s 1712–13 copy and bears ambiguous “Dr Rudd” attributions.

Critics argue that:

- some materials incorporated into the manuscript postdate the period proposed for the original;
- “Dr Rudd” may designate ownership, an exemplar, or a later attribution;
- Peter Smart, not Rudd, is the directly evidenced copyist;
- no autograph Goetic manuscript by Thomas Rudd survives.

Accordingly:

- **“Harley 6483/Peter Smart system” is documented.**
- **“Composed in full by Thomas Rudd in 1655” is a disputed reconstruction.**

Skinner and Rankine’s edition is bibliographically documented at [Open Library](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL11622811M/The_Goetia_of_Dr_Rudd); the attribution problem is summarized in the [Celephaïs Press addenda](https://celephaispress.blogspot.com/2021/06/addenda-et-scholia-2.html).

## 5. Lenain’s Reiiel, 1823

Lazare Républicain Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* provides the fullest early nineteenth-century table for Reiiel. On p. 64 he gives:

- no. 29, **Reiiel**;
- the attribute **“Dieu prompt à secourir”**;
- the holy name **Zimi**, assigned to the “language of the Peruvians”;
- rays 141 through 145, meaning the five-degree interval 20°–25° Leo;
- the fifteenth decadal genius, **Phupe**, under Mars;
- the five calendar dates 17 April, 28 June, 8 September, 19 November, and 30 January;
- 9:20–9:40 a.m. as the invocation interval;
- the fourth verse of Psalm 53.

**[Documented exact Latin verse]**

> “Ecce enim Deus adjuvat me: et Dominus susceptor est animae meae.”

Lenain says Reiiel protects against the impious, enemies of religion, and visible and invisible enemies. He rules religious sentiment, divine philosophy, and meditation. A person born under his influence is said to possess virtue and zeal for truth. The contrary genius encourages fanaticism, hypocrisy, irreligion, and dangerous maxims. See the [1823 facsimile](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1823__lenain___science_cabalistique.pdf), p. 64.

**[Tradition, not linguistics]** “God prompt to help” is Lenain’s attributed divine quality. It is not a transparent dictionary translation of the triad RYY.

### The psalm-number problem

Lenain’s notation produces several apparently conflicting citations:

- **Psalm 53**, in the Septuagint/Vulgate numbering;
- the **fourth poetic verse**, when the superscription is not counted;
- **Psalm 54:4** in common English Bible numbering;
- **Psalm 53:6** in older Vulgate versification that counts the two superscription lines;
- **Psalm 54:6** in the Nova Vulgata’s numbering.

These point to the same sentence. “Psalm 54:4” is the least confusing citation for an English-language reader; “Psalm 53, fourth verse” faithfully reports Lenain.

## 6. Papus, the Golden Dawn, Ambelain, and Haziel

### Papus

**[Documented reception]** Papus reprinted and popularized Lenain-derived Kabbalistic tables in *La Cabbale*, particularly in later nineteenth- and early twentieth-century editions. The [1903 edition](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/pdf/abg405b2732165.pdf) belongs to the transmission through which French occult correspondences circulated internationally.

### Golden Dawn

**[Esoteric correspondence]** The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn integrated the seventy-two names into a larger astrological, tarot, elemental, and angelic system. Two Shem angels can be placed within each ten-degree zodiacal decan, one per five-degree quinance.

For Reiyel:

- zodiacal longitude 140°–145°;
- 20°–25° Leo;
- first quinance of the third decan of Leo;
- traditionally the “day” half of that decan;
- containing card: Seven of Wands.

**[Caution]** Not every table now marketed as “Golden Dawn” occurs in the same form in the earliest Order manuscripts. Some are reconstructions from Mathers, Crowley, Lenain, and later publications.

### Robert Ambelain

**[Documented modern esoteric reception]** Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) further systematized seals, angelic offices, rites, planetary relationships, and protective uses. Its [digital edition](https://www.ordremartiniste.ca/docs-omc/Robert%20Ambelain/La%20Kabbale%20Pratique.pdf) is influential in modern French ceremonial magic.

**[Disputed]** Seals attributed by later authors to ancient Hebrew angelic tradition often cannot be traced earlier than modern French occult publications. An Ambelain or Haziel seal should not automatically be described as “the original seal from *Sefer Raziel*.”

### Haziel

**[Modern esoteric development]** The author publishing as Haziel popularized the seventy-two “guardian angels” through personality profiles, birth-date rulership, meditations, prayers, and practical petitions. This literature substantially shaped present-day websites about Reiyel. It represents modern devotional occultism, not the demonstrable content of ancient Exodus interpretation or Rudd’s manuscript. Haziel’s series is documented by [Éditions Bussière](https://www.editionsbussiere.com/product_info-products_id-41.html).

## 2. Crowley’s *777*: Asteroth and the Three of Pentacles

**[Documented esoteric correspondence]** Crowley’s *Liber 777* spells the name **Asteroth** and assigns it to the daytime half of the second decan of Capricorn, 10°–20° Capricorn. Its magical image compresses the Goetic description: a hurtful angel or infernal dragon-like figure with a viper and bad breath.

The decan has:

- Mars in Capricorn;
- the Three of Pentacles/Disks;
- the title “Lord of Material Works”;
- Astaroth as day demon;
- Andrealphus as night demon.

Crowley’s tables can be checked in the [PDF of *777*](https://www.astrumargenteum.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/777-by-Aleister-Crowley.pdf). The tarot title is found in the Golden Dawn’s [*Book T* manuscripts](https://benebellwen.com/wp-content/uploads/2013/02/mathers-and-felkin-golden-dawn-book-t-the-tarot-1888.pdf).

### Why each pip carries two Goetic spirits

The Golden Dawn/Crowley arrangement assigns one daytime and one nighttime spirit to each ten-degree decan. Thirty-six decans multiplied by two spirits gives seventy-two spirits. Therefore every minor-arcana pip from Two through Ten can carry a pair.

For the Three of Pentacles:

- **Day:** Astaroth, no. 29.
- **Night:** Andrealphus, no. 65.

This is different from the two Shem angels assigned to the two five-degree halves of a decan.

## 3. The Reiyel quinance and Seven of Wands

Sequentially allocating five degrees to each of the seventy-two angels from 0° Aries places angel twenty-nine at 20°–25° Leo.

That interval falls within:

- Leo III, 20°–30°;
- Mars in Leo;
- Seven of Wands;
- “Lord of Valour.”

**[Disputed modern synthesis]** Some modern charts assign Astaroth himself to Reiyel’s Leo quinance because the two share ordinal number twenty-nine. That is a legitimate modern Rudd-Lenain synthesis, but it is **not** Crowley’s 777 placement. Calling 20°–25° Leo “Astaroth’s 777 quinance” is inaccurate.

## 4. Enochian and other overlays

**[Negative historical finding]** The seventeenth-century Astaroth entry contains no Enochian call, aethyr, governor, tablet square, or angelic name from Dee and Kelley’s records.

**[Modern esoteric practice]** Golden Dawn and post-Golden Dawn magicians have combined Goetic evocation with:

- Lesser and Greater Pentagram or Hexagram rituals;
- Enochian elemental tablets;
- Enochian calls;
- planetary hours;
- tarot pathworking;
- the “Bornless” or “Headless” invocation;
- psychological interpretation;
- spirit-model, energy-model, and deity-model approaches.

These are later integrations. The Bornless invocation is ultimately Graeco-Egyptian, not Enochian.

**[Crowley’s interpretation]** In his 1904 prefatory essay, Crowley described the Goetic spirits as portions of the human brain. That is a psychological-esoteric interpretation, not a claim found in Weyer or the Lemegeton manuscripts. The essay is reproduced at [Hermetic Library](https://hermetic.com/crowley/goetia/the-initiated-interpretation-of-ceremonial-magic).

# Iconography

## 1. The manuscript seal

**[Documented manuscript image]** Weyer and Scot provide no individual seal for Astaroth. The seal belongs to the later Lemegeton manuscript tradition.

Its stable features are:

- a large enclosing circle;
- a central, vertically oriented angular figure;
- intersecting diagonal arms;
- approximately mirrored lateral hooks;
- small circles, loops, or rounded terminals at several ends;
- lower converging lines that give parts of the figure a star-like appearance.

**[Caution]** The manuscripts do not furnish a prose explanation such as “this line means Venus” or “the center is a pentagram.” Those interpretations are modern visual readings.

### Variants

- **Sloane 2731:** hand-drawn, with irregular line weight and slightly asymmetrical terminal loops.
- **Sloane 3825:** same essential topology, but differences in hook curvature, joining points, and enclosure.
- **Harley 6483:** used within a two-sided angel-demon lamen system.
- **Mathers-Crowley 1904:** regularized for print; the familiar modern version descends primarily from this engraved/redrawn plate.
- **Modern tattoo and altar art:** frequently simplifies the manuscript stroke order, closes open hooks, centers the glyph geometrically, or mistakes ornamental terminals for independent planetary symbols.

**[Finding]** No securely dated pre-seventeenth-century witness of this particular Astaroth seal has been established. The 1494 Florentine spirit catalogue contains some spirit signs, but they do not provide a demonstrated direct ancestor for the familiar Goetic Astaroth lamen.

## 2. Collin de Plancy

### The 1818 dictionary

**[Documented text]** Jacques Collin de Plancy’s first *Dictionnaire infernal* appeared in 1818. It had an Astaroth article but not Louis Le Breton’s now-famous portrait.

### The 1863 edition

The sixth edition of 1863 incorporated illustrations by Louis Le Breton, engraved principally by M. Jarrault.

**[Documented French, exact opening]**

> “Astaroth, grand-duc très-puissant aux enfers. Il a la figure d’un ange fort laid, et se montre chevauchant sur un dragon infernal”

De Plancy’s article repeats much of the Weyer tradition but adds or changes several points:

- Astaroth is a very powerful Grand Duke in hell.
- He is an ugly angel riding an infernal dragon.
- He carries the viper in his **left** hand, contrary to Weyer, Scot, and the *Goetia*.
- Some magicians place him in the West.
- He procures the friendship of great lords.
- Wednesday is recommended for his evocation.
- He is called the grand treasurer of hell.
- De Plancy links him to Sidonian and Philistine worship.
- He retains the past/future knowledge, secrets, discourse on the fall, liberal sciences, forty legions, stench, and silver ring.
- He is incorporated into stories of possession and Faustian demon processions.

The full article is at [French Wikisource](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.%2C_1863/Astaroth), and the full illustrated edition is at [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/download/dictionnaireinfe00coll_1/dictionnaireinfe00coll_1.pdf).

### Le Breton’s portrait

**[Documented modern iconography, 1863]** The portrait shows:

- a nude adult male humanoid;
- a crown marking ducal or princely status;
- large feathered angelic wings;
- long claw-like fingers and feet;
- a serpent held in the left hand;
- a crouching, furry or lupine hybrid mount;
- bat-like wings and a long serpentine tail on the mount.

The engraving can be viewed at [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Astaroth.jpg).

**[Interpretive finding]** Le Breton fused the textual angel, dragon-mount, viper, and ducal status into a single memorable figure. The naked crowned rider is not an ancient image of Astarte and is not copied from the Goetic seal. It is a nineteenth-century illustration.

**[Modern reception]** Le Breton’s rider has become more visually influential than any manuscript illustration. Contemporary demon encyclopedias, tarot decks, game bestiaries, tattoos, album art, and AI-generated images often reproduce it directly or blend it with Astarte/Ishtar imagery.

# People and works

## 1. Chronological source and practitioner history

### Apocryphal hagiographers, c. eighth–ninth-century witnesses

The *Passion of Bartholomew* supplies the earliest secure demon narrative. It establishes the idol/demon, apostolic defeat, sickness, sacrifice, and fiery black form, but not the Goetic office.

### Jacobus de Voragine, c. 1260s

The *Golden Legend* popularized the Bartholomew story throughout medieval Europe.

### Anonymous compiler of Munich Clm 849, fifteenth century

This manuscript demonstrates the name’s use in operative necromantic ritual before Weyer.

### Anonymous Florence compiler/copyist, 1494

The Florence office catalogue supplies the earliest known close parallel to the great Duke with forty legions.

### Johann Weyer, 1577

Weyer published the first printed, fully recognizable description: ugly angel, infernal dragon, right-hand viper, secrets, fall, liberal arts, forty legions, stench, and silver ring.

### Reginald Scot, 1584

Scot translated Weyer into English. His spelling and phrasing fed the English manuscript tradition that culminated in the *Goetia*.

### Christopher Marlowe and the *Doctor Faustus* B-text, 1616

**[Documented literary reception]** The longer 1616 B-text includes the spelling **Asteroth** among demons summoned to torment Benvolio and his companions.

**[Textual qualification]** The name is not in the shorter 1604 A-text. The B-text contains later theatrical additions and its authorship cannot simply be equated in every line with Marlowe alone. The text is searchable in [Project Gutenberg’s *Doctor Faustus*](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/811/811-h/811-h.htm).

### Sébastien Michaelis, 1612/13

**[Tradition/possession narrative]** Michaelis’ *Histoire admirable* places Astaroth among demons associated with the order of Thrones, temptation to idleness or sloth, and opposition by Saint Bartholomew. The information was presented as disclosed during possession and exorcism.

This is the major source for claims that:

- Astaroth had belonged to the Thrones;
- he tempts human beings to idleness;
- Saint Bartholomew is his special celestial opponent.

Those propositions are absent from the Weyer and Goetia entries.

### Loudun, 1632–34

**[Documented historical allegation]** Records of the Loudun exorcisms report a possessing demon identifying itself as Astaroth and accusing Urbain Grandier. The alleged pact used at Grandier’s trial includes Astaroth among infernal signatories.

**[Historical judgment]** The documents attest that the name was used in the proceedings. They do not independently prove possession or a genuine demonic pact. Modern historians commonly treat the pact as fabricated evidence within a politically charged prosecution. Michel de Certeau’s study preserves relevant exorcism minutes; an image description of the alleged pact notes that it was probably fraudulently produced by Grandier’s enemies.

### Anonymous Lemegeton compilers, later seventeenth century

They transferred the Weyer-Scot profile into a seventy-two-spirit Solomonic manual, added the seal and lamen requirement, reorganized the list, and surrounded it with ritual equipment and conjurations.

### Peter Smart and “Dr Rudd,” 1712–13

Smart copied Harley 6483. The manuscript integrated the Goetic spirits with seventy-two Shem angels. Thomas Rudd’s authorship or earlier ownership remains disputed.

### Francis Barrett, 1801

**[Documented later demonology]** Barrett’s *The Magus* reproduces the pseudo-Agrippan hierarchy in which Astaroth is prince of accusers and inquisitors. This comes through the Greek sense of *diabolos* as accuser or slanderer, not from Astaroth’s Weyer entry. A first-edition record and scan are held by the [Library of Congress](https://www.loc.gov/item/11015009/).

### Collin de Plancy, 1818/1863

De Plancy combined Weyer-derived material with Catholic demonology, possession stories, Faust legend, and new hierarchy. Le Breton supplied the enduring portrait.

### Lazare Républicain Lenain, 1823

Lenain systematized Reiiel’s divine attribute, zodiacal quinance, prayer time, calendar dates, psalm, offices, and contrary genius.

### Golden Dawn, late nineteenth century

The Order integrated decans, tarot pips, planetary rulers, Shem quinances, angelic hierarchies, and Goetic day/night rulers.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Aleister Crowley, 1904

Mathers prepared a translation/transcription from British manuscripts; Crowley published it with additional ritual and interpretive material. Their standardized names and seals became the dominant twentieth-century popular edition.

### Papus, 1903 and later editions

Papus transmitted Lenain-derived French Kabbalistic tables to a broad occult audience.

### Aleister Crowley, *Liber 777*

Crowley’s tables spell **Asteroth** and place him as the day demon of 10°–20° Capricorn, linked to the Three of Disks.

### Robert Ambelain, 1951

Ambelain expanded practical Kabbalistic angel magic and helped establish the modern French form of the seventy-two guardian-angel system.

### Poke Runyon

**[Modern operative reconstruction]** Runyon’s Ordo Templi Astarte explicitly explores the Baal/Astarte background of Bael and Astaroth. In his own operative arrangement he changes the positions of Astaroth and Stolas to align the system differently with zodiacal and Shem correspondences. This is a modern magical redesign, not a recovered manuscript reading. His system is described in [*The Book of Solomon’s Magick*](https://attfinnasanningen.se/dokument_offentligt/books/mystery_school/magic/poke%20runyon%20book%20of%20solomons%20magick.pdf).

### Lon Milo DuQuette and Christopher Hyatt

Their *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* (1992) popularized a psychological and practical approach. They discuss the tension between Astaroth’s masculine monstrous demon-image and the name’s relationship to Astarte. Practitioner anecdotes in that work are evidence for modern occult reception, not verification of the spirit’s powers. A scan is available [here](https://libraryofrickandria.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Aleister-Crowleys-Illustrated-Goetia-Aleister-Crowley-Christopher-S_-Hyatt-Lon-Milo-Duquette-Third-1992-2000-New-Falcon-Publications.pdf).

### Joseph H. Peterson, 2001

Peterson produced the first widely accessible modern edition to compare the surviving manuscript readings systematically. His work made “unbeautiful,” the manuscript foliation, Weyer parallels, and variant ritual instructions easier to distinguish from Mathers’ editorial English.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine, 2007

*The Goetia of Dr Rudd* published Harley 6483’s angel-demon system, including the Astaroth-Reiiel pairing. Its attribution and reconstruction have been influential but contested.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

**[Modern practitioner-scholarship]** Stratton-Kent has emphasized relationships among the spirit-office books, continental grimoires, *Grimorium Verum*, and earlier necromantic magic. His reconstruction of corrupt seals and spirit hierarchies is valuable for operative comparison but is not universally accepted as a critical stemma. His *The True Grimoire* is available [here](https://lib.wildla.in/book/Kent%2C%20Jake%20Stratton%20-%20The%20Trve%20Grimoire.pdf).

### Richard Kieckhefer, Jean-Patrice Boudet, Julien Véronèse, Jan Veenstra, and Owen Davies

These historians have supplied the context needed to move beyond claims that the *Goetia* appeared suddenly in the seventeenth century:

- Kieckhefer edited the Munich necromancer’s handbook.
- Boudet compared the French, Florentine, and Weyer spirit catalogues.
- Véronèse has studied medieval ritual magic and the *Livre des Esperitz* milieu.
- Veenstra has examined medieval magic, spirits, and courtly learned culture.
- Davies situates printed and manuscript grimoires within their social history.

Their work supports a **family of evolving catalogues**, not an unchanged Solomonic book descending intact from antiquity.

### Frater Achad

**[Negative finding]** No item-specific treatment of Astaroth by Frater Achad was located in the consulted primary bibliographies and searchable texts. Achad’s general Qabalistic writings should not be cited as evidence for an Astaroth doctrine without a precise passage.

# Related grimoires and parallel hierarchies

## 1. *The Book of Abramelin*

**[Documented text]** In the French/Mathers tradition, **Astarot** is one of eight sub-princes serving beneath the four superior chiefs Lucifer, Leviathan, Satan, and Belial.

**[Dating correction]** The book’s narrative claims a fifteenth-century setting, commonly 1458, but the principal surviving German manuscripts are from around 1608 and later. Consequently, Abramelin cannot securely replace the *Passion of Bartholomew* or Florence 1494 as the earliest physical evidence. Peterson’s [Abramelin edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/abramelin/abramelin.htm) permits the list to be checked.

## 2. *Grimorium Verum*

**[Documented parallel tradition]** *Grimorium Verum* places Astaroth with Lucifer and Beelzebuth as one of three superior powers. Astaroth has subordinate spirits including forms of Sargatanas and Nebiros. Some witnesses say he appears black and in human form.

**[Dating correction]** The book’s claimed Memphis 1517 imprint is fictitious. Its known textual and print history is substantially later, probably eighteenth or early nineteenth century. Peterson’s [bilingual edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/gv_bi.htm) supplies the textual comparison.

**[Finding]** This is a parallel continental hierarchy, not simply the *Goetia*’s Duke copied verbatim.

## 3. *Grand Grimoire*

**[Documented text]** The *Grand Grimoire* uses **Astarot** as a high infernal Duke beneath Lucifer and Beelzebuth. It says the superior spirits may send him in human form without noise or bad smell.

That last detail appears to answer or neutralize the older Goetic stench tradition. The full text is in Peterson’s [*Grand Grimoire* transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/grand.htm).

**[Correction]** Lucifuge Rofocale, not Astaroth, is the principal spirit of the book’s pact operation. Astarot belongs to its infernal administration.

**[Dating]** Claims of medieval or sixteenth-century publication are unsupported; its surviving printed history points to the nineteenth century.

## 4. *Testament of Solomon*

**[Documented text]** The *Testament of Solomon* does not contain the Goetic Duke Astaroth. It names an angel **Asteraôth** who frustrates one of the seven female spirits associated with the Pleiades.

**[Disputed conflation]** Modern demon dictionaries sometimes treat Asteraôth as an early Astaroth. The grammatical role is angelic and the narrative profile is unrelated, so identity is unproved. Peterson’s [*Testament of Solomon*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/testamen.htm) allows the passage to be checked.

## 5. The Solomonic ancestor claim

**[Tradition]** The *Goetia* frames its operations as the art by which Solomon bound spirits.

**[Historical finding]** The actual Astaroth catalogue entry is traceable through medieval and early modern European spirit-office literature. It is not found as such in the ancient or late-antique *Testament of Solomon*. “Solomonic” identifies the legendary authority and ritual genre, not demonstrated authorship by the biblical king.

**[Documented text]** *Paradise Lost* speaks of “Baalim and Ashtaroth” and then “Astoreth,” identified with Astarte, queen of heaven. Milton is using biblical and classical goddess lore. He does not describe the Goetic number 29, forty legions, dragon, viper, or seal. The poem is available at [Project Gutenberg](https://mirror.csclub.uwaterloo.ca/gutenberg/2/26/26-h/26-h.htm).

### Dante

**[Negative finding]** No Astaroth occurrence was located in the *Divine Comedy*. General claims that “Dante included Astaroth among the demons of the *Inferno*” appear unsupported. Dante’s named devils include figures such as Malacoda and Barbariccia, not Astaroth.

### Bartimaeus novels

**[Negative finding]** No Astaroth character was located in the searchable official descriptions, indexes, or standard character lists for Jonathan Stroud’s *Bartimaeus Sequence*, including *The Ring of Solomon*. The series’ Solomonic subject matter probably encourages mistaken association. Absence from those searches is not proof that the name can occur nowhere in every edition, but no attested passage was found.

### *Helluva Boss*

**[Negative finding as of September 2026]** Astaroth does not appear to be an established canonical character in released official material. Online identifications of Stella or another Goetia-family member with Astaroth are fan theories. They should not be reported as series canon without an episode, script, or official character statement.

### Persona

**[Qualification]** Astaroth belongs securely to the larger *Shin Megami Tensei* demon tradition. Claims about a specific *Persona* appearance should name the particular game and compendium rather than assuming that every SMT demon appears throughout all subsidiary series.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. “Astaroth first appears in Weyer”

**Verdict:** False as stated.

- **Earlier name:** biblical Ashtaroth/Astaroth and Latin Vulgate.
- **Earlier demon:** *Passion of Bartholomew*, manuscripts c. eighth–ninth century.
- **Earlier ritual use:** Munich Clm 849.
- **Earlier recognizable Duke profile:** Florence 1494.
- **First printed full profile:** Weyer 1577.

## 2. “Astaroth is simply the goddess Astarte”

**Verdict:** Etymological descent is strong; complete identity is a theological or occult interpretation.

The names are historically related. The Goetic male Duke, however, has a Christian demonological office, seal, rank, and ritual profile that cannot be projected unchanged back into Phoenician religion.

## 3. Gender

**[Documented history]** Astarte is grammatically and cultically female in the best-known ancient traditions. Weyer’s Astaroth is grammatically male and is a Duke.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Masculinization probably followed the Latin biblical form’s reinterpretation as a proper demon-name within a hierarchy whose titles were predominantly masculine.

**[Modern esoteric claim]** Some practitioners report Astaroth as female, androgynous, or able to assume either sex. That belongs to modern visionary testimony and theological reinterpretation, not the wording of Weyer or the *Goetia*.

## 4. Ashtoreth’s “vowels of shame”

**Verdict:** Influential but not uncontested. The polemical-vocalization theory has substantial historical currency; alternative linguistic explanations exist.

## 5. Is Astaroth a fallen Seraph or Throne?

- The *Goetia* says he discusses the fall and the reason for his own fall.
- It does **not** identify his former choir.
- Michaelis associates him with the **Thrones**.
- “Former Seraph” occurs in later demonologies and modern occult summaries.

Therefore “fallen angel” is consistent with the narrative, but a specific choir must be sourced to Michaelis or another later authority.

## 6. “He fell unwillingly”

Weyer and Scot explicitly transmit Astaroth’s claim that he did not fall of his own accord. The later *Goetia* weakens this into offering “the reason of his own fall.”

**[Legend]** This is reported speech attributed to the spirit within a demonological catalogue, not independently corroborated history.

## 7. Ugly, foul, unhealthy, unbeautiful, beautiful, or hurtful?

The source chain reads:

- Latin **angelica specie turpissima** — exceedingly ugly or foul angelic appearance.
- Scot: **fowle angell**.
- Sloane 2731: **unhealthfull Angel**.
- Sloane 3825: **unbeautifull angel**.
- Harley 6483: apparently **beautiful Angel**.
- Mathers: **hurtful Angel**.

“Foul/ugly” is closest to the Latin. “Beautiful” is probably a corruption; “hurtful” is Mathers’ interpretive rendering.

## 8. Which hand holds the viper?

- Weyer: right.
- Scot: right.
- Sloane manuscripts: right.
- Mathers-Crowley: right.
- De Plancy: left.
- Le Breton engraving: left.

Modern statements that “Weyer says left hand” usually inherit De Plancy rather than Weyer.

## 9. Is the ring silver?

- Weyer: explicitly silver.
- Scot: magical ring, metal omitted.
- Individual *Goetia* paragraph: magical ring.
- General *Goetia* equipment: gold or silver.
- De Plancy: restores silver.

Thus “silver ring” is securely Weyerian, but not literally present in every English witness.

## 10. Is Astaroth the treasurer of hell?

**[Later tradition]** De Plancy calls him the grand treasurer of hell. This is absent from Weyer, Scot, and the core *Goetia* paragraph.

## 11. Does he grant political friendships?

**[Later tradition]** De Plancy says he secures the friendship of great lords. The core entry does not.

## 12. Is Wednesday his day?

**[Later tradition]** De Plancy gives Wednesday. The core *Goetia* gives the general sunrise-to-noon timing for Dukes, not an individual weekday.

A Wednesday attribution is also awkward beside Duke = Venus, whose normal planetary weekday is Friday. That inconsistency demonstrates that the tables were assembled from more than one system.

## 13. Is he western or southern?

De Plancy reports western rulership “according to some magicians.” Later rank-direction tables may place Dukes in the South. No stable individual direction appears in Weyer’s entry.

## 14. Did Weyer invent the seal?

No. Weyer printed no Astaroth seal. The familiar seal comes from later Lemegeton manuscripts.

## 15. Is the seal an ancient symbol of Astarte?

No supporting evidence has been found. Its documented history is early modern Goetic, not Phoenician.

## 16. Is Asteraôth in the *Testament of Solomon* the same being?

Unproved and probably not. Asteraôth is an opposing angel in a different narrative role. Similar spelling alone is insufficient.

## 17. Why number 29 rather than 28?

Weyer gives 28; the reorganized *Goetia* gives 29. The difference results from the wider reordering and expansion of the catalogue, not a changed theological identity.

## 18. Did the *Goetia* always contain seventy-two spirits?

No. Its principal printed ancestor, Weyer, has sixty-nine. Seventy-two results from removing Pruflas and adding four spirits, accompanied by extensive reordering.

## 19. Is Reiiel inherently Astaroth’s opposite?

Only within the Rudd ordinal scheme. Earlier Jewish material does not pair RYY with Astaroth, and Lenain’s independent angelic table does not derive Reiiel’s nature by studying Astaroth.

## 20. What does Reiyel mean?

“God prompt to help” is the traditional Lenain attribution. The final **-el** clearly means “God,” but the lexical derivation of **RYY** is not securely demonstrated. Alternative modern meanings should be labeled devotional interpretations.

## 21. Which psalm verse is correct?

“Psalm 53:4,” “Psalm 54:4,” and “Psalm 53:6” can all refer to the same sentence under different numbering conventions. Lenain’s exact designation is the fourth verse of Psalm 53; common English citation is Psalm 54:4.

## 22. Is Astaroth assigned to Leo or Capricorn?

Both appear, but in different systems:

- **Reiyel’s quinance:** 20°–25° Leo.
- **Astaroth in Crowley’s 777:** 10°–20° Capricorn.
- **Modern Rudd-Lenain synthesis:** may transfer Reiyel’s Leo quinance to Astaroth.

Only the second is Astaroth’s actual 777 assignment.

## 23. Is Astaroth in Marlowe?

Yes, as **Asteroth**, but specifically in the 1616 B-text of *Doctor Faustus*, not the shorter 1604 A-text.

## 24. Is Astaroth in the *Grand Grimoire*?

Yes, as **Astarot**, a senior infernal Duke. But that hierarchy is later and distinct from the seventy-two-spirit *Goetia*.

## 25. Is Astaroth in Dante, *Bartimaeus*, or *Helluva Boss*?

- Dante: no attested occurrence found.
- *Bartimaeus*: no attested occurrence found.
- *Helluva Boss*: fan proposals exist, but no canonical Astaroth was verified.

# Open questions

1. **Transmission between the Bartholomew demon and the spirit-office Duke.**  
   The same name persists, but no continuous manuscript chain presently proves that Weyer’s description developed directly from the apostolic legend.

2. **The exact archetype behind Florence 1494 and Weyer.**  
   Their close agreement implies a shared source or textual family, but the complete parent manuscript has not been identified.

3. **Origins of the Astaroth seal.**  
   Its earliest secure witness remains in the seventeenth-century Lemegeton family. No convincing medieval or ancient prototype is known.

4. **The “beautiful angel” reading in Harley 6483.**  
   Whether this is simple scribal error, damaged copying, or intentional theological correction deserves direct palaeographical comparison with the manuscript and Smart’s other habits.

5. **Thomas Rudd’s authorship.**  
   The system’s connection with a “Dr Rudd” is real within the manuscript tradition, but the leap to a complete autograph work by Thomas Rudd remains disputed.

6. **Why the Goetic list was brought to exactly seventy-two.**  
   Deliberate imitation of the Shem is plausible, especially in light of Rudd’s later pairing, but explicit evidence from the original compiler is absent.

7. **Why Reiiel constrains Astaroth.**  
   Harley supplies the pairing and practice but no individual myth explaining their opposition.

8. **Origin of Crowley’s Goetic decan order.**  
   The published correspondence is clear, but its precise development through private Golden Dawn papers and editorial rearrangement requires further manuscript work.

9. **The gender transformation.**  
   Linguistic and theological mechanisms are reconstructable, but no single source announces that Astarte has been deliberately reclassified as a male Duke.

10. **Modern seal drift.**  
    Digital redrawing, tattoo templates, and generative art are creating new line forms that may eventually be mistaken for manuscript variants.

11. **Supernatural claims.**  
    The texts document what authors, copyists, exorcists, and practitioners asserted. Historical method cannot independently verify that Astaroth possesses forty legions, reveals the future, caused possession, or can be constrained by a ring or angelic lamen.

# Conclusions

Astaroth has at least three distinguishable historical lives.

First is the ancient name-complex of **ʿAshtart/Astarte/Ashtoreth/Ashtaroth**, referring to a West Semitic deity or her cultic manifestations. Christian biblical and hagiographical transmission turned that foreign divine name into the name of a demon. By the early medieval *Passion of Bartholomew*, Astaroth was already a temple demon defeated by an apostle.

Second is the **spirit-office Astaroth**. Fifteenth-century ritual manuscripts culminate in the Florence 1494 description and Weyer’s 1577 printed profile: a great Duke, hideous angel, infernal dragon-rider, right-hand viper-bearer, possessor of forty legions, revealer of secrets and the fall of spirits, teacher of liberal arts, and source of a dangerous stench warded by a silver magical ring. Scot translated this profile, and the seventeenth-century *Goetia* reorganized it as spirit twenty-nine, added a seal, and made the seal a necessary lamen.

Third is the **correspondential and artistic Astaroth**. Harley 6483 paired him with Reiiel through the Rudd system; Lenain gave Reiiel a Leo quinance and Psalm; the Golden Dawn and Crowley mapped Astaroth separately to Capricorn, Mars, and the Three of Pentacles; De Plancy and Le Breton fixed the crowned naked rider in visual culture; and modern occultists, novelists, filmmakers, comics creators, and game designers repeatedly reinvented the name.

The central corrections are therefore:

- Astaroth predates Weyer, but the complete Goetic profile does not occur in the earliest legend.
- Astarte is the strongest etymological background, but the Goetic Duke is not an unchanged ancient goddess.
- Weyer numbers him 28; the *Goetia* numbers him 29.
- All central early sources give forty legions.
- The original viper is in the right hand; De Plancy moved it to the left.
- Weyer’s ring is silver; later versions sometimes omit the metal.
- “Hurtful angel” is Mathers’ unstable rendering, not an exact translation of Weyer.
- Reiiel is genuinely the Rudd counterpart to Astaroth.
- Reiiel’s 20°–25° Leo quinance is not Astaroth’s 777 decan.
- Astaroth’s Crowley/Golden Dawn pip is the Three of Pentacles; the pip containing Reiiel’s quinance is the Seven of Wands.
