# Flauros: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of claims:

- **[Documented text]** — stated in a dated manuscript, printed primary source, or identifiable edition.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** — an inference made from textual transmission, codicology, philology, or comparison of sources.
- **[Esoteric correspondence]** — an occult attribution imposed upon the catalogue after its formation.
- **[Tradition]** — a claim transmitted within a magical or religious school, without necessarily being historically demonstrable.
- **[Disputed]** — competing readings or interpretations exist.
- **[Modern invention]** — a recent artistic, fictional, ritual, demonolatrous, or Internet-era development unsupported by the early sources.

Because the three principal descriptions are available in complete diplomatic transcriptions and facsimiles online, compact verbatim anchors are given below, followed by complete clause-by-clause accounts preserving every substantive assertion. The linked editions should be consulted for the original typography and full continuous paragraphs.

# The name and its origins

## 1. The earliest presently identifiable attestation: *Flavos*

**[Documented text — mid-fifteenth century]**

Flauros does not begin with Weyer. A recognizable precursor occurs as **Flavos** in the French *Livre des esperitz*, Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29, fols. 179r–182v, conventionally dated to the middle of the fifteenth century.

The entry begins:

> “Flavos eſt un grãt duc q̃ dõne vroye reſpõce…”

It says, in normalized English, that Flavos is a great duke, gives true answers to what is asked, destroys all the adversaries of the master who constrains him, and has twenty legions. The complete manuscript transcription and facsimile page are available at [French Wikisource](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3ALivre_des_esperitz_-_MS_O.8.29.djvu/6).

This entry already contains four elements later retained by Weyer:

1. The name beginning *Fla-/Flav-*.
2. The rank of duke.
3. Truthful answers.
4. Destruction of the constraining master’s adversaries.
5. Twenty legions.

The manuscript’s preliminary index appears to read **Flanos**, while the actual descriptive entry reads **Flavos**. That difference is an ordinary example of instability within a single handwritten witness and warns against treating any one spelling as an original, fixed proper name. [Complete *Livre des esperitz* transcription](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre_des_esperitz/Texte_entier).

**Finding:** claims that Flauros first appeared in 1577 are now obsolete unless carefully qualified as “first appeared in print.” The earliest presently identified textual ancestor is *Flavos*, approximately a century earlier.

## 2. From *Flavos* to *Flauros*

**[Scholarly reconstruction]**

The progression *Flavos → Flauros* is paleographically and phonetically modest:

- Latin and vernacular scribes readily alternate *u* and *v*.
- A copied *v* or minuscule sequence can be expanded or misread as *ur*.
- Names in spirit catalogues were often copied by writers who did not understand their linguistic origin.
- The offices and twenty-legion figure establish continuity independently of spelling.

Jean-Patrice Boudet’s study of Renaissance demonological “who’s who” catalogues identifies *Flavos/Flauros* as part of the textual ancestry leading from the *Livre des esperitz* to Weyer and later Goetic lists. Boudet cautiously raises a possible relation to **Floron**, another spirit name known in French magical literature; that is a comparison, not a demonstrated derivation. [Boudet, “Les who’s who démonologiques de la Renaissance et leurs ancêtres médiévaux”](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/pdf/1171).

### Did *Haures* arise by misreading *Flaures*?

**[Disputed; plausible paleographic reconstruction]**

A frequent explanation is that a tightly written initial **Fl-** was mistaken for **H-**, producing *Flaures → Haures*. The nearly identical offices and identical location in the catalogue make it overwhelmingly likely that *Flauros* and *Haures* are the same catalogue entity. But no surviving marginal note says that a particular scribe made this error, so the precise mechanism remains conjectural.

The Mathers–Crowley forms **Hauras** and **Havres** do not appear in Peterson’s principal manuscript columns. They may represent additional manuscript readings available to Mathers, editorial normalization, or speculative variants. Until Mathers’s exact working copy is identified, their provenance remains uncertain.

## 4. Etymology

### No established ancient deity

**[Finding]**

Unlike names such as Baal, Astaroth/Astarte, Amon/Amun, Belial, or Asmodai, **Flauros has no established derivation from a known ancient god, hero, place, or securely identified Greek, Latin, Hebrew, or Egyptian word**.

The name is first encountered as a proper name in a medieval French catalogue, not in an ancient inscription, classical mythographer, biblical text, or late-antique demonological list.

### Latin *flavus*

**[Disputed]**

The earliest form *Flavos* resembles Latin **flavus**, “yellow,” “golden,” or “fair-haired.” This is linguistically possible but evidentially weak:

- The manuscript gives no interpretation involving yellow, gold, sunlight, or blondness.
- The later leopard form could encourage a retrospective “yellow/spotted beast” association, but the leopard is absent from the earliest entry.
- No intermediate gloss identifies the word as Latin.

Thus *flavus* is a possible verbal resemblance, not an established etymology.

### Greek or Latin “fierce,” “flame,” or “burning” explanations

**[Modern folk etymology]**

Online explanations derive *Flauros* from Greek words allegedly meaning “fierce,” from Latin *flamma*, “flame,” or from invented or rare forms such as *flaurus*. These explanations generally begin with the spirit’s fiery eyes and destructive office and work backward. No early source supplies such a derivation.

### Horus

**[Modern invention/disputed occult identification]**

The claim that *Haures* is a corruption of **Horus**, or that Flauros is an infernal form of Horus, is not supported by the medieval, Weyerian, or manuscript record:

- *Flavos* is earlier than *Haures*.
- The consonantal and historical chain from Egyptian *Ḥr* through Greek *Hōros* to *Flavos/Flauros* is unexplained.
- No early source gives Flauros falcon imagery, solar kingship, Isis, Osiris, or Egyptian geography.

Paul Huson’s *Mastering Witchcraft* (1970) helped promote a ritual environment in which Goetic spirits were combined with explicitly pagan and Egyptian material. Huson acknowledges that he substituted preferred pagan terminology for portions of Judaeo-Christian grimoire practice and that his ritual system combined research with personal ingenuity. That makes the Horus association important in modern occult reception, but not evidence for the name’s origin. [Paul Huson on *Mastering Witchcraft*](https://www.paulhuson.com/), [searchable 1970 text](https://lib.wildla.in/book/Mastering%20Witchcraft.pdf).

### Sekhmet

**[Modern invention]**

Modern captions occasionally describe Louis Le Breton’s feline portrait as a “demonized Sekhmet.” No nineteenth-century source cited in this research states that Le Breton intended the Egyptian lioness goddess. The resemblance is generic feline iconography.

### Fleurety or Flereous

**[Disputed modern conflation]**

The **Grand Grimoire** names **Fleurety** as an infernal officer. Modern demonolatry sometimes equates Fleurety, Flereous, and Flauros. The case rests mainly on approximate sound and shared fiery associations.

Against identification:

- Fleurety belongs to a different hierarchy and textual complex.
- The name’s spelling history is separate.
- Fleurety’s descriptions do not reproduce the stable Flavos/Flauros offices.
- No early manuscript equates them.

The identification should therefore be labeled a modern syncretic tradition, not textual history.

# The description across the sources

## 1. *Livre des esperitz*, mid-fifteenth century

**[Documented text]**

The compact French entry says that Flavos:

- Is a great duke.
- Gives true answers to what is asked.
- Destroys the adversaries of the master who constrains him.
- Commands twenty legions.

It has no leopard, human second form, fiery eyes, cosmogony, fall of spirits, protection from temptation, triangle condition, timing rule, or seal. [MS O.8.29 transcription and facsimile](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Page%3ALivre_des_esperitz_-_MS_O.8.29.djvu/6).

The expanding description can consequently be observed rather than assumed:

```text
Flavos: duke + true answers + destroys adversaries + 20 legions
                         ↓
Weyer: adds leopard, human face, fiery eyes, temporal knowledge,
       theology, creation, fall, triangle warning, fire, protection
                         ↓
Goetia: reverses triangle rule, changes 20 to 36,
        adds a conjurer-commanded transformation and seal
```

## 2. Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*, 1577

### Bibliographical setting

**[Documented text]**

Johann Weyer printed the *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* as an appendix to the 1577 edition of *De praestigiis daemonum*. Weyer presented the catalogue critically, in a work opposing credulous demonology and the persecution of supposed witches; publication is not evidence that he recommended summoning its spirits.

Peterson’s transcription numbers Flauros **§62** and places the passage at approximately p. 930 of the 1577 printing; Collin de Plancy’s later citation points to Wierus around p. 929, probably owing to edition or pagination differences. [Weyer, Latin with Scot’s English parallel](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm#flauros).

### Latin

Verbatim anchor:

> “Flauros dux fortis: conspicitur forma leopardi & terribili. In humana specie vultum ostentat horrendum, & oculos flammeos.”

Complete content:

- Flauros is a strong duke.
- He is seen in a terrible leopard’s form.
- In human shape he displays a horrifying face and flaming eyes.
- He answers fully and truly concerning past, present, and future.
- If he is in the triangle, he lies in everything and deceives in other business.
- He willingly speaks about divinity, creation of the world, and the fall.
- He and other demons or spirits are constrained by divine power to burn and destroy the exorcist’s adversaries.
- If commanded by divine power, he does not permit the exorcist to be tempted.
- He has twenty legions under his authority.

Two details are sometimes blurred in modern summaries:

1. Weyer’s *lapsu*, “the fall,” is grammatically terse; the wider clause does not explicitly say “his and every other spirit’s fall” as fully as the later *Goetia*.
2. The burning of enemies is not represented as Flauros’s uncontrolled preference. He and the other spirits are compelled by divine power.

## 3. Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, 1584

### Context and page

**[Documented text]**

Scot incorporated an English version of Weyer’s catalogue in Book XV of *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, London: William Brome, 1584, around original p. 225. Like Weyer, Scot’s overall purpose was skeptical and anti-persecution: he printed conjuring material as exposure and criticism, not straightforward devotional endorsement. [Cornell first-edition record and scans](https://digital.library.cornell.edu/catalog/witchcraft081), [Internet Archive first-edition scan](https://archive.org/details/discouerieofwitc00scot/).

Verbatim anchor:

> “Flauros a strong duke, is seene in the forme of a terrible strong leopard, in humane shape…”

Complete content in Scot’s language and sequence:

- A strong duke.
- Seen as a terrible, strong leopard.
- In human shape he has a terrible countenance and fiery eyes.
- He answers truly and fully concerning things present, past, and to come.
- If he is in a triangle, he lies, deceives, and beguiles in business.
- He gladly talks of divinity, creation of the world, and the fall.
- By divine virtue he and the devils or spirits are constrained to burn and destroy the conjurer’s adversaries.
- If commanded, he does not allow the conjurer to be tempted.
- He has twenty legions.

Scot closely follows Weyer. The added English “strong” applied to the leopard may reflect his rendering of the general epithet rather than a distinct lost zoological detail. [Parallel transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm#flauros).

## 4. Sloane MS 3825 and Peterson’s edition

**[Documented text — seventeenth-century *Lemegeton* tradition]**

Sloane 3825, fol. 109v, reads **Flauros** and numbers him 64. Peterson uses this as his main copy-text while recording variants from Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, Harley 6483, and Mathers–Crowley.

Verbatim anchor:

> “The 64th spirit is named Flauros, he is a great duke, and appeareth at first like a mighty Terrable…”

Complete content:

- The sixty-fourth spirit is named Flauros.
- He is a great duke.
- He first appears as a mighty, terrible, strong leopard.
- At the exorcist’s command he assumes the shape of a man.
- The human form has fiery eyes and a terrible countenance.
- He gives true answers concerning all things past, present, and to come.
- **Unless** commanded into the triangle, he lies and deceives the exorcist in other matters or business.
- He gladly speaks of divinity, creation of the world, and his own and all other spirits’ fall.
- At the exorcist’s request, he destroys and burns the exorcist’s enemies.
- He will not suffer the exorcist to be tempted by any spirit “or otherwise.”
- He governs thirty-six legions.
- His seal is to be made and worn as a lamen.

This is printed on p. 35 of Peterson’s 2001 edition. [Peterson 2001, p. 35](https://jamesparsonslakebucket.s3-us-west-1.amazonaws.com/text/Peterson_Joseph_H_The_Lesser_Key_of_Solomon.pdf).

## 5. Mathers–Crowley, 1904

**[Documented text]**

Mathers’s English version, published and edited by Aleister Crowley as *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King* in 1904, puts the entry on printed p. 28 and gives the heading:

> “HAURES, or HAURAS, or HAVRES, or FLAUROS.”

Its substantive content follows the Sloane-type *Goetia*:

- Great Duke.
- Leopard, mighty, terrible, and strong.
- Human form demanded by the exorcist.
- Flaming, fiery eyes and a terrible face.
- True answers about present, past, and future.
- Lies if not commanded into the triangle.
- Creation, divinity, and the fall of himself and other spirits.
- Burning and destruction of the exorcist’s enemies.
- Protection from temptation.
- Thirty-six legions.
- Seal worn as a lamen.

The 1904 bibliography describes the first edition as x + 66 pages, printed by the Chiswick Press for Crowley’s Society for the Propagation of Religious Truth. [1904 bibliographical record](https://www.100thmonkeypress.com/biblio/acrowley/books/goetia_1904/goetia_1904.htm), [1904 facsimile/index](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/), [entry and seal reproduction](https://www.fromoldbooks.org/Mathers-Goetia/pages/064-Seal-of-Haures/).

## 7. The triangle contradiction

**[Documented textual contradiction]**

Weyer’s Latin is unambiguous in its ordinary sense:

> “Si fuerit in triangulo, mentitur…”

“If he is in the triangle, he lies.”

Scot preserves the same rule. The *Goetia* instead says “unless he be commanded into a Triangle,” and Mathers says “if he be not commanded into a Triangle.”

Possible explanations include:

- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A copyist inserted or lost a negation.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A *Lemegeton* compiler deliberately corrected the entry to conform to the book’s general ritual, in which the spirit is constrained in the Triangle of Art.
- **[Tradition]** Later practitioners usually follow the *Goetia* and regard the triangle as what compels truth.
- **[Unsupported harmonization]** Claims that both formulations mean the same thing lack grammatical support.

This is one of the most important item-specific textual faults in the catalogue.

## 8. Time and weather

**[Documented general *Goetia* instruction]**

The *Goetia*’s general “Observations” assign ranks different periods of manifestation. Dukes may be constrained from approximately sunrise or the first hour of the day until noon, in clear weather. This timing is attached to the rank, not repeated in Flauros’s entry.

**[Esoteric correspondence]**

Golden Dawn-derived practice adds:

- Venus as planetary ruler of dukes.
- Friday as Venus’s weekday.
- A planetary hour of Venus.
- Copper for the seal.
- Green as a common Venus color.

These are coherent within later ceremonial correspondence tables, but “Friday in the hour of Venus” is not part of the medieval *Flavos* entry or Weyer’s individual notice.

# The seal

## 1. Historical appearance

**[Documented text]**

No seal for Flauros appears in the *Livre des esperitz*, Weyer, or Scot. Individual spirit sigils enter this branch of the tradition with the *Lemegeton* manuscript complex.

Peterson observes that such seals are absent from the earlier Office-of-Spirits catalogues known to him, although analogous demon sigils occur in other magical manuscripts. [Peterson’s *Goetia* introduction](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

## 2. The familiar Goetic design

In Sloane 3825 and its printed descendants, the seal is an un-enclosed linear character whose principal features are:

- A central upright stroke.
- A small cross or crossbar at the top.
- Broad lateral strokes extending to either side, resembling wings, horns, or hooked arms.
- Angled and curved secondary strokes attached to the central structure.
- A branching or crossed termination below.

The design is graphically abstract. Interpretations as a leopard, falcon, flame, Egyptian eye, or planetary glyph are modern visual readings, not manuscript glosses.

The clearest readily accessible forms are Peterson’s [figure 64](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm#flauros) and the [1904 Mathers–Crowley reproduction](https://www.fromoldbooks.org/Mathers-Goetia/pages/064-Seal-of-Haures/).

## 3. Manuscript variants

**[Documented variation]**

The seal varies in proportions and stroke execution among witnesses:

- Sloane 3825 gives the form used by Peterson.
- Sloane 2731 preserves the same underlying character with scribal differences.
- Sloane 3648 associates its form with the spelling *Haures*.
- Mathers–Crowley regularizes and presents the character within the visual conventions of its printed series.
- Harley 6483’s Ruddian material places the demonic character into a larger protective scheme involving the corresponding Shem angel and biblical verse.

These should be understood as drawings copied by hand, not as typographic logos with a single exact geometry. A line shortened, a hook opened, or a curve flattened does not automatically constitute a different spirit.

## 4. Material

The entry says the character is to be worn as a **lamen**.

**[Esoteric correspondence]** The Mathers–Crowley rank rules assign dukes copper seals because the ducal rank is under Venus. This is the basis of modern “Flauros seal in copper” instructions; it is not stated in Weyer.

# The angel: Mehiel

## 1. Identification and pairing

| Field | Mehiel |
|---|---|
| Number | 64 of 72 |
| Hebrew | **מחיאל** |
| Core triad | **מחי** — mem, ḥet, yod |
| Theophoric completion | *-El*, “God,” producing *Meḥi’el/Mehiel* |
| Common transliterations | Mehiel, Mechiel, Méhiel; occasionally Mihiel |
| Ruddian demon | Flauros/Haures |
| Ruddian verse | Psalm 33:18 in modern Hebrew/Protestant numbering |
| Lenain numbering | Psalm 32:18 in the Vulgate numbering |
| Degrees | 316th–320th degrees of the sphere = 15°–20° Aquarius |
| Decan/card | 10°–20° Aquarius, Six of Swords, Mercury in Aquarius |
| Lenain gloss | “God who vivifies all things” |
| Lenain invocation time | 9:00–9:20 p.m. |
| Lenain offices | Protection in adversity and from rabies/rage and wild animals; favors scholars, teachers, orators, authors, printers, booksellers, and literature |

**[Documented Ruddian scheme]** Harley MS 6483 and the related material edited by Stephen Skinner and David Rankine pair the 64th Goetic spirit with the 64th Shem angel. Thus **Mehiel is the angel set over Haures/Flauros**. The pairing is ordinal: number 64 is set against number 64. [Skinner–Rankine source description](https://www.divinityatlas.com/sources/the-goetia-of-dr-rudd-harley-ms-6482-and-related-manuscripts), [WorldCat record](https://search.worldcat.org/title/637468388).

## 2. The seventy-two-letter construction

### Biblical substrate

**[Documented biblical text]**

Exodus 14:19–21 consists, in the traditional consonantal Hebrew text used for this operation, of three verses of seventy-two letters each.

### Kabbalistic operation

**[Tradition]**

The standard construction is:

1. Write Exodus 14:19 from right to left.
2. Write 14:20 beneath it in reverse order.
3. Write 14:21 beneath that in its normal order.
4. Read the resulting seventy-two vertical columns.
5. Each column yields a three-letter divine-name unit.
6. Christian Cabalists and angelological tables add endings such as **-el** or **-yah** to form pronounceable angel names.

For number 64, the core column is **מחי**. With *-El*, it becomes **מחיאל**, normally transliterated *Meḥi’el*, *Mechiel*, or *Mehiel*.

### Historical qualification

**[Scholarly reconstruction]**

*Shem ha-meforash* has not always meant “the seventy-two angels.” In earlier rabbinic usage it can denote the explicitly pronounced or distinguished divine name, often the Tetragrammaton. The specifically seventy-twofold construction became one important later meaning.

Medieval Jewish works, including traditions gathered in the *Sefer Raziel*, transmit seventy-twofold divine-name material. Christian Cabalists then systematized and angelized it. The complete modern package—angel number, moral office, zodiacal five degrees, daily calendar, psalm, demon opponent—is cumulative and not a single ancient Jewish system.

## 3. From Jewish Kabbalah to Christian Cabala

### *Sefer Raziel*

**[Tradition/documented medieval transmission]**

The medieval *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* is an important repository of divine names, angelic seals, astrological material, and the seventy-twofold name. It is not evidence that medieval Jews paired Mehiel with Flauros; Flauros belongs to a separate European spirit-catalogue lineage.

### Reuchlin and Agrippa

**[Documented Christian Cabala]**

Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517) and Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia* helped transmit the seventy-two names to Latin-reading Christian occultists. Agrippa explains the arrangement of the three Exodus verses and the addition of divine suffixes. [Agrippa, *Occult Philosophy*, Book III](https://www.esotericarchives.com/agrippa/op3.htm).

### Blaise de Vigenère

**[Documented transmission]**

Vigenère transmitted tables of the seventy-two angelic names that became especially important for the English Ruddian manuscript tradition. This is a more immediate textual route into Harley 6483 than Lenain, who lived a century later.

### Athanasius Kircher

**[Documented Christian Cabala, 1652–54]**

Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* reproduces and elaborates Christian-Cabalistic forms of the seventy-two names amid multilingual divine-name tables. Kircher did not originate the Flauros–Mehiel pairing; the pairing is associated with the Ruddian application of angel 64 against spirit 64. [Kircher Cabala extracts](https://www2.esotericarchives.com/kircher/cabala.htm).

## 4. Harley MS 6483 and “Dr Rudd”

**[Documented manuscript]**

Harley MS 6483, together with Harley 6482 and related papers, preserves a version of the *Goetia* in which the seventy-two Goetic characters are supplemented with:

- The corresponding Shem angels.
- Angelic characters or names.
- Biblical verses.
- Protective and coercive applications.

The angel is a **thwarting** or constraining power. Mehiel is invoked under divine authority to limit, bind, or counteract Flauros. This changes the ritual architecture: the operator is not relying solely on generic divine names but sets a specifically corresponding angel over each demon.

**[Disputed attribution]** “Dr Rudd” is traditionally identified as Thomas Rudd, the seventeenth-century mathematician and military engineer. Skinner and Rankine defend the connection, but the surviving manuscripts are compilations and the degree of Rudd’s personal authorship remains debated. “Ruddian manuscript tradition” is therefore safer than assuming every word is Thomas Rudd’s autograph.

Skinner and Rankine’s *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* first appeared through Golden Hoard Press in 2007. Their edition is based principally on Harley 6483, with material from Harley 6482, Sloane 3824, and Wellcome 3203. [Golden Hoard catalogue](https://www.sskinner.com/pdf/GHP-Catalogue-2022-2023.pdf), [publisher/retail description](https://www.llewellyn.com/product.php?ean=9780738723556).

## 5. Mehiel’s psalm

Lenain and the later tables give:

> “Ecce oculi Domini super metuentes eum…”

The verse in full sense is:

> Behold, the eye of the Lord is upon those who fear him and upon those who hope in his mercy.

It is **Psalm 32:18** in the Vulgate/older Catholic occult tables and **Psalm 33:18** in modern Hebrew and most Protestant numbering. These are the same verse, not two rival psalms.

## 6. Mehiel’s meaning

Lenain’s French is **“Dieu qui vivifie toutes choses”**: “God who vivifies all things.”

**[Esoteric gloss]** This is an occult theological attribution. The triad מחי does not transparently function as an ordinary biblical Hebrew personal name with that exact dictionary meaning. The interpretation depends on Cabalistic letter manipulation and the added *-El*.

Therefore:

- “Mehiel means God who vivifies all things” is accurate as a **Lenain/Haziel traditional gloss**.
- It should not be represented as uncontested historical Hebrew lexicography.

## 7. Lenain, 1823

**[Documented text]**

Lazare Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* gives angel 64:

- Name: Mehiel.
- Attribute: God who vivifies all things.
- A corresponding divine name, “Alli,” which Lenain attributes to the Mongol language.
- Zodiacal arc: degrees 316 through 320.
- Thirty-second decade/decan subdivision, associated with the genius Astiro.
- Five recurring days: 22 May, 2 August, 13 October, 24 December, and 6 March.
- Invocation: 9:00–9:20 p.m.
- Psalm 32:18, *Ecce oculi Domini…*
- Protection against adversity, rage/rabies, and fierce animals.
- Favor toward learned people, professors, orators, writers, printing, bookselling, and literature.
- The contrary genius governs false scholars, literary controversy, criticism, and disputes.

Lenain says Mehiel and the angels following him through number 72 belong to the ninth order, the **Choir of Angels**. This differs from later tables that group numbers 57–64 as **Archangels** under Michael. [1823 scan](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_ms05365), [searchable typeset edition](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1823__lenain___science_cabalistique.pdf).

## 8. Papus, Ambelain, and Haziel

### Papus

**[Documented later occult transmission]**

Gérard Encausse, “Papus,” republished and popularized French Cabalistic material, including Lenain’s tables. Papus is important as a transmitter, not the source of Mehiel’s zodiacal arc. [Papus, *La Cabbale*, 1903 edition](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/pdf/abg405b2732165.pdf).

### Robert Ambelain

**[Tradition, twentieth century]**

Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* repeats and ritualizes the seventy-two-genius system for modern ceremonial use, preserving Mehiel’s association with writing, scholarship, protection, and the relevant psalm. This material descends substantially from Lenain and French occultism rather than directly from the medieval *Sefer Raziel*. [Ambelain English translation/extract](https://www.markfoster.net/rn/texts/practicalkabbalah-part2.pdf).

### Haziel

**[Modern esoteric popularization]**

The writer known as Haziel turned the seventy-two names into a widely circulated “guardian angels” system, emphasizing birth dates, personality types, prayers, and life missions. In this reception Mehiel commonly rules approximately **3–7, 4–8, or 5–9 February**, depending on inclusive counting, ephemeris conventions, and popular table.

Haziel’s descriptions of literary inspiration, intellectual vivification, and guardianship expand Lenain’s nineteenth-century framework. They should not be retrojected into Rudd, Kircher, or medieval Jewish Kabbalah.

| Goetic rank | Planet | Metal |

| Prince/Prelate | Jupiter | Tin |
| Marquis | Moon | Silver |

This is the operative source of Flauros’s Venus/copper attribution. It does not mean that the Two of Disks is “a Venus card.” The card’s decan ruler is Jupiter; Venus belongs to Flauros’s **rank**.

## 3. Mehiel’s five degrees

Lenain’s **316th–320th degrees**, counted from Aries, correspond to:

- 315°–320° ecliptic longitude.
- 15°00′–19°59′ Aquarius.
- Conventionally written **15°–20° Aquarius**.

This is the second quinance of Aquarius’s middle decan:

| Zodiac sector | Attribution |
|---|---|
| 10°–20° Aquarius | Six of Swords |
| Planetary decan ruler | Mercury |
| First quinance, 10°–15° | Angel 63, Anauel/Anavel |
| Second quinance, 15°–20° | Angel 64, Mehiel |

Because each ten-degree decan contains two five-degree angels, each Golden Dawn pip card can carry two Shem angels. In this case the Six of Swords carries Anauel and Mehiel.

## 4. Crowley’s Flauros attribution

The table used in Crowley-derived *Goetia* literature divides the seventy-two demons into thirty-six diurnal and thirty-six nocturnal spirits. One day spirit and one night spirit occupy each ten-degree decan.

For Flauros:

| Field | Crowley/Golden Dawn-derived result |
|---|---|
| Decan | 1°–10° Capricorn |
| Approximate tropical dates | 22–30 December in the cited twentieth-century table |
| Day/night | **Night** |
| Tarot | **Two of Disks/Pentacles** |
| Decan ruler | **Jupiter in Capricorn** |
| Goetic rank ruler | **Venus** |
| Rank metal | **Copper** |

DuQuette, Hyatt, and David P. Wilson print this explicitly on pp. 202–203 of *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia*. [Scanned pages 202–203](https://libraryofrickandria.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Aleister-Crowleys-Illustrated-Goetia-Aleister-Crowley-Christopher-S_-Hyatt-Lon-Milo-Duquette-Third-1992-2000-New-Falcon-Publications.pdf).

The day spirit sharing this decan is number 28, Berith. Hence the Two of Disks carries two Goetic spirits in that system: Berith by day and Flauros by night.

## 5. Why Flauros and Mehiel have different zodiac positions

**[Finding]**

The Rudd pairing is based on shared ordinal number, while the Crowley demon-decan table reorganizes the demons into two groups of thirty-six. Lenain’s angelic zodiac assigns one consecutive five-degree sector to each angel.

Consequently:

- **Mehiel:** 15°–20° Aquarius; Six of Swords.
- **Flauros:** 1°–10° Capricorn by night; Two of Disks.

Modern charts that give Flauros the Six of Swords are merging the angel’s quinance with the demon’s Rudd pairing. That is a defensible modern symbolic synthesis if labeled as such, but it is not Crowley’s *777* attribution.

## 6. Enochian overlays

**[Modern esoteric synthesis]**

The early *Goetia* contains no Enochian calls. Crowley inserted Enochian material and interpretation into the 1904 publication’s prefatory apparatus, and twentieth-century magicians—especially in tarot-centered ceremonial systems—layered Goetic spirits, Shem angels, decans, tarot cards, and Enochian governors or sub-elements.

Lon Milo DuQuette’s *Tarot of Ceremonial Magick* and the DuQuette–Hyatt–Wilson *Illustrated Goetia* are important popular vehicles for this integrated model. Such overlays belong to modern Hermetic practice, not to Flavos’s medieval description or Weyer.

# Iconography

## 1. Before the nineteenth century

**[Documented absence]**

The early sources describe but do not portray Flauros:

- *Livre des esperitz*: no appearance.
- Weyer: leopard and terrible human face.
- Scot: same.
- *Goetia*: seal, but no figurative portrait.

The seal is an operative character, not a picture of the spirit.

## 2. Collin de Plancy, *Dictionnaire infernal*

### First edition, 1818

**[Documented text]**

Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* first appeared in 1818. Its early editions provided encyclopedic notices but not the famous Louis Le Breton demon gallery in its final form.

### Sixth edition, 1863, p. 278

The French entry begins:

> “Flauros, grand général aux enfers. Il se fait voir sous la figure d’un terrible léopard.”

It calls Flauros a great general in Hell and says:

- He appears as a terrible leopard.
- In human shape he has a frightful face and flaming eyes.
- He knows past, present, and future.
- He raises demons or spirits against his enemies, “the exorcists,” in de Plancy’s ambiguous punctuation.
- He commands twenty legions.

The entry cites Wierus, *De praestigiis daemonum*, p. 929. [De Plancy, 1863, p. 278](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.,_1863/Flauros).

### De Plancy’s interpretive shift

**[Documented ambiguity]**

The phrase *“ses ennemis les exorcistes”* can read as if exorcists are Flauros’s enemies. Weyer and Scot instead say that divine power makes the spirits destroy the **exorcist’s adversaries**. De Plancy has either compressed the syntax carelessly or altered the relationship.

### Louis Le Breton’s portrait

The 1863 page includes a portrait credited within the edition’s Louis Le Breton illustration program. It presents Flauros as a crouched, spotted feline or leopard-like creature, emphasizing the first manifestation rather than the requested human form. It does not clearly visualize the flaming eyes, theological discourse, or seal.

The image established the modern visual shorthand “Flauros = infernal leopard.” The 1863 edition contains sixty-nine celebrated demon illustrations associated with Le Breton, subsequently copied, redrawn, colorized, tattooed, and used in games and occult decks. [Complete 1863 facsimile](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e0/Jacques_Collin_de_Plancy_-_Dictionnaire_infernal.pdf).

## 3. Modern art

**[Modern invention]**

Major modern visual streams include:

- David P. Wilson’s illustration in *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia*.
- Tarot and oracle decks combining the leopard with Venusian green, copper, fire, or the Two of Disks.
- *Shin Megami Tensei* and *Persona* demon designs.
- *Fate/Grand Order*’s non-anthropomorphic Demon God Pillar.
- *Gundam Flauros*, whose model number deliberately reflects number 64.
- Demonolatry portraits presenting Flauros as male, female, feline-humanoid, Egyptian, or elemental fire.

No single modern depiction has manuscript authority.

# People and works

## Johann Weyer

**[Documented]** Weyer first printed the expanded Flauros description in 1577. His broader purpose was to expose supposed spirit catalogues and argue against witch persecution. He is a transmitter and critic, not securely an operator who “worked with Flauros.”

## Reginald Scot

**[Documented]** Scot translated or commissioned translation of Weyer’s notice in 1584 within a skeptical attack on witchcraft beliefs and fraudulent conjuring. His English wording became a major source for the English *Goetia*.

## The anonymous *Lemegeton* compiler or compilers

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Seventeenth-century English compilers transformed the Weyer/Scot catalogue by:

- Expanding it to seventy-two spirits.
- Rearranging the order.
- Adding seals.
- Adding or changing ritual instructions.
- Changing Flauros’s legions to thirty-six.
- Reversing the triangle condition.

## Thomas Rudd or the Ruddian compiler

**[Tradition/disputed authorship]** The Harley manuscripts associate “Rudd” with an angelically fortified Goetic system. Mehiel’s item-specific role originates here, not in Weyer.

## Mathers and Crowley

**[Documented]**

- S. L. MacGregor Mathers prepared the English manuscript translation or edition.
- Aleister Crowley published it in 1904, adding prefatory and interpretive material.
- Their edition made *Haures/Hauras/Havres/Flauros* the widely circulated fourfold name.
- Crowley’s correspondence tradition placed Flauros in the first Capricorn decan by night.

Claims that Mathers worked from a unique lost manuscript remain difficult to verify because his precise copy-text has not been recovered.

## Joseph H. Peterson

**[Documented modern scholarship]** Peterson’s 2001 *Lesser Key of Solomon* returned readers to manuscript readings, placed Flauros on p. 35, documented variants, and made Sloane 3825’s wording and foliation accessible. His continually updated Esoteric Archives edition also incorporates comparative evidence from the *Livre des esperitz*, Munich material, Florence manuscripts, and the *Book of Oberon*. [Weiser edition record](https://redwheelweiser.com/book/the-lesser-key-of-solomon-9781578632206/).

## Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

**[Documented modern edition]** Their 2007 *Goetia of Dr Rudd* published and contextualized Harley 6483’s double-seal/thwarting-angel system. It is the essential source for the historical Mehiel–Flauros pairing.

## Jake Stratton-Kent

**[Modern historical-practical reconstruction]** Stratton-Kent’s works, particularly *The True Grimoire*, *Geosophia*, and his writing on spirit catalogues, stress continuities between goetic magic, necromancy, classical daimones, and early modern grimoires. His work is influential in practical grimoire revival but does not establish an ancient deity behind Flauros. [*Geosophia* publisher page](https://scarletimprint.com/publications/p/geosophia).

## Paul Huson

**[Documented modern ritual synthesis]** *Mastering Witchcraft* gives a practical evocation of Flauros and combines Goetic material with paganized ritual substitutions, decan practice, incense, and modern witchcraft. Huson’s work is a major source of twentieth-century Flauros practice and likely helped spread Egyptian/Horus-oriented interpretations.

## Lon Milo DuQuette, Christopher Hyatt, and David P. Wilson

**[Documented modern occult reception]** *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* prints Flauros’s Capricorn-night, Two-of-Disks, Venus-copper correspondences and supplies modern illustrative treatment.

## Poke Runyon

**[Documented general relevance; item-specific limit]** Runyon has promoted Solomonic evocation through *The Book of Solomon’s Magick*, the Church of the Hermetic Sciences, and filmed ritual reconstruction. No securely documented, published Flauros-specific operation by Runyon was located in the sources consulted.

## Frater Achad

**[Negative finding]** No reliable Flauros-specific writing or working by Charles Stansfeld Jones/Frater Achad was located. His relevance is general Thelemic and Qabalistic history, not an attested Flauros dossier.

# Grimoire ancestors and parallel traditions

## *Livre des esperitz*

This is the earliest demonstrated item-specific ancestor: Flavos, duke, true answers, destroys adversaries, twenty legions.

## *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

Weyer identified his source family as a **Book of the Offices of Spirits**, also giving a title resembling *Liber dictus Empto*. Several related catalogues survive, including material now associated with the *Book of Oberon*. The precise exemplar Weyer used is lost.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The *Livre des esperitz*, Weyer’s catalogue, the English Offices-of-Spirits manuscripts, and the *Goetia* are related branches rather than a simple sequence of exact copies.

## Munich Handbook, Clm 849

The fifteenth-century Munich handbook contains named spirits later paralleled in the wider catalogue—such as forms related to Ose, Volac, and Cimeries—but no secure *Flavos/Flauros* entry has been identified. It demonstrates the larger ecosystem, not an item-specific Flauros ancestor.

## *Book of Abramelin*

The Abramelin spirit lists overlap with later European demonology at numerous points, but no secure Flauros/Haures or functionally exact Flavos has been demonstrated in the standard published name lists. [Peterson’s *Abramelin* materials](https://www.esotericarchives.com/abramelin/abramelin.htm).

## *Testament of Solomon*

The late-antique *Testament of Solomon* supplies the foundational legend of Solomon questioning and binding demons, including demons associated with illnesses, zodiacal forces, and thwarting angels. It does **not** name Flauros or present a recognizable leopard duke with this cluster of offices.

Thus:

- Solomon’s mastery of demons is an ancient literary ancestor of the genre.
- Flauros himself is not attested in the *Testament*.
- Rudd’s angel-against-demon system resembles the *Testament*’s logic of angelic thwarting, but direct item-by-item descent is unproved. [*Testament of Solomon* text](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/testamen.htm).

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Did Flauros exist before Weyer?

**Answer:** Yes, in a recognizable form.

**[Documented]** *Flavos* occurs in the mid-fifteenth-century *Livre des esperitz* with the same rank, truthful-answer office, destruction of adversaries, and twenty legions.

What remains uncertain is whether an even earlier Latin, French, Italian, or German exemplar lies behind the Cambridge manuscript.

## 2. Is Weyer’s Flauros number 61 or 62?

**[Disputed editorial numbering]**

Peterson’s current sequence numbers:

- Amy 61.
- Flauros 62.
- Balam 63.
- Alocer 64.

Some secondary lists call Flauros Weyer’s 61st spirit. Reasons include unnumbered early printing, different treatment of duplicated or unstable entries, and modern recounting errors. When citing Peterson’s enumerated transcription, **62** is the correct number.

## 3. How did Weyer’s 69 become the Goetia’s 72?

**[Documented comparison]**

The *Goetia* omits Weyer’s **Pruflas/Bufas** and adds four spirits absent from Weyer’s printed catalogue:

1. Vassago.
2. Seere.
3. Dantalion.
4. Andromalius.

Thus:

\[
69 - 1 + 4 = 72
\]

The resulting list was also rearranged. Peterson argues that seventy-two was likely desired partly because of the numerological importance of the seventy-twofold divine name. The final demon list can consequently function as a negative or infernal counterpart to the seventy-two angels. [Weyer–Goetia comparative material](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

## 4. Twenty or thirty-six legions?

Both are genuinely textual:

- Twenty: *Livre des esperitz*, Weyer, Scot, de Plancy.
- Thirty-six: Sloane 3825, Peterson, Mathers–Crowley, Ruddian *Goetia*.

There is no independent evidence with which to adjudicate a supernatural troop count. Textually, twenty is earlier; thirty-six is the standard *Goetia* reading.

## 5. Does the triangle compel truth or falsehood?

- Earlier Weyer/Scot: being **in** the triangle causes lying.
- Later *Goetia*: not being in the triangle causes lying.

The readings cannot honestly be presented as identical. The later rule is dominant in modern practice because it fits the *Goetia*’s ritual architecture.

## 6. Are Flauros and Haures two spirits?

**Answer:** Almost certainly not in the historical catalogue.

Their identical position, rank, manifestations, offices, and legion variants establish that they are manuscript spellings of one entry.

## 7. Is Fleurety the same spirit?

**Answer:** Not demonstrated.

This is a modern occult or demonolatrous conflation. It may be maintained as a working tradition, but it lacks a continuous manuscript equation.

## 8. Is Flauros Horus?

**Answer:** No historical evidence establishes it.

The earliest *Flavos* form makes a direct Horus derivation less plausible. The identification belongs to modern comparative occultism.

## 9. Is the seal Egyptian or a picture of a falcon?

**[Modern visual interpretation]**

No manuscript labels its components that way. Sigils are characters, not necessarily rebuses or portraits.

## 10. Is Flauros intrinsically a fire-element spirit?

**[Disputed later correspondence]**

The early evidence gives fiery eyes and an ability to burn enemies. It does not assign the classical element Fire, the southern quarter, a fire zodiac sign, or an elemental king.

Later systems infer fire from the destructive office. Yet the rank correspondence is Venus, the Crowley decan is an Earth sign, and Mehiel’s quinance is in an Air sign. “Fire spirit” is therefore a modern thematic classification.

## 11. Does Flauros teach theology truthfully?

The sources make the answer conditional:

- He gladly discusses divinity, creation, and the fall.
- He is also said to lie under one of the competing triangle conditions.

The texts do not provide the actual theological teaching, so no attributed doctrine can be checked against the catalogue.

## 12. Is de Plancy’s image an ancient portrait?

No. It is a nineteenth-century artistic interpretation based on Weyer’s leopard description.

## 13. Are the “enn,” offerings, colors, and personality traits ancient?

No. Formulae such as an “enn” for Flereous/Haures, as well as standardized candles, offerings, gender, sexual associations, and personal devotional profiles, come from modern demonolatry and individual practice. They may be religiously meaningful to practitioners but are not found in the medieval or early modern source chain.

# Open questions

1. **What manuscript stood between *Flavos* and Weyer?**  
   Weyer’s exact *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* exemplar has not survived or has not been identified.

2. **Is *Flavos* related to Floron?**  
   Boudet’s proposed comparison is suggestive, but shared etymology or identity remains unproved.

3. **How exactly did *Fl-* become *H-*?**  
   Paleographic misreading is plausible, but the decisive transitional manuscript has not been located.

4. **Where did Mathers obtain *Hauras* and *Havres*?**  
   The principal manuscripts summarized by Peterson do not account fully for both forms.

5. **Why did the legion count become thirty-six?**  
   It may be a copying error, deliberate harmonization, or substitution from another entry. No explanatory gloss survives.

6. **Why was the triangle rule reversed?**  
   The strongest reconstruction is adaptation to standard Goetic procedure, but simple negation error remains possible.

7. **Who drew the earliest Flauros seal?**  
   The seal is absent from the identifiable medieval ancestor and Weyer. Its exact source before the surviving *Lemegeton* manuscripts is unknown.

8. **How much of Harley 6483 is Thomas Rudd’s own work?**  
   The Rudd attribution remains a subject of manuscript and provenance debate.

9. **Did the Golden Dawn itself make every correspondence now advertised as “Golden Dawn”?**  
   Some modern tables blend authentic Golden Dawn decans with Rudd, Lenain, Crowley, DuQuette, and recent demonolatry.

10. **Can an ancient linguistic origin be recovered?**  
    At present, no ancient god, place, hero, or securely identified lexical source explains *Flavos/Flauros*.
