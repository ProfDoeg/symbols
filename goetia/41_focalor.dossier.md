# Focalor: Research Dossier

*Research completed 14 September 2026.*

## Evidentiary labels

- **[Documented text]** A reading actually present in the cited primary source or manuscript transcription.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** A conclusion proposed from textual comparison, manuscript history, philology, or historical context.
- **[Tradition]** A claim transmitted within magical, kabbalistic, demonological, or religious literature but not independently verifiable as history.
- **[Esoteric correspondence]** A symbolic or ritual attribution within a particular occult system.
- **[Disputed]** A reading or interpretation for which the sources disagree or the evidence is insufficient.
- **[Legend]** Narrative material presented by the source as supernatural history.
- **[Modern invention]** A post-grimoire elaboration, fictional adaptation, practitioner innovation, or unsupported contemporary attribution.

## The name and its origins

### Earliest secure attestation

**[Documented text]** The earliest presently secure appearance of the individual name is Johann Weyer's 1577 fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum*, where the *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* was printed as an appendix. Joseph Peterson’s transcription reads **Focalor**. The entry is on p. 926 of the 1577 printing and is no. 44 in Weyer's sequence. [Peterson’s Weyer edition and source introduction](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

**[Disputed textual detail]** Jean-Patrice Boudet’s comparative table prints the form **Focalur** for Weyer’s entry. Peterson’s transcription and name-collation instead give **Focalor**. This may represent a typographical feature of a particular Weyer impression, an editorial normalization, or a simple transcription difference. A census of the surviving 1577 copies would be required to determine whether *Focalur* ever stood in an original impression rather than only in modern scholarship. [Boudet, “Les who’s who démonologiques”](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/1019)

### The source Weyer said he used

**[Documented text]** Weyer identified his source as:

> *Liber officiorum spirituum, seu Liber dictus Empto. Salomonis, de principibus & regibus dæmoniorum.*

That is, approximately, “Book of the Offices of Spirits, or the book called *Empto of Solomon*, concerning the princes and kings of demons.”

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** A family of “Offices of Spirits” catalogues certainly predates the *Pseudomonarchia*. Johannes Trithemius listed works called *Liber officiorum* and *De officiis spirituum* in his *Antipalus maleficiorum* of 1508. Surviving relatives include the French *Livre des esperitz*, portions of Munich Clm 849, the Folger manuscript later published as *The Book of Oberon*, and other short catalogues. Weyer also admitted abridging his exemplar to make it less usable as a ritual manual. [Peterson’s manuscript survey](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

That does not prove that Focalor itself existed in the family before 1577.

### The *Livre des esperitz*

**[Documented absence]** Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29, fols. 179r–182v, contains the French *Livre des esperitz*, a catalogue of forty-seven spirits. It includes names ancestral to many entries in Weyer—Beal, Agarat, Amon, Furfur, Asmoday, Fenix, and others—but it contains no **Focalor**, **Focalur**, **Forcalor**, or **Furcalor**. [Transcription of the *Livre des esperitz*](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre_des_esperitz/Texte_entier)

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Boudet finds thirty-five spirits shared between the French catalogue and Weyer, alongside names peculiar to each text. The two therefore belong to the same broad textual family, but the surviving *Livre* is not simply Weyer's immediate source. In Boudet’s comparison, Focalor has no French ancestor. [Boudet PDF](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/pdf/1171)

### Munich Clm 849 and other medieval relatives

**[Documented absence]** The fifteenth-century Munich manual Clm 849 contains spirits corresponding to Barbatos, Gusion, Botis, Purson, Eligor, Gaap, Volac, Gemory, Cimeries, and Amy. It has no recognizable Focalor entry. [Digitized Clm 849](https://www.digitale-sammlungen.de/en/view/bsb00037155)

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Focalor therefore cannot currently be traced to the Munich manual, notwithstanding the demonstrable relationship between that manuscript and parts of Weyer's catalogue.

Other fifteenth-century catalogues surveyed by Peterson contain such names as Paymon, Astaroth, Forais/Fortas, Belial, and Amon, but no secure Focalor. Earlier sigils in these manuscripts do not match the later *Goetia* seals.

### No ancient deity behind the name

**[Documented finding]** No ancient Greek, Roman, Egyptian, Canaanite, Mesopotamian, Jewish, or other god, hero, or place named Focalor has been identified in a primary ancient source.

This is unlike:

- **Baal**, from Northwest Semitic *baʿal*, “lord”;
- **Astaroth**, ultimately associated with Astarte/ʿAštart;
- **Asmoday**, related to Avestan *Aēšma daēva* through Jewish Asmodeus/Ashmedai;
- **Belial**, Hebrew *bəliyyaʿal*, conventionally “worthlessness”;
- **Phenex**, the Greek and Latin phoenix.

Those recoverable etymologies should not be generalized to every Goetic name.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Focalor is most safely classified as a late-medieval or early-modern spirit-name of unknown formation, perhaps corrupted during manuscript transmission.

### Proposed etymologies

#### Latin *furca*, “fork”

**[Disputed]** Modern lists sometimes derive **Furcalor** from Latin *furca*, “fork” or “forked instrument.” This is possible only as wordplay on Mathers’s late variant. There is no early source explaining the name that way, no demonstrated semantic connection to Focalor’s maritime office, and no secure path from *furca* to *Focalor*.

#### Latin *focus* or *focalis*

**[Modern invention]** Web sources sometimes connect Focalor with Latin *focus*, “hearth,” or *focalis*, “of the hearth,” and then interpret the result as “focus,” “fire,” or concentration. The hypothesis is semantically at odds with the water-and-wind description and is unsupported by the manuscript chain.

#### “Foca,” seal

**[Modern wordplay]** Romance-language jokes connect the name with Spanish, Portuguese, or Italian *foca*, “seal.” This is a modern pun, not an etymology.

#### Lucifuge Rofocale

**[Disputed]** **ROFOCAL** is an exact anagram of **FOCALOR**. The full later name **Rofocale**, however, contains an additional *e*. Lucifuge Rofocale belongs to the later *Grand Grimoire* tradition and differs from Focalor in title, office, mythology, and ritual setting.

The resemblance has led modern encyclopedias and practitioners to propose that:

1. Rofocale was formed by reversing or rearranging Focalor;
2. Focalor and Rofocale are identical;
3. one is a title or subordinate form of the other.

No early text states any of these propositions. The anagram is real; identity is unproved. [A modern example of the debated identification](https://templodelucifer.blog/focalor-e-lucifuge-rofocale-origem-etimologia-e-significado-sob-uma-otica-luciferiana/)

## Number and order: Weyer’s 69 versus the Goetia’s 72

**[Documented text]** Weyer prints sixty-nine spirits, although his Belial entry refers internally to seventy-two. Peterson takes this as evidence that Weyer’s received catalogue had already been abbreviated or damaged.

**[Documented text]** The *Ars Goetia* contains seventy-two. It adds:

1. **Vassago**, at no. 3;
2. **Seere**, no. 70;
3. **Dantalion**, no. 71;
4. **Andromalius**, no. 72.

It omits Weyer’s fourth spirit, **Pruflas**, also called **Bufas**. Pruflas is already absent from Scot, which probably explains its absence from the English-derived *Goetia*. [Peterson’s comparison](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

The arithmetic is therefore:

> 69 Weyer spirits − 1 omitted Pruflas + 4 added spirits = 72.

The order of the shared spirits is extensively rearranged. Focalor moves from Weyer no. 44 to Goetia no. 41. Scot’s catalogue is unnumbered and omits Pruflas, so Focalor is effectively its forty-third surviving entry.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Peterson observes that the reordered material sometimes resembles a stack of spirit leaves or pages that was shuffled. A surviving Hockley manuscript actually places individual spirits on separate leaves and shows later dislocation. No definitive reconstruction of the reshuffling has been proved.

## The description across the sources

### Weyer, 1577: Latin in full

**[Documented text]** Johann Weyer, *De praestigiis daemonum*, fifth edition, Basel, 1577, *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*, no. 44, p. 926:

> **Focalor Dux magnus, prodit velut homo, habens alas gryphi forma. Accepta humana figura, interficit homines & in aquis submergit. Imperat mari & vento, navesque bellicas subvertit. Notandum omni exorcistæ, si huic mandetur, ne homines lædat, libenter obsequitur. Sperat se post mille annos reversurum ad septimum Thronum, sed fallitur. Triginta legionibus imperat.**

A close translation is:

> Focalor, a great Duke, comes forth like a man, having wings in the form of a griffin’s. Having assumed a human figure, he kills men and submerges them in the waters. He commands the sea and wind and overturns warships. Every exorcist should note that, if ordered not to harm men, he willingly obeys. He hopes to return after a thousand years to the seventh Throne, but is deceived. He commands thirty legions.

[Latin and English source text](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

#### Textual observations

- *Dux magnus* is “great duke,” not “king,” “prince,” or “general.”
- *Gryphi* means griffin/gryphon.
- *Submergit* means submerges or drowns.
- *Navesque bellicas subvertit* means “and overturns warships.” “Defeats ships” is looser.
- *Triginta* is unambiguously “thirty.”
- The second sentence’s *accepta humana figura* is redundant after “comes forth like a man.” It does not clearly describe a second manifestation.
- *Septimum Thronum* literally means “seventh Throne.” Whether that means the angelic order of Thrones, the seventh celestial throne, or heaven generally is not explained.

### Reginald Scot, 1584: English in full

**[Documented text]** Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, London: William Brome, 1584, Book XV, chapter 2:

> **Focalor is a great duke comming foorth as a man, with wings like a griphen, he killeth men, and drowneth them in the waters, and overturneth ships of warre, commanding and ruling both winds and seas. And let the conjuror note, that if he bid him hurt no man, he willinglie consenteth thereto: he hopeth after 1000. yeares to returne to the seventh throne, but he is deceived, he hath three legions.**

[Peterson’s parallel transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)  
[1584 edition catalogue record and page images](https://digital.library.cornell.edu/catalog/witchcraft081)  
[Brinsley Nicholson’s 1886 reprint](https://archive.org/details/b21720307)

**[Textual error]** Scot’s “three” is not an alternative Latin reading: it mistranslates Weyer's *triginta*, “thirty.” The error nevertheless entered the manuscript *Goetia* and became a genuine branch of the English textual tradition.

Peterson argues that Scot’s translator may have worked from a carelessly written manuscript rather than directly from one of Weyer's printed copies. He considers Abraham Fleming a probable translator, though the book itself does not name Fleming for this particular passage.

### Sloane 2731 and Sloane 3825

**[Documented text]** Peterson’s critical transcription, based primarily on Sloane 3825 and collated against Sloane 2731 and other witnesses, reads:

> **The 41 spirit in order is called Focalor he is a great duke & strong, & appeareth in ye forme of a Man with griffins wings; his office is, to kill men, and to drown them in ye waters, and to over throw ships of warre, for he hath power over both winds and seas, but he will not hurt any man or Thing, if he be commanded to ye contrary by ye Exorcist; he hath hopes to returne to ye 7th Throne after 1000 yeares; he governeth 3 Legions of spirits, his seal is this wch must be worne as a Lamin.**

[Peterson, *Lemegeton*, Focalor entry](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm)

Sloane 2731 places the passage on fol. 106r. Sloane 3825 supplies Peterson’s principal wording.

#### Differences from Weyer and Scot

| Detail | Weyer | Scot | Manuscript *Goetia* |
|---|---|---|---|
| Number | 44 | Unnumbered | 41 |
| Rank | Great duke | Great duke | Great duke and strong |
| Appearance | Like a man; griffin wings | Man; griffin-like wings | Man with griffin’s wings |
| Killing/drowning | Yes | Yes | Yes |
| Warships | “Overturns” | “Overturneth” | Usually “over throw”; NWU uniquely preserves “overturneth” |
| Wind and sea | Yes | Yes | Yes |

| Seal | None | None | Present and worn as a lamen |

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The verbal dependence is particularly close to Scot, including the erroneous “three.” This is strong evidence that the immediate English ancestor of the *Goetia* catalogue stood downstream from Scot rather than being an independent translation of Weyer.

### Harley 6483 and Rudd’s recension

**[Documented text]** Harley MS 6483, copied in 1712–13 and associated with Thomas Rudd’s magical material, reads **Forcalor**:

> **The one and fortieth Spirit is Forcalor he is a mighty Duke and Strong he appeareth in the form of a man with Griffin’s wings, his office is to kill men and to drown [them] in the waters & to kill men overthrow ships of war, for he hath power both over seas & winds but he will not hurt any man or thing if he be commanded to the contrary by the Exorcist, he hath hopes to return to the seventh Throne after 1000 years.**

The manuscript’s duplicated “kill men” is a copying defect. Skinner and Rankine print the entry on p. 142 of *The Goetia of Dr Rudd*. [Publisher’s bibliographic description](https://www.sskinner.com/reviews.htm)

**[Scholarly caution]** Harley 6483 is not Thomas Rudd’s autograph. Its relationship to Rudd’s own papers is substantial, but “Rudd’s Goetia” is an attribution to a recension and body of working material, not proof that Rudd personally copied every line.

### Mathers–Crowley, 1904: in full

**[Documented text]** S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Aleister Crowley, *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King*, London, 1904, p. 35:

> **(41.) FOCALOR.—The Forty-first Spirit is Focalor, or Forcalor, or Furcalor. He is a Mighty Duke and Strong. He appeareth in the Form of a Man with Gryphon’s Wings. His office is to slay Men, and to drown them in the Waters, and to overthrow Ships of War, for he hath Power over both Winds and Seas; but he will not hurt any man or thing if he be commanded to the contrary by the Exorcist. He also hath hopes to return to the Seventh Throne after 1,000 years. He governeth 30 Legions of Spirits, and his Seal is this, etc.**

[1904 text, p. 35](https://www.sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks05.htm)  
[1904 edition bibliography](https://www.100thmonkeypress.com/biblio/acrowley/books/goetia_1904/goetia_1904.htm)

Mathers:

- introduced the three-name formula **Focalor, Forcalor, Furcalor**;
- changed “kill” to “slay”;
- retained “overthrow”;
- restored **thirty** legions;
- did not restore Weyer's final *sed fallitur*, “but he is deceived.”

### Peterson, 2001

**[Documented edition]** Joseph H. Peterson’s *The Lesser Key of Solomon: Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis* was published by Weiser in 2001. Its critical approach retains the manuscript wording “3 Legions” while annotating Weyer’s correct Latin *triginta*. Peterson’s published errata explicitly direct readers to understand the reading as “<three> [thirty] legions.” [Peterson errata](https://ftp.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lkey_cor.htm)

The passage and seal occur around p. 26 of the print edition. Peterson’s online edition has continued to receive corrections and manuscript comparisons after 2001. [Publisher record](https://redwheelweiser.com/book/the-lesser-key-of-solomon-9781578632206/)

### What Focalor does—and does not do

**[Documented text]** Focalor’s complete historical office is unusually narrow:

- killing people;
- drowning them;
- overturning warships;
- exercising power over sea and wind;
- refraining from harm when expressly commanded.

The early texts do **not** say that Focalor:

- teaches languages, philosophy, rhetoric, logic, astronomy, or sciences;
- reveals treasures;
- supplies familiars;
- causes love;
- grants invisibility;
- transmutes metals;
- answers questions about past, present, or future;
- causes earthquakes;
- heals;
- governs emotion, grief, justice, or psychic development.

Those are offices of other spirits or later reinterpretations.

### The “Seventh Throne”

**[Documented text]** Weyer says Focalor hopes to return to the Seventh Throne after one thousand years and is mistaken. Scot repeats this. The *Goetia* preserves the hope but drops the explicit statement that he is deceived. Mathers follows the *Goetia*.

**[Tradition/inference]** Later lists often call Focalor “a former angel of the order of Thrones.” That is plausible as an interpretation of “return to the Seventh Throne,” but the entry never directly says “he was of the order of Thrones.” Other entries, such as Raum, explicitly do say that a spirit “was of the Order of Thrones”; Focalor’s does not.

**[Modern invention]** Stories that he supported Lucifer in a war in heaven, bore God’s chariot, belonged to a brotherhood of fallen Thrones, or wistfully remembers his celestial office are modern expansions.

**[Copying contamination]** Some sites give **1,200 years**. Focalor’s earliest text says **1,000**. Twelve hundred years belongs to neighboring traditions about spirits such as Marchosias and Phoenix.

## Rank, planet, metal, and hour

### Duke: Venus and copper

**[Esoteric correspondence]** In the *Goetia*’s ritual observations and in later Golden Dawn/Crowley tables, a Duke corresponds to:

- planet: **Venus**;
- metal: **copper**;
- conventional color: green, in later tables;
- rank-symbol: the ducal sign or seal.

The rank itself comes from the language of European aristocracy. It is not evidence for an ancient infernal government.

Crowley’s revised *777* gives the complete rank scheme as:

| Rank | Planet | Metal |
|---|---|---|
| King | Sun | Gold |
| Duke | Venus | Copper |
| Prince/Prelate | Jupiter | Tin |
| Marquis | Moon | Silver |
| President | Mercury | Mercury |
| Earl/Count | Mars | Iron |
| Knight | Saturn | Lead |

**[Textual complication]** The 1904 *Goetia* observations give Earls a mixed Venus/Luna attribution, whereas later *777* tables regularize Earls as Mars/iron. This discrepancy does not affect Focalor, whose Venus/copper assignment remains consistent.

### Hour of conjuration

Weyer’s rule is:

> *Duces à prima usque ad meridiem: & observatur cœlum clarum.*

Scot translates:

> “Dukes may be bound from the first houre till noone; and cleare wether is to be observed.”

[Weyer and Scot’s timing rules](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

**[Documented ritual prescription]** Focalor, as a Duke, is therefore assigned the period from the first hour until noon, with clear weather.

**[Interpretive caution]** *Prima* can refer to the canonical hour of Prime or to the first daylight/planetary hour. Mathers renders the practical sense as sunrise to noon. The text does not say that Focalor must be called only on Friday or only in a Venus planetary hour, although later planetary magic often adds those refinements.

**[Esoteric correspondence]** Crowley’s “by night” is a different attribution: it distinguishes the nighttime Goetic spirit attached to a zodiacal decan. It does not cancel the earlier ducal instruction to work between morning and noon. The systems are overlays from different compilations and cannot be reconciled without choosing which table takes precedence.

## The seal

### Absence from Weyer and Scot

**[Documented absence]** Neither the *Pseudomonarchia* nor Scot supplies individual spirit seals. The sigils are a feature of the later *Goetia* manuscript tradition.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Earlier magical catalogues do contain some demon sigils, but Peterson notes that the surviving earlier forms do not match the *Goetia* seals. Focalor’s seal therefore cannot presently be traced securely before the seventeenth-century *Lemegeton* witnesses.

### Form

**[Documented image]** In Peterson’s transcription the seal follows the words “worne as a Lamin.” The Mathers form is reproduced as “Figure 74” in later editions.

A verbal account can identify but cannot perfectly reproduce it:

- an enclosed, compact sigil rather than a pictorial image;
- a dominant angular vertical and diagonal framework;
- crossed strokes near the center;
- a wavy or serpentine line forming much of the left side;
- short terminal hooks or cross-like marks;
- in some printed redrawings, an enclosing single or double circular boundary and lettering around the ring.

The most reliable way to compare its exact geometry is through the facsimiles:

- [Peterson’s manuscript figure](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm)
- [Mathers–Crowley seal scan](https://www.fromoldbooks.org/Mathers-Goetia/pages/041-Seal-of-Focalor/)
- [Public-domain vector tracing of the 1904 form](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:41-focalor-sigilo-ars-goetia.svg)

### Variants

**[Documented textual tradition]** Sloane 2731, Sloane 3825, Sloane 3648, Harley 6483, Northwestern MS 65, and Mathers preserve recognizably the same basic sign but vary in:

- curvature versus angularity;
- length and direction of terminal strokes;
- whether crossings physically touch;
- proportion and orientation;
- the enclosing circle or lamen presentation;
- accompanying names or letters.

These are normal consequences of hand-copying a nonalphabetic glyph. A modern vector that regularizes all lines and symmetry is an editorial reconstruction, not an exact manuscript facsimile.

### Rudd’s angelic protection

**[Tradition and manuscript practice]** The Rudd recension integrates the corresponding Shem angel into the spirit’s ritual control. The Hebrew divine/angelic name is placed within the protective design or combined with the spirit’s seal so that the seal is not treated as the demon’s unrestricted signature.

Skinner and Rankine emphasize a “thwarting angel”: the angel corresponding by number is invoked as a superior authority capable of binding or constraining the Goetic spirit. [Golden Hoard description](https://www.sskinner.com/pdf/GHP-Catalogue-2022-2023.pdf)

**[Textual error]** The Rudd-associated material contains erroneous outer lettering in places, while the Hebrew within the seal preserves the controlling angel more accurately. This is one reason not to treat every letter in a modern redrawing as interchangeable.

## The angel: Hahahel

### Identification

| Field | Hahahel |
|---|---|
| Number | 41 |
| Hebrew triad | ההה |
| Expanded name | הההאל |
| Common transliteration | Hahahel, Hahah-El, Hah-ahel |
| Controlling spirit in Rudd | Focalor/Forcalor |
| Quinance | 20°–25° Libra |
| Psalm | Vulgate 119:2; modern/Hebrew 120:2 |
| Later choir | Virtues |
| Later archangel | Most commonly Raphael; some modern tables give Michael |
| Lenain gloss | *Deus Triunus*, “God in three persons” |
| Later offices | Religion, Christian mission, piety, strength of soul; protection from falsehood, calumny, impiety, and apostasy |

### Derivation from Exodus 14:19–21

**[Documented biblical fact]** In the Hebrew consonantal text used by the tradition, Exodus 14:19, 14:20, and 14:21 each contain seventy-two letters.

**[Kabbalistic procedure]** The verses are written in three rows:

1. verse 19 in normal Hebrew direction, right to left;
2. verse 20 beneath it in reverse direction, left to right;
3. verse 21 right to left.

Reading the resulting columns produces seventy-two three-letter groups. Rashi’s commentary on Sukkah 45a describes the principle as taking the first letter of the first verse, the last of the middle verse, and the first of the third, then proceeding correspondingly. [Laura Joffe’s discussion of Rashi and the 72-letter name](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/030908920202700205)

The forty-first column yields:

> **ה ה ה — He, He, He**

Christian kabbalists and magicians turn the triplet into a pronounceable angel-name by adding **אל, El**, “God”:

> **ההה + אל → הההאל — Hahahel**

**[Philological caution]** The Hebrew triplet ההה is a constructed divine-name unit, not an ordinary Hebrew word. “God in three persons” is therefore a theological interpretation of three repeated *he*s plus *El*, not a dictionary translation.

### Psalm verse

The Rudd and Lenain traditions attach:

> *Domine, libera animam meam a labiis iniquis et a lingua dolosa.*

A close English translation is:

> “O Lord, deliver my soul from lying lips and from a deceitful tongue.”

This is **Psalm 119:2** in the Vulgate’s numbering and **Psalm 120:2** in the Masoretic and most modern English numbering. [Latin psalm text and numbering](https://gregorien.info/chant/id/131/2/en)

Some manuscripts and modern tables have *labio mendacii* instead of *labiis iniquis*. Both express “lying” or “wicked lips”; the difference belongs to variant Latin psalter traditions and copying.

### Meaning and offices

**[Esoteric correspondence: Lenain/Papus]** Hahahel is glossed:

> **Dieu en trois personnes** — “God in three persons.”

The angel is said to rule or assist:

- Christianity and religious devotion;
- missionaries and those proclaiming scripture;
- greatness or nobility of soul;
- energy in divine service;
- resistance to impious people and calumniators.

Its contrary or “bad genius” is associated with apostasy, reneging on religion, scandal in the priesthood, and falsehood.

These are Christian esoteric readings, not Jewish biblical definitions of ההה.

### Historical development of the seventy-two angels

#### Rabbinic and medieval Jewish background

**[Documented history]** *Shem ha-meforash* originally meant the “explicit” or “distinctly pronounced” divine name, especially the Tetragrammaton. The expression did not originally mean “a list of seventy-two demons and their opposing angels.”

**[Kabbalistic tradition]** The seventy-twofold name derived from Exodus is explained in Rashi’s commentary and appears in works including the *Sefer ha-Bahir* and *Sefer Raziel*. Medieval legends say Moses used the name in the division of the sea and that it could protect, heal, exorcise, or perform wonders.

**[Scholarly caution]** *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* is a compilation, not a single work composed at one moment by the biblical angel Raziel. Its materials are medieval and heterogeneous; the first printed edition appeared at Amsterdam in 1701. It supplies important forms of the seventy-twofold divine name, but the full later Christian catalogue of angelic personalities, zodiacal degrees, psalm verses, and Goetic opponents is not simply copied intact from an ancient Jewish book.

#### Reuchlin, 1517

**[Documented text]** Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517) lists seventy-two angels or angelic names generated from the divine-name triplets, adding endings such as *-El* and *-Yah*. [Bernd Roling, *72 in His Name*](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv1zjg3h9)

**[Historical classification]** This is Christian Kabbalah: Jewish letter mysticism was being reinterpreted within a Christian theological and angelological framework.

#### Blaise de Vigenère

**[Documented transmission]** Blaise de Vigenère reproduced the seventy-two angelic names in sixteenth-century material derived from Reuchlin. Manuscript work connected with Vigenère later contributed to the Golden Dawn’s and Rudd recension’s angel tables.

#### Athanasius Kircher

**[Documented text]** Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–54) presents celebrated diagrams of the seventy-two names and connects them with angels, divine names, languages, and cosmological divisions. [Kircher material at Esoteric Archives](https://www.esotericarchives.com/juratus/kircher.htm)

**[Scholarly caution]** Kircher’s historical and linguistic reconstructions are products of seventeenth-century Christian antiquarianism. They should not be read as reliable accounts of ancient Egypt or as direct evidence for Israelite angelology.

#### Rudd and Harley 6483

**[Documented magical tradition]** Rudd’s decisive innovation was to pair the seventy-two angelic names with the seventy-two Goetic spirits by corresponding number. Thus:

> Angel 41 Hahahel → constrains spirit 41 Focalor.

The ordinary *Ars Goetia* does not itself name Hahahel. The pairing is an imposed controlling structure.

The angelic name serves several functions:

- divine authorization;
- a superior spiritual power over the demon;
- protection of the operator;
- containment of the spirit’s harmful office;
- a means of integrating the seventy-two demons into a Christian-angelic cosmology.

**[Tradition]** Skinner and Rankine regard the angel as the practical key that makes the Rudd system safer and more complete. That is their interpretation of the magical technology, not an empirically testable conclusion.

#### Lenain, 1823

**[Documented text]** Lazare Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* (Amiens, 1823) systematized the seventy-two “good genii,” their five-degree zodiacal domains, days, psalm verses, talismanic characters, virtues, and contrary influences. [Internet Archive scan](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_ms05365)

For Hahahel, Lenain’s scheme supplies the enduring cluster:

- angel no. 41;
- 20°–25° Libra;
- *Deus Triunus* / “God in three persons”;
- Psalm 119:2 Vulgate;
- protection from impiety and calumny;
- religion, missionaries, and spiritual dedication.

**[Calendar convention]** A straightforward calendar conversion produces approximately **14–18 October**, but modern lists sometimes give 10–14 or 13–17 October because they choose different dates for 0° Aries or apply calendar and endpoint conventions differently. The zodiacal degrees are more stable than the civil dates.

#### The Golden Dawn

**[Esoteric correspondence]** The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn absorbed the Shem angels into its Hermetic-Qabalistic system. It associated the seventy-two angels with:

- the zodiac divided into five-degree quinances;
- the thirty-six decans, two angels per decan;
- the thirty-six numbered Tarot cards, one angel for the first five degrees and another for the second five;
- angelic choirs, sephiroth, planetary dignities, and psalm verses.

[Golden Dawn Shem material](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/gd/publication/english/Schemhamphorash.html)

For Hahahel’s quinance, the enclosing decan is 20°–30° Libra, traditionally ruled by Jupiter and assigned to the **Four of Swords**, “Rest from Strife.”

#### Papus, 1903

**[Documented transmission]** Gérard Encausse, “Papus,” republished Lenain’s angel notices in *La Cabbale*, second edition, 1903, explicitly treating Lenain as an important practical expression of Judeo-Christian Kabbalah. His Hahahel notice repeats **“Dieu en trois personnes.”** [Papus, *La Cabbale*](https://www.cercle-papus.com/ouvrages/papus-la-cabbale.pdf)

Papus helped carry Lenain’s system into twentieth-century French occultism.

#### Robert Ambelain, 1951

**[Documented publication]** Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* appeared in 1951. It incorporated angelic seals, divine names, practical theurgy, and correspondences indebted to Lenain and related French occult material. [Bibliographic record](https://books.google.com/books/about/La_Kabbale_pratique.html?id=jF8ntAEACAAJ)

**[Esoteric correspondence]** Modern tables assigning Hahahel to the choir of Virtues, Raphael, solar Tiphereth, particular colors, stones, incense, and invocation procedures often draw on Ambelain or on systems downstream from him. These details should not be projected backward into Sefer Raziel or Rudd without manuscript evidence.

#### Haziel and late-twentieth-century angelology

**[Documented publication]** François Bernard-Termès, writing as **Haziel** (1927–2015), popularized individualized “guardian angel” readings, prayers, civil-calendar dates, twenty-minute daily periods, and practical petitions. His *Le grand livre des invocations et des exhortations: Prières adressées aux 72 anges servants ou génies de la cabale* appeared in 1990. [Bibliographic listing](https://www.books-by-isbn.com/authors/haziel/)

**[Modern esoteric development]** In this literature Hahahel becomes a guardian of people born around 14–18 October, with a daily period around 13:20–13:40 and themes of priesthood, mission, faith, persuasion, and religious vocation.

The twenty-minute period comes from dividing a day into seventy-two equal segments. It is not stated in Weyer, Scot, the *Goetia*, or the biblical verses.

#### Skinner and Rankine, 2007

**[Documented edition]** Stephen Skinner and David Rankine published *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* through Golden Hoard Press in May 2007, 448 pp., ISBN 978-0-9547639-2-3. A Llewellyn edition followed in 2010. [2007 edition details](https://www.sskinner.com/reviews.htm) [2010 edition](https://www.llewellyn.com/product.php?ean=9780738723556)

Their edition made the Rudd angel-demon pairings widely available and explicitly restored Hahahel as Focalor’s “thwarting” or constraining angel.

### Why each Tarot pip carries two Goetic spirits

Crowley’s arrangement distributes thirty-six spirits across the thirty-six decans by day and the other thirty-six across the same decans by night. Consequently each decan and its numbered Tarot card receives two spirits.

For 10°–20° Taurus:

- **Marbas**, spirit no. 5: day;
- **Focalor**, spirit no. 41: night;
- Tarot: **Six of Disks/Pentacles**;
- decan ruler: **Moon**;
- sign ruler: **Venus**.

The 1992/2000 *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* by Lon Milo DuQuette, Christopher Hyatt, and David P. Wilson prints for Focalor:

- 10°–20° Taurus;
- night;
- May 1–10;
- Six of Disks;
- Venus–copper.

[Relevant page of *Illustrated Goetia*](https://libraryofrickandria.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Aleister-Crowleys-Illustrated-Goetia-Aleister-Crowley-Christopher-S_-Hyatt-Lon-Milo-Duquette-Third-1992-2000-New-Falcon-Publications.pdf)

### The sequential Libra card

If spirit 41 is instead attached to angel 41’s sequential quinance:

- Hahahel/Focalor: 20°–25° Libra;
- angel 42 Michael/Vepar: 25°–30° Libra;
- the full decan: 20°–30° Libra;
- Tarot: **Four of Swords**;
- planetary decan ruler: **Jupiter**.

This internally coherent result belongs to the Rudd/Lenain-based synthesis. It is not Focalor’s placement in *777*.

### Element

**[Documented text]** Weyer gives power over sea and wind but does not assign an occult element.

**[Modern correspondence]** Water is the obvious modern attribution; some ceremonial tables assign Earth to Dukes as a rank class or derive an element from cardinal kings. “Water” and “Earth” therefore arise through different schemes. Neither should be presented as an explicit statement by Weyer.

### Direction and ruling king

**[Tradition]** Later systems place individual spirits beneath Oriens, Amaymon, Paymon, or Egyn according to decan, rank, or manuscript grouping. Modern tables accordingly disagree over whether Focalor is northern, eastern, or subordinate to Zimimar/Egyn.

No direction is given in Focalor’s individual entry. Any directional attribution must name the later table that produces it.

### Enochian overlays

**[Modern occult synthesis]** Crowley’s 1904 volume added material not native to the seventeenth-century *Goetia*, including revised preliminary invocations and Enochian-language elements. Later authors—especially Poke Runyon and Lon Milo DuQuette—combined Goetia with planetary, zodiacal, Tarot, crystal, or Enochian structures.

Rudd himself also attempted syntheses involving John Dee’s angel magic. This does not make Focalor an “Enochian spirit”: the name appears nowhere in Dee and Kelley’s received Enochian tables.

### Astrological timing in practice

Historically distinct options include:

1. **Rank rule:** call a Duke from the first daylight hour to noon in clear weather.
2. **Planetary rule:** prefer a Venus day or Venus hour and a copper lamen.
3. ***777* decan rule:** work during 10°–20° Taurus and treat Focalor as the nighttime spirit.
4. **Rudd/Lenain rule:** work during 20°–25° Libra or the corresponding calendar period.
5. **Haziel rule:** use the angel’s approximately twenty-minute daily interval.

These are cumulative only in modern synthesis. No historical source demonstrates that all conditions were intended to be satisfied simultaneously.

### Collin de Plancy

#### First edition, 1818

**[Documented absence]** The searchable full text of the 1818 first edition’s first volume does not contain a discrete Focalor article. Because letter F falls in that volume, the current evidence indicates that Focalor was not yet individually entered in the first edition. [1818 first-edition text](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/1re_%C3%A9dition,_1818/Tome_1/Texte_entier)

#### Later editions and 1863

**[Documented text]** By the second-edition period Focalor appears as “grand general aux enfers.” The sixth edition, Paris: Henri Plon, 1863, p. 279, reads in full:

> **Focalor, général aux enfers. Il se montre sous les traits d’un homme ayant des ailes de griffon. Sous cette forme il tue les bourgeois et les jette dans les flots. Il commande à la mer, aux vents, et renverse les vaisseaux de guerre. Il espère rentrer au ciel dans mille ans ; mais il se trompe. Il commande à trente légions, et obéit en rechignant à l’exorciste.**

[1863 Focalor article](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.,_1863/Focalor)

Translation:

> Focalor, a general in hell. He appears with the features of a man having griffin’s wings. In this form he kills townsmen and casts them into the waves. He commands the sea and winds and overturns warships. He hopes to re-enter heaven in a thousand years, but is mistaken. He commands thirty legions and obeys the exorcist grudgingly.

De Plancy cites “Wierus, *De praestigiis dæmonum*, p. 926.”

#### De Plancy’s innovations

- **“General in hell”** replaces Weyer’s precise “great duke.”
- **Bourgeois** literally means townsmen, citizens, or members of the bourgeoisie. Weyer has simply *homines*, “people/men.” This is de Plancy’s curious narrowing.
- Weyer says Focalor willingly obeys a prohibition against harm; de Plancy says he obeys **grudgingly**, *en rechignant*. The reluctance is not in Weyer.
- De Plancy correctly restores thirty legions and “he is deceived.”

### Was there a Louis Le Breton portrait?

**[Documented absence]** The 1863 Focalor page contains the paragraph but no dedicated Louis Le Breton portrait. Although that edition has hundreds of engravings and is famous for its images of demons, not every named spirit received one. Focalor has a de Plancy article but no securely identified Le Breton likeness.

Websites displaying a Breton engraving beside Focalor sometimes reuse a generic or another demon’s portrait. The seal is likewise from the *Goetia*, not from de Plancy.

### Modern art

#### David P. Wilson

**[Documented modern illustration]** David P. Wilson supplied individualized artwork for *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia*, initially published by New Falcon in 1992 and issued in later editions. ## People and works

### Johann Weyer

**[Documented history]** Johann Weyer (1515–1588), physician and former student of Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa, printed Focalor in 1577. His *De praestigiis daemonum* challenged the prosecution of supposed witches and treated many confessions as delusion, illness, or deception.

**[Historical nuance]** Weyer did not simply reject the existence of spirits. He opposed witch-hunting while retaining substantial elements of Christian demonology. His publication of the catalogue was framed as exposure and critique, and he deliberately omitted operational material.

### Reginald Scot

Scot’s *Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584) attacked witchcraft accusations, conjuring claims, superstition, and fraudulent marvels. By translating the demonic catalogue, Scot inadvertently became one of the most influential transmitters of Goetic material. The *Goetia*’s “three legions” and much of its wording show this dependence.

### Thomas Rudd and the Harley manuscripts

Thomas Rudd (1583?–1656) is associated with a Christian ceremonial system integrating:

- the *Lemegeton*;
- Shem angels;
- Heptameron conjurations;
- protective circles;
- Dee-related angel magic;
- explicitly controlling angelic names.

Harley 6483 was copied decades after Rudd’s death, so attribution requires caution. Its importance lies in the structured angel-demon pairing, including Hahahel–Forcalor.

### Mathers and Crowley

Mathers prepared a manuscript-based English edition in the late nineteenth century. Crowley published it in 1904, supplied an introduction and additional material, and encouraged a psychological reading in which evoked spirits could be regarded as portions of the human mind.

**[Documented edition history]** The 1904 title page names the work *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King, Translated into the English Tongue by a Dead Hand and Adorned with Divers Other Matters Germane Delightful to the Wise*. Approximately 200 paper copies were printed, with a small number on vellum. [Edition history](https://www.100thmonkeypress.com/biblio/acrowley/books/goetia_1904/goetia_1904.htm)

### Frater Achad

**[Documented absence]** Charles Stansfeld Jones, Frater Achad, was a major Crowley associate and wrote on Qabalah. No reliable source located for this dossier documents a distinctive Focalor working, doctrine, or textual contribution by Achad. Merely including him in general Thelemic bibliographies does not establish such a connection.

### Poke Runyon

Carroll “Poke” Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* reorganizes Goetic practice around a “Master Mandala,” crystal scrying, planetary and zodiacal structures, and a less coercive attitude to selected spirits. Runyon states that his original edition removed or softened some threats because his organization regarded Baal and Astarte as patron deities. [Runyon’s description of his editorial practice](https://www.pokerunyon.com/blog)

Focalor is listed in Runyon’s catalogue, but no source located establishes a uniquely important Focalor operation by Runyon personally.

### Lon Milo DuQuette and Christopher Hyatt

Their *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* supplies the widely reproduced modern table:

> Focalor — Duke — 10°–20° Taurus by night — May 1–10 — Six of Disks — Venus/copper.

DuQuette’s 1995 *Tarot of Ceremonial Magick* further integrates Goetia, Enochian magic, astrology, and Tarot. These are influential modern syntheses, not seventeenth-century attributions.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their 2007 *Goetia of Dr Rudd* is the principal modern edition for the Rudd recension. Its importance for Focalor lies in:

- the reading **Forcalor**;
- the manuscript variants;
- the Hebrew rendering;
- the controlling angel Hahahel;
- the psalm verse;
- the protective and constraining function of the angelic seal.

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 edition and continuously updated Esoteric Archives transcription provide the clearest publicly accessible collation of Sloane 3825, Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, Harley 6483, and Northwestern MS 65. His comparison establishes that:

- Focalor derives verbally through Scot from Weyer;
- “three” is erroneous;
- “overturneth” survives correctly only in the Northwestern witness among the collated *Goetia* manuscripts;
- Focalor is Weyer no. 44 and Goetia no. 41;
- the seals are a later layer.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s *An Inventorie of Demons* republishes and contextualizes Scot’s spirit catalogue, emphasizing the broader spirit-office tradition and its relationship to practical grimoire magic. His work is important to the modern recovery of the pre-Mathers textual stream. It does not supply an ancient origin for Focalor.

### Owen Davies

Owen Davies’s *Grimoires: A History of Magic Books* places the *Lemegeton*, printed occultism, manuscript circulation, and modern grimoire reception in social history. It is contextual scholarship rather than a Focalor monograph.

### Jean-Patrice Boudet

Boudet’s comparison of the *Livre des esperitz*, Weyer, Munich Clm 849, and related catalogues is the most important evidence for assessing Focalor’s possible ancestry. His table’s lack of a pre-Weyer parallel is a substantive negative finding.

### Jan Veenstra and Julien Véronèse

Their scholarship on medieval ritual magic, Solomonic literature, and necromantic manuscripts helps define the larger genre in which the spirit catalogues developed. Neither provides a verified medieval Focalor. They should therefore be cited for context, not as witnesses to the name.

## Grimoire ancestors and analogues

### *Liber officiorum spirituum*

Probable immediate textual family; possibly contained Focalor in a now-lost form, but no surviving pre-1577 witness presently proves it.

### *Livre des esperitz*

Closely related catalogue; no Focalor.

### Munich handbook, Clm 849

Contains several Weyer cognates; no Focalor.

### *Book of Oberon* / Folger V.b.26 or V.b.23 tradition

Contains expanded Offices-of-Spirits material and preserves passages Weyer censored. No presently demonstrated Focalor parallel has been identified.

### *Book of Abramelin*

**[Documented absence]** The major Abramelin name lists do not supply a securely identifiable Focalor. Similar short strings are insufficient evidence of identity unless office, rank, or textual placement also corresponds.

### *Testament of Solomon*

**[Documented absence]** Focalor does not occur in the *Testament of Solomon*. That late-antique work supplies the larger literary theme of Solomon interrogating and binding demons, but not this name or description.

Boudet observes that only a very small number of names—most notably Beelzebul and Asmodeus—bridge the *Testament* and the later Offices-of-Spirits catalogues. Focalor is not one of them.

**[Documented absence]**

## Modern occult reception

### Demonolatry

**[Modern tradition]** Contemporary demonolatry literature treats Focalor as an independently invocable being connected with:

- water and storms;
- grief and emotional release;
- controlled destruction;
- protection;
- banishing;
- sailors, drowned persons, or the dead at sea.

These extend far beyond the early office.

The chant or “enn”:

> **En Jedan on ca Focalor**

is circulated through S. Connolly’s modern demonolatry books and online lists. It is not present in Weyer, Scot, the *Lemegeton*, Rudd, de Plancy, Lenain, Mathers, or Crowley. [Modern source summary](https://grimorium.lat/en/d/focalor/)

### Modern offerings and correspondences

Associations with sandalwood, frankincense, myrrh, sea salt, shells, blue candles, particular stones, alcohol, or devotional altars are modern practitioner systems or personal gnosis. They are not historical Goetic data.

### Experiential claims

Practitioner reports describing Focalor’s temperament, gender, preferred spelling, appearance, voice, or displeasure at being called “Focalor” are personal religious or magical testimony. They document contemporary belief but cannot settle manuscript philology.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Thirty legions or three?

- **Weyer:** *triginta*, thirty.
- **Scot:** three.
- **Most manuscript Goetia witnesses:** three, inherited from Scot.
- **Mathers:** thirty.
- **Peterson:** retains the manuscript reading while correcting it critically.
- **Conclusion:** Thirty is the earliest and philologically strongest reading; three is an influential translation error.

### 2. Is it Focalor, Forcalor, or Furcalor?

- **Focalor** is the dominant Weyer, Scot, Sloane, Northwestern, Peterson, and later reading.
- **Forcalor** is documented in Harley 6483.
- **Furcalor** is popularized by Mathers’s collected variants.
- All refer to the same textual entity.
- Claims that the “real” spirit becomes angry over one spelling belong to modern testimony, not source criticism.

### 3. Focalor or Focalur in Weyer?

Peterson reads Focalor; Boudet prints Focalur. Until individual 1577 copies and typeforms are compared, the discrepancy should remain visible rather than silently normalized.

### 4. Focator

Wellcome MS 3203 reads **Focator**. Its spirit leaves were displaced and renumbered; the form is best explained as a copying or paleographic error. There is no separate Focator tradition with a distinct office.

### 5. Is “Furcalor” derived from *furca*?

Possible as late wordplay, but unsupported as historical etymology. The earlier dominant form is Focalor, not Furcalor.

### 6. Is Focalor Lucifuge Rofocale?

The names nearly anagram, but:

- Rofocale is later;
- the full names are not exact anagrams;
- ranks and offices differ;
- no primary grimoire identifies them.

The relationship is a modern hypothesis, not a demonstrated genealogy.

### 7. Was Focalor an angel of Thrones?

The text says he hopes to return to the Seventh Throne. It does not expressly state that he once belonged to the order of Thrones. Later biographies convert implication into certainty.

### 8. Did he hope to return after 1,000 or 1,200 years?

The earliest Focalor entry says **1,000**. Twelve hundred is contamination from other Goetic entries and later retellings.

### 9. Is there a second physical form?

No secure second form is described. The Latin’s “having assumed a human figure” follows an appearance already “like a man.” Later writers sometimes force this into a two-stage transformation by analogy with other spirits, but the text supplies no alternative animal form.

### 10. Does he sink ships or overthrow them?

Weyer’s *subvertit* means overturns. Scot correctly has “overturneth.” Many *Goetia* manuscripts have “over throw,” while the Northwestern manuscript most clearly retains “overturneth.” “Sink,” “wreck,” and “defeat” are interpretive paraphrases.

### 11. Does he kill “bourgeois”?

Only de Plancy says *bourgeois*. Weyer says *homines*, people or men. The social category is a nineteenth-century French textual curiosity, not part of the early description.

### 12. Does he willingly or grudgingly obey?

Weyer: *libenter obsequitur*, “he willingly complies.”  
Scot: “he willinglie consenteth.”  
De Plancy: *obéit en rechignant*, “obeys grudgingly.”

De Plancy reverses the tone.

### 13. Is Hahahel intrinsic to the Goetia?

No. The ordinary *Ars Goetia* never names Hahahel. The pairing is an angelic control system associated with Rudd’s recension.

### 14. Does “Hahahel” literally mean “God in three persons”?

No in the ordinary philological sense. The constructed name is H-H-H plus *El*. “Triune God” is a Christian-kabbalistic interpretation, probably encouraged by the three identical letters.

### 15. Raphael or Michael?

Later angelic hierarchies disagree. The common Lenain/Ambelain/Haziel scheme makes Hahahel the first angel of the Virtues under Raphael. Other modern tables place the Virtues or Hahahel under Michael. Rudd’s essential pairing does not depend on resolving this later archangelic overlay.

### 16. Libra or Taurus?

Both are real within different systems:

- **Libra 20°–25°:** angel no. 41’s sequential quinance.
- **Taurus 10°–20°, night:** Crowley’s Goetic decan table.

Presenting either without naming the system produces apparent contradiction.

### 17. Four of Swords or Six of Disks?

Again, different systems:

- **Four of Swords:** the Tarot card enclosing 20°–25° Libra.
- **Six of Disks:** the *777* decan assigned specifically to Focalor.

If the question is strictly “What does Crowley’s *777* assign to Focalor?”, the answer is **Six of Disks**.

### 18. Did de Plancy or Louis Le Breton portray him?

De Plancy described him. The 1863 page does not carry a dedicated Le Breton portrait. A seal displayed beside the text generally comes from the *Goetia*, while winged sea-demon images are modern.

### 19. Does Focalor appear before Weyer?

No surviving pre-1577 source located in this investigation securely names him. A lost *Liber officiorum spirituum* could have done so, but that is a possibility, not an attestation.

### 20. Is Focalor ancient?

No evidence supports an ancient identity. The Solomon attribution is part of grimoire legend, not a historical dating of the text to biblical Israel.

## Open questions

1. **Which exact Weyer impression first printed Focalor/Focalur?**  
   A copy-by-copy comparison of 1577 impressions and typeforms could resolve the vowel discrepancy.

2. **Did Weyer's lost immediate exemplar contain Focalor?**  
   Almost certainly some immediate source did, but no independently dated pre-1577 witness with the name has survived.

3. **What caused the formation of the name?**  
   No convincing linguistic etymology has been established.

4. **Why was the Goetic order rearranged?**  
   Physical displacement of individual leaves is plausible but not proved for the archetype.

5. **Where did the Focalor seal originate?**  
   The sigil appears in the seventeenth-century *Goetia* stream; no earlier cognate has been securely identified.

6. **How should *accepta humana figura* be understood?**  
   It may be redundant Latin, evidence of a lost first form, or residue from a stock two-stage description. No alternate form survives.

7. **What exactly does the Seventh Throne signify?**  
   It may be a celestial location, an angelic order, or a conventional way of saying heaven. The catalogue does not explain it.

8. **Who authored the individual Rudd innovations?**  
   Harley 6483 is later than Thomas Rudd’s lifetime. Distinguishing Rudd’s work from later copying, supplementation, and attribution remains necessary.

9. **When were the individual psalm verses first attached to each angel?**  
   Kircher, Rudd, Lenain, and later occultists transmit related tables, but the precise dependence of every verse assignment remains incompletely mapped.

10. **How did “Prince of Tears” influence contemporary occult practice?**  
    The title began as Dungeons & Dragons fiction, yet grief, mourning, and emotional-release attributions now circulate in practitioner literature, sometimes without acknowledgment of their game origin.
