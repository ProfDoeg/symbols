# Ronové: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes seven kinds of statement:

- **[Documented text]** — directly present in a dated primary source or manuscript transcription.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** — a modern textual or historical identification argued from source comparison.
- **[Tradition]** — an attribution transmitted within Jewish, Christian-kabbalistic, or ritual-magic literature.
- **[Esoteric correspondence]** — an occult system’s deliberate symbolic assignment, not a historical or philological identity.
- **[Disputed]** — a reading or attribution for which competent sources disagree or the evidence remains incomplete.
- **[Legend]** — a story transmitted as lore without historical corroboration.
- **[Modern invention]** — a demonstrably late addition, adaptation, or unsourced Internet-era claim.

A preliminary correction is important: it is not quite accurate to say that Ronové has no possible pre-Weyer attestation. A closely corresponding spirit called **Bonoree** occurs in a Florentine manuscript dated 1494. The exact spelling *Roneve*, however, is first securely documented in Weyer’s 1577 printed text.

## The name and its origins

### The source-chain spellings

Joseph H. Peterson’s manuscript comparison supplies the following sequence:

| Witness or edition | Date | Spelling |
|---|---:|---|
| Florence, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Plut. 89 sup. 38 | 1494 | **Bonoree** |
| Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* | 1577 | **Roneue**, conventionally normalized **Roneve** |
| Scot, *Discoverie of Witchcraft* | 1584 | **Ronove** |
| Sloane MS 3825 | seventeenth century | **Ronove** |
| Sloane MS 3648 | seventeenth century | **Ronove** |
| Sloane MS 2731 | begun 1686 | **Ronove** |
| Harley MS 6483 | copied 1712–13 | **Ronoue** |
| Northwestern University MS 65 | later witness | **Ronoue** |
| Mathers–Crowley | 1904 | **Ronové** |
| A. E. Waite | 1911 | **Ronobe**, “otherwise Roneve and Ronove” |
| Idries Shah | twentieth century | **Ronobe or Roneve** |
| de Plancy | nineteenth century | **Ronwe** |

The comparison is documented in Peterson’s [table of Goetic spirit names](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia_spirits.pdf).

### Roneue, Roneve, Ronoue

**[Documented text]** Early printing and handwriting commonly interchange *u* and *v*. Accordingly, Weyer's printed **Roneue** is normally represented as **Roneve**, while Rudd’s **Ronoue** can be read as *Ronove*. These need not represent distinct pronunciations or entities.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The transition may therefore be summarized:

> Bonoree → Roneve/Roneue → Ronove/Ronoue → Ronové/Ronwe/Ronobe

Only the first step is genuinely difficult. The later variations are ordinary scribal, typographic, French, or editorial alterations.

### Bonoree: the pre-Weyer precursor

**[Documented text]** Florence, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, MS Plut. 89 sup. 38 is a large collection of magical, astrological, and geomantic material, part of which was completed at Rome on 4 January 1494. Its catalogue confirms the date and provenance. The spirit catalogue at fol. 263r includes **Bonoree**. [The manuscript catalogue](https://ptolemaeus.badw.de/ms/357) describes the codex as late fifteenth century and at least partly copied at Rome in 1494.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Jean-Patrice Boudet and Peterson align Florence’s sixth spirit, **Bonoree**, with Weyer's Roneve and the Goetia’s Ronove. The correspondence is supported by position within the related catalogue family, rank, and the otherwise distinctive total of nineteen legions. Peterson’s comparison explicitly gives:

> Weyer 26. Roneve — Goetia 27. Ronove — Flor. no. 6: Bonoree.

See Peterson’s [Weyer commentary and comparative table](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

**[Disputed]** *Bonoree* should not simply be modernized to *Ronové*. The forms are sufficiently different that “probable textual ancestor” is more defensible than “the same spelling corrupted by one obvious copying error.” A direct critical edition and fresh inspection of fol. 263r would be needed to chart every intermediate letter-form.

### Is Ronové an ancient god, hero, place, or ordinary word?

**[Finding]** No reliable source examined derives Ronové from an ancient god, hero, geographical name, Greek word, Latin word, Hebrew noun, or Semitic deity.

This sharply distinguishes Ronové from names such as:

- Bael/Baal, traceable to the Northwest Semitic title *baʿal*, “lord”;
- Astaroth, historically related to Astarte;
- Amon, easily compared—though not always correctly identified—with Egyptian Amun;
- Asmoday, related through Jewish demonology to Ashmedai;
- Belial, from Hebrew *bĕli-yaʿal*;
- Phoenix, the Greek and Latin name of the legendary bird.

No equivalent ancient genealogy is attested for Ronové.

Several explanations circulate:

- **[Disputed/unsupported]** “Ron” as Hebrew “song” or “joy,” followed by an invented suffix. This has no demonstrated manuscript genealogy and does not explain *Bonoree*.
- **[Disputed/unsupported]** derivation from Latin *renovare*, “to renew.” Phonetic resemblance is not evidence.
- **[Disputed/unsupported]** Akkadian or Sumerian roots associated with speech.
- **[Modern speculation]** identification with **Oberon** by reversal, anagram, or scribal corruption. No early text equates the two, and *Ronove* is not a regular reversal of *Oberon*.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** corruption within the medieval Offices-of-Spirits manuscript family, perhaps beginning from *Bonoree*. This best fits the surviving documentary chain but still does not reveal an original lexical meaning.

### The accent in Ronové

**[Documented editorial form]** The acute accent becomes conspicuous in the Mathers–Crowley *Goetia* of 1904. It is absent from Weyer, Scot, and the principal English manuscripts.

**[Disputed]** The accent is often treated as proof of a French pronunciation, approximately “roh-no-VAY.” The sources do not establish that pronunciation. It is safer to regard **Ronové** as Mathers’s typographic or euphonic normalization.

### Ronwe and Ronobe

- **Ronwe** is the French form used by Collin de Plancy.
- **Ronobe** occurs in Waite and later derivative lists. It is readily explicable as a *b/v* transcription or printing variation.
- **Ronoweh** is principally a modern elaboration. It has no standing in the principal early witnesses checked here.

## The description across the sources

### 1494: Florence MS Plut. 89 sup. 38

**[Documented text]** The catalogue contains *Bonoree*, described in Boudet’s comparison as a great marquis and duke with nineteen legions.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Modern transcriptions circulating online give Bonoree essentially the same monster, rhetoric, languages, friendship and nineteen-legion profile as Roneve. Because I could not verify every word of that fuller transcription directly against the manuscript image, only the name/rank/legion correspondence is treated here as secure. A diplomatic edition of the folio remains desirable.

### 1577: Johann Weyer

Weyer printed the *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* as an appendix to the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum*. The Ronové entry is §26, column 921.

**[Documented text: Latin, in full]**

> **Roneve Marchio & Comes, assimilatur monstro. Singularem in Rhetoricis intelligentiam confert, famulos item fidos, linguarum cognitionem, amicorum & inimicorum favorem. Huic obediunt legiones novendecim.**

Source: Johann Weyer, *De praestigiis daemonum*, 5th ed., Basel, 1577, col. 921; transcribed in Peterson’s [*Pseudomonarchia daemonum*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

A close translation is:

> Roneve, a Marquis and Count, is likened to a monster. He confers singular understanding in rhetoric, likewise faithful servants, knowledge of languages, and the favour of friends and enemies. Nineteen legions obey him.

Observations:

- *Marchio & Comes* means “Marquis and Count.” English occult editions normally render *comes* as “Earl.”
- *assimilatur monstro* says only that he is “likened/resembled to a monster.” It supplies no zoological detail.
- *famulos item fidos* is a separate gift: “also faithful servants.”
- *linguarum cognitionem* is “knowledge of languages.”
- There is no second form.
- There is no staff, dolphin, harvest of souls, death office, treasure-finding, invisibility, love spell, transmutation, scientific instruction, or universal answer to questions.

### Weyer’s purpose and source

**[Documented historical context]** Weyer was a physician, student of Agrippa, critic of witch persecutions, and opponent of the demonological claims used to prosecute alleged witches. His *De praestigiis* was not an uncomplicated practitioner’s manual.

He calls his source:

> *Liber officiorum spirituum, seu Liber dictus Empto. Salomonis, de principibus & regibus dæmoniorum.*

That is, a “Book of the Offices of Spirits,” also called a book of Solomon concerning demonic princes and kings. Weyer says that he omitted material to prevent practical use. Peterson discusses this source and Weyer’s deliberate abbreviation in his [introduction](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

**[Disputed]** *Empto.* may be a damaged abbreviation or corruption, perhaps connected with *Emphoras/Semhamphoras*, but no consensus permits silently expanding it.

### 1584: Reginald Scot

Scot printed an English version of Weyer’s catalogue in Book XV of *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*. Peterson argues that the translation was probably made by Abraham Fleming and may have depended on an imperfect manuscript rather than directly on a surviving printed Weyer edition.

**[Documented text: English, in full]**

> **Ronove a marquesse and an earle, he is resembled to a monster, he bringeth singular understanding in rhetorike, faithfull servants, knowledge of toongs, favour of freends and foes; and nineteene legions obeie him.**

Source: Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, London, 1584, Book XV, chapter 2, p. 221; Peterson’s [transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/scot16.htm).

Scot’s version is very close to the Latin and preserves “faithfull servants” as an independent item.

**[Documented historical context]** Scot likewise printed magical claims principally to expose what he regarded as deception, superstition, clerical manipulation, and judicial cruelty. Later ritual readers extracted his texts from that skeptical argument.

### Seventeenth-century *Ars Goetia*: Sloane MS 3825

Peterson’s edition takes Sloane 3825 as its primary witness and records variants from other manuscripts.

**[Documented text: Sloane 3825, fol. 104r, in full]**

> **The 27th spirit is called Ronove, he appeareth in ye forme of a Monster, he teacheth ye art of Rhetorick very well, and giveth good servants knowledge of Tongues, favouer of friends & foes; he is a Marquiz & a great Earle, and there obeieth him [he commandeth] 19 Legions of spirits his seal is this.**

Source: [Peterson’s transcription of the *Ars Goetia*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm#ronove).

The catalogue has now changed in four ways:

1. Ronove has become spirit **27**, rather than Weyer’s 26.
2. “Singular understanding” becomes “teacheth the art … very well.”
3. “Count/Earl” is expanded to “great Earl.”
4. A seal is supplied.

The missing comma after “good servants” occasionally produces the nonsensical reading “gives good servants knowledge of tongues.” Weyer and Scot show that the intended series is:

> good servants; knowledge of tongues; favour of friends and foes.

### Sloane 2731 and Sloane 3648

**[Documented manuscript tradition]** Both preserve **Ronove**, number 27, with essentially the same office, rank, legions, and seal family. Sloane 2731 was begun in January 1686 and appears to have been assembled from more than one earlier copy. Sloane 3648 is usually dated to the later seventeenth century. Peterson discusses their relationships in his [Lemegeton manuscript catalogue](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm).

The substantive Ronové description does not acquire a new appearance or office in either witness.

### 1712–13: Harley MS 6483 and the Rudd recension

Harley 6483 is a fair copy made by Peter Smart in 1712–13 from material attributed to “Dr Rudd.” Skinner and Rankine argue that the underlying papers belonged to Thomas Rudd, but the manuscript is not Rudd’s autograph.

**[Documented text: Rudd recension, in full]**

> **The twenty seventh Spirit is Ronove he appeareth in [the] form of a Monster, he teacheth the art of Rhetoric very well, and giveth good knowledge of tongues, favour with friends or foes, he is a Marquis, and there is under his Command 19 Legions of spirits. His Seal is this which wear, when you invocate.**

Source: Stephen Skinner and David Rankine, eds., *The Goetia of Dr Rudd*, Golden Hoard, 2007, p. 129; searchable [transcription](https://pdfcoffee.com/the-goetia-of-dr-rudd-by-dr-stephen-skinner-david-rankine-pdf-free.html).

Notable variants:

- **Ronoue** in the manuscript spelling;
- “good servants” disappears;
- Earl disappears, leaving only Marquis;
- the operator is explicitly told to wear the seal while invoking;
- an angelic seal, angelic name, and Psalm verse accompany the demonic entry.

### 1904: Mathers–Crowley

**[Documented text: in full]**

> **(27.) RONOVÉ.—The Twenty-seventh Spirit is Ronové. He appeareth in the form of a Monster. He teacheth the Art of Rhetoric very well, and giveth Good Servants, Knowledge of Tongues, and Favours with Friends or Foes. He is a Marquis and Great Earl; and there be under his command 19 Legions of Spirits. His Seal is this, etc.**

Source: S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Aleister Crowley, *The Goetia: The Lesser Key of Solomon the King*, 1904, printed p. 19; [scan](https://lo2.org/pdf/celepha%C3%AFs/mathers/Mathers-and-Crowley.Goetia.pdf).

Mathers regularizes the punctuation and makes it impossible to mistake “Good Servants” and “Knowledge of Tongues” for one phrase. The edition establishes **Ronové** as the dominant twentieth-century spelling.

### 2001: Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s *The Lesser Key of Solomon* prints the Sloane-based reading on p. 19 and notes:

- Weyer: **Roneve**
- Scot: **Ronove**
- Crowley: **Ronovè/Ronové**

His critical work restores manuscript evidence that the 1904 edition sometimes normalized, rearranged, or silently altered. The [Peterson edition scan](https://logoilibrary.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Lesser-Key-of-Solomon.pdf) and [updated web transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm) are the most useful accessible comparative resources.

### Appearance and second form

**[Documented text]** Every principal source says only “monster.”

**[Finding]** No checked source tells the operator to demand a second Ronové form. The generic Goetia conjurations command spirits to appear “in a fair and humane shape, without any deformity,” but this is a general ritual formula, not a Ronové-specific transformation. It must not be rewritten as “Ronové first appears as a monster and then becomes a handsome man.”

## Number and ordering

### Weyer’s 69 and the Goetia’s 72

**[Documented text]** Weyer’s printed catalogue has 69 entries, though the Belial narrative refers to 72 spirits. The later *Ars Goetia* has 72.

The arithmetic is:

> Weyer’s 69  
> − Pruflas/Bufas, omitted from Scot and absent from the Goetia  
> + Vassago  
> + Seere  
> + Dantalion  
> + Andromalius  
> = 72.

The additions are Goetia numbers 3, 70, 71, and 72. Peterson documents this in the introductions to [Weyer](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm) and the [Goetia](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The Goetia compiler probably sought a symbolically significant total of 72. Peterson calls the result a kind of negative counterpart to the 72 angelic names. That does not prove that the demon catalogue was originally created as an inverse Shem list.

### Ronové’s changed position

Ronové is:

- **26** in Weyer;
- **26** in Scot’s sequence;
- **27** in the Goetia;
- **27** in Rudd, Mathers–Crowley, Peterson, and subsequent standard lists.

The change results from the Goetia’s reorganization, including the insertion of Vassago and extensive displacement of spirits relative to Weyer. It does not signify a newly discovered spirit.

## The seal

### Earliest occurrence

**[Finding]** Weyer and Scot give Ronové no seal.

**[Documented text]** The seventeenth-century Goetia manuscripts introduce one. Peterson notes that the Goetic seals are absent from the earlier Offices-of-Spirits texts known to him. The fifteenth-century Florence manuscript contains some demonic sigils, but they do not match the later Goetia series. See Peterson’s [Goetia introduction](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

### Verbal description

The conventional Ronové seal can be described without imposing symbolic interpretations:

> A primarily horizontal figure. Its left end turns into a rounded hook or curl. Short upright strokes rise from the central bar. Beneath the bar descend three stem-like elements ending in rounded or looped forms. The right side terminates in an angular or rounded enclosure crossed or joined by an additional short stroke.

The published 1904 form can be viewed as the [Ronové seal](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:27-Ronove_seal.png).

### Manuscript variants

**[Documented image evidence]** Sloane 3825, Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, and Harley 6483 preserve recognizably the same basic sigil. Differences concern:

- whether loops are fully closed;
- the curvature and length of the left hook;
- the spacing of the three lower elements;
- whether the central uprights touch or cross;
- the right-hand terminal’s degree of closure;
- ordinary changes caused by pen width and copying.

No wholly unrelated alternative Ronové seal was found in these four principal witnesses.

**[Modern invention]** Circular “lamens” bearing *RONOVE*, planetary symbols, or Roman lettering around the seal are later graphic presentations. The outer circle and lettering should not be mistaken for the manuscript’s core sigil.

**[Modern interpretation]** Claims that the three loops symbolize souls, tongues, planets, or infernal gates are not stated in the source manuscripts.

## The angel: Ierathel/Yerathel

### Identification and pairing

**[Documented Rudd tradition]** Harley 6483 places the twenty-seventh Shem angel with the twenty-seventh Goetic spirit. Thus:

> **Angel 27: Ierathel/Yerathel**  
> **Spirit 27: Ronove/Ronoue**

The pairing is reproduced in Skinner and Rankine’s *The Goetia of Dr Rudd*.

**[Important limitation]** Weyer, Scot, Sloane 3825, Sloane 2731, and the 1904 Mathers–Crowley catalogue do not individually identify Yerathel as Ronové’s ruler. It is specifically the Rudd system, later amplified by modern “angel versus demon” tables.

### The Hebrew construction

The 72-fold name is obtained from Exodus 14:19–21. Each Hebrew verse contains 72 letters. The three verses are arranged:

1. verse 19 in its ordinary direction;
2. verse 20 in reverse;
3. verse 21 in its ordinary direction.

Reading the resulting columns produces 72 three-letter groups. Agrippa describes the method explicitly in *De occulta philosophia* III.25 and explains that adding **אל**, *El*, or **יה**, *Yah*, produces pronounceable angelic names. See Agrippa’s [Book III, chapter 25](https://esotericarchives.com/agrippa/op3.htm).

The twenty-seventh triad is:

> **ירת** — Yod, Resh, Tav

With the divine suffix:

> **ירת + אל = ירתאל**

Possible transliterations include:

- Ierathel
- Jerathel
- Yerathel
- Yerath-El
- Yeratel
- Ieratel

### What “Shem ha-Mephorash” historically means

**[Documented Jewish usage]** *Shem ha-meforash* means “the explicit/distinguished Name.” In early rabbinic sources it most commonly refers to the Tetragrammaton, not automatically to 72 guardian angels.

**[Tradition]** Medieval Jewish sources also transmit divine names of 42 and 72 letters. The construction from Exodus 14 appears in *Sefer ha-Bahir* §§109–110 and is explained by Rashi in his commentary to Sukkah 45a. The 72-fold name is prominent in *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh*. A convenient primary-text presentation of the Bahir passage is [§§109–110](https://hekhal.org/texts/sefer-ha-bahir-109-110-targum/); the verses and Jewish commentaries can be examined at [Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Exodus.14.19-21?with=Kabbalah).

**[Historical distinction]** The three-letter groups were divine-name components before they became a uniform catalogue of personalized “guardian angels” with meanings, moral offices, birthdays, Psalm verses, and zodiacal rulerships.

### Christian-kabbalistic development

#### Reuchlin, 1517

**[Documented transmission]** Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* gives the 72-fold construction to Christian readers and supports the addition of divine suffixes. [1517 edition](https://books.google.com/books/about/DE_ARTE_CABALISTICA_ID_EST_DE_DIVINAE_RE.html?id=C3RgAAAAcAAJ).

#### Agrippa, 1533

Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia* III.25 explicitly derives the names from the Exodus verses and adds *El* or *Yah*. This is a major bridge between Jewish name mysticism and later Christian ceremonial magic.

#### Blaise de Vigenère, 1586

Vigenère’s *Traicté des chiffres* incorporates extensive kabbalistic material alongside cryptography and supplies material subsequently used in angelic tables and seals. [Original text](https://wulfric.artsci.utoronto.ca/rentexte/vigenere/chif_htm.htm).

#### Athanasius Kircher, 1652

Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* systematizes Christian-kabbalistic tables connecting the 72 names with nations, languages, zodiacal divisions, and angelic structures. His synthesis was enormously influential but historically eclectic. Peterson provides a [transcription of Kircher’s Cabala material](https://www.esotericarchives.com/kircher/cabala.htm).

#### Rudd/Smart, early eighteenth century

Rudd’s system superimposes the 72 angels on the now-72 Goetic spirits, one by one. This is the first checked witness that explicitly sets Yerathel over Ronové.

The principle is apotropaic and hierarchical: a superior angelic name, seal, and scriptural verse are attached to the inferior spirit’s seal so that divine authority constrains it. In the Ronové plate, the operator is told to wear the seal during invocation.

### Yerathel’s Psalm

Rudd gives:

> **Eripe me Domine ab homine malo, a viro iniquo eripe me.**

Rudd’s English:

> **Save me, O Lord, from the evil man and deliver me from the wicked doer.**

This is cited as **Psalm 139:1** because Rudd and Lenain follow Vulgate numbering. In most modern English Bibles it is Psalm 140:1, with the fuller verse sometimes numbered 140:2 when the superscription is counted separately.

The Latin more literally means:

> Rescue me, Lord, from the evil man; rescue me from the unjust/violent man.

### Lenain, 1823

Lazare Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* supplies the canonical nineteenth-century French profile.

**[Documented text, translated from pp. 62–63]**

> **27°. Ierathel.** His attribute: “God who punishes the wicked.” He corresponds to the holy name *Teos* in the language of the Copts. His ray begins at the 131st degree of the sphere and extends through the 135th degree inclusive, corresponding to the fourteenth decade and to the genius called Hepe, under the influence of Jupiter. He rules the following days: 15 April, 26 June, 6 September, 17 November, and 28 January. Invocation is made from 8:40 until 9:00 in the morning. The divine names and the first verse of Psalm 139 are pronounced: *Eripe me Domine ab homine malo, a viro iniquo eripe me.*

Lenain continues:

> He serves to confound the wicked and slanderers and to obtain deliverance from enemies. He protects against those who provoke and unjustly attack us. He rules the propagation of enlightenment, civilization, and liberty. A person born under his influence will love peace, justice, the sciences, and the arts and will distinguish himself in literature. The contrary genius rules ignorance, slavery, and intolerance.

Source: Lenain, *La science cabalistique*, Amiens, 1823, pp. 62–63; [digitized scan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/3/3a/La_science_cabalistique_ou_l%27art_de_conna%C3%AEtre_les_bons_g%C3%A9nies_(IA_sciencecabalisti00lena).pdf).

### Meaning and etymological caution

**[Tradition]** “God who punishes the wicked” is Lenain’s *attribut*. It is an interpretive divine title.

**[Finding]** It is not a straightforward lexical translation of **ירתאל**. The suffix **אל** unquestionably means “God” or “El,” but **ירת** is a constructed Exodus triad, not transparently the stem of a personal Hebrew name with Lenain’s French meaning.

Later meanings such as “God the propagator,” “confidence in God,” “the God who spreads light,” or “divine mission” are secondary meditative interpretations.

### Zodiacal five degrees

Lenain’s “131st through 135th degree” is conventionally normalized as:

> **130°00′–135°00′ ecliptic longitude = Leo 10°00′–15°00′.**

The apparent one-degree discrepancy results from ordinal versus zero-based notation: the “first degree” is the interval from 0° to 1°.

Popular calendars translate this into approximately **2–6 August**, sometimes 3–7 August. Civil-date ranges vary with epoch, leap-year convention, and whether boundary days are counted inclusively.

| Five secondary days | 15 Apr; 26 Jun; 6 Sep; 17 Nov; 28 Jan |
| Choir in later tables | Dominations |
| Governing archangel | Zadkiel/Tzadkiel |
| Sephirotic association | Chesed |
| Beneficent offices | Protection from enemies and slander; light, civilization, liberty; peace, justice, sciences, arts, literature |
| Contrary genius | Ignorance, slavery, intolerance |

### Papus

**[Documented reception]** Papus and the French occult revival circulated Lenain’s angelic calendar and related tables in late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century occultism. The Papus-directed *Almanach du Magiste* for 1894–95 explicitly marks “Influence de Ierathel” on 15 April, one of Lenain’s five secondary dates. [Scan](https://iapsop.com/archive/materials/almanach_du_magiste/almanach_du_magiste_v1_1894-1895.pdf).

**[Historical caution]** Papus did not originate Yerathel’s Lenain attributes.

### Robert Ambelain

Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* appeared in 1951 and repeats Lenain’s Ierathel profile almost word for word: attribute, degrees, Hepe, Jupiter, five days, 8:40–9:00, Psalm, protection, enlightenment, civilization, liberty, and literary ability. See the [English translation, pp. 12–13](https://www.benpadiah.com/otherstuff/elib/magick/Shemhamphorash.pdf).

**[Tradition]** Ambelain integrates the angels into Martinist and practical-kabbalistic ritual. His later warning that certain seals previously attributed to the angels were actually malefic “opposites” is his own experiential claim, not a historical consensus.

### Golden Dawn

**[Esoteric correspondence]** The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn incorporated the 72 names into a comprehensive system of:

- zodiacal quinances;
- decans;
- minor-arcana cards;
- angelic choirs;
- Hebrew divine names;
- planetary and elemental colour scales.

The Golden Dawn did not invent the Exodus derivation or Lenain’s list. Its distinctive contribution was systematic integration with Tarot and decan astrology.

### Haziel

**[Modern esoteric reception]** The author using the name **Haziel** popularized the 72 “guardian angels” in late twentieth-century French-language angelology, emphasizing birth dates, personal missions, psychological gifts, prayers, and devotional practice. This stream descends largely from Lenain and Ambelain, repackaged for a popular audience.

**[Historical caution]** Haziel’s guardian-angel psychology is not evidence for medieval Jewish belief about Yerathel, and it should not be retrojected into Rudd.

### Skinner and Rankine, 2007

*The Goetia of Dr Rudd* brought Harley 6483’s angel-demon pairs to a large modern audience and restored the Psalmic and angelic apparatus omitted from the best-known Crowley edition. The [Museum of Witchcraft and Magic catalogue](https://museumofwitchcraftandmagic.co.uk/library/the-goetia-of-dr-rudd/) identifies the 2007 Golden Hoard volume.

Their Thomas Rudd attribution is reasoned from manuscript provenance, associated papers, and historical context. Nevertheless:

- Harley 6483 is Peter Smart’s copy;
- the exemplar is lost or unidentified;
- “Rudd’s Goetia” is therefore a recension attributed to Rudd, not an autograph book conclusively written throughout by him.

## Correspondences

### Ronové and Yerathel: systems compared

| System | Entity | Assignment | Status |
|---|---|---|---|
| Weyer, 1577 | Roneve | No zodiac, tarot, or individual angel | Documented absence |
| Manuscript Goetia | Ronove | Marquis and Earl; rank metals and rank hours | Documented ritual scheme |
| Rudd | Ronoue | Angel 27, Ierathel, Psalm 139:1 | Documented Rudd correspondence |
| Lenain | Ierathel | 131st–135th degrees; Hepe; Jupiter; 8:40–9:00 | Documented angelology |
| Golden Dawn | Yerathel | Leo 10°–15°; first half of Leo’s second decan | Esoteric correspondence |
| Golden Dawn Tarot | Yerathel | Six of Wands decan, shared with Seheiah | Esoteric correspondence |
| Crowley, *777* | Ronove | Sagittarius’s third decan, day | Esoteric correspondence |
| Crowley, *777* | Ronove | Ten of Wands; 20°–30° Sagittarius | Esoteric correspondence |
| Crowley, *777* | Andras | Same decan, night | Esoteric correspondence |
| Later practical tables | Ronové | Approximately 13–21 December | Calendar approximation |

### The two different Tarot relationships

#### Yerathel

Yerathel occupies **Leo 10°–15°**. This is the first half of Leo’s second decan:

> Leo 10°–20° — Jupiter in Leo — **Six of Wands**, “Lord of Victory.”

The second quinance, Leo 15°–20°, belongs to Seheiah. Thus this pip carries two Shem angels.

#### Ronové

Crowley’s table places Goetic spirit 27 in:

> Sagittarius 20°–30° — Saturn in Sagittarius — **Ten of Wands**, “Lord of Oppression.”

Ronové is the day spirit, while spirit 63, Andras, is the night spirit. Thus this pip carries two Goetic spirits.

These pairings cross different catalogues:

- Yerathel 27 is not assigned the Ten of Wands merely because Ronové is demon 27.
- Ronové is not located at Leo 10°–15° merely because Rudd pairs him with Yerathel.

### Crowley’s *777*

In Table V, columns CLIX–CLX, Crowley gives:

> **27 … Ronove — A monster [probably a dolphin].**

The entry lies on key-scale row 25, Sagittarius, in the day/cadent column. The matching night/cadent entry on the same row is:

> **63 Andras — Angel with raven’s head. Rides black wolf, carries sharp sword.**

See [*777 Revised*, Table V](https://maestrosdelsaber.com/wp-content/uploads/ftp-files/Magia/a%20Crowley,%20Aleister/Mag%20Crowley,%20Aleister%20-%20777%20Tables.pdf).

**[Esoteric correspondence]** The Sagittarius assignment is a deliberate decanic distribution of all 72 demons: 36 by day and 36 by night.

**[Disputed editorial guess]** “[probably a dolphin]” appears nowhere in Weyer, Scot, the Goetia manuscripts, Rudd, or de Plancy. Crowley gives no evidence for it. It may reflect an attempt to particularize “monster,” or confusion with neighbouring Forneus, explicitly a sea monster.

### Rank, planet, and metal

The manuscript Goetia says:

> “The seals of those 72 kings are to be made in Mettalls,”

and assigns:

| Rank | Manuscript planet | Manuscript metal |
|---|---|---|
| King | Sun | Gold |
| Marquis | Moon | Silver |
| Duke | Venus | Copper |
| Prelate/Prince | Jupiter | Tin |
| Knight | Saturn | Lead |
| President | Mercury | Mercury |
| Earl/Count | Venus and Moon “equally alike” | Copper and silver |

Thus Ronové’s original Goetia combination is:

> Marquis = Moon/silver  
> Earl = Venus-and-Moon/copper-and-silver.

Peterson notes that Mars and iron are conspicuously absent from this manuscript table. See the [Goetia “Observations”](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

The later Golden Dawn/Crowley arrangement regularizes the hierarchy:

| Rank | Later planet | Later metal |
|---|---|---|
| King | Sun | Gold |
| Duke | Venus | Copper |
| Prince | Jupiter | Tin |
| Marquis | Moon | Silver |
| Earl | Mars | Iron |
| President | Mercury | Mercury |
| Knight | Saturn | Lead |

Hence modern tables give Ronové **Moon/silver and Mars/iron**. That is correct within the later system but not an exact transcription of the seventeenth-century observation.

### Conjuration time

The manuscript Goetia states:

> “Marquizes may be bound from 3 of the Clock in the after Noon till nine at night and from 9 at night till sunrising.”

It also states:

> “Counts or Earles may be bound in any hour of the day so it be in woods or any other place where Men resort not, or where no noise is.”

For a dual-ranked Marquis/Earl, the text supplies no rule explaining which schedule prevails. Possible attempts to combine them are later practitioner decisions.

The same observations recommend operations when the Moon is:

> 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, or 14 days old.

There is no Ronové-specific planetary hour in the early sources.

### Day or night

- **Manuscript-rank system:** partly nocturnal by the Marquis schedule, potentially daytime by the Earl schedule.
- **777 decan system:** explicitly a **day** spirit.
- **Lenain’s Yerathel:** invoked at **8:40–9:00 a.m.**

These are independent layers.

### Enochian overlay

**[Documented later overlay]** Harley 6483’s introduction claims that some spirits are “in Enoch’s Tables described” in an associated volume, while their seals were omitted there. Rudd/Smart’s manuscript environment therefore tries to connect Goetic and Enochian materials.

**[Finding]** No checked source identifies a particular Enochian governor, aethyr, square, or name as Ronové’s exact Enochian equivalent.

Crowley also supplied an “Angelic” or Enochian rendering of his preliminary invocation in the 1904 volume. This is Crowley’s ritual overlay, not evidence that Ronové originated in Dee and Kelley’s angelic diaries.

## Iconography

### Before 1863

**[Documented text]** The sources offer only the word *monster*. The seal—not a portrait—is Ronové’s sole individual graphic identifier in the Goetia manuscripts.

Consequently, depictions of:

- a staff;
- humanoid posture;
- horns;
- wings;
- hair colour;
- robes;
- a dolphin body;
- a skeletal or psychopomp form

are artistic additions.

### Collin de Plancy

Collin de Plancy first issued the *Dictionnaire infernal* in two volumes in 1818. The book underwent major revisions through its sixth edition in 1863. The Ronwe article can be securely quoted from the sixth edition, p. 584:

> **Ronwe, marquis et comte de l’enfer, qui apparaît sous la forme d’un monstre ; il donne à ses adeptes la connaissance des langues et la bienveillance de tout le monde. Dix-neuf cohortes infernales sont sous ses ordres.**

Translation:

> Ronwe, marquis and count of hell, who appears in the form of a monster; he gives his adherents knowledge of languages and everyone’s goodwill. Nineteen infernal cohorts are under his orders.

Source: de Plancy, *Dictionnaire infernal*, 6th ed., Paris, 1863, p. 584; [Ronwe article](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.%2C_1863/Ronwe).

De Plancy cites Weyer and compresses the office:

- rhetoric disappears;
- servants disappear;
- favour of friends and foes becomes the broader “goodwill of everyone”;
- nineteen legions become nineteen infernal cohorts.

**[1818 finding]** The 1818 scan and Wikisource framework were checked, but the relevant second volume is not fully transcribed there. I therefore do not backdate the exact 1863 wording to 1818. The dictionary begins in 1818; the precise Ronwe text quoted above is securely verified only in the 1863 edition.

### Louis Le Breton’s portrait

**[Documented image]** The 1863 edition includes a portrait captioned **Ronwe**, drawn by Louis Le Breton and engraved for the volume. It depicts an upright, grotesque, tailed humanoid monster holding a long staff or rod. The head and body are caricaturally animal or dragon-like rather than recognizably human. See the [1863 Ronwe engraving](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ronwe.jpg).

**[Modern reception]** The staff derives from this image, not from Weyer or the Goetia. Le Breton’s design became the default visual identity through repeated reproduction in demon encyclopedias, websites, prints, tattoos, and occult card decks.

### Modern art

Modern artists generally follow one of four paths:

1. direct redrawing of Breton’s monster and staff;
2. elaboration of Crowley’s “probably a dolphin” into an aquatic creature;
3. anthropomorphic magician, scholar, or rhetorician imagery;
4. psychopomp or “soul-harvester” imagery derived from modern lore.

Only the first has a nineteenth-century visual source; none is prescribed by the early text.

## People and works

### Chronological chain

#### Late antiquity: *Testament of Solomon*

**[Genealogical background]** The *Testament of Solomon* presents Solomon interrogating and constraining named demons with angelic assistance. It is a major ancestor of Solomonic demonology as a literary idea.

**[Finding]** Ronové, Roneve, Ronwe, and Bonoree do not occur in the checked text. It is a structural ancestor, not a name attestation. [Text](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/testamen.htm).

#### Fifteenth century: Munich handbook, Clm 849

The Munich necromancer’s handbook preserves a compact catalogue of spirits resembling later Offices-of-Spirits material. Its recognizable names include ancestors of Barbatos, Botis, Purson, Eligor, Gaap, Volac, Gemory, Cimeries, and Amy.

**[Finding]** It contains no secure Ronové equivalent. Its *Sucax* teaches languages but differs in name, rank, appearance, love-office, travel power, and number of legions. Equating Sucax with Ronové is not supported.

#### 1494: Florence MS Plut. 89 sup. 38

**Bonoree** is the earliest presently identified precursor.

#### Sixteenth century: *Livre des Esperitz*

The *Livre des Esperitz*, Cambridge Trinity College MS O.8.29, contains fifty spirits, thirty-five of which correspond to Weyer’s catalogue. Its surviving manuscript is generally dated to the fifteenth or sixteenth century, with modern palaeographic discussion often favouring the mid-sixteenth.

**[Finding]** It does not securely contain Ronové or Bonoree.

**[Disputed modern claim]** *Ducay* is sometimes proposed as Ronové because of partial overlap in language-related powers. Its other attributes and catalogue position differ. Boudet does not make that identification.

Julien Véronèse and Jean-Patrice Boudet are central modern scholars for this catalogue family; Boudet’s comparative article is [“Les who’s who démonologiques de la Renaissance et leurs ancêtres médiévaux”](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/1019).

#### *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

This is the closest general ancestor of Weyer’s catalogue. Weyer names it as his source family. Related recensions survive in Florence, Cambridge, continental manuscripts, and the Folger manuscript later published as *The Book of Oberon*.

#### *The Book of Abramelin*

**[Finding]** Abramelin’s demon lists contain many names later familiar in European ritual magic, but no secure Ronové form. It belongs to the wider late-medieval/early-modern environment rather than the direct textual chain. [Peterson’s Abramelin materials](https://www.esotericarchives.com/abramelin/).

#### Johann Weyer

First secure printed **Roneve**, 1577.

#### Reginald Scot and probably Abraham Fleming

First secure English **Ronove**, 1584.

#### The anonymous Goetia compiler

In the seventeenth century, the compiler:

- used Scot’s English catalogue;
- rearranged it;
- expanded it to 72;
- numbered Ronove 27;
- supplied individual seals;
- embedded the catalogue in a more elaborate ritual system.

#### Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

The Rudd-attributed scheme pairs Ronove with Ierathel. Peter Smart copied the surviving Harley manuscript in 1712–13.

#### Collin de Plancy and Louis Le Breton

De Plancy transmitted the compressed French **Ronwe** description. Breton created the first canonical portrait.

#### S. L. MacGregor Mathers

Mathers translated and regularized the Goetia material and established **Ronové** as the prominent modern spelling.

#### Aleister Crowley

Crowley edited and published the 1904 book, added ritual and Qabalistic material, and later assigned Ronove to the Sagittarius decan in *777*.

#### Frater Achad

**[Finding]** No Ronové-specific working or important interpretation was located in Frater Achad’s published material. His relevance is to later Thelemic Qabalah and alternative Tarot arrangements generally, not to the creation of Ronové’s standard attribution.

#### Poke Runyon

Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* and related Church of the Hermetic Sciences work helped revive operative Goetic practice in the late twentieth century. He discusses and reproduces the Goetic system, but no uniquely influential Ronové operation was located. Claims that he personally established a new Ronové tradition should therefore be sourced case by case.

#### Lon Milo DuQuette, Christopher Hyatt, and David P. Wilson

*Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* reproduces the Ronové entry and tabulates:

> Marquis/Earl; 20°–30° Sagittarius by day; approximately 13–21 December; Ten of Wands; Luna–silver; Mars–iron.

This book strongly popularized the modern practical correspondence set. [Searchable scan](https://libraryofrickandria.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Aleister-Crowleys-Illustrated-Goetia-Aleister-Crowley-Christopher-S_-Hyatt-Lon-Milo-Duquette-Third-1992-2000-New-Falcon-Publications.pdf).

#### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 edition was the first widely available critical presentation of all five books of the *Lemegeton* with manuscript variants. His online editions now supply the most detailed open comparison of Weyer, Scot, Sloane witnesses, and related catalogues.

#### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their 2007 edition foregrounded the Rudd angel-demon pairings and restored Yerathel to modern Ronové practice.

#### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s *Encyclopaedia Goetica*, *Pandemonium*, and *An Inventorie of Demons* emphasize the Offices-of-Spirits ancestry, spirit catalogues, conjurational context, and pre-Crowley textual tradition.

**[Historical caution]** Writing practical books about Goetia does not by itself prove a published, individually documented evocation of Ronové. Authorial discussion and personal working records should be distinguished.

#### Owen Davies, Jan Veenstra, Julien Véronèse, Jean-Patrice Boudet

These scholars contextualize medieval magic books, necromantic manuals, learned demonology, and the spirit-catalogue tradition. Boudet’s comparison is the most directly important for identifying Bonoree.

## Ronové in literature, film, games, and popular culture

### Negative findings: Dante, Milton, Bartimaeus, Hellboy, Helluva Boss

Searches were run for each proposed lead.

- **Dante:** no Ronové, Roneve, Ronwe, or recognizable Bonoree appears in the *Divine Comedy*.  
- **Milton:** no Ronové appears in *Paradise Lost* or *Paradise Regained*.  
- **Bartimaeus novels:** no attested Ronové character was located in the canonical novels or authoritative character listings.
- **Hellboy:** no canonical Ronové appearance was located in Dark Horse’s published-series descriptions or reliable indexes.
- **Helluva Boss:** no canonical Ronové character was located. Images and profiles found online are fan characters or speculative Goetia expansions.

These absences matter because generic lists increasingly attach every Goetic name to famous demon-oriented franchises without documentation.

### *Umineko When They Cry*

**[Documented modern adaptation]** Ronove is a major demon servant in 07th Expansion’s *Umineko no Naku Koro ni*, introduced prominently in *Banquet of the Golden Witch*. He serves as Beatrice’s butler or head servant and appears in the visual novels, manga/anime adaptations, and *Golden Fantasia* fighting game.

The elegant butler is a complete modern characterization. The name and demonic status are borrowed; the Goetia’s monster appearance and offices are not literally retained. See the franchise’s [official original-work site](https://07th-expansion.net/umi_gensaku) and the character documentation for [Ronove](https://07th-expansion.fandom.com/wiki/Ronove).

### *Welcome to Demon School! Iruma-kun*

**[Documented modern adaptation]** **Ronove Lomiere/Romière** is an exuberant, attention-commanding student. The name plainly belongs to the series’ broader use of demonological names; his charismatic ability can loosely recall Ronové’s favour and rhetoric, but the connection is adaptive rather than textual. The anime’s casting announcement is documented by [Crunchyroll](https://www.crunchyroll.com/news/latest/2021/3/24/tatsuhisa-suzuki-joins-the-class-of-welcome-to-demon-school-iruma-kun-season-2).

### *Shin Megami Tensei* and *Persona*

**[Correction]** Ronové is frequently claimed to be a summonable demon in *Shin Megami Tensei* or *Persona*. I found no reliable roster evidence for a standard Ronove demon in the principal games.

**[Documented limited appearance]** A character named Ronove occurs in the manga *Shin Megami Tensei IV: Prayers*. The manga is acknowledged through the official *SMT IV Final* supplementary-material page and publisher records. That does not establish a normal playable Persona/SMT demon.

### *Dungeons & Dragons: Tome of Magic*

**[Documented modern invention]** The 2006 supplement *Tome of Magic* reimagines Ronove as **“the Iron Maiden,”** a female human ascetic who became a vestige. She grants telekinesis, monk-like unarmed strikes, safe falling, and speed.

Its legend says that she taught enlightenment through denial, entered an iron coffin, was buried by her disciples, and was later found missing. Her manifestation is a rusted sarcophagus containing bones and black liquid. See the [2006 text, pp. 44–45](https://anyflip.com/ogyal/grwv/basic/).

None of this predates the game. It is an unusually elaborate but wholly fictional reuse of the name.

### *Genshin Impact*

**[Documented character; inferred allusion]** *Genshin Impact* includes **Ronova**, the Ruler of Death and one of the Shades. The game uses numerous names resembling demons or deities from historical catalogues.

**[Inference]** Ronova is very probably a modified allusion to Ronove, but no checked official statement by the developers explicitly confirms that derivation. The character’s death office must not be fed backward into Goetic lore.

### Soul-harvester lore

**[Modern invention]** Websites from the late 2010s describe Ronové as coming to earth to harvest the souls of old people and dying animals. They sometimes warn against summoning him near the elderly.

No such statement occurs in:

- the 1494 Florence catalogue as presently documented;
- Weyer;
- Scot;
- the Goetia manuscripts;
- Rudd;
- de Plancy;
- Mathers–Crowley;
- *777*;
- Peterson.

The story’s traceable prominence is web-era demonolatry. It may combine Breton’s monster, *Dungeons & Dragons*’ coffin legend, and the modern tendency to give each Goetic spirit a sharply individualized mythology. It should be labeled modern lore, not “ancient text.”

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Is Bonoree really Ronové?

**Evidence for:**

- same related Offices-of-Spirits catalogue family;
- compatible position in the sequence;
- very unusual nineteen-legion total;
- matching rank components;
- direct identification by Boudet/Peterson.

**Evidence against certainty:**

- substantial spelling difference;
- no demonstrated continuous chain of intermediate manuscripts;
- fuller Bonoree transcription not yet available in a universally accessible diplomatic edition.

**Conclusion:** strong scholarly identification, not absolute identity proven by etymology.

### 2. Does Ronové exist before 1577?

- Exact *Roneve*: not securely found before Weyer.
- Probable profile/name ancestor *Bonoree*: documented in 1494.
- Ancient deity: no evidence.

The best formulation is:

> The exact name Roneve is first securely printed in 1577; a probable ancestor, Bonoree, occurs in a related 1494 manuscript.

### 3. Number 26 or 27?

Both are correct within their sources:

- Weyer/Scot: 26.
- Goetia/Rudd/Mathers/Peterson: 27.

The difference is editorial ordering, not occult “promotion.”

### 4. Nineteen or twenty legions?

All principal texts give **nineteen**:

- *novendecim* in Weyer;
- “nineteene” in Scot;
- 19 in Sloane;
- 19 in Rudd;
- 19 in Mathers–Crowley.

**Twenty** is a modern copying error, prominently repeated by derivative websites and some Wikipedia versions.

### 5. Marquis and Earl, or only Marquis?

- Weyer: Marquis and Count.
- Scot: Marquis and Earl.
- Sloane: Marquis and Great Earl.
- Rudd: Marquis only.
- de Plancy: Marquis and Count.
- Mathers–Crowley: Marquis and Great Earl.

Rudd’s omission should be recorded as a recension variant, not used to erase the older dual rank.

### 6. Which planet and metal?

- Manuscript Goetia: Marquis = Moon/silver; Earl = Venus-and-Moon/copper-and-silver.
- Golden Dawn/Crowley: Marquis = Moon/silver; Earl = Mars/iron.

Both are historically attested systems. “Ronové’s metal is iron” without qualification is misleading.

### 7. Is the monster a dolphin?

Only *777* says “[probably a dolphin].”

No prior source does. The neighbouring spirit Forneus is explicitly a sea monster and has similar rhetorical and linguistic offices, making conflation plausible.

### 8. Is the monster holding a staff?

The staff is in Breton’s 1863 picture, not the verbal descriptions. It is an iconographic addition.

### 9. Is there a human second form?

No Ronové entry says so. Generic ritual demands for a fair human appearance do not constitute a biographical second-form description.

### 10. Are “good servants” familiars?

Weyer’s *famulos fidos* means faithful servants. It could be interpreted operationally as human servants, spirit servants, or assistants, but it is not the normal Goetic formula *bonos familiares*. Translating it automatically as “familiar spirits” goes beyond the text.

### 11. Does Ronové rule love?

The texts promise favour from friends and enemies. This can be used in modern practice as persuasion, reconciliation, popularity, patronage, or love magic, but the original office is not explicitly erotic.

### 12. Does Ronové answer all questions?

No. That office belongs to other spirits. Ronové’s stated intellectual powers are rhetoric and languages.

### 13. Is Yerathel Ronové’s universally traditional opposite?

No.

- It is absent from Weyer and Scot.
- It is absent from the ordinary Sloane catalogue.
- It appears in Rudd’s angelized arrangement.
- Skinner and Rankine made that arrangement widely accessible.

It is correct to say “Rudd pairs Yerathel with Ronové,” but overbroad to say “all Goetic tradition recognizes Yerathel as Ronové’s ruling angel.”

### 14. Does Yerathel literally mean “God who punishes the wicked”?

That is Lenain’s interpretive attribute. It is not a secure grammatical translation of the constructed triad **ירת**.

### 15. What is Yerathel’s Psalm number?

- Vulgate/Lenain/Rudd: Psalm 139:1.
- Most modern English/Hebrew-derived systems: Psalm 140:1.
- Some systems count the superscription and call the corresponding sentence 140:2.

The text of the verse is more reliable than the bare number.

### 16. Is Ronové Leo or Sagittarius?

- Yerathel: Leo 10°–15°.
- Ronové in *777*: Sagittarius 20°–30°.

“Ronové rules Leo 10°–15°” is a conflation of demon and counter-angel.

### 17. Six of Wands or Ten of Wands?

- Yerathel’s quinance lies in the **Six of Wands** decan.
- Ronové’s Crowley decan is the **Ten of Wands**.

Again, these belong to distinct distributions.

### 18. Did a misprint create the name?

The exact origin cannot be proven, but scribal corruption is plausible. *Ronobe* is almost certainly a later *b/v* variation; *Ronové* is editorially accented; *Ronwe* is a French contraction. The deeper Bonoree–Roneve transition remains unresolved.

### 19. Is Ronové a form of Forneus?

They are distinct neighbouring spirits in Weyer:

- Forneus: sea monster, rhetoric, fame, languages, friendship, 29 legions.
- Roneve: monster, rhetoric, servants, languages, friendship, 19 legions.

Their offices overlap heavily, and *777*’s dolphin conjecture may increase the confusion, but the catalogues consistently list them separately.

### 20. Is Ronové a form of Naberius?

Naberius also teaches rhetoric and has nineteen legions, but has a crow form and restores dignity. The clustering suggests formulaic catalogue composition, not identity.

### 21. What about modern “enns,” colours, plants, incense, directions, and offerings?

Formulas such as:

> *Kaymen vefa Ronove*

and assignments such as cinnamon, jasmine, purple, east, air, or special offerings belong to modern demonolatry systems, particularly those published or circulated in the late twentieth and twenty-first centuries.

They are not present in Weyer, Scot, the manuscript Goetia, Rudd, de Plancy, or *777*. Each should be cited to its modern creator rather than presented as ancient.

## Open questions

1. **The exact Bonoree text.** A fresh diplomatic transcription and high-resolution examination of Florence Plut. 89 sup. 38, fol. 263r, could confirm every word of the profile and clarify whether its initial letter cluster was ever corrected.

2. **Intermediate spellings.** No securely dated witness presently bridges *Bonoree* and Weyer’s *Roneue*. Other uncatalogued Offices-of-Spirits manuscripts may do so.

3. **The etymon.** Even if Bonoree is accepted, neither spelling yields a secure ancient or medieval lexical origin.

4. **The seals’ immediate source.** The Goetic Ronové seal appears in the seventeenth-century manuscript family, but its designer and the precise date of its insertion are unknown.

5. **The 1818 de Plancy entry.** The first edition’s second volume should be checked page by page against the verified 1863 article to determine whether Ronwe was already present with identical wording.

6. **Rudd authorship.** The Harley manuscript is a Peter Smart copy. How much of its angelized Goetia was composed, copied, or merely owned by Thomas Rudd remains open.

7. **Crowley’s dolphin.** No explanation has been located in Crowley’s published notes. Whether it was a guess, private clairvoyant impression, joke, or confusion with Forneus is unknown.

8. **Earliest soul-harvester claim.** The story is absent from the historical canon. Its exact first printed or web appearance remains unidentified.

9. **Modern practitioner records.** Numerous authors discuss Ronové, but verifiable dated records of specific workings are scattered and often private, pseudonymous, or retrospective.
