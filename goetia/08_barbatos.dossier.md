# Barbatos: Research Dossier

## Scope, method, and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes the following kinds of claim:

- **[Documented text]** — stated in a named manuscript, printed primary source, or official modern source.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** — an inference from textual comparison, codicology, or historical context.
- **[Tradition]** — a teaching transmitted within an occult, Christian-kabbalistic, or magical school.
- **[Esoteric correspondence]** — an intentionally constructed symbolic attribution rather than historical biography.
- **[Disputed]** — supported by competing readings or insufficient evidence.
- **[Legend]** — narrated as sacred or magical history without independent historical verification.
- **[Modern invention]** — a recent elaboration absent from the older source chain.

A quotation constraint matters here: Joseph Peterson’s web transcriptions and several hosted editions remain copyrighted even when the underlying early books are public domain. Accordingly, I give brief verbatim excerpts from each source, followed by a complete clause-by-clause account and links to the full texts.

### Latin *barbatus*

**[Documented language fact]** Latin *barbātus* means “bearded,” from *barba*, “beard,” plus the adjectival suffix *-ātus*. It can describe a bearded man and, contextually, an aged or philosophic-looking person. It does not inherently mean “philosopher.” See the [Lewis and Short entry](https://www.perseus.tufts.edu/hopper/text?doc=Perseus%3Atext%3A1999.04.0059%3Aentry%3Dbarbatus).

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Once the form *Barbatos* existed, a Latin-literate copyist or editor could readily hear it as *barbatus*. That makes “the Bearded One” a plausible learned reinterpretation.

**[Disputed]** It may not be the original derivation. The older **Barbarus**, **Barbaros**, and **Barbares** point instead toward Latin *barbarus* and Greek βάρβαρος, “foreign,” “non-Greek,” or “barbarian.” The manuscript history may therefore reflect either:

1. *barbarus/barbaros* becoming *Barbatos* through copying;
2. *Barbatos* being normalized toward a familiar Latin adjective;
3. two similar names being partially conflated.

No surviving source settles the direction of change.

### Is Barbatos an ancient god, hero, or place?

**[Finding: absence of evidence]** No ancient god, hero, city, or cult named Barbatos has been identified in Greek, Roman, Egyptian, Mesopotamian, Canaanite, or Jewish primary sources.

This distinguishes Barbatos from such Goetic names as:

- Bael/Baal, related to Northwest Semitic *baʿal*, “lord”;
- Astaroth, ultimately related to Astarte/ʿAshtart;
- Asmoday, related to Iranian/Jewish Ashmedai;
- Belial, from Hebrew *bĕli-yaʿal*;
- Phoenix, the Greek legendary bird.

**[Disputed modern claim]** Websites sometimes place Barbatos beside woodland gods, Robin Hood, the Wild Hunt, Pan, Herne, or Cernunnos. None of those identifications occurs in Weyer, Scot, the seventeenth-century *Goetia*, or the medieval Offices witnesses. Robin Hood enters only with Collin de Plancy in 1863 as a picturesque comparison; “Wild Hunt” appears to be much later occult folklore.

**[Finding]** Saint Barbatus of Benevento and historical Romans bearing *Barbatus* are homonyms. No textual genealogy connects them to the spirit.

## Earliest attestations and grimoire ancestry

### Late-antique Solomonic legend

**[Documented text]** The *Testament of Solomon*, a composite late-antique pseudepigraphon, narrates Solomon receiving a ring and compelling named demons to assist with the Temple. It supplies the broad literary model of Solomon interrogating and binding spirits.

**[Finding]** Neither **Barbatos** nor a clear **Barbarus** counterpart appears in the *Testament*. It is a genre ancestor, not an attestation of this spirit.

### Munich handbook, Clm 849

**[Documented text]** The mid-fifteenth-century Munich necromancer’s handbook Clm 849 contains **Barbarus**, described in language closely parallel to Weyer’s Barbatos. Richard Kieckhefer edited the manuscript in *Forbidden Rites*, pp. 291–93.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The shared rank, archer/hunter imagery, animal-language office, treasure-finding, and position among recognizable later spirits make Barbarus the strongest known pre-Weyer ancestor.

**[Variant]** The Munich witness reportedly gives **36 legions**, against Weyer’s 30.

**[Conclusion]** The earliest securely printed **Barbatos** is 1577, but the spirit-description’s ancestry extends at least to the fifteenth-century **Barbarus**.

### *Livre des Esperitz*

**[Documented text]** The French *Livre des Esperitz*, Trinity MS O.8.29, has **Barbas**, number 12:

> “Barbas est prince et a soubz lui xxxvi legions.”

The entry says that he understands birds and dogs, manifests things hidden in the earth, and governs 36 legions. The text is available at [French Wikisource](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre_des_esperitz/Barbas).

**[Disputed identification]** *Barbas* can also be an alias of **Marbas**, a separate spirit. In Weyer, “Marbas, alias Barbas” immediately precedes Amon and Barbatos. Consequently, an isolated *Barbas* may belong to either stream. Jean-Patrice Boudet’s comparison supports a relationship to Barbatos’s office, but not absolute identity.

### Folger MS V.b.26, the “Book of Oberon” source

**[Documented text]** A late-sixteenth-century English Offices-of-Spirits compilation preserves a fuller collateral form, “Barbates or Barbares.” Its huntsman appears with four minstrels and four trumpets made respectively of gold, silver, brass, and ivory. It teaches the understanding of birds, barking dogs, and animal cries; reveals innumerable treasures; and governs 29 legions.

This witness is important because it preserves **trumpets**, including their materials, while Scot reads “companies and great troops.” It also supports the woodland “shooter” or forest-man interpretation.

Daniel Harms, James R. Clark, and Joseph H. Peterson published the manuscript as *The Book of Oberon*; Peterson’s [Weyer introduction](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm) summarizes its importance.

### *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

**[Documented text]** Weyer called his source *Liber officiorum spirituum*, alternatively *Liber dictus Empto. Salomonis*. Johannes Trithemius had listed books with similar Offices titles in his *Antipalus maleficiorum* of 1508.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** “The *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*” was probably not one immutable book. Surviving manuscripts show a family of catalogues with changing names, ranks, offices, directions, seals, and rituals.

**[Disputed]** References to an Offices book before 1577 prove the catalogue genre existed, but do not by themselves prove that every later name—including the exact form *Barbatos*—was already present.

### The *Book of Abramelin*

**[Finding]** Barbatos is not securely present in the Abramelin spirit lists.

**[Chronological caution]** Claims that Abramelin is an early source for Weyer are especially problematic. The best German manuscript evidence places the extant Abramelin redaction around the end of the sixteenth or beginning of the seventeenth century, with possible dependence on printed demonological material. Peterson discusses the textual dating in his [Abramelin edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/abramelin/abramelin.htm).

### The Grand Grimoire

**[Documented text]** The *Grand Grimoire* includes Barbatos among eighteen subordinate spirits and places him under the general Satanachia, together with Pruslas and Aamon:

> “SATANACHIA, sur Pruslas, Aamon et Barbatos.”

This hierarchy appears in the nineteenth-century printed text represented by the 1845 B. Renault edition. Peterson judges the work probably not much earlier than the early nineteenth century, despite its legendary claim to reproduce a manuscript of 1522. See the [French text and translation](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/grand.htm).

**[Tradition]** “Barbatos serves Satanachia” is therefore genuinely attested in the *Grand Grimoire* tradition.

**[Legend]** The title-page claim of a 1522 Solomonic manuscript is not independent evidence for a sixteenth-century date.

## Number and order: Weyer’s 69 and the Goetia’s 72

### Barbatos’s two positions

- **[Documented text]** Weyer: number **6**.
- **[Documented text]** *Ars Goetia*: number **8**.

The initial sequence explains the shift:

| Weyer | Goetia |
|---:|---|
| 1 Bael | 1 Bael |
| 2 Agares | 2 Agares |
| — | 3 Vassago |
| 3 Marbas | 5 Marbas |
| 4 Pruflas | omitted |
| 5 Amon | 7 Amon |
| **6 Barbatos** | **8 Barbatos** |

The intervening Goetic number 4 is Gamigin, moved from much later in Weyer’s order; number 6 is Valefar, also reordered.

### Why 72?

**[Documented text]** Weyer’s surviving catalogue contains 69 substantive entries, although its Belial notice speaks of 72 kings or princes. The *Goetia* reaches 72 by:

- adding **Vassago** at number 3;
- adding **Seere**, **Dantalion**, and **Andromalius** as numbers 70–72;
- omitting Weyer’s **Pruflas/Bufas**.

Thus: 69 − 1 + 4 = 72.

**[Documented transmission]** Pruflas was already absent from Scot’s English catalogue. The *Goetia* follows Scot closely enough that this omission is powerful evidence of dependence on Scot or a text in his immediate line.

**[Disputed]** Some bibliographies call Weyer’s list “68” because of early numbering irregularities or ways of counting combined notices. Peterson and Boudet treat it as 69 substantive spirits.

**[Open reconstruction]** Peterson observes that the Goetic order looks like a stack of leaves or slips became disarranged. This is plausible, and later manuscripts do show displaced leaves, but no surviving exemplar proves exactly when the scrambling occurred. See the [complete concordance](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

## The description across the sources

## Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* (1577)

**Location:** fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum*, appendix, §6, printed p. 914.

Brief verbatim opening:

> “Barbatos magnus Comes & Dux, apparet in signo Sagittarii silvestris cum quatuor regibus tubas ferentibus.”

A close translation is: “Barbatos, a great Count and Duke, appears in the figure of a woodland archer with four kings bearing trumpets.”

The complete entry, clause by clause, states:

1. He is a great **Count and Duke**.
2. He appears as or under the sign/image of a **woodland archer**.
3. Four kings accompany him, **bearing trumpets**.
4. He understands birdsong.
5. He understands dogs’ barking.
6. He understands the lowing of cattle.
7. He understands the voices of all animals.
8. He detects treasures hidden by magicians and enchanters.
9. He belongs to the order of **Virtues**, partly to **Dominations**.
10. He presides over **30 legions**.
11. He knows the past and future.
12. He reconciles the dispositions of friends and enemies.

The full Latin and Peterson’s comparative English appear in the [Pseudomonarchia transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

### Textual observations

**[Documented text]** Weyer’s four kings carry *tubas*, trumpets.

**[Documented text]** Weyer does not say the Sun must astronomically be in Sagittarius.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** *In signo Sagittarii silvestris* means “in the image/form of a woodland archer.” *Signum* can mean a figure or likeness, while *sagittarius silvestris* naturally describes a forest archer.

**[Finding]** Weyer specifies no second form.

## Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584)

**Location:** Book XV, chapter 2, original pp. 217–19; Barbatos is on p. 218.

Brief verbatim opening:

> “Barbatos, a great countie or earle, and also a duke, he appeareth in Signo sagittarii sylvestris, with foure kings.”

Scot’s complete sense is:

1. Barbatos is a great county/count or earl and also a duke.
2. He appears *in signo sagittarii sylvestris*.
3. Four kings come with him.
4. These kings “bring companies and great troops.”
5. He understands singing birds, barking dogs, lowing cattle, and all living creatures.
6. He detects treasure hidden by magicians and enchanters.
7. He belongs to the Virtues, “which in part bear rule.”
8. He knows past and future.
9. He reconciles friends and “powers,” meaning powerful persons or authorities.
10. He governs 30 legions.

The full entry is in Peterson’s [transcription of Scot, Book XV](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/scot16.htm); a scan of the 1584 edition is held by [Cornell’s Digital Witchcraft Collection](https://digital.library.cornell.edu/catalog/witchcraft081).

### Trumpets become troops

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Scot’s translator probably read a manuscript *turmas* or otherwise misread *tubas*. Peterson suggests precisely this. The result is that Weyer’s “four kings bearing trumpets” becomes “four kings which bring companies and great troops.”

**[Documented counter-witness]** Folger MS V.b.26 independently preserves four trumpets and even names their materials. That strongly favors Weyer’s *tubas*.

**[Later synthesis]** The *Goetia* retains the four kings but combines them with “their companions in great troops.” Its spectacular retinue is therefore partly inherited from a translation error.

### Who translated Weyer for Scot?

**[Disputed attribution]** The demon catalogue appears in Scot’s book, but Joseph Peterson argues that the actual translation may have been by the prolific translator Abraham Fleming. The attribution rests on Fleming’s identified work elsewhere in Scot and on the catalogue’s linguistic errors. It remains a reconstruction, not an explicit signature beneath Barbatos.

## Sloane MS 3825 and the seventeenth-century *Goetia*

**Date:** seventeenth century, written no earlier than 1640/41; its *Ars Paulina* repeatedly uses the year 1641.

Brief verbatim opening:

> “The 8th spirit is called Barbatos he is a great duke & appeareth when ye sun is in Sagittarius.”

The entry then says, completely in substance:

1. Barbatos is the eighth spirit and a great duke.
2. He appears “when the Sun is in Sagittarius.”
3. Four noble kings and their companions attend in great troops.
4. He gives understanding of birdsong, animal voices, and barking dogs.
5. He breaks open treasures concealed by magical enchantment.
6. He was of the order of Virtues, some part of which “beareth rule still.”
7. He knows all things past and future.
8. He reconciles friends and persons in power.
9. He rules 30 legions.
10. His “seal of obedience” is to be worn before the operator.

The diplomatic transcription and manuscript variants are in Peterson’s [Goetia edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm#barbatos). In Peterson’s 2001 print edition the entry is on p. 10.

### Changes from Weyer

| Feature | Weyer | Sloane 3825 *Goetia* |
|---|---|---|
| Number | 6 | 8 |
| Rank | Count and Duke | Great Duke |
| Appearance | image of a woodland archer | when Sun is in Sagittarius |
| Retinue | four kings bearing trumpets | four kings with companions in troops |
| Cattle | explicitly named | subsumed under other creatures |
| Treasure | detects hidden treasure | breaks it open |
| Choir | Virtues, partly Dominations | Virtues; corrupt statement about part still ruling |
| Seal | none | drawn seal worn before operator |
| Legions | 30 | 30 |

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The Sun/Sagittarius statement is not reliable astrology. It is the accumulated result of leaving *signo* untranslated in Scot and then reading *Sagittarii* as the zodiacal sign rather than an archer.

### Sloane MS 2731

**[Documented text]** Sloane 2731, dated 18 January 1686/87 on its first page, also reads **Barbatos**, number 8, with essentially the same offices and seal. It descends from a prototype closely related to Sloane 3825 rather than being a mere copy of it. Peterson’s [Lemegeton manuscript survey](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm) describes the relationship.

### Sloane MS 3648

Although not expressly requested, this additional major witness also reads **Barbatos** and helps confirm that the standard name was stable within the English *Lemegeton* tradition.

## Harley MS 6483 and the “Goetia of Dr Rudd”

**[Documented manuscript]** Harley 6483 is a fair copy made by Peter Smart in 1712–13. It contains the Goetic catalogue with angelic material placed against it.

**[Tradition/attribution]** Smart’s copy is presented as material associated with Dr Thomas Rudd.

**[Disputed history]** Stephen Skinner and David Rankine argue that Smart copied an earlier Rudd text, perhaps produced in 1655–56. Their dating depends on textual sources available shortly before Rudd’s death and on reconstructed manuscript provenance. No surviving autograph by Thomas Rudd proves every element came from him.

### Harley’s Barbatos

**[Documented text]** Harley retains the normal name, number, rank, appearance, and offices. Its exceptional reading is **300 legions**.

**[Text-critical judgment]** Because Weyer, Scot, Sloane 3825, Sloane 2731, Mathers–Crowley, and Peterson agree on 30, Harley’s 300 is best treated as a scribal inflation—an extra zero—rather than an alternative ancient doctrine.

### Cahetel as constraining angel

**[Documented text]** Harley associates spirit 8 with the eighth Shem name, represented as **KAHTL/Cahetel**. The angelic name, character, and psalm are placed so that divine authority stands over the spirit.

**[Traditional practice]** In Skinner and Rankine’s reconstruction, the angelic and demonic signs form a two-sided lamen. The angelic face is oriented toward the magician’s heart; the demonic side faces outward. The corresponding angel’s name and psalm constrain the Goetic spirit.

**[Interpretive caution]** The manuscript establishes the pairing. The full explanation of a systematic “angel controls demon” technology is partly editorial reconstruction by Skinner and Rankine.

The principal published edition is Stephen Skinner and David Rankine, *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (Golden Hoard Press, 2007), ISBN 978-0-9547639-2-3; [publisher metadata](https://atlanticbooks.com/products/goetia-of-dr-rudd-9780954763923).

## Mathers–Crowley, *The Book of the Goetia* (1904)

**Location:** printed p. 13.

Brief verbatim opening:

> “The Eighth Spirit is Barbatos. He is a Great Duke, and appeareth when the Sun is in Sagittary.”

The remainder follows the Sloane text: four kings and great troops; understanding of birds and creatures; opening enchanted treasures; former membership in the Virtues; past-and-future knowledge; reconciliation of friends and powerful persons; 30 legions; and the seal worn before the operator.

The complete facsimile is available as a [1904 PDF](https://lo2.org/pdf/celepha%C3%AFs/mathers/Mathers-and-Crowley.Goetia.pdf), with an indexed scan at [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Index%3AThe_Book_of_the_Goetia_%28Mathers-Crowley%2C_1904%29.djvu).

**[Documented publication history]** Samuel Liddell MacGregor Mathers prepared the English manuscript-based text; Aleister Crowley edited and published it at Boleskine in 1904.

**[Finding]** The entry gives no evidence that either Mathers or Crowley personally evoked Barbatos. Editing or publishing a catalogue is not documentation of a spirit-specific operation.

## Rank, planet, metal, and time

### Duke: Venus and copper

**[Documented text]** The *Goetia*’s observations assign:

- kings to the Sun/gold;
- marquises to the Moon/silver;
- dukes to **Venus/copper**;
- prelates or princes to Jupiter/tin;
- knights to Saturn/lead;
- presidents to Mercury;
- earls jointly to Venus and the Moon, copper and silver.

Therefore Barbatos as a Great Duke takes **Venus and copper**. Because Weyer also calls him Count, one could theoretically add the Earl’s mixed Venus–Moon association, but the operative Goetic classification is Duke. The rule appears in Peterson’s [Goetia observations](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

### Hour of conjuration

**[Documented text]** The *Goetia* says dukes may be bound **from sunrise to noon in clear weather**.

**[Documented text]** It also calls days when the Moon is 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, or 14 days old the most profitable.

**[Tradition]** These rules are ritual timing conventions within the grimoire. They are not astronomical descriptions of a being.

**[Correction]** “When the Sun is in Sagittarius” is not a dependable timing rule because it arose from the woodland-archer mistranslation. A practitioner can follow it as a later tradition, but it should not be projected back into Weyer.

# The angel: Cahetel

## The three-letter name

**[Documented construction]** Exodus 14:19–21 consists, under the traditional consonantal count, of three Hebrew verses of 72 letters. The classic procedure writes:

1. verse 19 forward;
2. verse 20 backward;
3. verse 21 forward;

and reads the vertical columns as 72 triplets.

The eighth column yields **כהת**, conventionally transliterated K-H-T: **Kahat**, **Kahet**, or **Cahet**.

The late-twelfth-century *Sefer ha-Bahir* §110 explicitly describes the forward–reverse–forward arrangement and the division into 72 three-letter names. A provisional bilingual scholarly text is available at [Hekhal](https://hekhal.org/texts/sefer-ha-bahir-109-110-targum/). Rashi’s commentary on Sukkah 45a also belongs to the medieval interpretive history: [Rashi on Sukkah 45a](https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Sukkah.45a.2.1).

### From KHT to Cahetel

**[Christian-kabbalistic tradition]** Adding divine suffixes such as **-el** or **-iah** turns triplets into pronounceable angel names. KHT + EL produces forms such as:

- **Cahetel**
- **Cahethel**
- **Kahetel**
- **Kahatel**
- manuscript transliteration **KAHTL**

**[Historical caution]** The bare triplet KHT is medieval Jewish divine-name material. The fixed biography of an angel named Cahetel—choir, zodiac, office, psalm, dates, and guardian personality—is a Christian-kabbalistic and modern development.

## “Shem ha-Mephorash”

**[Historical caution]** In Jewish literature *shem ha-meforash* can mean the explicitly articulated or distinguished divine name, often the Tetragrammaton. It does not invariably mean “the 72 guardian angels.” The modern equation of the phrase with a fixed angelic roster is only one stage of the term’s history.

## Meaning

**[Tradition]** Lenain gives **Dieu adorable**, “Adorable God” or “God who is adored.”

**[Later tradition]** Haziel and related angel manuals emphasize “Divine Blessing” or “God who blesses those who praise him.”

**[Philological caution]** KHT is generated mechanically from three biblical letters. It is not a normal Hebrew word transparently meaning “adorable.” The meaning belongs to the Christian angelological interpretation of the completed name, not ordinary Hebrew lexicography.

## Psalm verse

Harley/Rudd and Lenain associate Cahetel with:

> “Venite, adoremus, et procidamus … ante Dominum qui fecit nos.”

The complete Vulgate verse is Psalm 94:6:

> “Venite, adoremus, et procidamus, et ploremus ante Dominum qui fecit nos.”

Meaning: “Come, let us worship and bow down; let us kneel/weepingly fall before the Lord who made us.” The Latin appears in the [Vulgate text](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalmi+94%3A6-8%2CPsalm+94%3A6-8&version=VULGATE%3BNIV).

**[Numbering issue]** It is Psalm **94:6** in the Septuagint/Vulgate sequence and Psalm **95:6** in the Hebrew/Masoretic and most English systems.

**[Manuscript corruption]** Skinner and Rankine report that Harley has a form resembling *procedamus* where the Vulgate reads *procidamus*, and its quotation may omit *et ploremus*. This is a copying error, not a different canonical psalm.

## Cahetel’s zodiacal five degrees

**[Esoteric correspondence]** Seventy-two names divide a 360-degree zodiac into sectors of five degrees. Starting at 0° Aries:

- angels 1–6 cover Aries;
- angel 7 covers 0°–5° Taurus;
- angel 8, Cahetel, covers **5°–10° Taurus**.

This is sometimes expressed as absolute zodiacal degrees 35°–40° or, with inclusive traditional counting, the “36th to 40th degrees.”

**[Later calendar conversion]** Modern books usually translate the sector as approximately **25–29 or 25–30 April**. Differences of one day reflect inclusive counting, equinox choice, and whether the author uses a symbolic 360-day year or actual solar longitude.

## Cahetel’s decan and tarot card

Cahetel lies in the first decan of Taurus:

- decan: 0°–10° Taurus;
- Chaldean sub-ruler: **Mercury**;
- Golden Dawn pip: **Five of Pentacles/Disks**, Mercury in Taurus;
- first quinance: Achaiah;
- second quinance: Cahetel.

**[Later convention]** Because Cahetel occupies the second half of the decan, later occult tables call it the “night” angel, with Achaiah as the “day” angel. This convention should not be attributed to the medieval *Bahir*.

## Historical development of the Cahetel complex

### Medieval Jewish material

- **[Documented text]** Rashi and the *Bahir* explain or attest the 72-name construction.
- **[Documented tradition]** *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* transmits numerous divine names, angelic names, seals, and magical arrangements. The familiar printed *Sefer Raziel* of Amsterdam, 1701, is a layered compilation, not a single ancient autograph.
- **[Finding]** The medieval Jewish materials do not supply the complete modern Cahetel guardian-angel profile.

Joshua Trachtenberg discusses the 72-name in *Jewish Magic and Superstition*, available at [Sacred Texts](https://sacred-texts.com/jud/jms/jms09.htm).

### Reuchlin

**[Documented Christian Kabbalah]** Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517) helped Christianize the 72 triplets, treating suffixed forms as angelic names. This is a major bridge between Jewish divine-name practice and later European angel catalogues.

### Kircher

**[Documented text]** Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus*, volume II, part I (1652), published a Christian-kabbalistic treatment and influential diagram of the 72 names. See Peterson’s [Kircher transcription and plates](https://www.esotericarchives.com/kircher/cabala.htm).

**[Historical role]** Kircher systematized and popularized material available to seventeenth-century learned magicians. He did not invent the underlying Exodus technique.

### Rudd/Smart

**[Documented text]** Harley 6483 applies the 72 angelic names directly against 72 Goetic spirits by matching ordinal positions. Thus spirit 8, Barbatos, is constrained by name 8, Cahetel.

**[Innovation]** No such Barbatos–Cahetel pairing exists in Weyer, Scot, Clm 849, or the early *Goetia* catalogue. It is a late-seventeenth- or early-eighteenth-century overlay preserved by Smart’s manuscript.

### Lenain, 1823

Lazare Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* (Amiens, 1823) gives angel 8 as **Cahethel** and glosses it “Dieu adorable.”

Its complete notice assigns:

- the 36th–40th degrees;
- a daily interval of approximately 2:20–2:40;
- Psalm 94:6;
- obtaining divine blessing;
- driving away evil spirits;
- agricultural productions necessary for humans and animals;
- the sacred name **Moti** in a Georgian correspondence;
- the decanic genius **Asicat**;
- a series of five recurring annual rulership dates.

These are **[documented nineteenth-century esoteric correspondences]**, not biblical data. The primary scan is at [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_ms05365).

### Papus

**[Documented reception]** Gérard Encausse, “Papus,” reproduced and disseminated much of Lenain’s system in *La Cabbale* (1903). A scan is held by the [Warburg Institute](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/pdf/abg405b2732165.pdf). Papus also prefaced a 1909 reissue of Lenain.

### Golden Dawn and Crowley

**[Esoteric development]** Golden Dawn materials integrated the zodiacal decans, Chaldean planetary rulers, and tarot pips into one large correspondence system. Crowley’s *777* published extensive Golden Dawn-derived tables.

**[Crucial distinction]** Crowley’s printed Goetic-decans table puts **Barbatos** in Gemini. It does not derive that placement from **Cahetel** or from the eighth five-degree sector.

### Ambelain

**[Documented modern tradition]** Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) elaborated the 72 angels for twentieth-century French practical occultism. Bibliographic record: [Open Library](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL22019447M/La_kabbale_pratique).

### Haziel

**[Modern angelology]** The author writing as Haziel greatly popularized guardian dates, moral qualities, invocations, and expansive personal profiles in late-twentieth-century French and translated angel manuals. For Cahetel, this tradition emphasizes blessing, harvests, removal of adverse spirits, eloquence, and material or social success.

**[Source-critical judgment]** The elaborate personality descriptions and birth-date guardianship are modern expansions of Lenain and Ambelain, not teachings recoverable from *Sefer Raziel* or the *Bahir*.

## Crowley’s *777* in detail

In *Liber 777*, Table V, columns CLVII–CLVIII, row 17—the Gemini row—Barbatos appears as the succedent demon by day:

> “8 … Barbatos — Accompanied by 4 noble kings and great troops.”

The relevant table is on PDF p. 35 in this [edition of 777](https://www.astrumargenteum.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/0777-by-Aleister-Crowley.pdf).

The table’s structure gives:

- Gemini’s first decan: Bael by day;
- Gemini’s second or succedent decan: **Barbatos by day**;
- Gemini’s third decan: Paimon by day;
- corresponding night demons: Sabnock, **Shax**, and Vine.

Hence Barbatos’s decan is **10°–20° Gemini**. Golden Dawn tarot doctrine attributes that decan to **Mars in Gemini** and the **Nine of Swords**, traditionally titled “Lord of Despair and Cruelty” or shortened in Crowley’s Thoth system to “Cruelty.” Golden Dawn decan tables are reproduced in the [Golden Dawn manuscripts](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/gd/publication/english/golden_dawn_manuscripts.html).

Lon Milo DuQuette, Christopher S. Hyatt, and David P. Wilson make the result explicit in *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia*: “10°–20° Gemini (day),” Nine of Swords, Venus/copper. A searchable copy is [available here](https://libraryofrickandria.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Aleister-Crowleys-Illustrated-Goetia-Aleister-Crowley-Christopher-S_-Hyatt-Lon-Milo-Duquette-Third-1992-2000-New-Falcon-Publications.pdf).

### Does Crowley give Barbatos a five-degree quinance?

**[Finding]** Not explicitly. *777* gives each demon a decan plus day or night, so two demons share each ten-degree decan. It does not say that the day demon rules its first five degrees and the night demon its second five.

**[Later reconstruction]** Some modern tables create literal quinances by giving the first five degrees to the day demon and the second five to the night demon. Under that convention Barbatos would receive 10°–15° Gemini and Shax 15°–20°. That is a convenient modern regularization, not a quotation from Crowley.

### Enochian material

**[Documented association]** Rudd-associated Harley manuscripts combine Solomonic, angelic, and material derived from John Dee’s angelic tables. Crowley also appended the “Preliminary Invocation” and other material not originally part of the Goetic catalogue.

**[Source distinction]** No Enochian name, Watchtower square, aethyr, or Dee/Kelley formula occurs in Weyer’s Barbatos entry or Scot’s translation. Any specifically Enochian Barbatos attribution is a later synthetic system.

# The seal

## What the primary texts actually say

**[Finding]** Weyer and Scot give no seal for Barbatos.

**[Documented text]** The English *Goetia* manuscripts add a “seal of obedience” and say it is to be worn before the operator.

**[Exactness issue]** No manuscript describes the geometry verbally. The exact seal is the drawing. Descriptions such as “rings representing four kings” are interpretations, not manuscript captions.

The Mathers–Crowley form can be inspected at:

- [Wikimedia Commons: Seal of Barbatos](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3ABarbatos_Seal.jpg)
- [high-resolution 1904 scan](https://www.fromoldbooks.org/Mathers-Goetia/pages/008-Seal-of-Barbatos/)
- Peterson’s [figure 8 in the Goetia transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm#barbatos)

## Verbal morphology

The standard seal is a broad, horizontally organized line-glyph rather than a circular pentacle. It consists of a lower horizontal framework, several upright strokes, and a linked upper sequence of curved or arch-like forms. Short transverse marks and terminal hooks vary by copy.

**[Manuscript variation]** Sloane 3825, Sloane 2731, Harley 6483, and Mathers’s printed redrawing preserve the same recognizable skeleton but vary in:

- width-to-height ratio;
- curvature of the upper arches;
- number and position of small cross-strokes;
- whether terminals are hooked, blunt, or looped;
- whether lines meet cleanly or overlap;
- relative length of the baseline and uprights.

**[Rudd addition]** Harley’s controlling-angel apparatus is not simply a second version of Barbatos’s sigil. It adds Cahetel’s name, character, and psalm as an opposing or governing layer.

## Unsupported modern “four rings” seal

**[Modern invention/error]** A recent web article describes Barbatos’s seal as “four concentric circles” crossed by another figure, calls Barbatos the thirty-first spirit, dates Harley earlier than Sloane 3825, and claims manuscript agreement on rings. All four assertions conflict with the primary record. It illustrates how generated or derivative occult content can manufacture symbolic explanations from the four kings while failing to inspect the actual seal.

# Iconography

## Before Collin de Plancy

**[Documented text]** The pre-nineteenth-century tradition offers verbal imagery but no surviving canonical portrait:

- woodland archer;
- forest man or shooter;
- huntsman;
- four kings;
- trumpets or companies.

The *Goetia* seal is an identifying character, not a portrait.

## Collin de Plancy, 1818 and 1863

Jacques-Albin-Simon Collin de Plancy first published the *Dictionnaire infernal* in 1818. The sixth edition, Paris: Henri Plon, 1863, p. 79, contains Louis Le Breton’s portrait.

Brief verbatim opening:

> “Barbatos, grand et puissant démon, comte-duc aux enfers, type de Robin des Bois.”

De Plancy describes him as:

- a great and powerful demon;
- a count-duke in hell;
- a Robin Hood type;
- an archer or hunter encountered in forests;
- preceded by four kings sounding horns;
- a teacher of divination from animal sounds;
- a knower of magically buried treasure;
- a reconciler of estranged friends;
- formerly of the Virtues or Dominations;
- commander of 30 infernal legions;
- knower of past and future.

The French text and printed page are at [Wikisource](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.,_1863/Barbatos).

### Le Breton’s image

**[Documented image]** Le Breton depicts Barbatos as a human huntsman rather than a hybrid monster: period hunting dress, a feathered or plumed cap, boots, equipment at his side, and a horn raised as if being sounded. The caption identifies him as a demon figured as a hunter.

The public-domain image and provenance are at [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File%3AIll_dict_infernal_p0093-79_barbatos.jpg), sourced from the [Bibliothèque nationale de France scan](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k5754923d/f90.image).

**[Iconographic innovation]** “Type de Robin des Bois” is de Plancy’s picturesque nineteenth-century comparison. It is not in Weyer.

**[Transmission effect]** Le Breton’s image became the standard modern “portrait of Barbatos,” frequently reproduced beside the unrelated Goetic seal. Many contemporary occult decks, demon encyclopedias, tattoos, and online profiles combine the 1863 hunter with the seventeenth-century sigil as if they originated together.

## Modern occult art

**[Modern reception]** Contemporary representations tend to follow one of four patterns:

1. Le Breton’s Renaissance huntsman;
2. a horned or antlered forest lord;
3. an animal-speaking ranger surrounded by birds and dogs;
4. a winged or armored demon constructed from game/anime aesthetics.

Only the first is directly descended from a historical printed portrait. Antlers, deer-skulls, green “nature demon” colors, Wild Hunt imagery, and elaborate infernal heraldry are modern artistic expansions unless an artist explicitly names another source.

# People and works

## Johann Weyer

**[Documented history]** Weyer was a physician, former student of Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa, and critic of witch persecutions. He published the *Pseudomonarchia* to expose what he regarded as the errors, impostures, and blasphemies of magicians, deliberately omitting material to make the work less usable.

**[Important nuance]** He was not a modern secular skeptic; he accepted spiritual realities while opposing claims that accused witches exercised the powers attributed to them.

## Reginald Scot and Abraham Fleming

**[Documented history]** Scot’s *Discoverie* attacks witchcraft prosecutions, credulity, fraudulent conjurers, and superstition. His catalogue preserves the first English Barbatos.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Abraham Fleming may have translated the Weyer material. The errors suggest reliance on a difficult manuscript copy rather than a clean printed edition.

## Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

- **Thomas Rudd:** traditional author or owner of the angel-magic system.
- **Peter Smart:** documented copyist of Harley 6483 in 1712–13.
- **Skinner–Rankine position:** Smart preserved a mid-seventeenth-century Rudd original.
- **Critical position:** the attribution and reconstructed provenance remain less secure than the physical date of Smart’s manuscript.

## Collin de Plancy and Louis Le Breton

De Plancy rewrote the catalogue for a broad nineteenth-century demonological dictionary; Le Breton supplied the lasting huntsman portrait for the 1863 edition.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Aleister Crowley

Mathers prepared the Goetic text from British Museum manuscripts. Crowley published it in 1904 and added Thelemic editorial material.

**[Finding]** Neither man is documented as conducting a Barbatos-specific operation in the sources consulted.

## Arthur Edward Waite

Waite summarized Barbatos in *The Book of Black Magic and of Pacts* (1898), later revised as *The Book of Ceremonial Magic*. His notice is influential but textually unreliable: it substitutes sciences for animal language and invents three companies.

## Frater Achad

**[Finding]** No primary diary, publication, or reliable biography located in the contested-material search documents a Barbatos operation by Charles Stansfeld Jones, Frater Achad. General participation in Crowley’s milieu is not sufficient evidence.

## Poke Runyon

**[Documented reception]** Poke Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* and Church of the Hermetic Sciences material helped popularize a modern operative Goetic system, especially mirror-based or “dark mirror” evocation.

**[Finding]** No reliable published record located here establishes a specifically Barbatos-centered operation by Runyon. He belongs in the history of modern Goetic practice generally.

## Lon Milo DuQuette, Christopher Hyatt, and David P. Wilson

Their *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* systematizes the 777 decan, tarot, metal, and planetary correspondences and explicitly prints Barbatos as 10°–20° Gemini by day, Nine of Swords, Venus/copper.

## Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson produced the 2001 critical edition and extensive online transcriptions. His major Barbatos-specific contributions are:

- locating the entry in its manuscript chain;
- identifying **Barbarus** in Clm 849;
- correcting woodland archer versus solar Sagittarius;
- noting trumpets versus troops;
- comparing the name variants;
- explaining the 69/72 discrepancy.

## Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their 2007 *Goetia of Dr Rudd* published and interpreted the Rudd/Smart angel–demon pairing. Their work is indispensable for Cahetel’s role but should be separated into manuscript evidence and editorial reconstruction.

## Jake Stratton-Kent

**[Documented intellectual context]** Stratton-Kent’s *Encyclopaedia Goetica*—*The True Grimoire*, *Geosophia*, and *The Testament of Cyprian the Mage*—argues for reading goetia within broader ancient, late-antique, and living magical traditions rather than only through nineteenth-century ceremonial magic.

**[Finding]** The works provide interpretive context, but no securely documented Barbatos-specific working was located in the sources consulted.

## Owen Davies, Jan Veenstra, Jean-Patrice Boudet, Julien Véronèse, Richard Kieckhefer

These scholars contribute different parts of the historical reconstruction:

- **Kieckhefer:** edition and study of Clm 849.
- **Boudet:** detailed comparison of Renaissance demonological catalogues and medieval ancestors.
- **Veenstra:** learned magic, spirits, and the transmission of magical names.
- **Véronèse:** medieval ritual magic and Solomonic textual traditions.
- **Davies:** the social and print history of grimoires.
  
None supplies evidence for an ancient cult of Barbatos.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. When does the name first appear?

- **Exact “Barbatos”:** securely printed in Weyer, 1577.
- **Probable ancestor:** Barbarus in fifteenth-century Clm 849.
- **Intermediate forms:** Barbaros, Barbas, Barbates/Barbares, Barbais.
- **Judgment:** the description is older than 1577; the precise spelling is not yet securely attested earlier.

## 2. *Barbatus* or *barbarus*?

- **For *barbatus*:** exact phonetic/orthographic proximity and clear Latin meaning “bearded.”
- **For *barbarus/barbaros*:** earlier manuscript forms.
- **Judgment:** “bearded” is plausible as the immediate interpretation of Weyer’s form, but an evolution from “barbarian/foreign” is at least equally textually significant.

## 3. Ancient deity theory

**[Finding]** Unlike Baal or Astarte-derived names, Barbatos has no demonstrated ancient divine prototype. Claims linking him to Pan, Cernunnos, Herne, a Roman forest god, or a pre-Christian archer deity are speculative.

## 4. Woodland archer or Sagittarius?

- **Weyer:** woodland archer.
- **Scot:** leaves the Latin phrase untranslated.
- **Goetia:** Sun in Sagittarius.
- **Judgment:** woodland archer is the earlier and philologically stronger reading; solar Sagittarius is a later textual error that acquired esoteric life of its own.

## 5. Trumpets or troops?

- **Weyer:** trumpets.
- **Folger:** four trumpets, with four materials.
- **Scot:** companies and troops.
- **Goetia:** kings with companions in troops.
- **Judgment:** trumpets is original; troops arise through mistransmission.

## 6. Four kings: who are they?

**[Finding]** No core source names them.

**[Speculation]** Modern writers identify them with the four directional kings—Oriens, Paimon, Amaymon, and Egyn—but Weyer does not say this in Barbatos’s entry. The identification is conceivable within the wider Offices cosmology, not proven.

## 7. Legion totals

| Source | Legions |
|---|---:|
| Clm 849 Barbarus | 36 |
| *Livre des Esperitz* Barbas | 36 |
| Folger Barbates/Barbares | 29 |
| Weyer | 30 |
| Scot | 30 |
| Sloane *Goetia* | 30 |

The changes show ordinary scribal instability. Thirty is overwhelmingly supported in the direct Weyer–Goetia chain.

## 8. Count and Duke, or Duke alone?

Weyer and Scot preserve a double rank. The *Goetia* drops Count and retains Duke. De Plancy restores “count-duke” from Weyer.

**[Practical correspondence]** Later Goetic tables usually follow Duke, hence Venus/copper.

## 9. Barbatos versus Marbas/Barbas

Weyer’s third spirit is **Marbas, alias Barbas**; his sixth is **Barbatos**. Earlier witnesses also contain *Barbas*. This creates a genuine risk of conflation.

The offices separate them:

- Marbas/Barbas: secrets, disease and healing, mechanics, transformation.
- Barbatos stream: animal voices, enchanted treasure, reconciliation.

An early *Barbas* with animal-language and treasure offices is likely Barbatos-related; one with healing and mechanics belongs to Marbas.

## 10. Was Barbatos a Virtue and a Domination?

**[Documented text]** Weyer says he is from the order of Virtues and partly Dominations.

**[Goetic corruption]** The later wording that “some part beareth rule still” turns the name of the choir Dominations into an ordinary statement about ruling.

**[Legend]** That Barbatos “fell with Lucifer” is a general Christian demonological inference. The entry says he formerly belonged to heavenly orders but does not narrate his fall.

## 11. Is Rudd’s pairing ancient?

No. The 72 divine-name construction is medieval; the pronounceable Christian angel roster is early modern; the one-to-one angel-versus-Goetic-demon system appears in the Rudd/Smart material.

The pairing is authentic to Harley 6483, but not to Weyer or ancient Judaism.

## 12. Does Cahetel literally mean “Adorable God”?

It is the established Lenain/Papus gloss. It is not a straightforward translation of KHT as a normal Hebrew word. Treat it as a traditional divine-name interpretation.

## 13. Psalm 94 or 95?

Both labels refer to the same psalm under different numbering systems:

- Septuagint/Vulgate: 94:6;
- Hebrew and most English Bibles: 95:6.

## 14. Cahetel in Taurus versus Barbatos in Gemini

This is not a contradiction once the layers are separated:

- Cahetel receives the eighth sequential five-degree segment: 5°–10° Taurus.
- Crowley distributes Goetic spirits through 36 decans by day and night: Barbatos receives 10°–20° Gemini by day.

The ordinal Rudd pairing and the astrological 777 arrangement were produced for different purposes.

## 15. Does each demon have exactly five degrees?

Crowley’s *777* does not say so. It gives two demons—day and night—to each ten-degree decan. Five-degree demon sectors are a later attempt to make “two per decan” spatial instead of temporal.

## 16. Is the seal ancient?

No known Barbatos seal occurs in Weyer, Scot, Clm 849, or the related Florence spirit seals. The recognizable Goetic sigil is first securely documented in seventeenth-century English *Lemegeton* manuscripts.

## 17. Modern “enns,” directions, colors, incense, and elements

Phrases such as a unique Barbatos “enn,” fixed cardinal direction, green candle, cedar incense, woodland elemental rank, or antlered manifestation do not occur in the historical chain discussed above. Some belong to twentieth- or twenty-first-century demonolatry systems. They may be meaningful within those systems but are **modern inventions**, not recovered medieval data.

## 18. Moral character

The oldest entry assigns no explicit moral temperament—neither “good,” “evil,” “honest,” nor “deceitful.” His listed offices are predominantly divinatory, conciliatory, and treasure-related. Later classification as an infernal demon comes from the catalogue’s Christian cosmology; favorable modern devotional portrayals come from newer demonolatry.

# Open questions

1. **What was Weyer’s immediate manuscript?**  
   His *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* exemplar is lost. The exact relationship among Clm 849, Coxe 25, Trinity O.8.29, Folger V.b.26, and Weyer cannot yet be reduced to a secure stemma.

2. **Was *Barbarus* originally “foreign” or already a corruption of a bearded-name form?**  
   The surviving spellings permit both directions.

3. **Are the *Livre des Esperitz* Barbas and Weyer’s Barbatos the same entry?**  
   Their animal-language and treasure offices favor connection; the Marbas/Barbas alias prevents certainty.

4. **Who are the four kings?**  
   The directional kings are a reasonable hypothesis, but none is named.

5. **Why do the legion counts move from 36 to 29 to 30?**  
   No known source explains the arithmetic.

6. **Where did the Goetic seal originate?**  
   Earlier Offices manuscripts may preserve spirit signs, but the Florence examples do not match the later Goetic set. A lost seventeenth-century prototype probably intervenes.

7. **When did the catalogue’s leaves become reordered?**  
   Dependence on Scot is clear; the specific physical or editorial event producing the Goetic sequence is not.

8. **How much of Harley 6483 is Thomas Rudd’s?**  
   Smart’s 1712–13 copy is material evidence. A 1655–56 Rudd autograph remains reconstructed.

9. **What exactly is written in the anomalous Hebrew on Barbatos’s angelic figure?**  
   Skinner and Rankine report a defective upper inscription and a central form corresponding to KAHTL. Fresh multispectral or high-resolution examination could clarify individual letters.

10. **When were the Shem angels first assigned individual psalm verses?**  
    The 72-name extraction is medieval; the fixed psalm catalogue requires a separate textual history and should not automatically be credited to the *Bahir* or *Sefer Raziel*.

11. **What authority first converted the day/night decan demons into literal five-degree sectors?**  
    It is widespread in modern tables but not stated in *777*.

12. **Are any alleged modern appearances actually independent uses of Latin *barbatus*?**  
    A character called Barbatos may derive directly from the Goetia, from DC/Gundam/Genshin, or simply from another modern work. Name identity alone cannot establish the line of borrowing.
