# Beleth: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes the status of claims as follows:

- **[Documented text]** — stated in a surviving manuscript, early printed text, or reliable transcription.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** — a historical or textual relationship argued by modern scholarship.
- **[Tradition]** — a claim transmitted within grimoire or occult literature without independent historical confirmation.
- **[Esoteric correspondence]** — a deliberately constructed magical, astrological, tarot, or kabbalistic association.
- **[Disputed]** — proposed but contested or unsupported by a demonstrable textual chain.
- **[Legend]** — narrative presented by the source as sacred or magical history rather than verifiable history.
- **[Modern invention]** — a post-grimoire adaptation not present in the earlier textual tradition.
- **[Negative finding]** — something searched for but not found in the relevant sources.

Short original-language extracts are quoted below, but complete entries are paraphrased clause by clause because the online editions cannot be reproduced here at unlimited verbatim length. Page and section references and links to the complete primary texts are supplied.

## The name and its origins

### 1. Earliest recoverable attestations

#### The *Liber Iuratus Honorii*

**[Documented text; fourteenth-century manuscript tradition]** The earliest reasonably secure occurrence of the name is not Weyer’s. In the *Liber Iuratus Honorii*, the *Sworn Book of Honorius*, represented by British Library Sloane MS 3854 and other witnesses, **Bileth** appears as one of the ministers of **Harthan**, king of the western spirits.

This group is associated with the west, the west wind, the Moon, cloudlike or watery manifestations, transport, silver, horses, and knowledge of secret or past matters. Bileth is not yet described as the mounted musical king of love found in Weyer. He is a subordinate in a different hierarchy.

**Conclusion:** the name **Bileth** is demonstrably pre-Weyer, probably by more than two centuries, but identity between the *Sworn Book* minister and Weyer’s king remains a **scholarly inference**, not a documented genealogy. See the [Latin/English *Liber Iuratus* transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/juratus/juratus.htm).

#### The *Liber Bileth* and *Maʿaseh Bilar* family

**[Documented text]** A fifteenth-century Latin compilation in Florence, Biblioteca Nazionale Centrale MS II.iii.214, contains a text conventionally called the ***Liber Bileth***. Bileth occurs repeatedly among demonic kings and ritual authorities.

**[Scholarly reconstruction; disputed details]** Jean-Patrice Boudet and, more recently, Gal Sofer have situated this material within a multilingual Solomonic complex that includes Latin *Bileth/Biled* and Hebrew ***Maʿaseh Bilar*** traditions. The exact direction of transmission—Hebrew to Latin, Latin to Hebrew, or common derivation from an Arabic or Mediterranean intermediary—is unresolved. These texts establish the antiquity of the name-family but do not yet supply Weyer’s complete pale-horse, trumpet, and love description.

#### Coxe MS 25

**[Documented text]** A late-fifteenth-century magical manuscript catalogued by H. O. Coxe contains the declaration:

> “Ego sum magnus Bileth, rex maximus.”

That is: “I am great Bileth, the greatest king.” This is an important intermediate witness because Bileth is now explicitly a king, bringing the medieval name closer to Weyer’s classification.

#### Luigi Pulci’s *Morgante*

**[Documented literary occurrence, 1483]** Pulci includes **Bilette** in a comic-epic catalogue of demonic names in the final form of *Morgante*. This proves that a recognizable name had escaped—or was already shared with—the learned necromantic manuscript world by the late fifteenth century.

**[Open question]** Pulci may have drawn on a magical catalogue, oral demonological nomenclature, or another literary list. The occurrence does not include Beleth’s later attributes.

#### Florence MS Plut. 89 sup. 38

**[Documented text]** A Florentine magical collection dated 1494 contains spellings in the same general range, including **Belech** or a closely comparable reading, alongside spirit characters. This is further evidence for a pre-Weyer name-family, although exact identity depends on palaeographic and textual comparison.

### 2. The *Livre des Esperitz*

**[Negative finding]** The fifteenth-century ***Livre des Esperitz*** is one of the clearest ancestors of the catalogue eventually printed by Weyer: approximately thirty-five of its spirits have recognizable Weyer counterparts. But **Beleth/Bileth is not found in the surviving list**.

Consequently:

- The *Livre* is an ancestor of the **catalogue form**.
- It is not presently an attestation of **Beleth himself**.
- Weyer or his source probably drew Beleth from another branch of the broader *Offices of Spirits* tradition.

This distinction is important because modern summaries sometimes say that every Weyer spirit came directly from the *Livre des Esperitz*. The extant text does not support that.

### 3. The *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* family

**[Documented textual relationship]** Weyer’s catalogue belongs to the heterogeneous ***Liber Officiorum Spirituum***, “Book of the Offices of Spirits,” family. Surviving witnesses reorganize names, ranks, directions, and offices.

Folger Shakespeare Library MS V.b.26, compiled around 1577–1583, gives **Bileth** a strikingly different position and office:

- He is numbered second in a correction in the margin.
- He teaches liberal arts.
- He makes consecrations efficacious for good or evil.
- He can confer invisibility.

A marginal note says, in effect, that Bileth should be second and Astaroth third. This is not Weyer’s King Byleth whose sole declared office is love. It demonstrates that the name circulated through unstable catalogues and was not always attached to one fixed biography.

### 4. Weyer, 1577

**[Documented text]** Johann Weyer’s ***Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*** first appeared as an appendix to the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum* in **1577**. The parent work dates to 1563, which causes the frequent but incorrect claim that the *Pseudomonarchia* itself was published in 1563.

Weyer’s form is **Byleth**, number **20**.

### 5. Scot, 1584

**[Documented text]** Reginald Scot’s *Discoverie of Witchcraft*, Book XV, reproduces the catalogue in English. Its form is **Bileth**. The translation is commonly connected with Abraham Fleming and contains several departures from Weyer's Latin.

### 6. The *Lemegeton*, seventeenth century

**[Documented text]** The anonymous compiler of the *Lemegeton* adapted Scot’s English catalogue, incorporated additional ritual material, rearranged the spirits, expanded the total to seventy-two, and supplied seals.

The major manuscripts requested here read:

| Witness | Approximate date | Headword |
|---|---:|---|
| Sloane MS 2731 | seventeenth century | **Beleth** |
| Sloane MS 3825 | seventeenth century | **Beleth** |
| Harley MS 6483 | copied 1712–1713 | **Beleth** |

Sloane 3825 is the principal manuscript underlying Joseph Peterson’s online transcription and 2001 edition. The comparative spelling table also records **Beleth** in Sloane 3648 and other *Lemegeton* witnesses.

### 7. Mathers and Crowley, 1904

**[Documented text]** Mathers and Crowley print:

> “Beleth (or Bileth, or Bilet)”

They thereby canonized three forms in twentieth-century Anglophone occultism. **Byleth**, however, remained prominent because it was Weyer’s Latin headword and de Plancy’s French spelling.

### 8. Peterson, 2001 and later digital revisions

**[Documented editorial history]** Joseph H. Peterson’s *The Lesser Key of Solomon* was published by Weiser in 2001. Its base text is chiefly Sloane 3825, collated with Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, and Harley 6483. Peterson’s online text began in 1999 and has continued to be corrected; its current state should not be treated as a photographic reproduction of the static 2001 printing.

Peterson’s headword is **Beleth**, while manuscript and printed variants are preserved in notes and in his [comparative table](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia_spirits.pdf).

## Etymology: what can and cannot be established

### No secure derivation

**[Negative finding]** No ancient god, hero, city, or ordinary Greek, Latin, Hebrew, or German word has been securely demonstrated as the source of **Bileth/Byleth/Beleth**.

This separates Beleth from names with comparatively transparent histories:

- **Baal** derives from Northwest Semitic *baʿal*, “lord/master.”
- **Astaroth** is historically connected with Astarte/Ashtart.
- **Amon** was associated by early-modern demonographers with the Egyptian Amun.
- **Asmoday** belongs to the Asmodeus/Ashmedai tradition.
- **Belial** comes from biblical *bĕliyyaʿal*, conventionally “worthlessness” or “without worth,” though its formation has been debated.
- **Phoenix** is simply the Greek and Latin fabulous bird-name.
- **Goetia** comes from Greek *goēteía*, sorcery or the activity of a *goēs*; it explains the title, not Beleth’s name.

### Baalat or *bēltu*

**[Disputed modern etymology]** Some occult writers relate Beleth to Semitic **Baʿalat**, “Lady/Mistress,” or Akkadian **bēltu**, “lady,” sometimes identifying the supposed source with Baalat Gebal, Astarte, or another goddess.

The proposal is phonetically tempting: *Bileth/Beleth* resembles forms built on *bel-/balat*. But there is no recovered manuscript chain showing that a divine name **Baʿalat** was transformed into medieval **Bileth**. The earliest Bileth witnesses already treat the word as a personal spirit-name, without an Astarte genealogy.

**Verdict:** possible comparative speculation, not established etymology.

### Lilith

**[Disputed]** Richard Kieckhefer suggested or entertained comparison between **Byleth** and **Lilith**. Jean-Patrice Boudet explicitly cautioned that the catalogue treats them as different demons.

Jake Stratton-Kent and related practitioners later developed a Bileth–Lilith connection, sometimes reading Bileth as a queen or as a “mirror” of Lilith. This reconstruction draws on variant spellings, later spirit catalogues, and comparative magical practice.

There is no palaeographic sequence demonstrating *Lilith → Bileth*, nor do Weyer, Scot, or the *Lemegeton* call Beleth Lilith, female, or a queen.

**Verdict:** an interesting practitioner reconstruction, not a settled philological derivation.

### Hebrew בלה

**[Disputed]** Hebrew **בלה**, *bālāh*, can mean “wear out,” “become old,” “decay,” or “perish.” The resemblance to *BLA/BLT* has encouraged occult etymologies. But the Rudd/Mathers spelling **בלאת** appears to be a retro-Hebraization of a European magical name, not evidence that medieval Bileth was coined from this root.

### Belial, Berith, Bael, and Bethel

**[Negative finding]** Beleth is not demonstrably a corruption of:

- **Belial**
- **Berith/Baal-Berith**
- **Bael**
- **Bethel**

They sometimes collide in indexes, handwritten copies, automatic transcriptions, or popular demonologies because of the opening consonants. Their early descriptions and textual histories are different.

## Number and order: Weyer’s 69 and the Goetia’s 72

### Weyer

**[Documented text]** Weyer prints sixty-nine spirits. **Byleth is number 20**.

### The Goetia

**[Documented text]** The *Goetia* has seventy-two. **Beleth is number 13**.

The difference is produced by both additions and omission:

| Change | Result |
|---|---|
| Vassago inserted as Goetia 3 | Later Weyer correspondences shift |
| Seere added as 70 | New |
| Dantalion added as 71 | New |
| Andromalius added as 72 | New |
| Weyer’s Pruflas/Bufas, number 4, omitted | One removed |
| Net change | 69 + 4 − 1 = 72 |

Reordering is broader than a simple seven-place shift. The *Lemegeton* compiler moved several spirits, so Beleth’s Weyer number 20 becoming Goetia 13 must be read within a reconstructed catalogue rather than as a single insertion error.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Peterson argues that numerological pressure toward **72** probably encouraged the expansion, making the demonic catalogue a counterpart to the seventy-two angelic names. Nothing proves that the earliest *Offices of Spirits* list was originally constructed as an anti-Shem table.

## The description across the sources

## Weyer’s *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* (1577)

Weyer’s entry is number 20, printed on approximately pp. 916–917 of the 1577 edition.

### Latin extract

> “Byleth Rex magnus & terribilis, in equo pallido equitans, quem præcedunt tubæ, symphoniæ, & cuncta Musicæ genera.”

Gloss: Byleth is a great and terrible king, riding a pale horse, preceded by trumpets, symphonies, and every kind of music.

### Complete contents, clause by clause

**[Documented text]**

1. Byleth is a **great and terrible king**.
2. He rides a **pale horse**.
3. Trumpets, symphonies, and every kind of music precede him.
4. At first appearance he is extremely angry or furious, this being represented as an attempt to deceive or terrify the exorcist.
5. The exorcist must take a **hazel wand** in hand.
6. The hand is extended toward the east and south.
7. A **triangle** is made outside the magical circle.
8. Byleth is commanded into that triangle.
9. If he refuses, the operator reads the Bonds or Chains of Spirits.
10. Weyer adds that if no suitable place is available for a triangle, a bowl or vessel of wine may be used.
11. Once Byleth enters, he is to be received courteously.
12. Because he is a king, he is to be shown the respect or homage accorded to royal spirits.
13. The exorcist is warned that royal spirits boast or glory in their status.
14. A **silver ring** is worn on the middle finger of the left hand and held before the face, as is done when dealing with Amaymon.
15. Byleth’s office is to cause all—or extremely intense—love between men and women until the exorcist’s desire is fulfilled.
16. He formerly belonged to the angelic **Order of Powers**.
17. He hopes to return to the **seventh throne**.
18. Weyer immediately distances himself from this claim, saying it is scarcely credible.
19. Byleth rules **85 legions**.

The [complete Latin and English presentation](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm#byleth) should be consulted for punctuation and variant readings.

### The wine vessel

**[Documented text]** The wine vessel is a genuine Weyer detail.

**[Textual change]** It disappears from the ordinary *Lemegeton* Beleth paragraph and hence from many twentieth-century summaries.

**[Interpretive caution]** It is not the same object as Solomon’s legendary glass or brass spirit vessel. Weyer proposes it as a local substitute when a triangle cannot conveniently be drawn.

## Scot’s *Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584)

Scot places the material in Book XV, chapter 2, retaining **Bileth**.

### English extract

> “Bileth is a great king and a terrible, riding on a pale horsse, before whome go trumpets, and all kind of melodious musicke.”

### Complete contents, compared with Weyer

**[Documented text]** Scot preserves nearly all the memorable features:

- Great and terrible king.
- Pale horse.
- Trumpets and melodious music.
- Furious arrival.
- Hazel wand.
- South/east gesture.
- Triangle outside the circle.
- Bonds and Charges if he refuses.
- Respectful reception.
- Royal pride.
- Silver ring on the left middle finger before the face.
- Power over love between men and women.
- Former membership in the Powers.
- Hope of occupying the seventh throne.
- Eighty-five legions.

Scot’s English occasionally obscures Weyer’s syntax. This matters especially where material concerning Bileth appears inside the neighboring entry for Gaap.

The [complete Scot catalogue](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/scot16.htm) allows direct comparison with Weyer.

## Ham, son of Noah, and the “art of Bileth”

### What Weyer and Scot actually say

**[Documented text; legend]** The Ham story is not located in Byleth’s own numbered entry. It occurs under **Gaap**.

Weyer’s narrative says that after the Flood **Cham/Ham, son of Noah**, was the first to invoke wicked spirits. He invoked Byleth and composed an art and a book in Byleth’s name, known to many *mathematici*.

Scot likewise says that Ham invoked Bileth and made an art and book associated with him.

### What “mathematici” means

**[Historical interpretation]** In Renaissance polemical language, *mathematici* may encompass astrologers, diviners, and occult mathematical practitioners, not merely modern mathematicians.

### What the passage does not say

**[Negative finding]**

- It does not say that the historical Ham really authored a surviving grimoire.
- It does not say that Bileth invented mathematics.
- It does not establish that an actual antediluvian *Book of Bileth* existed.
- It does not clearly identify the medieval Latin *Liber Bileth* with Ham’s legendary book.

The coincidence between the legend and the surviving title *Liber Bileth* is historically suggestive, but the transmission remains unproven.

## Sloane MS 2731 and Sloane MS 3825

**[Documented text]** The *Lemegeton* version follows Scot closely while normalizing the spirit as **Beleth**, moving him to number 13, and adding a seal.

Its distinctive wording begins:

> “The Thirteenth Spirit is called Beleth … He is a mighty King and terrible. He rideth on a pale horse…”

### Full semantic content

The manuscript version states that:

1. Beleth is the thirteenth spirit.
2. He is a mighty and terrible king.
3. He rides a pale horse.
4. Trumpets and musical instruments go before him.
5. His first manifestation is furious.
6. The operator must hold a hazel stick.
7. The stick or hand is extended toward the south and east.
8. A triangle is made outside the circle.
9. Beleth is commanded into it by the Bonds and Charges of Spirits.
10. He must be courteously received because he is a great king.
11. The operator must pay him homage.
12. The silver ring is worn on the middle finger of the left hand and held before the face.
13. This is compared with the procedure for Amaymon.
14. Beleth causes all possible love between men and women until the operator’s desire is fulfilled.
15. He belonged to the Order of Powers.
16. He governs eighty-five legions.
17. His seal is to be worn during the working.

### Significant changes from Weyer

- The wine-vessel substitute is omitted.
- Weyer’s extended comments on royal boasting are shortened.
- The skeptical comment about restoration to the seventh throne is shortened or lost in some witnesses.
- A graphic seal and lamen instruction are added.
- The entry is embedded in the *Goetia’s* general circle, triangle, curse, welcome, and license-to-depart ritual.

Peterson’s [Sloane 3825-based transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm#beleth) records variants from other manuscripts.

## Harley MS 6483 and the Rudd material

**[Documented manuscript]** Harley 6483 was copied by **Peter Smart** in 1712–1713. Its first book is titled *Liber malorum Spirituum, seu Goetia*. It transmits the ordinary Beleth description and seal but overlays the Goetic series with the seventy-two angelic names.

**[Tradition]** The controlling angel for spirit 13 is angel 13, **Iezalel/Jezalel**.

**[Ritual technology]** In the Rudd arrangement, the demonic seal and its Shem angelic counterseal form a two-sided lamen:

- The Goetic character faces outward toward the manifested spirit.
- The angelic name or seal is placed on the reverse, toward the magician.
- The paired angel, divine names, psalm, and protective apparatus are intended to command, constrain, or counterbalance the demon.

**[Scholarly dispute]** Stephen Skinner and David Rankine argue that Smart copied material derived from the magician and military engineer **Thomas Rudd** and that the underlying arrangement may date to approximately 1655–1656. The surviving manuscript itself is later, and the exact extent of Rudd’s authorship remains debated. “The Goetia of Dr Rudd” is therefore a useful name for a textual-magical recension, not an unassailable autograph attribution.

## Mathers–Crowley, 1904

**[Documented text]** *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King*, prepared from Mathers’s work and published by Aleister Crowley, reproduces the standard Beleth paragraph on pp. 16–17 and prints two alternative seals.

It standardizes:

- Number 13.
- “Beleth (or Bileth, or Bilet).”
- King.
- Pale horse and musical procession.
- Furious first appearance.
- Hazel wand and triangle.
- Deference appropriate to a king.
- Silver ring.
- Love.
- Powers.
- Eighty-five legions.
- Lamen.

### The “flaming breath”

**[Editorial addition]** A footnote explains the silver ring as protection from the spirit’s flaming or fiery breath.

That specific explanation is not present in Weyer’s Beleth paragraph, Scot’s entry, or the ordinary *Lemegeton* Beleth text. It may have been inferred from other royal-spirit warnings, especially traditions about Astaroth’s noxious breath and Amaymon’s dangerous presence.

It should therefore be labeled **Mathers–Crowley commentary**, not an ancient Beleth attribute.

### No specific second form

**[Negative finding]** The Beleth entry gives no transformation into a second animal or human form. This distinguishes him from spirits such as Gamigin, who explicitly changes form at the operator’s request.

The *Goetia’s* general conjurations can command a spirit to appear in a fair, comely, or human shape, but that is general ritual language. It is not a Beleth-specific second form. The cat-headed de Plancy image is also not such a form.

## Peterson’s 2001 edition

**[Documented editorial history]** Joseph H. Peterson’s *The Lesser Key of Solomon* places Beleth on approximately pp. 12–13 of the Goetia catalogue. Its importance lies in manuscript collation:

- Sloane 3825 supplies the principal text.
- Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, and Harley 6483 supply variants.
- Weyer, Scot, and related spirit catalogues are used for textual comparison.
- Peterson distinguishes manuscript material from Mathers–Crowley’s editorial expansions.

The edition confirms **85 legions** and treats the seal as part of the *Lemegeton* tradition, not Weyer’s catalogue. The publisher’s [bibliographic record](https://redwheelweiser.com/book/the-lesser-key-of-solomon-9781578632206/) gives publication date 1 May 2001, 304 pages, ISBN 978-1-57863-220-6.

## The angel: Iezalel

## The seventy-two names

**[Jewish mystical tradition]** Exodus 14:19–21 consists, in the consonantal Hebrew text used by the tradition, of three consecutive verses of seventy-two letters each. The usual construction is:

1. Write verse 19 from right to left in its normal direction.
2. Write verse 20 beneath it in reverse order.
3. Write verse 21 beneath that in normal order.
4. Read the resulting vertical columns as seventy-two three-letter names.

This boustrophedon arrangement is medievally attested and is discussed by Rashi in connection with the seventy-two-letter divine name. Versions of the triplets appear in the ***Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh*** tradition.

### From trigrams to angels

**[Historical development]** The biblical operation produces consonantal triplets, not biographies of guardian angels. Christian kabbalists expanded many of the triplets with divine suffixes:

- **־אל**, *-El*
- **־יה**, *-Yah*

The thirteenth triplet is **יזל**, Y-Z-L. With *El* it becomes **יזלאל**, rendered:

- Iezalel
- Jezalel
- Yezalel
- Yesalel

Orthography varies because Hebrew **י** may be romanized I, J, or Y, while the vowels are supplied by later traditions.

## Historical chain of Iezalel’s attributes

### *Sefer Raziel*

**[Documented mystical tradition]** The *Sefer Raziel* corpus transmits the seventy-two-letter name and associated angelic material, but it is a layered medieval compilation rather than a single book dictated to Adam by an angel.

**[Legend]** The work’s own mythology presents Raziel as revealing celestial secrets to Adam. That is sacred-magical provenance, not a datable account of composition.

**[Negative finding]** The fully developed modern Iezalel profile—five zodiacal degrees, a May date range, twenty-minute birth interval, psychological character, marriage fidelity, and a “contrary angel”—is not recoverable as a single package from the early *Sefer Raziel*.

### Reuchlin and Agrippa

**[Documented Christian kabbalah]** Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517) helped circulate suffix-expanded versions of the seventy-two names in Christian learned magic. Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa incorporated related material into *De occulta philosophia*.

At this stage the names were becoming a Christian angelic hierarchy, but the later guardian-angel personality system was still undeveloped.

### Athanasius Kircher

**[Documented text; esoteric reconstruction]** Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–1654) prints and diagrams the seventy-two names within a vast Christian-kabbalistic synthesis. Kircher connects the name-system with peoples, languages, divine attributes, zodiacal arrangements, and universal sacred history.

Kircher is a crucial bridge between earlier learned cabala and the tables used by seventeenth- and eighteenth-century Christian magicians. His history and Egyptian interpretations are not accepted as modern philology.

### Rudd/Smart

**[Documented manuscript tradition]** Harley 6483 mechanically pairs the spirit and angel catalogues by number:

> Spirit 13, Beleth → Angel 13, Iezalel.

This is the earliest relevant source in which the angel demonstrably functions as Beleth’s direct magical counterforce.

The pairing does not arise from Beleth’s name, office, or supposed nature. It arises from ordinal position.

### Lenain, 1823

**[Documented occult system]** Lazare Républicain Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* gives the thirteenth angel the divine attribute:

> “Dieu glorifié sur toutes choses.”

Lenain associates Iezalel with:

- Zodiacal degrees 61–65.
- Friendship and reconciliation.
- Conjugal fidelity.
- Aptitude for learning.
- Happiness or facility in study.
- Excellent memory.
- Skill or distinction.
- A contrary influence of ignorance, error, and falsehood.

These are **Lenain’s occult correspondences**, not meanings derivable solely from the Hebrew letters.

### Psalm verse

Lenain and later tables associate Iezalel with the verse whose Hebrew begins:

**הָרִיעוּ לַיהוָה כָּל־הָאָרֶץ**

A standard English rendering is:

> “Make a joyful noise unto the LORD, all the earth: make a loud noise, and rejoice, and sing praise.”

This is **Psalm 98:4** in modern Hebrew, Jewish, and most Protestant numbering, but **Psalm 97:4** in the Vulgate/LXX sequence.

**[Textual problem]** Lenain and Papus sometimes label it “verse 6” even while printing words corresponding to Vulgate Psalm 97:4. This appears to be a citation or transmission error subsequently repeated by occult tables.

### Papus

**[Documented occult transmission]** Papus reproduced or adapted Lenain’s angel notices in *La Cabbale* and related writings. Through Papus, the Lenain system entered the broad French occult revival.

### Golden Dawn

**[Esoteric correspondence]** Golden Dawn-style tables coordinated:

- Seventy-two Shem angels.
- Five-degree zodiacal sectors.
- Decans and tarot pips.
- Angelic choirs.
- Planetary and elemental dignities.
- Goetic spirits.
- Divine names and psalms.

In this environment Iezalel commonly becomes a **Cherubim** angel. Later tables give a governing archangel such as **Jophiel**, although French-derived sources are not unanimous.

### Ambelain

**[Documented twentieth-century occultism]** Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* continued the French Lenain–Papus system. It expanded ritual instructions, angelic hierarchies, favorable periods, and moralized contrary influences.

### Haziel

**[Modern esoteric development]** “Haziel,” the pen name of François Bernard-Termès, popularized the seventy-two angels as personal guardian angels in late-twentieth-century French and translated literature.

Haziel’s Iezalel material typically adds:

- Personality descriptions for persons born during the angel’s date range.
- Relationship advice.
- Vocational tendencies.
- Prayers and petitions.
- A personal guardian-angel framework.
- More rigid daily time slots.

These are modern elaborations of the Lenain/Papus stream. They should not be retrojected into *Sefer Raziel* or Harley 6483.

## How Iezalel constrains Beleth

**[Rudd tradition]** The logic of the lamen is juridical and hierarchical:

```text
Divine name and authority
          ↓
Iezalel, angel 13
          ↓
Beleth, spirit 13
          ↓
Requested operation
```

The demon is not invoked as an equal independent god. The angelic name is supposed to place the operation under superior divine authority.

**[Historical caution]** The ordinal pairing does not imply that pre-Rudd magicians believed Iezalel had always ruled Beleth. Weyer never mentions Iezalel; Scot never mentions Iezalel; the earliest ordinary *Lemegeton* text does not require this exact pair. It belongs to the Rudd recension.

Skinner and Rankine published the manuscript system as ***The Goetia of Dr Rudd*** in 2007. Their edition made the angel–demon lamens accessible and strongly influenced twenty-first-century “balanced” or angelically constrained Goetic practice.

## Why each pip carries two spirits

The Golden Dawn tarot system assigns one numbered minor card to each ten-degree decan. The Shem system assigns one angel to each five-degree quinance. Consequently, each decan contains two angels and, under the Rudd ordinal pairing, two demons.

For the first Gemini decan:

| Degrees | Angel | Demon |
|---|---|---|
| 0°–5° Gemini | Iezalel 13 | Beleth 13 |
| 5°–10° Gemini | Mebahel 14 | Leraje 14 |
| Whole decan | Eight of Swords | Jupiter in Gemini |

This construction is internally coherent, but it is not the same as Crowley’s dedicated Goetic-decan column.

## Crowley’s *777* placement

**[Documented esoteric correspondence]** In *777*, Beleth appears in the table of Goetic demons assigned to decans by day and night. The placement is:

- First decan of Leo.
- Day or ascendant demon.
- Five of Wands.
- Saturn in Leo.
- Crocell as the corresponding night demon.

Thus Beleth can acquire three different planetary references without contradiction inside the layered system:

1. **Sun** — planet of the rank King.
2. **Saturn** — planetary ruler printed for the Five of Wands decan.
3. **Jupiter** — planetary ruler of the Eight of Swords decan under the separate Iezalel/Gemini overlay.

Confusion arises when modern tables silently combine all three.

## Astrological timing

**[Goetic tradition]** The working instructions prescribe:

- Kings from 9 a.m. until noon.
- Kings again from 3 p.m. until sunset.
- Fair, clear weather.
- Even lunar days through the fourteenth day.

**[Later astrological magic]** Golden Dawn and post-Golden Dawn practitioners may further select:

- A solar hour or Sunday because Beleth is a king.
- A Leo decan date because of *777*.
- 0°–5° Gemini because of Iezalel.
- A Jupiter hour because of the Eight of Swords.
- A Saturn hour because of the Five of Wands.
- The angel’s twenty-minute daily interval.

These cannot all be called original Goetic requirements. They are alternative later strategies built from different correspondence tables.

## Enochian and later overlays

**[Negative finding]** There is no Enochian name, aethyr, tablet square, or Enochian call in Weyer’s, Scot’s, or the ordinary *Lemegeton* Beleth entry.

**[Historical relationship]** Material attributed to Rudd sometimes stands near or within collections influenced by John Dee’s angelic magic. That codicological proximity does not make Beleth an Enochian entity.

**[Modern syncretism]** Twentieth-century ceremonial magicians, including systems associated with Crowley and Lon Milo DuQuette, have combined:

- Goetic seals.
- Enochian tablets or calls.
- Tarot pips.
- Zodiacal decans.
- Shem angels.
- Planetary colors and weapons.

Those combinations can be legitimate within their authors’ systems, but they are overlays, not restored medieval Beleth material.

## The seal

### Absence from Weyer and Scot

**[Negative finding]** Neither Weyer’s *Pseudomonarchia* nor Scot’s catalogue supplies a Beleth character. The elaborate sigil is therefore not evidence from 1577 or 1584.

### Manuscript seals

**[Documented text/image]** The *Lemegeton* manuscripts insert a “seal” or “character” after the description. The Sloane 3825 form can be seen in Peterson’s [figure 13](https://www.esotericarchives.com/gifs/g13.jpg).

The figure is a linear monogram rather than a representational picture:

- A long horizontal framework dominates it.
- Central and terminal strokes rise or descend from that axis.
- Curved loops and hooked ends produce several enclosed or almost enclosed compartments.
- It contains no horse, trumpet, crown, Hebrew word, or recognizable alphabetic inscription.
- Its identity depends on topology—where lines join, cross, curl, and terminate—so the manuscript image is more exact than any verbal description.

### The two Mathers–Crowley forms

**[Documented image]** The 1904 edition prints two alternative Beleth characters. Modern clean reproductions can be examined as [seal 1](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:13-Beleth_seal01.png) and [seal 2](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:13-Beleth_seal02.png).

They share the same broad family resemblance but differ in:

- Curvature and closure of loops.
- Relative length of horizontal bars.
- Placement of upright and pendant strokes.
- Degree of symmetry.
- Treatment of terminal hooks.

The second should not automatically be called a “secret seal,” a feminine form, or an angelic form. In the 1904 printing it is simply an alternative transmitted character.

### Rudd’s double lamen

**[Rudd tradition]** Harley 6483 turns the seal into one face of a paired ritual object. Iezalel’s name or character occupies the reverse and faces the operator’s heart. This is a functional innovation relative to Weyer and Scot.

### Seal metal

**[Esoteric correspondence]** The *Goetia’s* rank instructions say that principal kings’ seals should be made in **gold**. Thus “Beleth’s metal is gold” is a rank-derived ritual rule, not a property stated inside his individual description.

## Iconography

## Collin de Plancy

### First edition, 1818

**[Documented publication history]** Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* first appeared in 1818. The work went through extensive ideological and textual revision. Claims attributed to “de Plancy 1818” must therefore be checked against a specific edition rather than assumed from the illustrated 1863 text.

**[Negative finding]** The celebrated cat-headed Byleth portrait does not originate as an 1818 plate. It belongs to the illustrated sixth edition of 1863.

### Sixth edition, 1863

The article appears on p. 125 under **Byleth**. Its most distinctive sentence is:

> “Il se montre assis sur un cheval blanc, précédé de chats qui sonnent du cor et de la trompe.”

Translation: he appears seated on a white horse, preceded by cats sounding horn and trumpet.

De Plancy repeats much of the grimoire tradition:

- Great and terrible king.
- Fierce arrival.
- Hazel wand and triangle.
- Deferential reception.
- Silver ring.
- Love.
- Former membership in the Powers.
- Hope of restoration.
- Royal command of legions.

But he makes two notable departures:

1. **Pale horse → white horse.**
2. **85 legions → 80 legions.**

### Louis Le Breton’s image

**[Documented image; artistic interpretation]** Louis Le Breton’s engraving does not illustrate the complete mounted procession. It isolates the bizarre musical element as an anthropomorphic, catlike demonic musician. The figure has a feline head or face, a compact humanoid body, and a horn or trumpet; small animals or mice reinforce the cat motif.

The image is therefore an artistic condensation of de Plancy’s newly introduced “cats sounding horns,” not an illustration preserved from Weyer.

**[Textual conclusion]** Cats are absent from:

- Weyer.
- Scot.
- Sloane 2731.
- Sloane 3825.
- Harley 6483.
- Mathers–Crowley’s prose.

The cat procession and subsequent “king of cats” identity begin with, or at least are first securely documented in, de Plancy’s nineteenth-century retelling.

### Why cats?

**[Open question]** De Plancy gives no source. Possibilities include:

- A visual joke based on caterwauling as “music.”
- Confusion with another feline Goetic spirit.
- A lost intermediary demonology.
- De Plancy’s own literary embellishment.

No earlier source recovered for this dossier supports the cats.

## Modern art

**[Modern invention]** Modern portrayals usually descend from one of three lineages:

1. **The de Plancy lineage:** cat-headed musician, horn, mice, “king of cats.”
2. **The Goetia lineage:** mounted king, pale horse, trumpeters, crown, military procession.
3. **The Lilith/Baalat lineage:** female ruler, queen, dark goddess, or androgynous love spirit.

Only the second is closely based on Weyer’s description. The first is de Plancy’s nineteenth-century iconography. The third comes from disputed etymologies and modern practitioner experience.

Modern seals are frequently redrawn with altered intersections or mislabeled on image repositories. A digital trace should be checked against a manuscript or the 1904 facsimile before being treated as a historical form.

## People and works

## Johann Weyer

**[Documented history]** Weyer was a physician and critic of aggressive witch prosecution. His demon catalogue was embedded in a work arguing that many supposed witches were deluded, ill, or deceived rather than commanding real infernal forces.

Publishing the catalogue does not prove that Weyer personally evoked Byleth. His purpose was partly expositional and polemical.

## Reginald Scot

Scot used similar material in an English attack on witch-belief and fraudulent conjuring. His book paradoxically became a major English source for later ceremonial magicians because it printed rituals and spirit descriptions while attempting to discredit them.

## The anonymous *Lemegeton* compiler

The compiler’s contribution was decisive:

- Reduction and reorganization of Scot’s catalogue.
- Expansion to seventy-two.
- Incorporation of seals.
- Standardized ceremonial apparatus.
- Establishment of Beleth as number 13.

## Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

Thomas Rudd is traditionally credited with angelically balancing or constraining Goetic spirits. Peter Smart is the named early-eighteenth-century copyist of Harley 6483.

**[Disputed attribution]** The extent to which every component derives from Rudd himself is unresolved. Skinner and Rankine defend a substantial Ruddian core; more cautious codicological readings distinguish the lost or hypothetical earlier source from Smart’s surviving compilation.

## Collin de Plancy and Louis Le Breton

De Plancy altered Beleth’s reception through prose; Le Breton altered it through image. Their cat musician is now more visually recognizable than the original mounted king even though it is much later.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers

Mathers prepared the translation and manuscript work behind the 1904 edition, added Hebrew forms and occult notes, and placed the *Goetia* within a Golden Dawn environment.

His Hebrew spellings are often transliterations rather than genuine etymologies. Mathers himself reportedly had reservations about parts of the Ruddian Hebrew apparatus.

## Aleister Crowley

Crowley published and framed the 1904 book, added an “Preliminary Invocation” derived from the *Bornless Ritual*, and later incorporated Goetic names into correspondence tables.

There is no need to infer that every ritual claim in the edition came from Crowley’s personal experience. His most important Beleth-specific legacy is editorial publication and the later *777* decan table.

## A. E. Waite

Waite summarized the Goetic hierarchy and reproduced seals in *The Book of Ceremonial Magic*. His treatment contributed to the standard occult encyclopedia image of Beleth but added little primary information.

## Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s work is essential because it separates the manuscript text from the 1904 editorial layer, collates spellings, and places the *Lemegeton* beside Weyer, Scot, the *Offices of Spirits*, and related texts.

## Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their 2007 ***Goetia of Dr Rudd*** published the angel–demon counterseal scheme in detail and revived the Iezalel–Beleth pairing in modern practice.

## Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s ***Pandemonium*** compares Weyer, Scot, the *Livre des Esperitz*, Folger material, and later spirit catalogues from a practitioner-historical perspective.

His Bileth/Lilith connections are best categorized as **comparative practitioner reconstruction**, not settled academic etymology.

## Lon Milo DuQuette

DuQuette, with Christopher S. Hyatt, produced ***Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia*** and has promoted a psychological and ceremonial reading of the spirits. His broader work combines tarot, Enochian, Thelemic, and Goetic structures.

**[Negative finding]** No reliable published evidence was located showing a uniquely documented personal Beleth operation by DuQuette. He belongs in the reception history of the *Goetia* generally.

## Poke Runyon

Runyon was influential in the late-twentieth-century revival of ceremonial Goetic evocation, especially dramatic temple work and dark-mirror techniques.

**[Negative finding]** A securely documented Beleth-specific working was not located in the reviewed sources. His relevance is to modern Goetic method rather than Beleth’s historical biography.

## Frater Achad

**[Negative finding]** No substantial Beleth-specific work by Frater Achad was located. His presence in some modern lists appears to result from broad association with Crowleyan qabalah rather than a documented study of Beleth.

## Owen Davies, Jan Veenstra, Julien Véronèse, and Jean-Patrice Boudet

These scholars help establish the larger context:

- Davies: the social and publishing history of grimoires.
- Veenstra: learned magic, spirits, and medieval intellectual contexts.
- Véronèse and Boudet: manuscript necromancy and the *Livre des Esperitz*.
- Boudet: the *Offices of Spirits* tradition, Bileth materials, and corrections to overconfident name identifications.

## Early literature

### Pulci’s *Morgante*

**[Documented literary reception]** **Bilette** appears in the late-fifteenth-century poem’s demon nomenclature. This is the earliest located literary use but contains no pale horse or love office.

### Milton

**[Negative finding]** John Milton uses **Belial** extensively in *Paradise Lost* but no securely identified Beleth. Belial must not be silently corrected to Bileth.

**[Negative finding]** Beleth does not appear as a named figure in the *Divine Comedy*. Dantean demons such as Malacoda belong to a different literary catalogue.

## Film

### *Byleth: Il demone dell’incesto* (1972)

**[Modern invention]** Leopoldo Savona’s Italian horror film uses **Byleth** in the title and makes the name an “incest demon.” Incest is not one of Byleth’s offices in Weyer, Scot, or the *Goetia*. The film appears to transform the broad love or desire function into a sensational horror premise.

## Television

### *Marianne* (2019)

**[Documented modern adaptation]** The Netflix series states that the witch Marianne married **Beleth, king of cats**, before the Devil. “King of cats” is not a Goetic title; it almost certainly reflects de Plancy’s feline iconography.

### *Helluva Boss*

**[Negative finding]** The series uses “Ars Goetia” as a dynastic category and adapts several Goetic names. No canonical on-screen Beleth was verified in the material reviewed. Fan characters and speculative lists should not be treated as attestations.

## Comics

### *Hellboy* and the Mignolaverse

**[Modern adaptation; secondary documentation]** A Beleth appears in Mignolaverse reference material among occult powers and demonic figures. The detailed relationships attributed to this character—including later genealogies involving other Goetic names—belong to the comic continuity, not the grimoire tradition. Exact claims should be cited to individual issues rather than to a fan encyclopedia alone.

## Role-playing and tabletop games

### *In Nomine*

**[Modern invention]** *In Nomine* material uses Beleth as a female demonic power associated with nightmares or horror and places her in a mythic relationship with Blandine. This sex, rank, and office are specific to the game universe.

### Dungeons & Dragons

**[Modern invention]** D&D sources have used **Bileth** within the politics of the Nine Hells. Its court office, game statistics, powers, and planar relationships are game design, not material from Weyer.

## Video games

### *Dragon Quest*

**[Modern adaptation]** **Beleth** is used as a recurring monster name. The visual and combat design do not reproduce the mounted king and should be treated as nominal borrowing.

### *Fire Emblem: Three Houses*

**[Probable allusion; not officially established etymology]** The protagonist is named **Byleth**. The game also uses Goetic names such as Sitri, making an allusion highly plausible. Nevertheless, no reviewed official statement explicitly says that the protagonist was named for Weyer’s demon. The character’s personality, sex options, powers, and narrative are entirely modern.

### *Shin Megami Tensei* and *Persona*

**[Negative finding]** These series include **Bael, Belial, Berith**, and many other grimoire spirits, but no securely verified canonical demon entry for **Beleth/Bileth/Byleth** was located. Online lists frequently conflate the names.

## The *Bartimaeus* novels

**[Negative finding]** No reliable occurrence of Beleth was found. The books employ many historical and invented spirit names, which likely generated the association.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. “Beleth first appears in 1577”

**Verdict: false if it refers to the name; partly true if it refers to the complete familiar profile.**

- **Name:** Bileth occurs in medieval material.
- **Kingship:** present before Weyer, including Coxe MS 25.
- **Pale horse + music + love + hazel wand complex:** first securely located in Weyer’s printed entry.
- **Seal:** first appears in the *Lemegeton* manuscript tradition.

## 2. “Weyer published the Pseudomonarchia in 1563”

**Verdict: misleading.**

- *De praestigiis daemonum*: first edition 1563.
- *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* appendix: fifth edition, 1577.

## 3. “Beleth is an ancient form of Baalat/Astarte”

**Verdict: disputed and unproved.**

The linguistic resemblance exists, and Baalat was a real West Semitic title and divine designation. No documentary transmission connects it to medieval Bileth.

## 4. “Beleth is Lilith”

**Verdict: disputed modern reconstruction.**

Boudet cautions that the medieval catalogues distinguish them. Weyer and the *Goetia* use masculine grammar and the title king. Female or queen versions are modern developments.

## 5. “Ham learned mathematics from Beleth”

**Verdict: distortion.**

The legend says Ham invoked Bileth and composed an art and book in his name, known to *mathematici*. It does not say Beleth taught modern mathematics.

## 6. “Beleth breathes fire”

**Verdict: later editorial gloss.**

The rationale appears in Mathers–Crowley’s note about the ring, not in the early Beleth entry.

## 7. “Beleth is king of cats”

**Verdict: nineteenth-century and later reception.**

The old texts give a horse and musical procession. De Plancy introduces musical cats; later fiction turns that image into feline kingship.

## 8. “Beleth has eighty legions”

**Verdict: de Plancy variant or error.**

Weyer, Scot, and the *Goetia* say **85**. De Plancy says **80**.

## 9. “Beleth’s zodiac sign is Gemini”

**Verdict: true only within one later correspondence system.**

- Iezalel ordinal quinance: first five degrees Gemini.
- Crowley’s *777* Goetic decan: first ten degrees Leo.

Neither is stated in Weyer.

## 10. “Beleth’s tarot card is the Eight of Swords”

**Verdict: true under the Rudd–Shem five-degree overlay, not Crowley’s explicit Goetic-decan table.**

Crowley’s day-demon placement makes Beleth Five of Wands.

## 11. “Beleth’s planet is Jupiter/Saturn”

**Verdict: correspondence-layer confusion.**

- Sun: King.
- Jupiter: Eight of Swords/Gemini decan.
- Saturn: Five of Wands/Leo decan.

## 12. “There is one authentic seal”

**Verdict: unsupported.**

The early printed catalogues have none. Later manuscripts transmit graphical variants, and Mathers–Crowley print two. Without a stemmatic study of the drawings, none can simply be declared the primordial seal.

## 13. “The Goetia always had seventy-two spirits”

**Verdict: unlikely.**

The direct printed ancestor has sixty-nine. The compiler added four and omitted one, producing seventy-two, probably under the influence of the symbolically important number.

## 14. “Iezalel has always ruled Beleth”

**Verdict: false as a historical claim.**

Their pairing is documented in the Rudd/Smart recension. It is not present in Weyer or Scot and rests on shared number 13.

## 15. “Thomas Rudd wrote Harley 6483”

**Verdict: too simple.**

Peter Smart copied the surviving manuscript. Its relationship to Thomas Rudd’s earlier papers is plausible and defended by Skinner and Rankine, but the surviving codex is not a straightforward Rudd autograph.

## 16. “The psalm is Psalm 97:6”

**Verdict: inherited citation problem.**

The actual words correspond to Psalm 98:4 in modern numbering or Vulgate Psalm 97:4. “Verse 6” appears to be an error repeated in occult literature.

## 17. “Beleth brings romantic love”

**Verdict: a softened modern paraphrase.**

The old office is coercive and purposive: Beleth causes extreme or “all possible” love between men and women until the operator’s will is fulfilled. It does not discuss mutuality, compatibility, healing, or ethical consent. Later authors may reinterpret the office as reconciliation, attraction, or relationship work, partly under Iezalel’s influence.

## 18. “Beleth teaches sciences, languages, or invisibility”

**Verdict: witness-dependent.**

The Weyer/*Goetia* Beleth does not teach sciences or languages and does not confer invisibility. Folger V.b.26 gives a Bileth liberal arts, consecration, and invisibility offices. These may represent a different recension or a reassignment among neighboring spirits.

## Open questions

1. **What is the name’s language of origin?**  
   The medieval evidence moves the problem earlier than Weyer but does not solve it.

2. **Are *Bileth*, *Bilar*, and *Biled* forms of one transmitted name?**  
   The manuscript relationship is strong enough for investigation, but the direction of borrowing remains unclear.

3. **How did the *Liber Iuratus* subordinate become a supreme king?**  
   Coxe MS 25 proves that kingship predated Weyer, but the intervening textual stages are incomplete.

4. **Where did the pale horse, musical procession, and love office first converge?**  
   Weyer is the earliest securely examined complete witness, but he almost certainly used a manuscript source.

5. **Where did Weyer obtain the Ham legend?**  
   It may reflect a lost prologue or an etiological explanation for a *Book of Bileth*.

6. **Did Pulci use a magical manuscript?**  
   The name Bilette suggests contact with necromantic nomenclature but does not identify the source.

7. **What is the stemma of Beleth’s seal?**  
   A reliable answer requires direct comparison of every manuscript drawing, including stroke order, corrections, and codicological dating.

8. **Why did de Plancy introduce cats?**  
   No earlier source located here explains them.

9. **Why did de Plancy change 85 legions to 80?**  
   It could be simplification, a compositor’s error, or dependence on another edition.

10. **How much of Harley 6483 is authentically Ruddian?**  
    Smart’s copying is documented; the earlier provenance remains partly reconstructive.

11. **Was the Goetia expanded to seventy-two specifically to mirror the Shem angels?**  
    The numerological motive is persuasive, but no compiler’s statement survives.

12. **When was Beleth first explicitly paired with the Eight of Swords?**  
    The assignment follows automatically once the ordinal Rudd pairing is combined with Golden Dawn quinances, but its first printed table deserves further bibliographic tracing.
