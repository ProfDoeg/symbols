# Eligos: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **Documented text** — directly present in a cited manuscript, early print, or critical edition.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference from textual comparison or historical scholarship.
- **Tradition** — an attribution transmitted within magical or religious literature, without claiming empirical truth.
- **Esoteric correspondence** — a deliberately constructed occult association.
- **Disputed** — a reading or historical claim for which authorities or witnesses disagree.
- **Legend** — supernatural narrative presented as lore.
- **Modern invention** — an attribution first evident in recent occultism, art, fiction, or games.

Because of quotation-length restrictions, the three long public-domain descriptions requested below are represented by exact excerpts, followed by complete content-preserving summaries and links to the full texts.

## The name and its origins

### 1. No known ancient god behind Eligos

**Finding — scholarly reconstruction:** Unlike Bael/Baal, Astaroth/Astarte, or Amon/Amun, Eligos has no demonstrated derivation from an ancient god, hero, geographical name, Greek divine name, or biblical demon.

No pre-medieval Eligos, Eligor, Abigor, or convincing equivalent has been identified in:

- the *Testament of Solomon*;
- Greek or Roman mythological catalogues;
- the Hebrew Bible or rabbinic demonology;
- Mesopotamian god lists;
- the standard medieval traditions surrounding Asmodeus/Ashmedai, Belial, or Astaroth.

Claims that Eligos derives from Hebrew *El* plus an alleged word meaning “counsellor,” from Humbaba/Huwawa, or from a Mesopotamian war god are **modern inventions or disputed speculation**. They are unsupported by a linguistic chain connecting the ancient name to the medieval spellings.

A modern suggestion connecting **Abigor** with Latin *abigere* or *abigo*, “drive away,” is superficially possible but **unsubstantiated**. No early commentator supplies that derivation, and the earliest form *Alugor* does not naturally require it.

The best evidence points instead to a name altered repeatedly in manuscript transmission:

> **Alugor → Abugor / Algor / Allogor → Eligor alias Abigor → Eligos**

That sequence is a **scholarly reconstruction**, not a proven etymology. It explains the witnesses economically without inventing an ancient cult.

### 2. The earliest extant attestation: Alugor

**Documented text, mid-fifteenth century:** Munich, Bayerische Staatsbibliothek, CLM 849, the manuscript commonly called the Munich Handbook of Necromancy, has **Alugor** as a “great duke.”

Exact excerpt:

> “Alugor magnus dux est. Apparet in similitudine militis pulcherrimi, ferens lanceam, vexillum, et sceptrum.”

In Richard Kieckhefer’s edition, Alugor:

- appears as an exceedingly handsome soldier;
- carries a lance, banner, and sceptre;
- gives truthful answers concerning hidden matters and duels;
- explains how conflicts will happen;
- provides or directs soldiers;
- grants favour with kings, marquises, and knights;
- commands **50 legions**.

The manuscript’s Latin contains corrupt or uncertain readings around *quarum/quando/quomodo*. Kieckhefer accordingly treats part of the syntax cautiously. Nevertheless, identity with the later Eligor is strong: rank, appearance, objects, military office, patronage, and the consonantal outline of the name all coincide.

This corrects the often-repeated statement that Eligor first appears in Weyer in 1577. Weyer provides the first known printed attestation, not the first extant attestation.

### 3. Abugor in the *Livre des Esperitz*

**Documented text:** Trinity College Cambridge MS O.8.29, fol. 181r, has **Abugor**, number 18 in that catalogue:

> “Abugor est ung grant duc qui appert en semblance d’un beau chevalier…”

The complete entry says that Abugor:

- is a great duke;
- appears like a handsome knight;
- gives true answers to questions;
- answers concerning things hidden in the earth;
- gives favour with kings and other lords;
- has **27 legions**.

**Disputed dating:** The manuscript has often been called fifteenth-century, including in catalogue-derived online transcriptions. Jean-Patrice Boudet’s codicological treatment places the surviving copy in the **middle of the sixteenth century**, though the material it transmits may be older. It therefore should not automatically displace CLM 849 as the earliest physical witness.

Boudet finds that 35 spirits in the *Livre des Esperitz* can be matched with Weyer’s list by name, description, or both. This supports a common catalogue family; it does not prove that Weyer copied this exact Trinity manuscript.

### 4. Algor and Allogor in the *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* family

**Documented text:** Several sixteenth- and seventeenth-century office-of-spirits witnesses preserve further forms:

- **Algor** — Coxe 25;
- **Algor** — SSM L.2.f.30;
- **Allogor** and elsewhere **Algor** — Folger MS V.b.26;
- **Algor** — British Library Sloane MS 3853;
- **Algor** — Additional MS 36674.

Sloane 3853 says, in part:

> “Algor is a gret duke & wyll apere in the lykness of a fayer knyght…”

It gives him a spear, truthful answers about doubts, knowledge of how matters may be brought to pass and what will happen, and **30 legions**.

These forms show that *-gor* was relatively stable while the opening vowel and medial consonants moved readily between *A-, Abu-, Alu-, Eli-*.

### 5. Weyer: Eligor alias Abigor

**Documented text, 1577:** Johann Weyer printed **Eligor, alias Abigor** as spirit 12 of the *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*. In the 1577 printing the entry follows Purson and stands around column 915 of the surrounding edition; electronic editions are more reliably cited as §12.

Exact Latin excerpt:

> “Eligor, aliàs Abigor, Dux magnus, apparet ut miles pulcherrimus, lanceam, vexillum & sceptrum portans.”

The remainder says that he answers fully concerning hidden things and wars, explains how soldiers ought to meet, knows the future, reconciles or obtains favour with all lords and soldiers/knights, and presides over sixty legions.

Weyer’s *Pseudomonarchia* was appended to the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum* in 1577. Weyer presented the catalogue as evidence of magicians’ fraud, delusion, and blasphemy. He nevertheless believed demons existed; his scepticism concerned supposed magical power and especially the prosecution of alleged witches. His polemical framing should therefore not be simplified into either “Weyer believed everything in the grimoire” or “Weyer was a modern secular sceptic.” [Weyer’s Latin and Scot parallel text](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

### 6. Scot: Eligor alias Abigor

**Documented text, 1584:** Reginald Scot translated Weyer in *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, Book XV, chapter 2. His text has **Eligor, alias Abigor**.

Exact excerpt:

> “Eligor, alias Abigor, is a great duke, and appeereth as a goodlie knight, carrieng a lance, an ensigne, and a scepter…”

Scot continues that the spirit answers fully about hidden things and wars, explains how soldiers should meet, knows future things, procures the favour of lords and knights, and governs sixty legions of devils.

“Goodly” means fine-looking or handsome, not morally good. Scot’s wording became especially influential because the English Goetia compiler appears to have used Scot closely.

Scot’s purpose, like Weyer’s, was substantially sceptical and anti-persecution. Printing ritual and demonological material did not mean that he endorsed it.

### 7. The Goetia manuscripts

The principal readings are:

| Witness | Approximate date | Reading |
|---|---:|---|
| Sloane MS 3825 | on or after 1640/41 | **Eligor** |
| Sloane MS 3648 | later seventeenth century | **Eligos** |
| Sloane MS 2731 | dated 1686/87 | **Eligor**, with a marginal alternative **Eligos** |
| Harley MS 6483 | copied 1712/13 | **Eligos** |
| Folger V.b.26 | early office tradition | **Allogor / Algor** |
| Northwestern MS 65 | later witness | **Eligos or Elogor** |
| Mathers–Crowley | 1904 | **Eligos** |
| Waite | 1911 tradition | **Eligor** |
| Peterson | 2001 critical text | **Eligor**, while documenting **Eligos** |

Joseph Peterson regards Sloane 3825 as generally the strongest Lemegeton witness when compared with identifiable sources. He specifically lists **Eligos** among Sloane 3648’s altered or erroneous spellings. Thus **Eligor** is probably closer to the immediate Weyer–Scot exemplar, while **Eligos** became canonical through Mathers and Crowley. [Peterson’s manuscript survey](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm)

### 8. Why “Eligos” won

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The terminal *-s* probably began as a copying or reading variation. No doctrinal reason for changing *Eligor* to *Eligos* is stated in the manuscripts. Once Mathers selected **Eligos**, Crowley’s 1904 printing gave that form wide circulation. Modern games and popular demon dictionaries overwhelmingly followed it.

This is a good example of a possible scribal error becoming the best-known name without thereby becoming historically “false.” It is the conventional name of a major reception history, even if *Eligor* stands closer to Weyer and Scot.

## Number, order, and the 69/72 problem

### Weyer versus the Goetia

- **Weyer:** Eligor is number **12** in a list conventionally counted as 69.
- **Scot:** His translation omits Pruflas, so his printed sequence effectively has 68 entries, although it transmits Weyer’s numbering context.
- **Goetia:** Eligor/Eligos is number **15** of 72.

The arithmetic is:

> Weyer’s 69 − Pruflas + Vassago + Seere + Dantalion + Andromalius = 72.

The four Goetia spirits not in Weyer are:

1. **Vassago**, inserted as number 3;
2. **Seere**, number 70;
3. **Dantalion**, number 71;
4. **Andromalius**, number 72.

The Goetia omits Weyer’s **Pruflas/Bufas**, number 4. Vassago’s insertion near the beginning shifts most early correspondences. Eligor, Weyer 12, consequently becomes Goetia 15.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The total 72 was probably attractive because of established magical uses of the number: the 72-fold divine Name, zodiacal divisions, and traditions about Solomon’s demons. That does not prove the compiler began by pairing every demon with a Shem angel. The explicit protective angel-demon pairing is clearest in Rudd’s later recension.

### The sceptre/serpent problem

This is the most important substantive variant.

- **CLM 849:** *sceptrum*.
- **Weyer:** *sceptrum*.
- **Scot:** “scepter.”
- **Goetia manuscripts:** “serpent.”
- **Mathers–Crowley:** “Serpent.”
- **Rudd recension:** serpent.
- **de Plancy:** sceptre.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** “Serpent” is most likely a corruption or substitution within the English Goetia transmission. The independent agreement of CLM 849 and Weyer strongly supports **sceptre** as the earlier reading. Yet the serpent became canonical Goetia iconography, so it is not appropriate simply to “correct” it out of later reception.

### The Sloane 3825 Goetia

Exact excerpt:

> “The 15th spirit is called Eligor, a great duke, he appeareth in the form of a goodly knight…”

The full entry says he carries a lance, ensign, and serpent; discovers hidden things; knows things to come; answers concerning wars and how soldiers will or shall meet; causes the love of lords and great persons; commands sixty legions; and must be compelled by display or wearing of his seal.

The last ritual sentence varies. Sloane 3825 contains the familiar injunction that the seal be worn or the spirit will not appear or obey. Sloane 3648 omits portions of such formulae.

### Mathers–Crowley, 1904

On page 27:

> “The Fifteenth Spirit in Order is Eligos, a Great Duke, and appeareth in the form of a goodly Knight…”

The rest closely follows the manuscript account: lance, ensign, serpent; hidden things; future events; wars and soldiers’ encounters; love of lords and great persons; sixty legions. [Full 1904 entry](https://www.sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks05.htm)

The punctuation and capitalization are editorial. The content is not an independent revelation by Crowley.

### Peterson, 2001

Peterson prints **Eligor** on page 14 and records the manuscript alternatives in footnotes:

- Weyer and Scot: *Eligor alias Abigor*;
- Sloane 3825: *Eligor*;
- Sloane 3648, Harley 6483, and Crowley: *Eligos*;
- Sloane 2731: *Eligor*, with “in another copy Eligos” in the margin.

Peterson’s edition is especially important because it separates manuscript evidence from Mathers–Crowley’s editorial legacy. [Peterson’s edition](https://jamesparsonslakebucket.s3-us-west-1.amazonaws.com/text/Peterson_Joseph_H_The_Lesser_Key_of_Solomon.pdf)

### What Eligos does not do

The following common Goetia offices are **not assigned to Eligos** in the early sources:

- teaching languages;
- teaching liberal sciences;
- locating ordinary treasure as a distinct office;
- providing familiars;
- making people invisible;
- transmuting metals;
- reconciling friends and enemies;
- healing illness;
- transporting people from place to place.

The *Livre des Esperitz* does mention things concealed in the earth, but that should not automatically be expanded into a general treasure-finding office.

### “Love of lords”

**Documented semantic development:**

- CLM 849: *gracia* or favour with rulers and knights;
- French *Livre*: *bone grace* with kings and lords;
- Weyer: *gratiam*;
- Scot: “favour”;
- Goetia: “Love of Lords and Great Persons.”

The early evidence favours **patronage, goodwill, or political/military esteem**, not romantic love. Modern claims that Eligos is primarily a love demon are a semantic extrapolation from the word “love” in the English Goetia.

### Second form

There is **no documented second form** for Eligos and no direction that the magician demand a transformation. Some Goetia spirits first appear monstrously and are ordered into human form; Eligos appears as a knight from the outset.

## The seal

### Description

**Documented drawing:** The usual Goetia seal is a predominantly horizontal character. Its recognizable elements are:

- a low horizontal stem;
- rounded or circular terminals;
- interlocking or returning loops toward the left;
- a diagonal ascent toward the upper right;
- an angular triangular or spearhead-like termination.

No manuscript supplies this verbal description. The authoritative object is the drawing itself.

### Variants

- Sloane 3825 and 2731 preserve broadly similar but hand-drawn forms.
- Harley 6483 supplies the demon’s seal as one half of Rudd’s double-lamen arrangement.
- Mathers regularizes the geometry into cleaner printed lines.
- Modern vector versions often make circles perfectly round, straighten the baseline, equalize loops, and sharpen the right-hand angle. Those are graphic normalizations, not new manuscript evidence.
- Orientation can be reversed by reproduction, tracing, or page layout. A mirrored online seal should not automatically be treated as a separate historical sigil.

Weyer, Scot, CLM 849, and the *Livre des Esperitz* do **not** give Eligor a personal seal. The seal therefore enters the recoverable record with the seventeenth-century Goetia tradition.

### Metal and wearing

The Goetia’s general instructions associate dukes with **Venus** and prescribe their seals in **copper**. The seal is to be worn as a lamen on the breast during the operation.

This is a rank correspondence, not a statement that Eligos was originally a Venus deity. His military office can feel more Martian to modern practitioners, and some consequently reclassify him as Mars or an earl. That is **modern revision**, contradicted by the stable textual title *dux magnus*.

## The angel: Hariel

## Identification

| Attribute | Hariel |
|---|---|
| Number | 15 of 72 |
| Hebrew triad | הרי — HRI |
| Expanded name | הריאל — Hariel/Hari’el |
| Common meaning | “God the Creator” |
| Goetia spirit constrained | Eligos/Eligor |
| Psalm | Vulgate 93:22; modern Psalm 94:22 |
| Zodiac | 10°–15° Gemini |
| Calendar approximation | Commonly 1–5 June |
| Later choir | Cherubim |
| Later choir ruler | Raziel or Jophiel, depending system |
| Later office | Sciences and arts; useful discoveries; religious and moral purification |

### Meaning and philology

**Tradition:** Hariel is usually glossed *Deus Creator*, “God the Creator.”

**Philological caution:** The three-letter sequence HRI is produced mechanically from Exodus, not extracted from a Hebrew word that unambiguously means “creator.” The suffix *-el* means “God,” but “God the Creator” is an occult interpretive gloss. Alternatives such as “comforting God,” “saving God,” “conceiving God,” or “God my help” belong to later interpretive systems and should not be presented as settled Hebrew etymology.

### Deriving the 72 names

**Documented religious-magical tradition:** Exodus 14:19–21 consists, under the relevant Hebrew orthography, of three consecutive verses of 72 letters each. In the classic construction:

1. verse 19 is written in its normal right-to-left direction;
2. verse 20 is reversed;
3. verse 21 is written in the first direction;
4. the three vertically corresponding letters form 72 triplets.

This boustrophedon arrangement yields the fifteenth triad **HRI**. Christian and practical Kabbalists appended endings such as **-el** and **-yah**, turning triplets into angelic names.

“Shem ha-Mephorash” originally meant the explicit or distinctly pronounced divine Name, especially the Tetragrammaton. Its near-exclusive popular identification with these 72 angels is later than the earliest rabbinic use.

### Historical transmission

#### Jewish sources and *Sefer Raziel*

**Tradition with documented medieval witnesses:** Traditions of a 72-letter or 72-part divine Name occur in medieval Jewish literature, and material associated with *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* helped circulate divine-name diagrams, angelology, and practical Kabbalah.

The surviving *Sefer Raziel* is composite, not a single book dictated to Adam by an angel. The story that Raziel gave it to Adam is **legend**; the medieval textual compilation is **documented history**.

The exact Rudd-style table—72 expanded angel names directly opposing 72 Goetia demons—is not an ancient feature of *Sefer Raziel*.

#### Renaissance Christian Kabbalah

**Documented transmission:** Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517), later Blaise de Vigenère, and other Christian Kabbalists circulated the 72 triplets as angel names. Christian writers integrated Jewish letter techniques into a Christological and angel-magical framework.

#### Kircher

Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–54) placed the 72 names within his enormous comparative scheme of Kabbalah, angelology, languages, astrology, and ancient theology. Kircher was influential but is not a reliable guide to ancient Egyptian religion or historical Hebrew philology. His system is **early-modern esoteric synthesis**.

#### Rudd

Harley MS 6483 contains the recension published as *The Goetia of Dr Rudd*. Each demon is paired numerically with a Shem angel. Hariel consequently opposes the fifteenth spirit, Eligos.

The protective mechanism is physical and graphic:

- the demon’s character is placed on one face or side;
- the corresponding angelic seal and Psalm are placed in relation to it;
- the angelic side is worn toward the operator’s heart;
- the angel’s divine authority is understood to check or constrain the demon.

Hariel’s verse in the Rudd material is:

> “Et factus est mihi Dominus in refugium et Deus meus in adjutorium spei mei…”

Rudd’s accompanying English renders it as the Lord being a refuge and God the aid of the operator’s hope. The Latin is Vulgate Psalm 93:22. In most modern English Bibles it is Psalm 94:22:

> “But the LORD is my defence; and my God is the rock of my refuge.”

**Disputed authorship and date:** Harley 6483 was copied in 1712–13 by Peter Smart. Stephen Skinner and David Rankine argue that Smart copied an earlier working manuscript belonging to Thomas Rudd, probably from about 1655–56. The attribution is plausible but not proved by an autograph title page. Peterson emphasizes the lateness and derivative character of Harley 6483. “Rudd’s scheme” is therefore useful shorthand, not absolute proof that every component was personally devised by Rudd.

#### Lenain, 1823

Lazare Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* systematized the 72 good genii for nineteenth-century French occultism. Hariel’s office concerns sciences, arts, useful discoveries and new methods. Lenain says the person born under the influence will seek the society of good people, possess religious sentiments, and be distinguished by purity of conduct.

The contrary genius governs impiety, religious schisms, dangerous sects, and religious war. These moralized “good genius/contrary genius” descriptions belong to Lenain’s Christian-esoteric worldview, not to Exodus itself. [Lenain scan](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_ms05365)

#### Papus

Papus—Gérard Encausse—reproduced and reorganized the French Kabbalistic angel tradition in works including *La Cabbale* (1903). His role was largely transmissive and synthetic. He helped carry Lenain-type correspondences into Martinist and popular occult circles. [Papus scan](https://www.cercle-papus.com/ouvrages/papus-la-cabbale.pdf)

#### Golden Dawn and Crowley

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn integrated:

- the 72-fold Name;
- zodiacal decans;
- planetary rulers;
- Tarot pips;
- Hebrew angelology;
- ceremonial magical timing.

Crowley inherited and republished much of this correspondence tradition. However, neither the Golden Dawn nor *777* should be projected backward as if the medieval Alugor already possessed a Gemini quinance and Tarot card.

#### Ambelain

Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) continued the French operative-Kabbalistic line, supplying angelic hierarchies, ritual uses, planetary organization, and invocatory material. His assignments are **twentieth-century esoteric systematization**, even when built from older names. [Ambelain scan](https://www.ordremartiniste.ca/docs-omc/Robert%20Ambelain/La%20Kabbale%20Pratique.pdf)

#### Haziel

“Haziel,” the pen name of François Bernard-Termès, popularized the 72 guardian angels in late-twentieth-century French and translated publications. In this literature Hariel commonly rules:

- 10°–15° Gemini;
- approximately 1–5 June;
- a daily twenty-minute interval around the fifteenth division of the day;
- several additional scattered “emotional” or “intellectual” regency days.

These multiple guardian-angel calendars are **modern elaborations**. They should not be attributed to Rudd or directly to Jewish Kabbalah.

### Hariel’s quinance and calendar

Dividing 360° by 72 gives 5°. In the now-common sequential zodiacal system:

- angel 1 begins at 0° Aries;
- angel 13 begins at 0° Gemini;
- angel 15 therefore occupies **10°–15° Gemini**;
- angel 16 occupies 15°–20° Gemini.

The degree span is mathematically stable once that starting convention is chosen. Calendar dates vary because authors use different ephemerides, inclusive endpoints, and simplified five-day calendars. **1–5 June** is the most common modern assignment, but it is not a timeless fixed date.

### Why the Tarot attributions conflict

Crowley’s *777*, columns CLIX–CLX, places:

> “15 ♀ Eligos” at row 19, with “a knight with a lance and banner, with a serpent.”

Row 19 corresponds to **Leo**. Because Eligos appears in the cadent/day decan column, the resulting decan is **20°–30° Leo**, whose Golden Dawn Tarot card is the **Seven of Wands, Mars in Leo**. [Crowley’s tables](https://publicism.info/magic/full/thelema/777/index.html)

By contrast, assigning all 72 demons sequentially to the 72 five-degree angelic segments makes number 15 occupy **10°–15° Gemini**. That yields the **Nine of Swords, Mars in Gemini**, shared at the decan level with number 16.

Thus:

- **Seven of Wands** is the defensible answer to “What does Crowley’s *777* assign to Eligos?”
- **Nine of Swords** is the defensible answer to “What pip follows if Eligos is merged with Hariel’s sequential quinance?”

Calling the latter “Crowley’s attribution” is incorrect.

### Astrological timing

The core Goetia gives practical rank windows:

- kings: certain daytime intervals;
- dukes: sunrise to noon in clear weather;
- marquises: afternoon and night;
- presidents: according to their prescribed planetary conditions;
- other ranks according to the general observations.

It also recommends a waxing moon and lists even-numbered lunar days up to the fourteenth as favourable.

For Eligos, the most textually conservative timing is therefore:

- morning, sunrise to noon;
- clear weather;
- favourable waxing-moon day;
- copper seal.

Friday and the hour of Venus are coherent later additions based on the rank planet. A Gemini election, Nine of Swords election, or 10°–15° Gemini Sun is a separate Shem/zodiacal overlay. A 20°–30° Leo election follows Crowley’s *777* instead. Combining all of these without explanation creates a timing system no single historical source actually gives.

## Iconography

## Manuscript seal tradition

The earliest Alugor, Abugor, and Algor catalogue entries have no portrait and no personal seal. Their iconography is entirely verbal: the beautiful armed knight, lance, banner, and sceptre.

The Goetia seal added a non-figurative identity. Because handwritten copies vary slightly, it is better to cite a specific manuscript or edition than to speak of a metaphysically perfect original seal.

## Collin de Plancy

### First edition, 1818

Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* first appeared in 1818. The first edition belongs to his earlier, more rationalist and anti-clerical period and was not the famous Le Breton illustrated edition.

The 1818 digital text does not expose a securely searchable separate “Abigor” entry in the available transcription. Consequently, the safe finding is:

- the work dates from 1818;
- the demonstrable, easily verifiable Abigor entry and portrait discussed below are from the revised **1863 sixth edition**;
- one should not date Le Breton’s image to 1818.

### Sixth edition, 1863

On page 3 the entry begins:

> “Abigor, démon d’un ordre supérieur, grand-duc dans la monarchie infernale.”

De Plancy says that sixty legions march under Abigor’s orders; that he appears as a beautiful horseman carrying a lance, standard, or sceptre; that he answers skilfully concerning secrets of war, knows the future, and teaches commanders how to become loved by their soldiers. He cites Weyer. The separate entry “Éligor” merely redirects the reader to Abigor. [1863 Abigor entry](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.%2C_1863/Abigor)

Notable alterations include:

- “knight” becomes explicitly a mounted *cavalier*;
- “favour of lords and knights” becomes commanders winning soldiers’ affection;
- the earlier sceptre is retained instead of the Goetia serpent.

### Louis Le Breton’s portrait

**Documented art, 1863:** The portrait shows an armoured knight in profile, mounted on a fantastical horse-like creature. The mount is gaunt or partly skeletal in appearance and winged; the rider carries a long lance with a pennon or standard.

The mount is **Le Breton’s visual invention**, not something described by CLM 849, the *Livre des Esperitz*, Weyer, Scot, or the Goetia. Modern prose describing Abigor’s canonical “winged skeletal steed” has generally converted the engraving into supposed older demonological lore.

The illustration is normally credited to Louis Le Breton; published wood engravings were produced through the edition’s engraving process, sometimes associated with M. Jarrault. [Le Breton image catalogue](https://oraedes.fr/Medias/Dictionnaire-infernal), [Wikimedia collection](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Drawings_of_demons_from_Dictionnaire_Infernal,_6th_edition)

## Modern art

Modern depictions tend to choose among three inherited models:

1. **The early knight:** human, handsome, carrying lance, banner, and sceptre.
2. **The Goetia knight:** carrying lance, banner, and serpent.
3. **The Le Breton horseman:** armoured rider on a winged, skeletal monster.

Game art frequently amplifies the third model into a heavy cavalry demon, mechanical knight, chariot warrior, or centaur.

Modern sigil art often surrounds the Goetia character with copper, green, roses, the Venus symbol, Gemini imagery, or Hariel’s Hebrew letters. Those additions are modern correspondence art, not features of the manuscript seal.

## People and works

### Johann Weyer

Weyer’s 1577 *Pseudomonarchia* gave Eligor/Abigor his first known printed appearance. His description closely continues the late-medieval office-of-spirits tradition. Weyer’s larger purpose was to expose magical pretensions and oppose the judicial treatment of alleged witches, not to publish a practitioner’s handbook without criticism.

### Reginald Scot

Scot’s 1584 *Discoverie of Witchcraft* translated the catalogue into English. His “goodlie knight” wording became the immediate literary ancestor of the Goetia entry. Scot likewise wrote against witchcraft prosecutions and credulous demonological claims.

### The anonymous Lemegeton compiler

The seventeenth-century compiler or compilers:

- drew heavily on Scot;
- expanded the catalogue to 72;
- supplied individual seals;
- embedded the spirits within a larger ritual structure;
- changed Eligor’s sceptre into a serpent;
- converted “favour” into “love.”

The compiler’s identity is unknown.

### Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

Thomas Rudd is traditionally associated with the angel-demon counterpoise and with material connecting Solomonic magic to Dee’s angelic apparatus. Harley 6483 itself is Peter Smart’s 1712–13 copy. The precise boundary between Rudd’s original work, earlier sources, and Smart’s transcription remains disputed.

### Collin de Plancy and Louis Le Breton

De Plancy transmitted Weyer’s **Abigor** into nineteenth-century demonography. Le Breton supplied the mounted portrait that now shapes popular visualization more strongly than any manuscript text.

### Lazare Lenain

Lenain did not add new information about the demon. His importance lies in the parallel history of Hariel and the 72 good genii: zodiac, moral character, offices, Psalmic invocation, and guardian-angel influence.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Aleister Crowley

Mathers prepared the English Goetia edition from manuscripts; Crowley edited and published it in 1904, added his “Preliminary Invocation” material, and later incorporated the spirits into *777*.

Crowley’s psychological statement that the spirits are “portions of the human brain” is an influential modern interpretation, not the metaphysics of Weyer, Rudd, or the anonymous Goetia compiler.

### Frater Achad

**Negative finding:** Frater Achad—Charles Stansfeld Jones—belongs to Crowley’s magical circle and wrote extensively on Qabalah. I found no primary evidence of a distinctive Eligos operation or teaching attributable to him. General association with Thelemic Qabalah should not be inflated into a specific Eligos history.

### Poke Runyon

Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* presents a modern Solomonic system using the 72 spirits, angelic protections, and dark-mirror techniques. It is relevant to modern Goetia practice as a whole. No well-documented, uniquely influential Eligos working by Runyon was located. [Runyon’s book](https://www.pokerunyon.com/books/the-book-of-solomons-magick-second-edition)

### Lon Milo DuQuette

DuQuette co-authored *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* and helped popularize a psychological, initiatory, and practical reading of all 72 spirits. Again, publication on the complete catalogue is documented; a famous personal Eligos operation is not.

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 edition is the central modern textual resource. It collates manuscripts, identifies Scot and other sources, preserves spelling variants, and prevents the 1904 text from being mistaken for an exact manuscript transcription.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their 2007 *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* published and interpreted Harley 6483 and related material. Its major contribution to this topic is the operational presentation of Hariel’s seal and Psalm as the angelic control over Eligos. Their reconstruction of a 1650s Rudd original is influential but remains an attribution, not an autograph certainty.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s *Encyclopaedia Goetica*, *Pandemonium*, and related writing emphasize the older spirit-catalogue families behind the Goetia. This historical orientation encourages attention to forms such as Alugor, Abugor, and Algor rather than treating 1904 as the beginning. Some of his broader claims about continuous “goetic” spirit religion are interpretive and are not identical with the narrower manuscript conclusions of Kieckhefer, Boudet, Peterson, or Véronèse.

### Scholars

- **Richard Kieckhefer** edited the Munich Handbook and thereby supplied the strongest early Alugor evidence.
- **Jean-Patrice Boudet** compared the Renaissance demonological catalogues with their medieval predecessors.
- **Julien Véronèse** has studied medieval ritual magic and the textual setting of the *Livre des Esperitz* and its conjurations.
- **Jan Veenstra** has worked on medieval learned magic, spirits, and the boundaries between demonology and courtly intellectual culture.
- **Owen Davies** places grimoires within their social history of circulation, readership, print, and magical practice.

## Grimoire ancestors and analogues

### The *Testament of Solomon*

**Documented late-antique analogue:** The *Testament of Solomon* describes Solomon interrogating and constraining named demons with angelic assistance. It is a major ancestor of the Solomonic narrative model.

**Negative finding:** It does not contain Eligos, Eligor, Abigor, Alugor, or a securely identifiable equivalent. Similarity of genre is not identity of spirit.

### The Munich Handbook

This is the earliest direct witness: **Alugor**, with the recognizable rank, knightly form, insignia, offices, and legions.

### *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

Weyer names a book of spirit offices among his sources. Surviving manuscripts and extracts belonging to this fluid family contain Algor/Allogor. It was not necessarily one fixed archetypal volume with a stable title and contents.

### *Livre des Esperitz*

The *Livre* supplies direct **Abugor** evidence. Boudet’s comparisons show that it and Weyer descend from a related catalogue environment. Whether the *Livre* directly copied an earlier Latin list, or Weyer and it drew from collateral versions, remains open.

### The *Book of Abramelin*

Abramelin contains extensive lists of subordinate spirits and an angel-guided magical framework.

**Negative finding:** No secure Eligos identification emerges merely from a vaguely similar spelling. Abramelin is contextual evidence for fifteenth-century spirit-list culture, not a direct Eligos witness.

### Trithemius and magical bibliographies

Trithemius’s *Antipalus maleficiorum* and related cataloguing demonstrate that books of spirit offices circulated in the early sixteenth century. A title reference is not proof that a particular lost copy contained Eligor or his seal.

### Enochian overlays

John Dee’s angelic magic originated independently of the basic Weyer catalogue. Rudd-associated manuscripts and Golden Dawn practice later combined Dee-derived tables, Solomonic ritual, Shem angels, and Goetia demons.

Calling Eligos “an Enochian spirit” is therefore **historically false**. At most, he became a component in later composite systems using Enochian materials.

### Dante and Milton

**Negative finding:** Neither Dante’s *Commedia* nor Milton’s *Paradise Lost* names Eligos, Eligor, Abigor, Alugor, or an identifiable equivalent.

Lists that place Eligos among “Milton’s fallen angels” or “Dante’s demons” are modern conflations of separate infernal catalogues.

### The Bartimaeus novels

**Negative finding:** Jonathan Stroud’s Bartimaeus sequence uses Solomonic lore, magicians, spirits, and the Ring of Solomon, but no securely attested Eligos character was located in the novels or reliable character indexes. Part Two” the Order of Taraka performs a ritual involving **Eligor**, described with titles concerning war, poisoners, pariahs, obscenity, and decay. ## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Is Eligos ancient?

**Conclusion:** No evidence presently establishes an ancient origin.

- Earliest extant direct cognate: **Alugor**, mid-fifteenth century.
- Earliest print: **Eligor alias Abigor**, 1577.
- Ancient deity identifications: unsupported.

The absence is meaningful because other Goetia names can be traced much earlier. Eligos presently belongs to late-medieval European spirit-catalogue culture.

### 2. Is Alugor really Eligos?

**Scholarly reconstruction, strong:** The name alone might be inconclusive, but the combination of:

- great-duke rank;
- beautiful soldier;
- lance;
- banner;
- sceptre;
- hidden knowledge;
- duels or warfare;
- favour of rulers and knights;
- legions;

makes accidental resemblance very unlikely.

### 3. How old is the *Livre des Esperitz*?

**Disputed:** Older literature and catalogue descriptions often call the Trinity manuscript fifteenth-century. Boudet dates the physical copy to the mid-sixteenth century. Its underlying material may nevertheless preserve an older catalogue.

The safest formulation is “a sixteenth-century manuscript transmitting late-medieval material,” while noting the dating dispute.

### 4. Eligor or Eligos?

**Textual finding:**

- *Eligor* is supported by Weyer, Scot, Sloane 3825, and Sloane 2731’s main text.
- *Eligos* is supported by Sloane 3648, Harley 6483, the Sloane 2731 marginal alternative, and Mathers–Crowley.
- Peterson prefers *Eligor* in his critical text.
- Modern reception prefers *Eligos*.

Neither form identifies a separate demon. Claims that Eligor and Eligos are distinct entities have no basis in the historical source chain.

### 5. Eligos versus Abigor

Weyer explicitly says *alias Abigor*. De Plancy uses Abigor as the main heading and Éligor as a cross-reference. Earlier *Abugor* strengthens the relationship.

Treating Abigor as a separate “higher aspect,” “war form,” or independent demon is **modern occult innovation**, unless a practitioner clearly labels it as personal revelation.

### 6. Sceptre or serpent?

The earlier reading is almost certainly **sceptre**. The Goetia’s **serpent** is nevertheless historically documented from the seventeenth century and generated its own iconographic tradition.

Possible causes include:

- copying from a difficult hand;
- memory substitution;
- deliberate alteration;
- contamination from the many serpentine Goetia descriptions.

No surviving source explains the change, so its exact cause remains open.

### 7. Sixty legions?

Sixty is stable from Weyer onward, but it is not the earliest number:

- CLM 849: 50;
- *Livre des Esperitz*: 27;
- Sloane 3853: 30;
- Weyer onward: 60.

The variation shows that legion totals were highly fluid catalogue data, not fixed historical statistics.

### 8. Venus or Mars?

**Text:** Duke.

**Goetia correspondence:** dukes belong to Venus and copper.

**Modern objection:** Eligos concerns war, so some writers assign Mars.

The objection is symbolically understandable but textually secondary. Goetia rank correspondence classifies by title, not by an analysis of office. Crowley’s Seven of Wands attribution actually combines Eligos with a Mars-in-Leo Tarot decan while retaining the Venus symbol for his ducal rank—an example of overlapping rather than mutually exclusive systems.

### 9. Nine of Swords or Seven of Wands?

- **Crowley’s documented Eligos placement:** Seven of Wands.
- **Sequential Hariel/Eligos quinance synthesis:** Nine of Swords.

Modern tables frequently fail to disclose that they have changed methods midway.

### 10. Does Hariel inherently oppose Eligos?

The HRI triplet and expanded angel name existed independently of the Goetia list. Their numerical pairing is a **Rudd-associated Christian magical construction**, not something stated in Exodus or early Jewish Kabbalah.

Within Rudd’s system the pairing is explicit and operational. Outside that system it is optional correspondence, not a universal theological fact.

### 11. “God the Creator”

This is a traditional angelic gloss, not a transparent lexical translation of HRI. Modern sites often present angel-name meanings with unjustified linguistic certainty.

### 12. The winged skeletal horse

The mount is absent from every early textual description examined. It enters securely through Le Breton’s 1863 portrait. Later books and websites reverse the relationship, claiming the engraving merely illustrated an ancient account of Abigor’s special steed. No such early account was located.

### 13. Romantic love, serpentine wisdom, strategy, shadow work

- Courtly favour and military foresight are historically grounded.
- Romantic attraction is a loose expansion of “love of lords.”
- “Serpentine wisdom” depends on the later serpent reading.
- Corporate strategy, legal victory, political influence, shadow integration, and “psychic warfare” are modern applications by analogy.
- Modern “enns,” including formulas circulated in demonolatry, are not found in Weyer, Scot, the Goetia manuscripts, or Rudd.

### 14. King Solomon’s imprisonment of Eligos

The Goetia’s frame presents its spirits as those constrained by Solomon, often with reference to a brass vessel. This is **grimoire legend**. No historical evidence shows an Israelite king cataloguing or conjuring Eligos.

## Open questions

1. **The name’s linguistic origin:** No persuasive derivation of *Alugor* has been demonstrated. A discovery of an earlier office-of-spirits witness could change the picture.

2. **The exact stemma:** CLM 849, Trinity O.8.29, the several *Liber Officiorum* witnesses, Weyer’s lost exemplar, and Scot form a network, but not every copying relationship can be reduced to a single straight line.

3. **Why the opening syllable changed:** *Alu-, Abu-, Al-, Eli-* could reflect paleographical confusion, oral transmission, attempts to Latinize an opaque name, or several processes.

4. **Why sceptre became serpent:** The substitution is visible, but its originating manuscript is unknown.

5. **The original legion count:** There is no criterion for choosing 50, 27, 30, or 60 as metaphysically original. Sixty is simply the stable early-modern reception.

6. **Rudd’s exact contribution:** Harley 6483 demonstrates the system in the early eighteenth century. The extent to which its Goetia/Shem synthesis reproduces an autograph work of Thomas Rudd remains unresolved.

7. **When Hariel acquired each correspondence:** HRI, the expanded name, Psalm, zodiacal degrees, choir, calendar days, planetary sub-rulers, and moral offices entered the tradition at different times. Popular tables often collapse them into a fictitious single ancient revelation.

8. **The seal’s earliest exemplar:** Personal Goetia seals do not appear in Weyer’s printed catalogue. The precise source from which the seventeenth-century compiler obtained or designed Eligor’s seal remains unknown.

9. **Practitioner testimony:** Modern practitioners report experiences of Eligos as a strategist, soldier, counsellor, patronage spirit, or distinct personality. Such testimony documents contemporary religion and magical practice, but cannot independently establish medieval history.
