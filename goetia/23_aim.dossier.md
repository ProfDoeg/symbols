# Aim: Research Dossier

*Research current to 14 September 2026.*

## Evidentiary key

- **Documented text** — stated or depicted in a primary source.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a conclusion drawn by editors or historians from manuscripts, textual dependencies, or chronology.
- **Tradition** — an attribution transmitted within an occult or religious system, without implying historical or empirical verification.
- **Esoteric correspondence** — a deliberately constructed magical, astrological, or qabalistic association.
- **Disputed claim** — proposed but unsupported, contradicted, or dependent on uncertain attribution.
- **Legend** — part of the narrative world of Solomon, demons, or angels.
- **Modern invention** — a recent occult, artistic, literary, cinematic, or game elaboration not found in the early sources.

# The name and its origins

## The source-chain spellings

Joseph Peterson’s comparative table supplies the clearest compact collation of the principal witnesses. It gives the following readings for this entry: [Peterson, “Goetia spirits comparison”](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia_spirits.pdf).

| Witness | Date | Form |
|---|---:|---|
| Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* | 1577 | **Aym uel Haborym** |
| Scot, *Discoverie of Witchcraft* | 1584 | **Aym or Haborim** |
| Sloane MS 3825 | seventeenth century | **Aim** |
| Sloane MS 3648 | seventeenth century | **Aim** |
| Sloane MS 2731 | dated 18 January 1686/7 | **Aim** |
| Harley MS 6483 | copied 1712/13 | **Aim** |
| Mathers–Crowley | 1904 | **Aim** |
| Waite | 1898/1911 | **Aini**, while acknowledging Weyer’s Aym/Haborym |
| Idries Shah | twentieth century | **Aini** |
| Wellcome MS 3203 | nineteenth century | entry omitted in Peterson’s comparison |

### What the variants mean textually

**Documented text:** Weyer clearly writes one entry with alternatives: *Aym vel Haborym*, “Aym or Haborym.” Scot preserves that structure. Nothing in either source suggests two separate beings.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The shorter *Aim/Aym* became dominant when the *Goetia* rearranged the catalogue. *Haborym* survived in dictionaries, French demonology, art, and modern media.

**Disputed:** Claims that Aim and Haborym were originally separate spirits later conflated have no supporting pre-Weyer witnesses. Although copying aliases sometimes did create duplicate spirits elsewhere in demon catalogues, no surviving source exhibits two distinct entries here.

**Later variants:**

- **Aini** is first prominent in Waite’s editorial tradition. It is not the reading of Weyer or the principal Lemegeton manuscripts.
- **Harborim/Harborym** are modern orthographic expansions, especially common in English-language occult writing and games.
- **Aborym** is a French simplification.
- **Amy** is generally a conflation with the distinct Goetic spirit Amy/Avnas, number 58. Weyer lists Aim and Amy separately.
- **Caim/Caym** is likewise a different spirit, number 53 in the Goetia.
- **Raum**, number 40, becomes relevant only because de Plancy reports—and doubts—a claim that Haborym and Raum are identical.

## First secure attestation

**Documented text:** The first securely dated appearance of the exact dossier is Johann Weyer’s 1577 *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*, printed as an appendix to the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum*. The parent work first appeared in 1563, which is why many modern websites incorrectly date the demon list itself to 1563. Peterson’s edition distinguishes the dates and reproduces the 1577 text [Weyer/Peterson](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

**Finding:** No securely identified pre-1577 text names *Aim*, *Aym*, *Haborym*, or *Haborim* with these offices.

## Possible manuscript antecedent: Bachimy/Albermi/Cabeym

A related Florence catalogue compared by Peterson and by Jean-Patrice Boudet includes a spirit written approximately **Bachimy vel Albermi vel Cabeym**, described as a great and strong duke with twenty-six legions.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Its identical rank and legion count make it a plausible relative of Weyer’s Aim dossier. The corrupted cluster *Bachimy/Albermi/Cabeym* might stand somewhere in the textual ancestry of *Aym/Haborym*.

**Disputed:** Phonetic resemblance alone is insufficient to identify it securely. It is better described as a possible cognate office-entry, not as a verified spelling of Aim.

## Does the name preserve an ancient god, hero, or place?

**Finding:** No ancient deity, hero, geographical name, Greek noun, Latin noun, Hebrew demon, or late-antique spirit has been demonstrated behind Aim.

That is markedly different from several neighboring Goetic names:

- Baal transparently continues Northwest Semitic *baʿal*, “lord.”
- Astaroth derives through demonological transmission from Astarte.
- Asmoday/Asmodeus has a Persian and Jewish textual history.
- Belial descends from biblical Hebrew *bĕliyyaʿal*.
- Phoenix is Greek *phoinix* and evokes the classical bird.
- Cerberus and Naberius became entangled through recognizable classical material.

No equivalent chain exists for Aim.

### Proposed Hebrew derivations

**Disputed claim:** *Haborym* has been connected with Hebrew **חברים**, *ḥaverim*, “companions” or “associates.” The resemblance is superficial: consonants, vowels, and historical pathway are not established, and the proposed meaning does not explain the demon’s offices.

**Modern invention:** Recent websites derive *Aym* from Hebrew forms supposedly meaning “terrors,” or Haborym from roots meaning “burning,” “destruction,” or “hidden dread.” These pages do not cite a medieval Hebrew form or manuscript chain. Their interpretations appear to have been inferred backwards from the firebrand.

**Modern speculative symbolism:** Fred Gettings’s *Dictionary of Demons* suggested that Aim’s cat head and incendiarism might recall Bast or Sekhmet. This is an iconographic analogy, not an etymology. No source between Egypt and Weyer connects Aim with either goddess.

### Pronunciation

No early source supplies pronunciation.

- English occultists commonly pronounce *Aim/Aym* like English “aim.”
- *Haborym* is variously pronounced *HA-bor-im*, *ha-BOR-im*, or *ha-bo-REEM*.
- These are modern conventions, not recoverable historical phonology.

# Order and number: Weyer’s 69 and the Goetia’s 72

Weyer’s catalogue contains 69 entries, although the Belial notice refers to a total of 72 kings or spirits. The *Ars Goetia* reaches 72 by omitting one Weyer spirit and adding four [Weyer/Peterson comparison](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

| Change | Spirit |
|---|---|
| Present in Weyer but omitted from the Goetia | **Pruflas**, also Bufas |
| Added near the beginning | **Vassago**, Goetia 3 |
| Added at the end | **Seere**, Goetia 70 |
| Added at the end | **Dantalion**, Goetia 71 |
| Added at the end | **Andromalius**, Goetia 72 |

The arithmetic is 69 − 1 + 4 = 72.

Aim moves drastically:

- **Weyer:** paragraph/entry 57 in Peterson’s numbering.
- **Scot:** normally counted as spirit 56 in a sequential count that excludes headings or treats Weyer’s numbering differently.
- **Goetia:** spirit 23.

Peterson’s comparison maps Goetia 23 explicitly to Weyer 57. Modern sources alternately calling Weyer’s Aim “56” and “57” therefore need not be describing different editions; frequently they are simply using different counting conventions.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The Goetia depends more immediately upon an English Scot tradition—apparently including readings associated with the enlarged 1665 edition—than upon direct consultation of Weyer’s Latin. The reordered catalogue, seals, ritual framework, and four added names belong to the later compilation.

# Grimoire ancestors—and the absence of Aim

## The Testament of Solomon

The Greek *Testament of Solomon*, composed in late antiquity but preserved in much later manuscripts, describes Solomon interrogating demons about their names, forms, activities, thwarting angels, and means of constraint.

**Historical importance:** Its question-and-answer structure and use of angelic adversaries provide a remote conceptual ancestor for Rudd’s system.

**Negative finding:** Aim/Haborym does not occur in the *Testament*. Skinner and Rankine observe that only Asmodeus and perhaps Ornias/Orias provide obvious name overlaps with the Goetia. It is not a source for Aim’s three heads, viper, firebrand, wit, or twenty-six legions.

## Munich handbook, Clm 849

The fifteenth-century Munich manual edited by Richard Kieckhefer contains necromantic operations and a small group of office spirits, including names such as Volach, Taob, Tuveries, Hanni, and Sucax.

**Negative finding:** It contains no Aim or Haborym. Some of its other spirits have possible Goetic descendants, demonstrating a shared cataloguing environment but not Aim’s presence.

## *Livre des Esperitz*

Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29, fols. 179r–182v, preserves the Old French *Livre des Esperitz*, generally dated to the fifteenth century. It names approximately forty-six spirits and is the oldest surviving extensive French demon catalogue. Boudet showed substantial overlap between it and Weyer [Boudet, “Les who’s who démonologiques”](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/1019); a diplomatic digital text is available through [Grimorium](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/livre-esperitz/read/).

**Negative finding:** No Aim/Haborym or certain cognate appears.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The *Livre* is an ancestor of part of the catalogue tradition, not a complete direct exemplar of Weyer. Spirits could have entered the chain from other branches.

## *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

Weyer says his source was a *Liber officiorum spirituum*, also styled a book of Empto/Empto Salomonis. Trithemius listed a work under a similar title in his *Antipalus maleficiorum* around 1508.

**Documented:** The title and genre precede Weyer.

**Uncertain:** No surviving manuscript can be proved to be Weyer’s exact exemplar. Folger MS V.b.26 and later *Office of Spirits* manuscripts preserve related but divergent material. Aim is not securely located in an extant pre-Weyer witness.

## Abramelin

The fifteenth-century German Abramelin tradition has extensive lists of spirits and a Solomonic-style sequence in which angelic attainment precedes the command of demons.

**Negative finding:** The principal Abramelin demon lists do not securely attest Aim/Haborym. Similar operations—answering questions, creating fires, destroying strongholds—are generic magical objectives and are not proof of identity.

# The description across the sources

## Weyer, 1577: Latin, in full

Johann Weyer, *De praestigiis daemonum*, fifth edition, Basel: Johannes Oporinus, 1577, *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*, printed p. 928 in the scan used by Peterson:

> **Aym vel Haborym Dux magnus & fortis: progreditur tribus capitibus, primo serpenti simili, altero homini duos ☆ habenti, tertio felino. In vipera equitat, ingentem facem ardentem portans, cuius flamma succenditur castrum vel civitas. Omnibus modis ingeniosum reddit hominem: de abstrusis rebus verè respondet. Imperat legionibus vigintisex.**

[Latin text and scan collation](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

A close English gloss:

> **Aym or Haborym, a great and strong Duke, comes forth with three heads: the first like a serpent, the second like a man having two stars, the third feline. He rides upon a viper, carrying an immense burning torch, by whose flame a castle or city is set alight. He renders a person ingenious in every way; he answers truly concerning hidden matters. He commands twenty-six legions.**

### Points of language

- *Dux magnus & fortis* — “a great and strong duke.”
- *tertio felino* — literally “the third feline/catlike.” It does not mean calf.
- *facem* — torch or firebrand.
- *castrum* — fortified place, castle, or stronghold.
- *ingeniosum* — ingenious, clever, resourceful; Scot’s “wittie” is a reasonable sixteenth-century rendering.
- *abstrusis rebus* — hidden, obscure, concealed matters; Scot renders this more narrowly as “privie matters.”
- *vigintisex* — twenty-six.

## Scot, 1584: English, in full

Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, London: William Brome, 1584, Book XV, chapter 2. The original uses black-letter typography and edition-dependent foliation; Book XV, chapter 2 is therefore the most reliable cross-edition locator. Peterson’s diplomatic online edition reproduces it as follows:

> **Aym or Haborim [Haborym] is a great duke and a strong, he commeth foorth with three heads, the first like a serpent, the second like a man having two ☆ the third like a cat, he rideth on a viper, carrieng in his hand a light fier brand, with the flame whereof castels and cities are fiered, he maketh one wittie everie kind of waie, he answereth truelie of privie matters, and reigneth over twentie six legions.**

[Scot, Book XV, chapter 2](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/scot16.htm); [1584 Cornell facsimile catalogue](https://digital.library.cornell.edu/catalog/witchcraft081)

The bracketed correction *Haborym* is Peterson’s editorial note, not Scot’s printing.

### Scot’s position

Scot reproduced conjuring texts to expose what he regarded as superstition, fraud, or absurdity. Publication in his book does not show that he accepted Aim as real. The translation is sometimes attributed to Abraham Fleming because Fleming translated other Latin materials for Scot; Peterson treats that attribution as probable rather than certain.

## Sloane MS 3825: the principal Peterson text

Peterson’s 2001 edition uses Sloane 3825 as its primary witness. The entry occurs at fol. 103v; in the Weiser edition it is p. 17:

> **The 23d spirit is called Aim, a greatt duke and strong, he appeareth in ye forme of a very handsome man in Body, [but] wth 3 heads, The first like a serpent ye second like a man wth 2 starrs in his forehead, The Third [head] is lik a Catt; he rideeth on a viper, carring a fire brand in his hand burning, whereth [wherewith] he sets Citties Castles & greate places on fire he maketh one witty [in] all manner of wayes, and giveth true answares to privy matters, he governeth 26 Legions of Infernall spirits; his seal is thus to be made, and worne as a Lamen before you.**

[Peterson’s Lemegeton transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm); Joseph H. Peterson, ed., *The Lesser Key of Solomon*, York Beach: Weiser, 2001, p. 17.

What the manuscript adds to Weyer/Scot:

- Aim is now number 23.
- The composite figure has a “very handsome” human body.
- The man-head’s two stars are explicitly on the forehead.
- “Great places” joins cities and castles.
- The twenty-six legions are “Infernal spirits.”
- Aim now has an individual seal that must be worn as a lamen.

What it preserves:

- **Cat**, not calf.
- The same rank, mount, firebrand, offices, and legion number.

## Sloane MS 2731

Sloane 2731, dated 18 January 1686/7, reads **Aim** and preserves substantially the same description and seal tradition. It is a composite manuscript and drew on more than one earlier witness, including material associated with Sloane 3648. A manuscript-oriented diplomatic presentation is available from [Grimorium’s Sloane 2731 edition](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/goetia-sloane2731/).

**Textual finding:** Sloane 2731 does not support *Aini* or the calf reading.

## Harley MS 6483 and the “Rudd” text

Harley 6483 was copied in 1712/13. Skinner and Rankine print the Aim entry on pp. 124–25:

> **The 23th Spirit is Aim he is a great & strong Duke, he appeareth in [the] form of a very handsome man in body with three heads the first like a serpent, the second like a man, having 2 stars in his forehead, the third like a Cat; he rideth on a Viper carrying a fire brand in his hand burning, wherewith he sets Cities castles and great places on fire, he maketh one witty all manner of ways, giveth true Answers to private matters he governeth twenty six Legions of inferior spirits, his Seal is thus, which wear.**

The facing angelic material gives:

> **Dominus custodit introitum tuum et exitum tuum ex nunc et usque in seculum.**

Skinner and Rankine translate:

> **The Lord keep thine incoming and thine outgoing from this time forth for evermore.**

This is identified as Vulgate Psalm 120:8, corresponding to Psalm 121:8 in Hebrew and most English Protestant numbering. See Stephen Skinner and David Rankine, eds., *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (2007), pp. 124–25; the publisher describes the edition and its “thwarting angel” method [here](https://www.sskinner.com/pdf/GHP-Catalogue-2022-2023.pdf).

### Is Thomas Rudd the author?

**Documented:** The Harley manuscript group contains the name of Dr Thomas Rudd and material attributed to him. Harley 6483 itself is an eighteenth-century copy.

**Traditional editorial attribution:** Skinner and Rankine call it Rudd’s *Liber Malorum Spirituum seu Goetia*.

**Disputed:** The surviving copy does not by itself prove that Rudd personally composed every part of the system. “Rudd” may identify owner, source, earlier compiler, or attributed authority. The safest language is “the Rudd recension” or “the material preserved in Harley 6483.”

## Mathers–Crowley, 1904: in full

S. L. MacGregor Mathers, trans., Aleister Crowley, ed., *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King*, Inverness: Society for the Propagation of Religious Truth, 1904, printed p. 29:

> **(23.) Aim.—The Twenty-third Sprit is Aim. He is a Great Strong Duke. He appeareth in the form of a very handsome Man in body, but with three Heads; the first, like a Serpent, the second like a Man having two Stars on his Forehead, the third like a Calf. He rideth on a Viper, carrying a Firebrand in his Hand, wherewith he setteth cities, castles, and great Places, on fire. He maketh thee witty in all manner of ways, and giveth true answers unto private matters. He governeth 26 Legions of Inferior Spirits; and his Seal is this, which wear thou as aforesaid, etc.**

[1904 text and page break](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks05.htm); [Hermetic Library transcription](https://hermetic.com/norton/classics/goetia/aim)

The first-edition printing contains “Sprit,” an obvious typographical error for “Spirit.”

### The cat–calf change

The early chain is consistent:

- Weyer: *felino*.
- Scot: cat.
- Sloane 3825: cat.
- Sloane 2731: cat.
- Harley 6483: cat.

Mathers–Crowley prints **calf**.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** “Calf” is a late editorial or copying alteration, not an equally ancient reading. It may have resulted from eye-skip, handwriting, or an attempted harmonization with the many composite animal figures in the catalogue. No evidence currently shows that Mathers translated a Latin word meaning calf.

## What Aim does not do in the source text

Aim is not said to:

- teach languages;
- teach the liberal sciences as a curriculum;
- find treasure;
- procure love;
- give familiars;
- confer invisibility;
- transmute metals;
- change the dead;
- reveal past, present, and future generally;
- heal illness;
- command weather;
- transform into a second form at the conjurer’s demand.

Those are offices of other Goetic spirits. Some modern profiles transfer them to Aim by combining generic Goetic abilities, personal visionary accounts, or neighboring entries.

## “Private matters”

Weyer’s *abstrusis rebus* means hidden or obscure things. Scot’s “privie matters” can mean secrets or matters withheld from public knowledge, not necessarily romantic or sexual secrets. The later Goetia follows Scot’s wording.

# The seal

## Earliest appearance

**Negative finding:** Weyer’s *Pseudomonarchia* supplies no individual seals. Scot’s translation of the list likewise supplies none.

**Documented text:** Aim’s seal enters the surviving chain with the seventeenth-century *Ars Goetia* manuscripts.

## Shape

The standardized Mathers–Crowley image can be consulted directly here:

[![Seal of Aim, Mathers–Crowley 1904](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Special:Redirect/file/23-Aim_seal.png)](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:23-Aim_seal.png)

At the level common to the manuscript family, it is:

1. a predominantly bilateral character;
2. built around a central upright or descending axis;
3. with two upper arms curving or angling away from the center;
4. small circular or looped terminals on opposed portions of the figure;
5. short horizontal cross-strokes;
6. a lower central extension or hook;
7. no alphabetic legend incorporated into the published 1904 form.

The visual balance is closer to a doubled or mirrored line-character than to a circle-enclosed planetary seal.

## Variants

- **Sloane 3825:** Peterson’s base form, printed on p. 17 of the 2001 edition. The drawing has more manuscript irregularity and less typographic symmetry than 1904.
- **Sloane 2731:** the same recognizable design family, with different curvature, spacing, and terminal closure.
- **Harley 6483:** redraws the demon character and coordinates it with an angelic reverse. The operative object is therefore not merely Aim’s seal but a two-sided lamen.
- **Mathers–Crowley:** regularizes line weight and symmetry for engraving/printing.
- **Modern vector versions:** clean and geometrically normalize the 1904 plate; they should not be mistaken for facsimiles of Sloane or Harley.

**Important limitation:** No manuscript says that small differences in stroke thickness or curvature change the identity of the spirit. Modern claims that every variant is a different “address” are practitioner interpretations.

## Material

The Goetia’s general instructions say the seal of a duke is to be made in **copper**. Copper belongs conventionally to **Venus**. Thus “Aim is Venusian” is not stated in his individual entry; it is deduced from the rank table.

# Timing and appearance in ritual

## Rank timing

The *Goetia* “Observations” prescribe that dukes may be bound:

> from sunrise until noon, in clear weather.

This is a general rule for dukes, not a statement attached uniquely to Aim.

## Lunar timing

The same observations favor operations on the Moon’s 2nd, 4th, 6th, 8th, 10th, 12th, and 14th days. Other lunar days are called unprofitable.

## Human form

The general ritual says the magician may show the Hexagram of Solomon to compel a spirit into obedience and an acceptable human form.

**Distinction:** That general instruction must not be rewritten as an Aim-specific “second form.” Aim’s own entry gives only the handsome-bodied, three-headed manifestation.

## Later planetary timing

**Esoteric correspondence:** Once dukes were assigned to Venus, later practitioners preferred Friday or a Venus planetary hour.

**Later astrological overlay:** A Crowley/Golden Dawn practitioner might instead elect a time when the Sun is in Aim’s assigned Scorpio decan.

Neither Friday nor 10°–20° Scorpio appears in Weyer, Scot, or Aim’s manuscript entry. They are later integrations.

## Derivation of the 72 triplets

Exodus 14:19–21 comprises three Hebrew verses of seventy-two letters each in the Masoretic consonantal text. The later method writes:

1. verse 19 from right to left;
2. verse 20 beneath it in reverse order;
3. verse 21 again in the first direction.

Reading vertical columns produces seventy-two three-letter groups. The twenty-third is **MLH**.

This arrangement is explained in medieval Jewish commentary, notably Rashi’s commentary associated with Sukkah 45a, and occurs in the wider medieval reception of the seventy-twofold Name. A modern scholarly discussion is Laura Joffe’s study of the 72-letter Name [Journal for the Study of the Old Testament](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/030908920202700205). The relevant biblical text and commentaries are available through [Sefaria](https://www.sefaria.org/Exodus.14.19-21?with=Kabbalah).

### Terminological caution

The expression *Shem ha-Mephorash* has had more than one historical referent. In early rabbinic usage it could denote the explicitly pronounced divine Name, especially the Tetragrammaton. Later esoteric literature applies it to 42-letter and 72-fold constructions.

The “72 names” are therefore:

- seventy-two **three-letter triplets**, not seventy-two independent biblical angel names;
- transformed into angel names by adding suffixes such as **-el** or **-iah**;
- only later supplied systematically with zodiacal degrees, Psalms, moral qualities, and guardian-angel birth dates.

## Meaning of Melahel

Lenain gives:

> **Melahel. Son attribut (Dieu qui délivre des maux).**

“His attribute: God who delivers from evils/ills.”

This appears on pp. 59–60 of *La science cabalistique* (Amiens, 1823) [digital scan](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1823__lenain___science_cabalistique.pdf).

**Tradition:** “God who delivers from evils” became Melahel’s standard French occult meaning.

**Philological caution:** It is not a transparent word-for-word translation of the Hebrew consonants MLH. Hebrew roots built from these consonants can concern filling, salt, sailing, or related senses depending on vocalization. Lenain’s phrase is a theological attribution, not demonstrated historical etymology.

## Degrees and dates

Lenain says Melahel’s ray begins at the **111th degree** and extends through the five-degree interval. Counting from 0° Aries:

- Aries = degrees 0–29;
- Taurus = 30–59;
- Gemini = 60–89;
- Cancer = 90–119;
- degree 110/111 therefore lies at approximately 20° Cancer.

The operational range is conventionally written **20°00′–24°59′ Cancer**.

Popular angel calendars translate that span into approximately **12–16 July**, sometimes 13–17 July depending on whether endpoints, civil dates, and the year’s solar ingress are calculated precisely. The degrees are the more stable attribution.

### Lenain’s five dispersed days

Lenain also assigns five days scattered through the year. Modern reproductions disagree because later authors recalculated or shifted the calendar:

- one Lenain/Ambelain-derived series gives approximately 11 April, 22 June, 2 September, 13 November, and 24 January;
- Haziel-derived popular calendars commonly give 12 April, 26 June, 9 September, 21 November, and 30 January.

**Disputed transmission:** These are not alternative readings of Exodus. They are products of different occult calendrical schemes.

## Psalm verse

Harley 6483 gives:

> **Dominus custodit introitum tuum et exitum tuum ex nunc et usque in seculum.**

The corresponding King James wording is:

> **The LORD shall preserve thy going out and thy coming in from this time forth, and even for evermore.**

The apparent discrepancy—Psalm 120 versus 121—is numbering, not a different text:

- Vulgate/Septuagint: Psalm 120:8;
- Masoretic, KJV, and most modern English Bibles: Psalm 121:8.

Some modern Melahel pages instead cite Psalm 121:5. That is a secondary alteration; Harley 6483’s Aim page clearly carries verse 8.

# The angelic tradition: historical chain

## Jewish sources

### Rashi and the three-verse construction

**Documented tradition:** Medieval Jewish commentary explains the alternating-direction construction from Exodus 14:19–21. This is the foundation for MLH as triplet 23.

### *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh*

*Sefer Raziel* is a medieval Hebrew magical compilation rather than a book by a single ancient author. Its materials are older than its first printings, but the text’s layers are difficult to date uniformly.

**Documented tradition:** It transmits the 72-fold divine Name and related angelic/magical materials.

**Caution:** The full Lenain package—Melahel’s exact French meaning, five zodiacal degrees, Psalm, scattered days, and psychological portrait—should not be projected wholesale into *Sefer Raziel*.

## Christian Cabala

### Reuchlin and Vigenère

Christian Cabalists treated the triplets as pronounceable angelic names by adding divine suffixes. Johann Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517) is important to this reception.

Blaise de Vigenère’s papers supplied a prominent early-modern angel-name and seal tradition later used in material associated with Rudd and, indirectly, Golden Dawn work.

### Kircher

Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (Rome, 1652–54) assembled tables combining Hebrew Cabala, angelic names, divine names in supposedly national languages, astrology, and Christian universal history. [Peterson’s transcription of Kircher’s *Cabala Hebraeorum*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/kircher/cabala.htm) and the [digitized volume](https://www.esonet.org/wp-content/uploads/2010/06/Oedipus_Aegyptiacus02.pdf) show this encyclopedic setting.

**Historical assessment:** Kircher was an influential transmitter and synthesizer, not a reliable guide to ancient Egyptian language or to a single continuous Jewish tradition.

## Rudd/Harley 6483

Rudd’s system joins each demon by ordinal number to the corresponding Shem angel:

- demon 1 ↔ angel 1;
- demon 2 ↔ angel 2;
- …
- Aim 23 ↔ Melahel 23.

The rationale is not that Aim and Melahel share a linguistic root or astrological nature. Their connection is ordinal.

### How Melahel constrains Aim

Harley 6483’s operational innovation is the two-sided lamen:

- Aim’s seal faces outward toward the summoned spirit.
- Melahel’s name/character and Psalmic protection are placed on the reverse, toward the magician’s heart.

Skinner and Rankine explain that the corresponding Shem angel is the demon’s **controlling** or **thwarting** angel. The publisher summarizes the book as explaining how the 72 Shem angels are used to evoke and bind the 72 demons [Llewellyn description](https://www.llewellyn.com/product.php?ean=9780738723556).

**Tradition:** Melahel’s divine name and Psalm provide lawful celestial authority over Aim’s infernal office.

**Historical parallel:** The construction resembles the *Testament of Solomon*, where demons disclose angelic adversaries, but Aim and Melahel themselves do not occur in that ancient text.

## Lenain, 1823

Lazare Républicain Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* consolidates earlier Christian Cabalistic and magical tables. For Melahel it gives:

- number 23;
- meaning “God who delivers from evils”;
- ray beginning at degree 111;
- Psalm 120:8;
- the 7:20–7:40 time;
- five annual days;
- protection from weapons and dangers of travel;
- rule over water, crops, and medicinal plants;
- courage and honorable undertakings;
- an adverse genius linked to damaged vegetation and disease.

This is the first readily traceable source for much of what modern angel websites present as ancient, undifferentiated “Kabbalah.”

## Papus, 1903

Gérard Encausse, “Papus,” incorporated and republished Lenain material in the enlarged 1903 *La Cabbale: tradition secrète de l’Occident* [scan](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1903__papus___cabbala_tradition_secrete_de_loccident.pdf).

**Transmission:** Papus helped move Lenain’s synthesis into French occult revivalism. This did not create the Aim–Melahel pairing; it popularized the angelic side of the apparatus.

## Golden Dawn and Crowley

The Golden Dawn inherited Christian Cabalistic angel names, tarot decans, planetary ranks, and zodiacal tables. Crowley’s *777* reorganized these correspondences for compact reference.

**Crucial distinction:** The Golden Dawn’s tarot decans and Crowley’s 36-by-day/36-by-night Goetic scheme are not the same as Lenain’s ordinal angel quinances.

## Ambelain, 1951

Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique*, Paris: Niclaus, 1951, continued the French practical-Cabala system, explicitly treating angelic names, seals, Psalms, times, hierarchies, and theurgy. Bibliographic details are confirmed by [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books/about/La_Kabbale_pratique.html?id=jF8ntAEACAAJ); an English translation is available [here](https://www.markfoster.net/rn/texts/practicalkabbalah-part1.pdf).

The now-familiar attribution of Melahel to:

- the choir of Thrones;
- Binah;
- Zaphkiel;
- medicinal plants;
- weapons and travel;

was stabilized in this French occult milieu.

## Haziel

“Haziel,” the pen name attached to a large late-twentieth-century French and Spanish angelology corpus, popularized the idea that each five-degree angel is the guardian of people born during a particular five-day period.

For Melahel, Haziel-derived calendars typically give 12–16 July and greatly expand the psychological and devotional material.

**Modern tradition:** These personality profiles, affirmations, life missions, meditations, and “vibratory contacts” are not found in Exodus, Rashi, or the early *Sefer Raziel*. They are modern angelology constructed upon Lenain/Ambelain correspondences.

## Why each tarot pip carries two Goetic spirits

The Golden Dawn assigns one pip card to each ten-degree decan. Crowley’s later Goetic table assigns:

- spirits 1–36 to the 36 decans **by day**;
- spirits 37–72 to the same 36 decans **by night**.

Aim is number 23. Counting from the first decan of Aries, decan 23 is Scorpio II. Adding 36 gives Orias, number 59, the night ruler of the same decan. Both therefore attach to the Six of Cups.

This construction is mathematically neat but demonstrably later than Weyer and the Goetia manuscripts.

## The rank planets

Crowley’s revised *777* tradition provides:

| Rank | Planet | Metal |
|---|---|---|
| Knight | Saturn | Lead |
| Prince/Prelate | Jupiter | Tin |
| Earl/Count | Mars | Iron |
| King | Sun | Gold |
| Duke | Venus | Copper |
| President | Mercury | Mercury or mixed metal |
| Marquis | Moon | Silver |

[777 Revised correspondence note](https://hermetic.com/_media/93beast.fea.st/files/section1/777/liber_777_revised.pdf)

The original Goetia manuscript rule is less perfectly systematic: it prescribes copper-and-silver equally for earls, leaving iron/Mars absent. Mathers suggested correcting or rationalizing this. The neat Earl–Mars–iron row is therefore partly editorial.

Aim is unaffected by that dispute: all forms agree on Duke–copper, and copper’s standard planetary ruler is Venus.

## Astrological timing in different traditions

| Method | Time selected |

| Guardian-angel calendar | Approximately 12–16 July |
| Crowley/777 Goetic decan | Sun at 10°–20° Scorpio |
| Tarot operation | Six of Cups for Aim directly; Four of Cups when approaching the Melahel quinance |

These methods are cumulative only in eclectic modern practice. The early sources never require all of them simultaneously.

# Enochian and other later overlays

## Crowley’s Enochian material

The 1904 edition is not simply Mathers’s Goetia transcription. Crowley added:

- a prefatory psychological interpretation;
- the “Preliminary Invocation” or “Bornless Ritual” material;
- supplementary diagrams;
- versions of conjurations in the language associated with John Dee and Edward Kelley.

The bibliographical description of the first edition identifies these additions [100th Monkey Press](https://www.100thmonkeypress.com/biblio/acrowley/books/goetia_1904/goetia_1904.htm).

**Documented:** Enochian-language conjurations occur in Crowley’s edition.

**Negative finding:** There is no early Enochian name, tablet square, aethyr, or governor uniquely identified as Aim.

## Harley’s “Enoch’s Tables” claim

A catalogue description of Harley 6483 says that some spirits are “in Enoch’s Tables described in the former volume,” while their seals are supplied in the present book [Harley 6483 description](https://www.alchemywebsite.com/mss/mss479.htm).

**Disputed reconstruction:** Skinner and Rankine investigate possible connections between names abbreviated in Dee-related tables and Goetic spirits. The relationship is intriguing but does not prove that Dee evoked Aim or knew the Goetia’s Aim entry.

## Modern Qliphothic and demonolatry correspondences

Twenty-first-century practitioners have assigned Aim to:

- Golachab, Thagirion, Gamchicoth, or several simultaneously;
- the “Black Sun”;
- Uranus, Neptune, or Jupiter;
- invented “enns” and channeled names;
- herbs, colors, animals, chakras, dreamwalking, portals, or “shadow work.”

An example published in 2019 openly labels several names as channeled and gives the platypus among Aim’s animals [V. K. Jehannum](https://vkjehannum.wordpress.com/2019/03/14/aym-or-haborym-goetia-23/).

**Modern invention:** Such claims document living occult reception. They are not readings from Weyer, Scot, Sloane, Harley, or Mathers–Crowley.

# Iconography

## The manuscript image

The earliest securely attested visual identifier is not a portrait but the Goetic seal. Weyer and Scot describe the apparition verbally and offer no portrait.

The manuscripts thus separate two kinds of image:

- the seal as an operative character;
- the verbal composite body imagined by the reader or artist.

## Collin de Plancy, 1818 and 1863

Jacques-Albin-Simon Collin de Plancy first published the *Dictionnaire infernal* in 1818. The celebrated Louis Le Breton engravings belong to the illustrated sixth edition of 1863, not to the first edition.

De Plancy’s 1863 entry, pp. 316–17, reads:

> **Haborym, démon des incendies, appelé aussi Aym. Il porte aux enfers le titre de duc ; il se montre à cheval sur une vipère, avec trois têtes, l’une de serpent, l’autre d’homme, la troisième de chat. Il tient à la main une torche allumée. Il commande vingt-six légions. Quelques-uns disent que c’est le même que Ranm ; ce qui nous paraît au moins douteux.**

[French text](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_éd.,_1863/Haborym)

Translation:

> Haborym, demon of conflagrations, also called Aym. He bears the title of duke in hell; he appears mounted on a viper, with three heads—one serpent, another man, the third cat. He holds a lighted torch. He commands twenty-six legions. Some say that he is the same as “Ranm”; this seems to us doubtful at the least.

“Ranm” is almost certainly a typesetting or reading error for **Raum**.

### Le Breton’s portrait

[Louis Le Breton’s Haborym engraving](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ill_dict_infernal_p0333-317_haborym_demon.jpg) depicts:

- a small humanoid rider;
- a central human head;
- lateral animal heads, read as serpent and cat;
- the body astride a long, winding viper;
- one arm lifting a burning torch;
- an overall diagonal composition driven by the serpent’s coils and the flame.

The engraving follows the cat tradition, not Mathers’s later calf.

### “Demon of holocausts”

Older English occult dictionaries sometimes translate *démon des incendies* as “demon of holocausts.” In nineteenth-century usage *holocaust* could mean a great conflagration or burnt offering.

**Modern caution:** Rendering the phrase that way now invites an anachronistic association with the twentieth-century Holocaust. “Demon of fires” or “conflagrations” is the accurate contextual translation.

## Modern visual art

Modern depictions tend to follow one of three templates:

1. **Le Breton template:** small grotesque three-headed rider on a giant serpent.
2. **Mathers–Crowley template:** handsome humanoid body with serpent, human, and calf heads.
3. **Game/fantasy template:** a fire-aligned reptile, warlord, queen, or divine title only loosely connected with the textual body.

Digital art sometimes makes all three heads animalistic, replaces the firebrand with a spear or club, or assigns Aim armor and infernal regalia. None of those additions is in the early description.

# People and works

## Johann Weyer

Weyer was a physician and critic of major elements of witch prosecution. *De praestigiis daemonum* argued that many accused witches were deluded, ill, or exploited and should not be treated as powerful servants of Satan.

His publication of demon names was polemical and antiquarian, not an endorsement of their objective truth. He also withheld or abridged conjurations, saying in effect that he did not wish to furnish a working manual.

For Aim, Weyer is the first secure textual witness.

## Reginald Scot

Scot’s *Discoverie* attacked witch persecution, Catholic ritual claims, magical fraud, and credulity. Yet its reproductions of conjuring texts became an important source for later magicians.

For Aim, Scot:

- converted *Aym vel Haborym* to *Aym or Haborim*;
- translated *felino* as cat;
- converted *ingeniosum* to “wittie”;
- converted *abstrusis rebus* to “privie matters.”

## Anonymous Lemegeton compilers

The Goetia compiler or compilers:

- rearranged Weyer/Scot’s order;
- expanded the list to 72;
- added seals;
- embedded the catalogue in a ritual system;
- gave Aim a handsome body and explicitly located the stars on the human forehead.

The *Lemegeton* is a seventeenth-century compilation from older materials, not a work by the biblical Solomon.

## Thomas Rudd and the Harley copyists

The Rudd recension paired the 72 demons with 72 angels and Psalms. Whether Rudd authored the entire arrangement remains uncertain, but his name became attached to its transmission.

For Aim, this is where Melahel becomes essential.

## Collin de Plancy and Louis Le Breton

De Plancy turned the grimoire entry into a compact demonological dictionary article. Le Breton supplied the enduring portrait in 1863. Their Haborym is feline and incendiary.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers

Mathers prepared the English Goetia text used by Crowley. He standardized names and seals but introduced or perpetuated errors, including Aim’s calf.

## Aleister Crowley

Crowley published Mathers’s material in 1904, added Enochian and interpretive components, and later included the spirits in correspondence tables.

Crowley’s prefatory interpretation treats demons psychologically as portions of the human brain that can be controlled through magical method. That reading is his modern esoteric interpretation, not the position of Weyer, Scot, or the manuscript compiler.

## Frater Achad

A review of Charles Stansfeld Jones/Frater Achad bibliographies does not establish an Aim-specific operation, essay, or correspondence. His qabalistic works influenced later Thelemic thought, but he should not be listed as someone known to have “worked with Aim” without additional evidence [Achad bibliography](https://www.100thmonkeypress.com/biblio/achad/texts/texts.htm).

## Poke Runyon

Carroll “Poke” Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* and associated filmed ritual popularized an evocatory system using a dark mirror and a synthesized angelic-Goetic structure [publisher description](https://www.pokerunyon.com/books/the-book-of-solomons-magick-second-edition).

**Finding:** Runyon is important to modern practical Goetia generally, but no well-documented public Aim operation comparable to his Astaroth material was found.

## Lon Milo DuQuette, Christopher Hyatt, and David Cherubim/Wilson

*Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* (1992) reframed the Crowley text for modern practitioners and added illustrations, commentary, and a deliberately psychosexual interpretation [Open Library record](https://openlibrary.org/works/OL8554132W/Aleister_Crowley%27s_Illustrated_Goetia).

Its discussions belong to modern practical occultism; they do not independently establish Aim’s ancient history.

## Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 *Lesser Key of Solomon* was a major advance because it returned to manuscript witnesses, especially Sloane 3825, and recorded variants rather than silently regularizing them.

For Aim, Peterson restores:

- cat rather than calf;
- the manuscript spelling Aim;
- Weyer/Scot parallels;
- the seal in its manuscript context;
- the distinction between the textual catalogue and later editorial overlays.

## Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their *Goetia of Dr Rudd* (Golden Hoard Press, 2007) transcribed Harley 6483 with material from related manuscripts and reconstructed the operational role of the angelic counterseal, brass vessel, and changing magical circles.

For Aim, their pp. 124–25 verify the Melahel pairing and Psalm.

## Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s *Encyclopaedia Goetica* trilogy and *The True Grimoire* reinterpret goetia through late-antique, early-modern, and spiritist continuities. His work is influential in contemporary traditionalist and “spirit-model” practice [bibliographic record](https://obnb.uk/p15819193-the-trve-grimoire-encyclopaedia-goetica-volume-1).

**Finding:** His importance is theoretical and contextual; no distinct ancient derivation of Aim established by him was located.

## Owen Davies

Davies’s *Grimoires: A History of Magic Books* situates the *Lemegeton* within the social history of magical books rather than validating individual spirits as ancient survivals [Oxford Academic](https://academic.oup.com/book/47570).

## Jan R. Veenstra

Veenstra’s “The Holy Almandal: Angels and the Intellectual Aims of Magic” concerns the *Ars Almadel* and medieval angelic magic. It is relevant to the *Lemegeton*’s wider ancestry but does not attest Aim.

## Jean-Patrice Boudet and Julien Véronèse

Jean-Patrice Boudet—not Véronèse alone—published the particularly relevant comparison of Renaissance demonological “who’s who” lists and their medieval ancestors. Julien Véronèse’s broader scholarship concerns medieval ritual magic, magical books, pacts, and Solomonic traditions.

Neither supplies a secure pre-Weyer Aim.

# Literature, comics, film, television, and games

## Earlier literature

### Dante

**Negative finding:** Aim/Haborym does not appear in Dante’s *Divine Comedy*. Dante’s named demons belong to a different literary and theological repertoire.

### Milton

**Negative finding:** Aim/Haborym does not appear in Milton’s *Paradise Lost*. Milton uses names such as Moloch, Chemos, Baalim, Ashtaroth, Belial, and Mammon; similarity of infernal rank does not place Aim in the poem.

## Comics

### Hellboy

Haborym appears in Mike Mignola’s short comic “Pancakes.” When the young Hellboy eats pancakes, demons realize that he has become attached to human life; Haborym tells Astaroth that Hellboy “will never come back to us now.”

**Modern invention:** This is a comic role, not an adaptation of Aim’s Goetic offices. Haborym is used as a recognizably infernal name.

### DC Comics: Sabbac

In versions of the DC character Sabbac, the letters of the name encode powers drawn from infernal beings; **A** has been expanded as **Aym**.

**Modern invention:** Sabbac’s acronymic power system is a comics construction. It does, however, attest twentieth-century use of the Aym spelling.

## Role-playing games

### *Dungeons & Dragons*, *Tome of Magic* (2006)

Aym appears as a first-level vestige called **Aym, Queen Avarice**. She is reinvented as a dwarven queen whose greed contributed to the ruin of her realm. The binder receives fire-associated abilities and is subjected to avaricious influence.

The supplement was published in March 2006 by Wizards of the Coast; its bibliography is documented [here](https://dmreference.com/Fantasy/WotC/Books/Dungeons_Dragons/Tome_of_Magic.htm), and the Aym entry is reproduced in the searchable text [here](https://dtdnd.neocities.org/books/player/Tome%20of%20Magic.pdf).

**Modern invention:** Gender, dwarven history, greed, and vestige mechanics are new. The fire power probably adapts the Goetic firebrand.

### Dungeon Crawl Classics

Recent *Dungeon Denizens* material elaborates Harborym as a “Thane of Flames” with a castle called the Conflagration, cultists, combat statistics, and a fiery club. These are explicit game inventions, although the variant names, three heads, and burning office come from the Goetia.

## Japanese games: Megami Tensei

Aim/Haborym is attested in:

- *Digital Devil Story: Megami Tensei II*;
- *Kyūyaku Megami Tensei*;
- *Giten Megami Tensei: Tokyo Revelation*;
- *Devil Children: Light Book and Dark Book*;
- the English *DemiKids* versions.

The Japanese forms include アイン (*Ain*), アイム (*Aimu*), and ハボリム (*Haborimu*). Designs range from a purple reptilian humanoid to a fire-loving demon riding a viper. A detailed appearance list is available at the [Megami Tensei Wiki](https://megatenwiki.com/wiki/Aim).

**Negative finding:** No specific appearance of Aim in the main *Persona* games was located. “Megami Tensei franchise” should not automatically be rewritten as “Persona.”

## *Genshin Impact*

In *Genshin Impact*, **Haborym** is the divine or demon-name associated with the Pyro Archon’s office and particularly with Mavuika. The game preserves the name’s fire association while entirely reinventing its bearer and mythology [Mavuika storyline reference](https://genshin-impact.fandom.com/wiki/Mavuika/Storyline).

**Modern invention:** Mavuika is not the male three-headed Goetic duke; “Haborym” functions as an inherited or divine title inside the game’s Archon naming system.

## *Tactics Ogre*

The character name Hobyrim and related naming around Arycelle/Alocer have been read as Goetic borrowings. This is a plausible naming reference rather than an adaptation of Aim’s grimoire narrative.

## *Helluva Boss*

**Negative finding as of September 2026:** No canonical character explicitly identified as Aim, Aym, or Haborym was found in released *Helluva Boss* material. Fan-made “Meet the Goetia” projects and speculative character lists are not canonical attestations.

## Bartimaeus novels

**Negative finding:** Aim/Haborym was not located as a named character in Jonathan Stroud’s Bartimaeus sequence. The books use Solomon and a broad demon/spirit vocabulary, but that does not establish this particular spirit.

## Film

No substantial theatrically released film role explicitly identifying a character as Aim/Haborym was verified. Generic online claims that Aim appears in unspecified exorcism films generally lack credits, dialogue, or production evidence.

## Music and contemporary art

Composer Lera Auerbach’s project *Goetia* incorporates Goetic name-strings, including forms such as Aim/Hayborym/Haborym/Aini [composer’s page](https://leraauerbach.com/compositions/goetia/).

A musical artist called Haborym also released recordings under that name. These uses attest the name’s modern aesthetic appeal but do not add historical demonological evidence.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Was Aim number 56 or 57 in Weyer?

Both numbers appear in modern writing.

- Peterson’s paragraph numbering: **57**.
- A sequential count used by some demon lists: **56**.
- Goetia: **23**.

This is a counting problem, not evidence for two Aims.

## 2. Cat or calf?

The weight of the source chain favors **cat**:

- *felino* in Weyer;
- cat in Scot;
- cat in Sloane 3825;
- cat in Sloane 2731;
- cat in Harley 6483;
- cat in de Plancy.

**Calf** first becomes influential through Mathers–Crowley. It is late and textually inferior, though now traditional in its own right.

## 3. Are Aym and Haborym separate?

No early source treats them as separate. Weyer’s *vel* and Scot’s “or” identify alternatives.

**Conclusion:** One dossier with variant names is the best-supported reading.

## 4. Is Aim the same as Raum?

De Plancy reports that “some” made this identification and immediately calls it doubtful. Aim and Raum have different:

- numbers;
- ranks;
- appearances;
- offices;
- legion counts;
- seals.

No early evidence supports the merger.

## 5. Is Aim the same as Amy?

No. Amy is a separate president, number 58 in the Goetia and a separate entry after Aim in Weyer’s list.

The similarity is orthographic and has produced dictionary errors.

## 6. Is Aim the same as Caim/Camio?

No. Caim is Goetia 53, a president associated with birds, animals, waters, disputation, and ashes or burning coals. Fire imagery may have encouraged modern conflation, but the manuscripts distinguish them.

## 7. Does Aim descend from Bast or Sekhmet?

This is an imaginative comparison based on:

- the cat head;
- fire;
- Egyptian feline deities.

No textual or linguistic chain supports it. It should be labeled modern comparative speculation.

## 8. Does Haborym mean “companions”?

The proposed Hebrew *ḥaverim* connection is phonologically and historically unproved. It also fails to explain why Weyer gives *Aym* as the alternative form.

## 9. Does Aim mean “terror”?

Modern pages have proposed Hebrew אים or similar spellings. No early source writes Aim in Hebrew that way, and the vocalization and meaning are not demonstrated. Rudd-era Hebrew spellings are transliterations after the fact, not proof of Hebrew origin.

## 10. Is Aim an ancient demon?

Only in the broad legendary claim that Solomon commanded him.

Historically:

- the Solomonic demon-binding tradition is ancient;
- the exact name and dossier are first secure in 1577;
- a related Florence entry may be older;
- there is no ancient Aim attestation.

## 11. Did Weyer publish the list in 1563?

Weyer’s *De praestigiis* began publication in 1563. The *Pseudomonarchia* appendix is securely present in the fifth edition of 1577. Writing “Weyer, 1563” for Aim compresses the publication history and is misleading.

## 12. Did Solomon write the Goetia?

No evidence supports biblical authorship. The surviving work is a seventeenth-century compilation using sixteenth-century and earlier sources.

“Solomon bound Aim” belongs to the grimoire’s legendary frame.

## 13. Does Aim have a prescribed sacrifice or offering?

Aim’s entry specifies none. General offerings appear for particular spirits such as Paimon, and later practitioners create their own offerings. They should not be attributed to the original Aim notice.

## 14. Does Aim rule fire as an element?

The entry gives a firebrand and an incendiary office. It does not formally call Aim an elemental ruler of Fire.

“Element: Fire” is a reasonable later correspondence, not a quotation from Weyer.

## 15. Does Aim make people malicious or deceptive?

Weyer says *ingeniosum*; Scot says “wittie.” Neither necessarily means malicious. Modern demonology often turns cleverness into cunning, deception, crime, or manipulation. Those are interpretations.

## 16. Is the Aim–Melahel pairing ancient Kabbalah?

No.

- MLH derives from the medieval 72-name construction.
- Aim is securely attested in 1577.
- Pairing demon 23 with angel 23 belongs to the Rudd recension.
- Lenain’s degree and guardian-angel apparatus is nineteenth century.
- Haziel’s birth-date psychology is late twentieth century.

The pairing is historically important within Western ceremonial magic, but not ancient Jewish doctrine.

## 17. Which tarot card belongs to Aim?

Two answers circulate because two questions are being conflated:

- Aim’s **Crowley/777 Goetic decan** → Six of Cups.
- Melahel’s **ordinal Shem quinance** → first half of the Four of Cups decan.

Calling either one uniquely “Aim’s ancient tarot card” is anachronistic.

# Open questions

1. **What exact manuscript stood behind Weyer?**  
   His title *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* is known, but no surviving copy has been proved to be his precise exemplar.

2. **Is Bachimy/Albermi/Cabeym an ancestor of Aim/Haborym?**  
   Shared rank and twenty-six legions are suggestive. The names are too unstable for certainty.

3. **Where did the three-headed imagery originate?**  
   Composite demon bodies are widespread in medieval and early-modern magic. No particular ancient image has been identified as Aim’s model.

4. **Why two stars?**  
   No source explains them. Planetary, dual-luminary, initiatory, or “hidden knowledge” readings are modern symbolism.

5. **How did cat become calf?**  
   Mathers’s working manuscript or notes may have contained the error, but the precise palaeographic step has not been demonstrated.

6. **Who first wrote the seal?**  
   It is absent from Weyer and Scot and appears in the Lemegeton manuscript family. Its graphic construction and any relation to cipher alphabets remain unproved.

7. **Who first paired all 72 demons with all 72 angels?**  
   Rudd-associated material is the best-known witness, but whether Rudd originated the scheme or copied an earlier table is unresolved.

8. **How much of Harley 6483 is genuinely Rudd’s?**  
   The eighteenth-century copy preserves Rudd attribution and material, but authorship of every component remains uncertain.

9. **What was the immediate source of Lenain’s detailed angel notices?**  
   Lenain cites and synthesizes earlier Cabalists, but the exact line-by-line sources of all degree, day, Psalm, and moral attributions remain incompletely mapped.

10. **Can an exact pre-1577 form yet be found?**  
    Newly catalogued or digitized office-of-spirits manuscripts may eventually clarify the Bachimy/Aim relationship. At present, absence before 1577 is the responsible conclusion.
