# Purson: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes:

- **[Text]** Directly documented in a historical source.
- **[Reconstruction]** A scholarly conclusion from comparing sources or manuscripts.
- **[Tradition]** A claim transmitted within magical or religious tradition but not independently demonstrable.
- **[Correspondence]** An occult attribution imposed on the spirit by a later system.
- **[Disputed]** A claim for which sources conflict or the evidence is insufficient.
- **[Legend]** A traditional narrative not presented as historical fact.
- **[Modern]** A modern adaptation, invention, practitioner claim, or fictional use.

Because quotations reproduced from online editions must remain brief, the Weyer, Scot, and *Goetia* sections provide short diplomatic excerpts followed by complete clause-by-clause translations or paraphrases. Page references allow the entire public-domain passages to be checked directly.

## The name and its origins

### 1. The earliest surviving Curson

**[Text]** The earliest presently identifiable occurrence is not Weyer’s 1577 book. The fifteenth-century Munich necromantic handbook, BSB Clm 849, includes **Curson** as the fourth member of an eleven-spirit catalogue. Richard Kieckhefer edited this material in *Forbidden Rites: A Necromancer’s Manual of the Fifteenth Century*. The manuscript itself is available in the [Bavarian State Library facsimile](https://www.digitale-sammlungen.de/de/view/bsb00037155).

Kieckhefer’s comparative table identifies Curson as:

- a king;
- a man with a crowned leonine face;
- carrying a viper;
- riding an animal or, in the table’s English summary, appearing on horseback;
- preceded by trumpets;
- knowing present, past, future, hidden matters, and treasures;
- capable of a human or aerial body;
- answering questions about hidden matters, divinity, and creation;
- providing excellent familiars;
- commanding 22 legions.

**[Reconstruction]** This is recognizably the same catalogue-entry that becomes Weyer’s Pursan/Curson. The agreement is too extensive to rest merely on the similar name: the leonine face, serpent, procession of trumpets, divination, treasures, two kinds of body, cosmological answers, familiars, and 22 legions travel together.

**[Disputed]** The precise route from Clm 849’s source-material to Weyer is unknown. It is safer to say that they inherit a shared catalogue tradition than that Weyer copied the surviving Munich manuscript.

### 2. The *Livre des esperitz*

The French *Livre des esperitz*, surviving in Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29, is another important fifteenth- or early-sixteenth-century witness to the catalogue behind Weyer. Its [complete French text](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre_des_esperitz/Texte_entier) does **not**, however, contain an unambiguous Purson or Curson spelling.

**[Disputed reconstruction]** Some modern comparisons identify its king **Diusion** as an antecedent or displaced analogue of Purson. Diusion is a handsome knight who answers truthfully, reveals buried things, and procures the favor of rulers, with 24 legions. Those offices overlap only partially, and the name does not closely correspond. Diusion should therefore not be printed as a simple Purson synonym.

The *Livre* also has a duke whose name resembles **Gorsin**, but this is not enough to identify him with Curson. Jean-Patrice Boudet’s detailed comparison shows that the *Livre* and Weyer share a large block of catalogue material but also contain spirits absent from one another. His conclusion supports a common or branching tradition rather than a one-to-one translation of every entry: [Boudet, “Les who’s who démonologiques de la Renaissance…”](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/pdf/1019).

### 3. Gorsyar and Gorsior

A closer parallel appears in the *Liber/Officium de spiritibus* family.

**[Text]** Folger MS V.b.26, copied around 1577–1583, describes **Gorsyar** or **Gorsior** as a king with a leonine face and diadem, a fierce viper, a bear, and trumpets. He reveals hidden treasure and answers truthfully. He differs chiefly in being assigned six legions. See the [Folger manuscript record](https://digitalcollections.folger.edu/bib228887-238418) and the [transcribed *Officium*](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/liber-officiorum/read/).

Sloane 3853/Wellcome 110 material likewise has “Gorsior—a kynge” with essentially this appearance and six legions in Joseph Peterson’s [*Offices of Spirits* transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/offices.htm).

**[Reconstruction]** Gorsior/Gorsyar is probably a scribally divergent representative of the Curson catalogue-entry. The total descriptive complex, not merely the name, supports the connection.

**[Disputed]** This does not establish that *Gorsior* was etymologically transformed into *Curson*, or vice versa. Both may descend from a lost form.

### 4. Weyer and Scot: Pursan becomes Purson

**[Text]** Weyer’s 1577 Latin prints **“Pursan, alias Curson.”** Scot’s English version in 1584 gives **“Purson, alias Curson.”** The vowel shift from *Pursan* to *Purson* is therefore first securely visible in Scot.

The later *Lemegeton* manuscripts normally retain **Purson**, sometimes capitalized **PVRSON**. Peterson’s manuscript comparison records:

See Peterson’s [cross-manuscript name table](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia_spirits.pdf).

### 5. Is there an ancient god behind the name?

**[Finding]** No ancient god, hero, ethnic group, or place has been securely identified behind *Curson/Pursan/Purson*.

This distinguishes Purson from names such as:

- Bael/Baal, connected with Northwest Semitic *baʿal*, “lord”;
- Astaroth, ultimately related to Astarte/ʿAštart;
- Amon, sometimes connected—rightly or wrongly in individual sources—with Egyptian Amun;
- Asmoday, connected through Asmodeus to Iranian/Jewish demonological forms;
- Belial, from Hebrew *bĕliyyaʿal*;
- Phenex, transparently influenced by Greek/Latin *phoenix*.

No corresponding linguistic chain exists for Purson.

Several proposed derivations circulate:

- **[Disputed/modern] Latin *persona***, “mask,” “role,” or “person,” sometimes linked to Purson’s ability to take different bodies. The semantics are attractive, but there is no manuscript bridge and the earliest form **Curson** works against treating this as established.
- **[Modern folk etymology] English “person” or “parson.”** The continental Latin manuscript tradition precedes the English spelling and offers no evidence for an English pun.
- **[Modern invention] Horus, Amun-Ra, or another solar lion deity.** These equations generally reason backward from Purson’s title of King, the later solar rank attribution, or the lion face. Neither Weyer nor the medieval witness associates Curson with Egypt. Horus is characteristically falcon-headed, and the names supply no credible philological route.
- **[Coincidental resemblance] Persian *pursān*, “asking/inquiring.”** No historical transmission has been established.
- **[Modern unsourced variants] Potiro, Potirus, Person, Fersone, Pus-ron.** These appear in recent encyclopedias or derivative websites but are not readings of the central Weyer–Scot–Sloane–Harley chain.

The honest etymological verdict is therefore: **unknown**.

## Number, order, and construction of the 72

### Weyer’s 69

**[Text]** The *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*, first added to the fifth edition of Weyer’s *De praestigiis daemonum* in 1577, contains 69 spirits. Purson is no. 11.

### Scot’s 68

**[Text]** Scot translates the catalogue but omits Weyer’s fourth spirit, **Pruflas/Bufas**. Purson is consequently the tenth entry actually printed, although it corresponds to Weyer no. 11.

### The *Goetia*’s 72

**[Text]** The *Ars Goetia* omits Pruflas and supplies four names not present in Weyer’s 69:

1. **Vassago**, inserted as Goetia no. 3;
2. **Seere**, no. 70;
3. **Dantalion**, no. 71;
4. **Andromalius**, no. 72.

Thus 69 minus Pruflas plus four additions produces 72.

The order of the inherited entries is also substantially rearranged. Purson moves from Weyer no. 11 to Goetia no. 20. Peterson observes that portions look as though catalogue leaves or groups were reordered; no surviving manuscript gives the compiler’s explanation. See his [introduction and Weyer comparison](https://www2.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm).

**[Reconstruction]** The completed number 72 probably facilitated comparison with the 72-fold divine name and its angels. That is plausible, especially in Rudd’s subsequent pairing, but the anonymous *Goetia* compiler does not explicitly state, “I changed Weyer’s list in order to match the Shem HaMephorash.”

## The description across the sources

## Fifteenth-century Munich handbook

**[Text]** Curson already possesses almost the complete later identity in Clm 849: king, crowned lion-face, viper, trumpets, divination, treasure, human/aerial embodiment, cosmological knowledge, familiars, and 22 legions.

The most important later change is the mount. The Munich summary indicates horseback or leaves the beast less clearly specified; the later *Officium*, Weyer, Scot, and principal *Goetia* line settle on a bear.

No individual Purson seal belonging to this entry has been identified in the Munich catalogue.

## Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* (1577)

**Edition:** fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum*, Basel, 1577, appendix, spirit 11, approximately pp. 914–915. Online Latin: [Peterson’s Weyer transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

Brief diplomatic excerpt:

> “Pursan, aliàs Curson, magnus Rex … homo facie leonina … ursoque insidens, quem semper præcedunt tubæ.”

Complete semantic rendering:

- **Pursan, otherwise Curson**, is a great king.
- He comes forth as a man with a lion’s face.
- He carries a very fierce viper.
- He rides upon a bear.
- Trumpets always precede him.
- He knows present, past, and future matters.
- He discloses hidden things and reveals treasures.
- He can assume a human body or an aerial one.
- He answers truthfully concerning earthly and hidden things, divinity, and the creation of the world.
- He produces excellent familiars.
- Twenty-two legions obey him.
- Those legions are partly of the order of Virtues and partly of the order of Thrones.

Important textual cautions:

- **[Text]** Weyer says the *legions* are drawn partly from Virtues and Thrones.
- **[Modern overreading]** This is often paraphrased as “Purson himself was formerly both a Virtue and a Throne.” The text does not say that.
- **[Text]** Weyer gives Purson no individual seal.
- **[Text]** No teaching of languages, liberal sciences, love, invisibility, or transmutation is assigned to him.
- **[Text]** His relevant knowledge is divinatory, terrestrial, secret, theological, and cosmological.

Weyer published the catalogue within an argument against witch-persecution and belief in the literal powers ascribed to magicians. His title, “pseudo-monarchy of demons,” is polemical. Publishing the claims does not mean Weyer endorsed them.

## Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584)

**Edition:** London, 1584, Book XV, chapter 2, pp. 218–219. Online transcription: [Peterson’s Scot text](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/scot16.htm).

Brief excerpt:

> “Purson, alias Curson, a great king, he commeth foorth like a man with a lions face…”

Scot’s complete account says that Purson:

- is a great king;
- appears as a lion-faced man;
- carries a “most cruel” viper;
- rides a bear;
- is always preceded by trumpets;
- knows hidden things and everything present, past, and future;
- “bewraieth” treasure—that is, reveals or discloses it;
- takes either a human or aerial body;
- answers truthfully about earthly and secret things, divinity, and creation;
- brings forth the best familiars;
- rules 22 legions, partly Virtues and partly Thrones.

**[Textual history]** Scot’s English closely follows Weyer. Peterson notes that the translation may have been prepared or assisted by Abraham Fleming; attributing every translated sentence personally to Scot is therefore not certain.

## The *Officium de spiritibus* branch

**[Text]** Gorsyar/Gorsior in Folger V.b.26 and related material retains the lion’s face, diadem, viper, bear, trumpets, hidden treasure, and truthful answers. It assigns only six legions.

**[Reconstruction]** The six-legion reading may reflect a different catalogue branch, a copying error, or deliberate alteration. It demonstrates that legion totals were not immune to scribal change.

## Sloane 3825: Peterson’s base text

Peterson’s 2001 edition uses Sloane 3825 as its principal *Goetia* witness, collating Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, Harley 6483, and other copies. Purson appears on fols. 103r–103v; in the 2001 edition, pp. 15–16.

Brief excerpt:

> “The 20th spirit is called Purson a great king; he appeareth commonly like a man wth a Lyons face…”

The complete notice says:

- Purson is the twentieth spirit and a great king.
- His customary form is a lion-faced man.
- He carries a cruel viper.
- He rides a bear.
- Many sounding trumpets go before him.
- He knows hidden things.
- He discovers treasures.
- He tells present, past, and future events.
- He assumes a human or aerial body.
- He answers truthfully about all earthly matters, secret and divine matters, and the creation of the world.
- He brings forth good familiars.
- He governs 22 legions, partly of Virtues and partly of Thrones.
- His mark or seal is the character to which he owes obedience.
- The exorcist is to wear it during the operation.

See Peterson’s [2001 *Lesser Key of Solomon* scan](https://logoilibrary.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Lesser-Key-of-Solomon.pdf) and [online introduction](https://www2.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm).

The important innovation is the seal. Weyer and Scot give none; the *Lemegeton* makes the character an operational lamen.

## Sloane 2731 and other manuscripts

**[Text]** Sloane 2731 spells the name **Purson** and transmits substantially the same description and seal. Sloane 3648 uses **PVRSON** in capitals.

Sloane 2731 has sometimes been dated from a written “1572,” but its actual textual and codicological position is later and complicated. The extant *Lemegeton* family is normally placed in the later seventeenth century. Sloane 3805 explicitly dates its partial copy to 13 January 1685; its catalogue record is summarized at the [Alchemy Website manuscript description](https://www.alchemywebsite.com/mss/mss390.htm).

**[Reconstruction]** The present *Goetia* text probably used a post-1665 edition of Scot or closely related material. A date appearing in a manuscript should not automatically be treated as the date when the entire work was first composed.

## Harley 6483 and the “Rudd” version

Harley 6483 was copied in 1712–1713 by Peter Smart from papers associated with Thomas Rudd. Calling it “Rudd’s manuscript” is conventional shorthand, but the surviving copy is not simply a holograph written throughout by Rudd.

Skinner and Rankine’s 2007 transcription reads, in part:

> “Purson a great King … carrying a cruel viper … and riding on a boar…”

**[Variant]** “Boar” differs from:

- the bear in Weyer;
- the bear in Scot;
- the bear in the *Officium* Gorsior entry;
- the bear in Sloane 3825;
- the bear in Mathers–Crowley.

Whether this is Peter Smart’s reading, a copying error, or a genuine feature of Rudd’s exemplar remains unresolved without a fresh manuscript examination. Skinner and Rankine print the text in *The Goetia of Dr Rudd*, around the Purson entry on p. 101. See the [publisher catalogue](https://www.sskinner.com/pdf/GHP-Catalogue-2022-2023.pdf) and [Llewellyn edition record](https://www.llewellyn.com/product.php?ean=9780738723556).

## Mathers–Crowley, *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King* (1904)

**Edition:** Foyers, Inverness, 1904, p. 28. Online text: [Sacred Texts, Purson entry](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks05.htm).

Brief excerpt:

> “The Twentieth Spirit is Purson, a Great King. His appearance is comely, like a Man with a Lion’s face…”

The complete account retains:

- the lion-faced man;
- cruel viper;
- bear;
- sounding trumpets;
- hidden knowledge;
- treasure-finding;
- past, present, and future;
- human or aerial embodiment;
- truthful terrestrial, secret, divine, and cosmological answers;
- good familiars;
- 22 legions from Virtues and Thrones;
- the seal to which he owes obedience, worn during the operation.

Mathers’s “comely” likely reflects the manuscript adjective rather than a new iconographical feature. Crowley published and framed the edition, adding prefatory and magical material; the underlying translation and manuscript work is substantially Mathers’s.

### Does Purson have a demanded “second form”?

**[Finding]** Not in the manner sometimes implied by modern summaries.

The text says that Purson **can take a human or aerial body**. It does not say that the conjurer must command him to abandon the lion-faced form for a separate second manifestation. Other Goetic entries explicitly prescribe a transformation demanded by the operator; Purson’s notice does not.

### Offices explicitly absent

No principal source in this chain assigns Purson:

- languages;
- rhetoric;
- mathematics;
- astronomy as a liberal science;
- herb or stone lore;
- love or sexual attraction;
- invisibility;
- transmutation;
- bodily flight;
- military protection.

Those belong to other Goetic spirits or later practitioner elaborations. Purson’s actual source-defined domain is hidden knowledge, temporal divination, treasure, cosmology/theology, embodiment, and familiars.

## The seal

### Historical emergence

**[Text]** Neither the Munich catalogue entry nor Weyer nor Scot supplies an individual seal for Purson. The recognizable seal enters the evidence in the seventeenth-century *Lemegeton* manuscript family.

### Form

The familiar Mathers–Crowley seal is a circular lamen containing a broadly bilateral glyph:

- a central upright organizes the design;
- paired curls or looped strokes spread laterally through its middle;
- the upper and lower portions terminate in small hooks or prong-like strokes;
- a short asymmetric flourish appears in some renderings;
- the 1904 printed form regularizes the freehand manuscript character and encloses it within a named circle.

A public-domain rendering can be inspected at [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:20-Purson_seal.png).

### Manuscript variants

**[Text]** Sloane 3825, Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, and Harley 6483 preserve the same underlying character but differ in:

- how tightly the central loops close;
- curvature and length of the lateral strokes;
- whether terminal hooks touch adjoining lines;
- line weight and symmetry;
- the treatment of the surrounding lamen.

**[Open palaeographical limitation]** Available web reproductions do not support a reliable stroke-by-stroke diplomatic apparatus for every witness. Claims that one minor pen-hook constitutes an entirely different “secret” seal are not justified without examining the original folios at full resolution.

### Rudd’s combined angel–demon apparatus

**[Tradition/Text]** Harley 6483 associates each demon with the correspondingly numbered Shem angel. In the Rudd arrangement, the spirit’s character is not used in isolation: the angelic name, angelic character, and biblical verse provide a counter-authority or constraint.

Skinner and Rankine describe this as a safety and control system. The angel is often called the demon’s “thwarting” or “controlling” angel in modern summaries.

**[Caution]** The spirit seal itself is not a medieval Hebrew character. Hebrew spellings such as **PVRSON/PVRShVN** are later attempts to transliterate the European name for gematria or ceremonial use.

## The angel: Pahaliah

### Derivation from Exodus

**[Text/tradition]** Exodus 14:19–21 consists, in the relevant Hebrew consonantal reckoning, of three verses of 72 letters. The 72 triads are produced by writing:

1. verse 19 in its normal Hebrew direction;
2. verse 20 in reverse;
3. verse 21 in the normal direction;

and then reading the three-letter columns. This alternating or boustrophedon procedure yields 72 triplets. The twentieth is **פהל**, Pe-He-Lamed.

A useful historical discussion appears in Joshua Trachtenberg’s [*Jewish Magic and Superstition*](https://sacred-texts.com/jud/jms/jms09.htm) and Valentina Izmirlieva’s [*All the Names of the Lord*](https://academic.oup.com/chicago-scholarship-online/book/22965).

**[Historical caution]** *Shem ha-Mephorash* has not always meant this one 72-fold construction. Jewish sources apply the expression “explicit/distinguished name” to more than one divine-name tradition.

### From triad to angel-name

**[Reconstruction]** The bare triads are divine-name units. Christian cabalists and angelological tables expanded them with endings such as **-el** or **-iah/-yah**, converting them into pronounceable angel names. Johann Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517) is a major printed witness to this Christian-cabalistic angelization and its Latin meanings.

Thus:

- פהל is the extracted triad;
- יה is a divine suffix;
- פהליה becomes Pahaliah.

### Meaning

**[Tradition]** Reuchlin-derived and later tables gloss Pahaliah as “Redeemer” or “Liberator”; Lenain gives **“Dieu rédempteur.”** A modern collation of the Reuchlin meanings is available at [The Seventy-Two Names](https://www.mr-kaplan.com/Seventy%20Two/).

**[Philological caution]** “God the Redeemer” is a received Christian-cabalistic gloss, not a straightforward dictionary translation of the isolated root פהל. The triad was mechanically extracted before the moral meaning was attached.

## Sefer Raziel and the medieval tradition

**[Tradition/Textual history]** The 72-fold name is prominent in versions of *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh*, a medieval compilation containing much earlier materials. It is not historically a single book written in heaven and handed physically to Adam, although that is its framing legend.

**[Legend]** Raziel, angel of divine secrets, gives Adam a book containing celestial and terrestrial wisdom after the expulsion from Eden. Later legendary transmission carries it through Noah and Solomon.

**[Historical finding]** Elements of the compilation were known in the Middle Ages; the familiar printed Hebrew book is considerably later. One should not assume that every modern Pahaliah attribute—five-day guardianship, psychology, color, crystal, incense—appears in an early *Sefer Raziel* text.

## Reuchlin, Agrippa, and Kircher

A simplified line of transmission is:

1. Jewish 72-fold divine-name techniques;
2. medieval *Raziel* and related magical/cabalistic materials;
3. Reuchlin’s Christian Cabala, 1517;
4. Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia*, especially Book III, 1533;
5. Blaise de Vigenère and other Renaissance Christian cabalists;
6. Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus*, 1652–1654;
7. Rudd’s seventeenth-century material, copied in Harley 6483 in 1712–1713;
8. Lenain, 1823;
9. Papus and Golden Dawn-era occultism;
10. Ambelain and Haziel’s twentieth-century angel literature.

Kircher’s tables helped consolidate angelic names, zodiacal organization, divine names, and Psalm material. Kircher transmitted and systematized an inherited Christian-cabalistic construction; he did not discover the Exodus triads independently.

## Rudd’s pairing

**[Text/tradition]** Rudd’s scheme matches ordinal numbers:

- spirit 20: Purson;
- angel 20: Pahaliah.

The angel is placed over, against, or upon the demon’s seal as a divine constraint. The operative logic is that the infernal character alone invokes or commands the spirit, while the corresponding divine/angelic authority compels obedience and protects the exorcist.

**[Disputed attribution]** The system is genuinely present in the Harley 6483 complex, but exactly how much was devised by Thomas Rudd personally, inherited by him, or organized by later copyists remains a matter of manuscript attribution.

## Lenain, *La science cabalistique* (1823)

Lenain’s no. 20 is **Pahaliah**, “Dieu rédempteur.” The printed entry, around pp. 58–59, gives:

- ray from the 96th through 100th ordinal degrees of the zodiac;
- corresponding modern boundaries of 5°–10° Cancer;
- the tenth “decade” or quinance;
- an association with Sothis under the influence of Venus;
- invocation time 6:20–6:40;
- five dispersed calendar days: approximately 8 April, 19/20 June, 30 August/1 September, 10/13 November, and 21/25 January, with discrepancies among editions and later tables;
- Psalm 119:2 in Vulgate numbering;
- power against enemies of religion and for religious conversion;
- government over religion, theology, morality, chastity, piety, and ecclesiastical vocation;
- a contrary genius governing irreligion, apostasy, libertinism, and renegades.

The primary French edition is [Lenain, *La science cabalistique*](https://archive.org/search?query=Lenain+science+cabalistique+1823). A searchable copy is also available through [Scribd](https://fr.scribd.com/document/408555208/1823-lenain-science-cabalistique-pdf).

**[Historical context]** Lenain’s conversion language is explicitly Christian and belongs to his nineteenth-century French framework. It is not an original meaning contained in Exodus.

## Psalm verse

Lenain gives Vulgate Psalm 119:2:

> “Domine, libera animam meam a labiis iniquis, et a lingua dolosa.”

In the Masoretic, Protestant, and most modern numbering, this is Psalm 120:2:

> “Deliver my soul, O LORD, from lying lips, and from a deceitful tongue.”

See [Vulgate Psalm 119:2](https://www.biblegateway.com/passage/?search=Psalmi+119%3A2&version=VULGATE) and the [numbering explanation for Psalm 120](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Psalm_120).

**[Variant]** Some later sources quote Psalm 120:1–2 together. Others assign Pahaliah Psalm 116:4. The latter is not the reading in Lenain’s principal entry and appears to arise from later rearranged tables.

## Papus, Ambelain, and Haziel

- **Papus:** *La Cabbale* and the Papus-associated republication of Lenain transmitted the angelic tables to the French occult revival. See [Papus, *La Cabbale*](https://www.cercle-papus.com/ouvrages/papus-la-cabbale.pdf).
- **Robert Ambelain:** *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) incorporated the 72 angels into a practical, hierarchical ceremonial system, adding seals and sephirotic/archangelic organization. [English scan](https://www.societyofguardians.org/resources/practicalkabbalah1.pdf).
- **Haziel:** The late-twentieth-century books published under the name Haziel popularized the angels as personal guardian intelligences governing consecutive five-day birth periods. In this presentation Pahaliah commonly rules approximately 27 June–1 July.

**[Modern tradition]** The guardian-birthday psychology—personality descriptions, relationships, professions, stones, colors, and life missions—is mostly an Ambelain/Haziel-era development. It must not be retrojected into *Sefer Raziel*, Reuchlin, or Rudd.

### Why one tarot pip carries two spirits

Crowley’s decanic Goetic table assigns:

- 36 decans;
- one day spirit and one night spirit to each decan;
- therefore 72 spirits total.

Each tarot pip from Two through Ten corresponds to one decan. Consequently, each pip carries two Goetic spirits.

Rudd’s Shem system divides the zodiac into 72 five-degree sections. Each ten-degree tarot decan contains two quinances, hence two angel/demon ordinal pairs.

These produce different partners for Purson:

- **Ordinal/Rudd:** Pahaliah–Purson shares the Two of Cups decan with Leuviah–Sallos.
- ***777* day/night:** Purson shares the Three of Swords decan with Gomory.

### Astrological timing

The Mathers–Crowley “Observations” assign chief kings the hours from nine until noon and from three until sunset. The seal of a king is to be made in gold. The relevant [Goetia observations](https://hermetic.com/norton/classics/goetia/observations) and [rank table](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks07.htm) supply these rules.

Later practitioners commonly combine:

- Sunday;
- a planetary hour of the Sun;
- waxing Moon or favorable lunar conditions;
- the Sun’s passage through the spirit’s zodiacal sector;
- Pahaliah’s daily 6:20–6:40 segment.

**[Modern synthesis]** No surviving Purson paragraph commands this combined election. It joins separate rank, zodiacal, angelic, and astrological systems.

## Iconography

## Textual image

The persistent image contains four elements:

1. lion-faced man;
2. fierce viper;
3. bear;
4. trumpet procession.

The crown or diadem is clearest in the Munich and *Officium* material. Weyer does not explicitly say that Purson himself blows a trumpet: the trumpets precede him. Later art often transfers one trumpet into Purson’s own hands.

## Collin de Plancy

Collin de Plancy included an entry under **Pursan ou Curson** in the *Dictionnaire infernal*. The 1863 sixth edition, p. 564, summarizes Weyer:

> “Pursan ou Curson, grand roi de l’enfer… avec une tête qui rappelle le lion…”

The entry says that he:

- is a great infernal king;
- appears as a man whose head resembles a lion;
- carries a viper;
- sometimes rides a bear;
- is preceded by trumpets;
- reveals past, present, future, hidden things, and treasures;
- answers about divinity and creation;
- supplies familiars;
- commands 22 legions.

See the [1863 Wikisource entry](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.%2C_1863/Pursan), the [Library of Congress edition](https://www.loc.gov/item/63056292/), and the [complete scan](https://upload.wikimedia.org/wikipedia/commons/e/e0/Jacques_Collin_de_Plancy_-_Dictionnaire_infernal.pdf).

### Louis Le Breton’s engraving

**[Text/art history]** The illustrated 1863 edition includes a Louis Le Breton image for Pursan. It presents the figure in profile astride a shaggy bear, with a leonine or bestial human head and a conspicuous long trumpet. The engraving condenses the textual procession: rather than invisible heralds sounding before him, the central figure is associated directly with the trumpet.

The image helped establish the now-standard visual shorthand “lion-man on bear with trumpet.” It should not be mistaken for a medieval image. It is nineteenth-century Romantic demonological art.

## Modern art

Modern depictions commonly add:

- a literal lion’s mane;
- royal robes or a crown;
- multiple brass instruments;
- an enormous serpent;
- solar disks and gold;
- Cancerian water imagery;
- anthropomorphic or erotic forms;
- the Goetic seal as jewelry or tattoo.

**[Modern]** These are creative developments. Gold and solar imagery derive from the King correspondence; water and Cancer derive from Pahaliah’s quinance; neither appears in Purson’s medieval description.

## People and works

### Richard Kieckhefer

His 1998 edition of Clm 849 changed the history of the entry by demonstrating that **Curson existed in a fifteenth-century necromantic catalogue**, well before Weyer. This is the most important correction to claims that Purson was invented in 1577.

### Johann Weyer

Weyer printed *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* in 1577. He was a physician and critic of witch persecution. Purson appears as no. 11. Weyer’s polemical purpose complicates the common modern description of him simply as a working demonologist.

### Reginald Scot and Abraham Fleming

Scot’s *Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584) gave the English-reading world “Purson, alias Curson.” Like Weyer, Scot argued against credulous witch prosecutions. The demon catalogue belongs inside that skeptical project. Fleming may have supplied or assisted with the translation.

### The anonymous *Lemegeton* compiler

The compiler or compilers:

- reorganized the catalogue;
- expanded it to 72;
- inserted Purson as no. 20;
- added an individual seal;
- integrated the spirit into an exorcistic ritual system.

### Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

The Harley 6483 complex is associated with Rudd, while the surviving copy was produced by Peter Smart in 1712–1713. Its major contribution is the explicit angel-over-demon system: Pahaliah constrains Purson.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Aleister Crowley

Mathers prepared the English manuscript edition. Crowley published it in 1904 with his own framing material. Crowley’s *777*, first published anonymously in 1909, subsequently located Purson in the daytime second decan of Libra.

### A. E. Waite

Waite reproduced a critical and often hostile summary of ceremonial magic, calling the spirit “Purson, otherwise Curson.” His treatment helped make Goetic material available outside specialist manuscript circles. See [Waite, *The Book of Ceremonial Magic*](https://logoilibrary.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/bookofceremonialmagic.pdf).

### Collin de Plancy and Louis Le Breton

De Plancy carried the Weyer account into nineteenth-century French demonology. Le Breton provided the influential portrait.

### Lenain and Papus

Lenain systematized the 72 angelic names, degrees, times, Psalms, and moral offices in 1823. Papus transmitted Lenain’s system into French occult revivalism.

### Robert Ambelain and Haziel

Ambelain reorganized the angels into a practical twentieth-century cabalistic system. Haziel’s popular books transformed the quinances into guardian-angel birth periods and personality profiles.

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 edition collated the *Lemegeton* manuscripts and supplied the most accessible critical comparison of Weyer, Scot, Sloane 3825, Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, and Harley 6483. His online texts are foundational for checking exact wording and spelling.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their *Goetia of Dr Rudd* (2007) brought Harley 6483’s angelic control system into print, including Pahaliah’s pairing with Purson and the noteworthy “boar” reading.

### Poke Runyon

Runyon’s *Book of Solomon’s Magick* incorporates the complete Goetic catalogue into his modern ceremonial system. His tables associate Purson with Pahaliah and sometimes with the cardinal king Corson.

**[Modern reconstruction]** Runyon’s dramatic, initiatory, and “neo-romantic” presentation is not a diplomatic reproduction of the seventeenth-century manuscripts. His **Corson/Purson** association should not erase the source distinction between Purson/Curson and Corson, one of the four cardinal kings.

### Lon Milo DuQuette and Christopher Hyatt

*Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* (1992) popularized the Golden Dawn/Crowley decan table. It gives Purson approximately 10°–20° Libra, daytime, Three of Swords, while retaining Sun and gold for his rank as King.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s *Encyclopaedia Goetica* and *True Grimoire* writings place the *Goetia* within a larger European spirit-catalogue tradition and restore attention to names such as Pruflas. No independently documented Purson-specific operation by him was located in the sources consulted.

### Owen Davies, Jan Veenstra, Julien Véronèse, Jean-Patrice Boudet

These scholars contribute to the wider history of grimoires, medieval spirit magic, and Solomonic texts. Boudet is particularly important for comparing Weyer with the *Livre des esperitz*. Véronèse’s work illuminates medieval ritual-magic transmission more generally; it does not by itself prove a particular Purson identification.

### Frater Achad

**[Negative finding]** No reliable Purson-specific treatment or recorded working by Frater Achad was found. General participation in Crowleyan occultism is not sufficient evidence that he wrote about or invoked this particular spirit.

## Grimoire ancestors and collateral texts

### *Testament of Solomon*

**[Finding]** Purson/Curson is absent from the ancient or late-antique *Testament of Solomon*. That work contains Ornias, Beelzeboul, Asmodeus, Onoskelis, Ephippas, the decan spirits, and other entities, but not Purson.

The shared theme of Solomon commanding demons is a broad legendary ancestor, not evidence that Purson was known in antiquity.

### Munich handbook, Clm 849

This is the earliest concrete source presently identifiable for Curson.

### *Livre des esperitz*

It belongs to the same broad catalogue environment but lacks a secure Curson spelling. Diusion is at most a disputed analogue.

### *Liber/Officium de spiritibus*

Gorsyar/Gorsior supplies the strongest parallel outside the direct Weyer spelling chain.

### *Book of Abramelin*

**[Negative finding]** No secure Purson, Curson, or Pursan entry was found in the principal Abramelin spirit lists consulted. Some names in Abramelin overlap later Solomonic catalogues, but that does not justify inserting Purson without a manuscript reading.

### Weyer to Scot to *Lemegeton*

This is the demonstrable early-modern printed chain, although the *Lemegeton* compiler also had access to manuscript or ritual materials beyond the printed catalogue.

His family’s concealment or anti-recognition ability creatively echoes hiddenness, while his musical role invites comparison with Purson’s trumpets. The name is attested; interpreting every character trait as a deliberate grimoire reference remains inferential.

**[Modern fiction]** A character named Purson occurs among the series’ many demonologically named beings. The borrowing is nominal; the medieval offices are not reproduced systematically.

A creature named Purson appears in *Final Fantasy XI*-related game documentation, including current private-server catalogues such as [HorizonXI](https://horizonffxi.wiki/Purson). The name functions as a monster-name borrowing.

**[Modern music]** Rosalie Cunningham formed the English psychedelic/occult-rock band Purson in 2011. Cunningham explicitly explained that the group found the name while searching demonology and liked the figure’s lion head, bear, serpent, and trumpets. ## Controversies and disputes

### 1. “Purson first appeared in Weyer”

**Verdict:** False as a statement about surviving evidence.

Curson occurs in fifteenth-century Clm 849. Weyer is the earliest known printed attestation of **Pursan**, not the earliest attestation of the entity.

### 2. Curson, Gorsior, and Diusion

- Curson to Pursan/Purson is strongly supported by the complete description.
- Gorsior/Gorsyar is probably a cognate catalogue-entry, but its six legions and divergent name require caution.
- Diusion is a much weaker proposed parallel. It should not be treated as an established spelling of Purson.

### 3. Bear or boar

- Munich: horseback or incompletely specified animal in modern summaries.
- *Officium*: bear.
- Weyer: *ursus*, bear.
- Scot: bear.
- Sloane 3825 and Mathers–Crowley: bear.
- Skinner and Rankine’s Harley 6483 transcription: boar.

The weight of the tradition favors **bear**. “Boar” remains a genuine late witness variant worth recording.

### 4. Who sounds the trumpets?

The older language says trumpets precede Purson. It does not necessarily say Purson plays them. Le Breton and much modern art give the central figure a trumpet, visually collapsing herald and king.

The widespread phrase “internal trumpets” is not in Weyer, Scot, or the standard *Goetia* text and appears to be a modern corruption or interpretive embellishment.

### 5. Is Purson a fallen Virtue and Throne?

No primary notice says that. It says his legions are partly from those orders. Turning this into Purson’s personal pre-fall biography is a later inference.

### 6. Pahaliah’s meaning

“God the Redeemer” is a traditional Christian-cabalistic meaning. It is not a secure lexical derivation from the extracted letters פהל. Treat it as an inherited gloss, not historical Hebrew etymology.

### 7. Which Psalm?

The strongest Lenain-line reading is Vulgate 119:2, equivalent to modern Psalm 120:2. Psalm 120:1–2 is an expanded later practice. Psalm 116:4 belongs to variant modern tables.

### 8. Which zodiac sign and tarot card?

Both of the following are historically real later correspondences, but they belong to different arrangements:

- Pahaliah/Purson by Rudd ordinal number: Cancer, Two of Cups.
- Purson in Crowley’s Goetic decan table: Libra, Three of Swords.

A source that says “Crowley’s *777* assigns Purson 5°–10° Cancer” has conflated the systems.

### 9. Solar king versus Venus or Saturn

There are three different planetary statements:

- Sun: Purson’s rank as King.
- Venus: ruler of Pahaliah’s Cancer decan.
- Saturn: ruler of Purson’s *777* Libra decan.

They answer different questions and need not be forced into one “true planet.”

### 10. Corson

**Corson**, one of the four cardinal or directional kings invoked in some *Goetia* operations, is not automatically Purson/Curson. Similar spelling encouraged modern conflation, especially in synthetic systems, but the source texts normally distinguish the Goetic spirit Purson from the superior king Corson.

### 11. Egyptian or pagan-god identity

No evidence links Purson historically to Horus, Amun-Ra, Sekhmet, Mithras, Apollo, or another ancient deity. Lion and solar symbolism make such comparisons imaginative, not genealogical.

### 12. Number 72 as proof of antiquity

The completed 72-spirit catalogue does not prove an ancient Hebrew origin for each demon. Purson’s earlier catalogue position is 11, not 20. Matching Purson to Pahaliah depends on the *Goetia*’s later order.

### 13. Demonic “enns”

Modern demonolatry supplies vocal formulas or “enns” for Purson. These do not occur in Weyer, Scot, Sloane 3825, Sloane 2731, or Harley 6483. They are modern liturgical inventions, whatever their value within contemporary practice.

### 14. De Plancy as an independent witness

De Plancy is important for reception history and iconography, but his descriptive text chiefly recycles Weyer. It is not independent evidence for Purson’s medieval form.

## Open questions

1. **What is the original name-form?**  
   Curson is earliest, but Gorsior/Gorsyar could preserve another branch or an older consonantal structure. No etymon has been established.

2. **What animal is intended in Clm 849?**  
   A fresh diplomatic reading of the Latin and any adjacent iconography could clarify whether the earliest witness means an ordinary horse, an unnamed mount, or something later transformed into a bear.

3. **How directly are Clm 849, the *Officium*, and Weyer related?**  
   Their common descriptive blocks imply shared source-material, but the lost intermediary catalogues have not been reconstructed completely.

4. **Is Gorsior truly Curson?**  
   The descriptive agreement is strong; the name and six-legion count prevent certainty.

5. **Does Diusion belong in Purson’s genealogy?**  
   At present this is only a tentative catalogue alignment. It should not be represented as fact.

6. **What exactly does Harley 6483 read at the mount?**  
   Skinner and Rankine print “boar.” Direct examination under high-resolution imaging could distinguish an original word, correction, or editorial reading.

7. **Can every seal be collated stroke by stroke?**  
   This requires new high-resolution images of the relevant Sloane and Harley folios. Printed redrawing has obscured small scribal differences.

8. **Who designed the 72-spirit order?**  
   The compiler’s identity and precise reason for the rearrangement remain unknown.

9. **How much of the angelic apparatus is Thomas Rudd’s?**  
   Harley 6483 documents the system, but the division among Rudd’s work, inherited papers, Peter Smart’s copying, and later organization remains unsettled.

10. **When was Pahaliah first printed in exactly that spelling?**  
    Reuchlin is an early major printed witness to the angelized 72 names, but a comprehensive incunabular and manuscript survey could possibly identify an earlier full form.

11. **Did the *Goetia* reach 72 specifically to enable the Shem pairing?**  
    It is a persuasive reconstruction, not an explicit statement by the compiler.

12. **Are alleged modern media appearances canonical?**  
    Purson is frequently added to franchise wikis because the franchise uses other Goetic names. Each claim needs primary credits, scripts, bestiaries, or episode evidence.

---

## Overall conclusion

Purson is not an ancient pagan god reduced to demonhood, so far as the evidence shows. Nor was he invented by Weyer. The earliest surviving form is **Curson**, a king in a fifteenth-century necromantic catalogue whose appearance and offices already closely match the later spirit.

The documented development is:

- fifteenth-century **Curson** in Clm 849;
- parallel or cognate **Gorsyar/Gorsior** material in the *Officium de spiritibus* tradition;
- **Pursan, alias Curson**, Weyer no. 11 in 1577;
- **Purson, alias Curson**, in Scot’s 1584 English;
- **Purson**, reorganized as no. 20 and supplied with a seal in the seventeenth-century *Ars Goetia*;
- **Pahaliah** placed over Purson by ordinal number in the Rudd/Harley 6483 system;
- standardized print and art through Mathers, Crowley, de Plancy, and Le Breton;
- divergent twentieth-century astrological, guardian-angel, psychological, artistic, and fictional adaptations.

His stable historical core is unusually coherent: a leonine king with serpent, mount, and trumpets; revealer of secrets, treasures, time, divinity, and creation; giver of familiars; ruler of 22 legions. The bear, although challenged by Harley’s boar, is the dominant reading. The etymology remains unknown. The most persistent modern error is not a misspelling but a conflation of layers: medieval catalogue description, seventeenth-century seal, Rudd’s Cancerian angelic pairing, and Crowley’s Libra decan are repeatedly presented as though they came from one source.
