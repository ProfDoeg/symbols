# Phenex: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes the status of claims as follows:

- **[Documented text]** — stated in a named primary source or visible manuscript witness.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** — a conclusion proposed from textual comparison, dating, paleography, or historical context.
- **[Tradition]** — part of a transmitted religious, magical, or folkloric system, without implying historical or metaphysical fact.
- **[Esoteric correspondence]** — an occult attribution imposed on the spirit or angel by a later system.
- **[Disputed]** — a reading or historical claim for which the sources conflict or the evidence is inadequate.
- **[Legend]** — a supernatural narrative reported by a source.
- **[Modern invention]** — a recent elaboration not found in the historical source chain.

“Phenex” is treated here as a textual and cultural figure. Descriptions of conjuration, angels, seals, and astrological timing report what historical or modern sources say; they do not establish that the entities or operations are objectively real.

## The name and its origins

### The source-chain spellings

Joseph H. Peterson’s manuscript comparison supplies the clearest synopsis of the spellings. Phenex’s row reads as follows: Weyer **Phœnix**; Scot **Phoenix**; Sloane 3825 **Phoenix**; Sloane 3648 **Phenex**; Sloane 2731 **Phoenix**—the manuscript transcription itself reads **Phenix**; Harley 6483 **Phenix**; Northwestern University MS 65 **Phenix**; Mathers–Crowley **Phenex (or Pheynix)**; Waite **Phoenix**; and Wellcome MS 3203 **Phenix**. **[Documented text]** Contrary to a recurring online assumption, **Phenex** was not simply invented by Crowley in 1904. It occurs in Sloane 3648. Crowley’s conspicuous contribution is the printed alternative **Pheynix**.

**[Disputed]** **Phoeniex** appears widely in modern encyclopedic and demonological websites, but it is absent from Peterson’s comparison of Weyer, Scot, the major Goetia manuscripts, Mathers–Crowley, Waite, Shah, and Wellcome 3203. It is best treated as a modern transposition or spelling error unless an earlier witness is produced.

### Greek and Latin background

**[Documented linguistic fact]** Latin *phoenix* derives from Greek **φοῖνιξ**, *phoînix*. The Greek word has several historically attested senses:

- a Phoenician;
- purple or crimson;
- a date palm;
- the fabulous phoenix bird.

These senses are recorded in the [Liddell–Scott–Jones lexicon](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn%3Acite2%3Ascaife-viewer%3Adictionaries.v1%3Alsj-n112002/).

**[Disputed etymology]** It is tempting to translate the name as “the crimson one” or “Phoenician bird.” Those are plausible associations, not a settled etymology. Specialists disagree over whether *phoînix* belongs to a Greek root connected with blood-red or purple, is a Pre-Greek word, or is an eastern loan. A recent linguistic discussion characterizes its etymology as “fraught with uncertainty” and surveys sharply divergent proposals. [Journal of Hellenic Studies discussion](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/journal-of-hellenic-studies/article/deconstructing-the-phoenician-myth-cadmus-and-the-palmleaf-tablets-revisited/D4E45EBCCFD0177FAADF8047DA514BDE)

### The ancient bird

**[Documented ancient text]** Herodotus, *Histories* 2.73, reports an Egyptian story about a sacred phoenix associated with Heliopolis. He says he had seen only a picture of it. It was eagle-sized, gold and red, appeared at five-hundred-year intervals, and carried its dead father to the temple of the Sun in an egg or casing of myrrh. Herodotus does not describe spontaneous combustion or resurrection from its own ashes. [Herodotus 2.73](https://lexundria.com/hdt/2.73/mcly)

**[Tradition]** The familiar fire, self-immolation, and rebirth imagery was elaborated through later Greek, Roman, and Christian literature. Ovid, Pliny, Tacitus, early Christian writers, and medieval bestiaries progressively made the phoenix an emblem of recurrence, immortality, and resurrection.

**[Scholarly reconstruction; disputed]** The Egyptian **Bennu**—a heron-like sacred bird connected with Heliopolis, the Sun, creation, and renewal—is often proposed as an ancestor or analogue of the Greek phoenix. The resemblance is substantial, but an exact one-to-one derivation is not demonstrable. Egyptian accounts of Bennu do not reproduce the complete classical self-burning phoenix narrative.

**[Modern esoteric reconstruction]** Poke Runyon and some later authors explicitly identify Phenex with the Egyptian Bennu. That is symbolically understandable but is not stated by the *Livre des Esperitz*, Weyer, Scot, the Goetia manuscripts, Rudd, or Mathers–Crowley.

### God, hero, or place?

Unlike Baal, Astarte, Amun, Asmodeus/Ashmedai, or Belial, Phenex has no demonstrable ancient god behind the demonic name.

- **[Documented]** The demon’s name is transparently that of the fabulous bird in Weyer: *apparet uti avis phœnix*, “he appears like the phoenix bird.”
- **[Disputed]** Bennu is a possible mythological ancestor of the bird, not evidence that Phenex was formerly an Egyptian god.
- **[No evidence]** The Greek hero Phoenix, tutor of Achilles, has no documented connection with this spirit.
- **[No evidence]** Phoenicia as a place or the Phoenicians as a people are not invoked by any source in the demonological chain.

The best historical explanation is therefore mundane: a catalogue spirit was named for, or transformed into, the fabulous phoenix bird.

## The description across the sources

## 1. *Livre des Esperitz*: the earliest surviving Fenix

The *Livre des Esperitz* survives in Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29, fols. 179r–182v. Its date is contested. Older scholarship often placed it in the fifteenth century; paleographical arguments have also placed the hand as late as the middle of the sixteenth century. It is therefore a possible pre-Weyer attestation, but not a securely dated one.

### Old French, fol. 182r

> **Fenix est vng grand marquis q[ui] appert en belle figure, et [a] la voix moult doulce, et il est courtoys, et [respont] tresbien a toutes les choses q[ue] on luy veult demander, [quant] on [le] commande. Et si les fait ygnelement [promptement] sans dilation. Et a XXV (25) legions.**

A close translation is:

> Fenix is a great marquis who appears in a fair form and has a very sweet voice; he is courteous and answers very well all the things that one wishes to ask him, when he is commanded. And he performs them promptly, without delay. He has twenty-five legions.

The manuscript and transcription are available in the [digital *Livre des Esperitz*, fol. 182r](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/livre-esperitz/read/#f182r); see also Jean-Patrice Boudet’s study, [“Les who’s who démonologiques de la Renaissance et leurs ancêtres médiévaux”](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/1019).

### What is and is not present

**[Documented text]**

Present:

- great Marquis;
- fair appearance;
- very sweet voice;
- courtesy;
- answers questions;
- acts promptly;
- 25 legions.

Absent:

- an explicit phoenix-bird appearance;
- a child’s voice;
- dangerous or distracting singing;
- compelled human form;
- wonderful sciences;
- poetic ability;
- hope of returning to a celestial throne;
- 20 legions;
- a seal.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The close name, rank, voice, disposition, and position near Stolas make this Fenix a strong textual ancestor or cognate of Weyer’s Phœnix. But Weyer’s much fuller description cannot be derived word for word from this surviving entry. Either Weyer used a fuller branch of the *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* tradition, or the entry was expanded between the two witnesses.

## 2. Weyer’s *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*, 1577

Weyer added the *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* to the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum* in 1577. Phenex is §68, p. 931.

### Latin, verbatim

> **[§ 68.] Phœnix magnus Marchio: apparet uti avis phœnix puerili voce: sed antequam se sistit coram exorcista, cantus emittit dulcissimos: tunc autem cavendum exorcistæ cum suis sociis, ne suavitati cantus aures accommodent, sed ille mox huic iubeat humanam assumere speciem, tunc mirè loquetur de cunctis scientijs admirandis. Poeta est optimus & obediens. Sperat se post mille ducentos annos ad septimum thronum rediturum. Viginti præest legionibus.**

[Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia*, §68](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

### Literal English gloss

> Phœnix is a great Marquis. He appears as the phoenix bird, with a childlike voice. But before he stations himself before the exorcist, he sends forth the sweetest songs. Then the exorcist and his companions must take care not to lend their ears to the sweetness of the song, but he must immediately command him to assume human form. Then he will speak wonderfully about all wonderful sciences. He is an excellent poet and obedient. He hopes that after twelve hundred years he will return to the seventh throne. He presides over twenty legions.

### Analysis

**[Documented text]**

- Rank: *magnus Marchio*, great Marquis.
- First form: phoenix bird.
- Voice: childlike.
- Preliminary manifestation: exceptionally sweet song.
- Instruction: do not “lend the ears” to its sweetness.
- Second form: human, if commanded.
- Office: discourse on all wonderful sciences.
- Additional character: excellent poet and obedient.
- Legend: return to the seventh throne after 1,200 years.
- Legions: 20.

**[Important negative finding]** Weyer never states what happens if the conjurer listens. “Siren-like,” entrancement, death, soul theft, or possession are modern extrapolations.

**[Important negative finding]** Weyer does not say that Phenex is “deceived in this hope.” That warning occurs in some parallel spirit entries, especially Marchosias in later versions, and has been transferred to Phenex by modern summaries.

**[Legend]** The return after 1,200 years is reported as Phenex’s hope. It is not a historical chronology and cannot be dated from the reign of Solomon.

## 3. Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, 1584

The English catalogue appears in Book XV, chapter 2. Peterson considers it likely that the translation was made by Abraham Fleming, who translated other Latin material for Scot, but that attribution is inferential.

### Scot’s English, verbatim, first edition p. 391

> **[68] Phœnix [Phoenix] is a great marquesse, appearing like the bird Phœnix, having a childs voice: but before he standeth still before the conjuror, he singeth manie sweet notes. Then the exorcist with his companions must beware he give no eare to the melodie, but must by and by bid him put on humane shape; then will he speake marvellouslie of all woonderfull sciences. He is an excellent poet, and obedient, he hopeth to returne to the seventh throne after a thousand two hundreth yeares, and governeth twentie legions.**

[Scot, first-edition scan](https://archive.org/details/discouerieofwitc00scot/page/391/mode/1up) and [full searchable text](https://archive.org/stream/discouerieofwitc00scot/discouerieofwitc00scot_djvu.txt)

**[Documented text]** Scot closely follows Weyer. His “give no eare to the melodie” became the immediate English source of much of the Goetia wording.

**[Historical context]** Scot printed the conjurations and spirit catalogue as part of a skeptical attack on witchcraft prosecutions and clerical fraud. Weyer likewise opposed the execution of alleged witches, although his own position on spirits and magic was more complex than simple modern disbelief.

## 4. Sloane MS 2731

The online diplomatic edition identifies Sloane 2731 as a 1686 manuscript. Its Phenex entry occurs on manuscript page 9.

### Verbatim manuscript transcription

> **37 The 37th spirit in order is Phenix, he is a Great Marques; and appeareth like the Bird Phenix; haveing a Childs voyce; he singeth many rare sweet Notes before the Exorcist, which he must Not Regard; but by & by he must bid him put on human shap; then he will Speak [aptly] of all wonderfull Sciences; if Desired, he is a good and Excellent poet; and willing he will doe your Request; he hath hopes to Returne to the 7th Throne after 1200 yeers afore, as he said to Salomon; he Governeth 20 Legions of Spirits, his seal is thus to be Made & worne, &c**

[Sloane 2731 transcription and page image](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/goetia-sloane2731/read/#p012)

The reading “1200 yeers afore” is awkward and may reflect scribal corruption. Other manuscripts and editions read “after 1200 years more.”

### Additions and changes

Compared with Scot, Sloane 2731:

- numbers Phenix 37 rather than 68;
- intensifies the notes as “many rare sweet Notes”;
- makes the performance of requests explicit;
- introduces “as he said to Salomon”;
- supplies an individual seal.

The manuscript still does not describe Phenex’s human appearance.

### Offices not belonging to Phenex

**[Negative documented finding]** The historical Phenex entries do **not** grant:

- languages;
- treasures;
- love or sexual attraction;
- familiars;
- invisibility;
- transmutation;
- weather control;
- military power;
- healing;
- explicit prophecy.

The earliest Fenix answers questions generally. The later Phenex speaks about sciences, composes poetry, obeys, and performs requests. Importing other Goetic offices into Phenex is a modern conflation.

## Number and order

### Weyer’s 69 and the Goetia’s 72

**[Documented textual history]** Weyer’s catalogue contains 69 spirits even though its Belial entry refers to 72 kings or spirits. The Goetia reaches 72 through this arithmetic:

- starts from Weyer’s 69;
- omits **Pruflas/Bufas**, Weyer’s no. 4, which was already absent from Scot;
- adds **Vassago** as Goetia no. 3;
- adds **Seere**, **Dantalion**, and **Andromalius** as nos. 70–72.

Thus 69 − 1 + 4 = 72. Peterson notes that the close dependence on Scot, including Scot’s omission of Pruflas, may help date the formation of the Goetia. [Peterson’s discussion](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

### Why Phenex moves from 68 to 37

The Goetia compiler did not preserve Weyer’s order. The entries appear to have been rearranged in blocks, perhaps through the handling or disordering of exemplar leaves. Peterson compares it to a scrambled stack of pages, while emphasizing that the precise cause remains unknown.

Phenex is:

- no. 68 in Weyer;
- no. 68 in Scot;
- no. 37 in the standard Goetia;
- immediately preceded by Stolas and followed by Halphas in the Goetia.

In Weyer, the order is Haagenti 67, Phœnix 68, Stolas 69. The Goetia moves Stolas immediately before Phenex.

### The misleading “Weyer no. 67”

**[Documented bibliographical error]** Some modern Rudd and Goetic tables call Phenex “Wierus 67.” In the original 1577 numbering it is §68.

**[Scholarly explanation]** The 1660 *Opera Omnia* printing repeated a numbered section around §38. Subsequent automatic numbering then shifted later entries by one. “Weyer 67” can therefore record dependence on that defective later numbering rather than a genuinely different demon order.

## Rank, metal, planet, and time

### Marquis: Moon and silver

**[Esoteric correspondence]** The rank-to-planet system printed with the Mathers–Crowley Goetia assigns:

| Rank | Planet | Metal |
|---|---|---|
| King | Sun | Gold |
| Duke | Venus | Copper |
| Prince/Prelate | Jupiter | Tin |
| Marquis | Moon | Silver |
| President | Mercury | Mercury |
| Earl/Count | Venus | Copper |
| Knight | Saturn | Lead |

Phenex, as a Marquis, is therefore lunar and silver in that system. [Mathers–Crowley classified list](https://hermetic.com/norton/classics/goetia/classified-list-of-the-72-chief-spirits-of-the-goetia-according-to-respective-rank)

**[Historical qualification]** Weyer’s individual Phenex entry says nothing about the Moon or silver. Nor does the *Livre des Esperitz*. These are later ritual classifications of rank.

### Time for Marquises

Weyer’s general instructions state:

> **Item Marchiones à nona usque ad completorium, vel à completorio usque ad finem diei.**

Scot renders it:

> **Marquesses may be bound from the ninth houre till compline, and from compline till the end of the daie.**

[Weyer and Scot, timing rules](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

**[Documented text]** *Nona* is the canonical hour of None, conventionally associated with the ninth daylight hour and approximately 3 p.m. Compline is the night office.

**[Disputed modern interpretation]** Modern tables often reduce the instruction to “3 p.m.–9 p.m.” This is only an approximation. Canonical hours varied seasonally and institutionally, and Weyer’s wording gives two connected or alternative windows rather than a modern fixed clock schedule.

## The seal

### Earliest appearance

**[Documented text]**

- The *Livre des Esperitz* provides no Fenix seal.
- Weyer’s printed catalogue provides no individual Phœnix seal.
- Scot provides no individual seal.
- Individual Phenex/Phenix seals appear in the Goetia manuscript tradition.

Sloane 2731 labels the image “Phenix his seal” and then instructs that it be “Made & worne.” [Sloane 2731 image and transcription](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/goetia-sloane2731/read/#p012)

### Description

Because the sources communicate the seal visually rather than verbally, no prose description can be “exact” in the same sense as a facsimile.

The common core is:

- a horizontally extended, angular glyph;
- short vertical and cross-like strokes at the left and center;
- small circular or dot-like terminals;
- a conspicuous loop, oval, or hooked enclosure at the right;
- in many printed versions, an enclosing circular border that distinguishes the lamen as an object.

The familiar 1904 version can be inspected as a [facsimile of the Seal of Phenex](https://www.fromoldbooks.org/Mathers-Goetia/pages/037-Seal-of-Phenex/) or as a [public-domain vector derived from the printed form](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:37-phenex-sigilo-ars-goetia.svg).

### Manuscript variants

**[Documented scribal variation]** Sloane 2731, Sloane 3825, and Harley 6483 preserve the same recognizable character family, but differ in:

- the angle and length of the left-hand bars;
- whether small endpoints are dots, rings, or simple pen terminals;
- the proportions and closure of the right loop;
- the spacing between the cross-strokes and loop;
- whether an outer circular lamen-border is emphasized.

**[Caution]** These are normal manuscript-copying differences. No early source explains the individual strokes or says that one microscopic variant is uniquely valid.

### Harley 6483’s double-seal context

**[Tradition documented in Rudd material]** Harley 6483 places demonic seals in a larger protective structure involving the corresponding Shem angel. Skinner and Rankine call these “double seals”: the spirit’s character is controlled or counterbalanced by the angelic name, character, and Psalm.

The angelic material is an added control layer, not an earlier alternative version of the Phœnix seal.

## The angel: Aniel

### Name and meaning

**[Documented extraction]** The triplet behind Aniel is **אני**, formed from one letter in each of Exodus 14:19–21. The three verses each contain 72 Hebrew letters in the traditional consonantal text. They are arranged in alternating directions—first forward, second backward, third forward—so that 72 vertical three-letter units result.

**[Historical qualification]** *Shem ha-Mephorash*, “the explicit/distinguished Name,” does not always mean this 72-fold construction in Jewish sources. Earlier rabbinic usage can refer to the Tetragrammaton, while other texts speak of names of 12, 42, or 72 letters. Treating the phrase as exclusively synonymous with “72 guardian angels” is a later occult simplification.

**[Tradition]** Christian kabbalists made the triplets pronounceable as angelic names by adding divine suffixes such as **-el** or **-yah**. Thus אני becomes Aniel/Anaiel.

**[Disputed etymology]** Lenain’s “God of virtues” is a traditional occult attribute, not a secure lexical translation of the Hebrew triplet. Hebrew *ani* can mean “I,” but explanations such as “I am God” are speculative and ignore the mechanical extraction of the letters.

**[Technical note]** The numerical value of the bare triplet אני is 61: 1 + 50 + 10. The fully expanded אניאל has a different value. Websites that call “Aniel” 61 are usually calculating the triplet while displaying the expanded angel name.

## From Jewish mysticism to Christian angelology

### Medieval Jewish sources

**[Documented tradition]** The 72-fold divine Name is found in medieval kabbalistic transmission associated with the *Bahir* and the *Sefer Raziel* complex. The surviving *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* is itself a compilation of different materials and textual periods; the commonly consulted printed edition appeared at Amsterdam in 1701.

**[Historical qualification]** These sources transmit divine names and angelological techniques, but they do not contain the complete nineteenth-century package of five-day birth rulerships, psychological descriptions, exact civil-calendar dates, and paired Goetic demons.

### Reuchlin and Christian Kabbalah

**[Documented text]** Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517) presented the 72 triplets as angelic names made pronounceable with divine suffixes. His treatment influenced later Christian kabbalists, including Agrippa and Kircher.

Blaise de Vigenère’s *Traicté des chiffres* (1586) reproduced and developed the material. Rudd’s angel tables are especially close to the Reuchlin–Vigenère branch.

### Agrippa and Kircher

**[Tradition]** Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa transmitted the 72-name material in the Christian magical synthesis surrounding *De occulta philosophia*. Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–1654) displayed the 72 names in elaborate kabbalistic diagrams and discussed them within his universalizing Christian interpretation of Egyptian and Hebrew wisdom.

Kircher’s famous diagrams helped make the 72-name system visually canonical, although Kircher’s scholarship and decipherments are not accepted as modern Egyptology. [Peterson’s Kircher material](https://www.esotericarchives.com/juratus/kircher.htm)

## Rudd’s pairing and constraint

### Manuscript provenance

Harley MS 6483 is a fair copy made around 1712–1713 by the lawyer Peter Smart. It transmits material attributed to the English mathematician and magician Dr Thomas Rudd, who died in 1656.

**[Disputed provenance]** Stephen Skinner and David Rankine argue that the underlying Rudd material was assembled around 1655–1656. The surviving manuscript is later, and the precise extent of Rudd’s personal authorship cannot be independently demonstrated for every section.

### Phenex and Aniel

**[Documented manuscript tradition]** In the Rudd arrangement, the 37th Goetic spirit, **Phenix**, corresponds to the 37th Shem angel, **Aniel**.

**[Ritual tradition]** Rudd’s innovation is not merely to list an angel next to a demon. The appropriate angel, divine names, and Psalm are invoked as superior authority before or during the evocation of the corresponding spirit. Aniel therefore constrains, balances, or controls Phenix.

Skinner and Rankine interpret this as a missing protective key to Goetic practice: the 72 angels are the celestial counterweights of the 72 spirits. See Thomas Rudd, Stephen Skinner, and David Rankine, *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (Golden Hoard Press, 2007). [WorldCat edition record](https://search.worldcat.org/title/637468388)

**[Scholarly caution]** The idea that every historical Goetia operation always required its same-number Shem angel is not supported by Weyer, Scot, Sloane 2731, or Sloane 3825. It is specifically the Rudd recension’s method.

## Lenain’s Aniel, 1823

Lazare Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* gives the fullest early nineteenth-century notice.

### Text and translation

Lenain identifies:

> **37e. Aniel … Son attribut (Dieu des vertus).**

He assigns Aniel’s ray from the 181st through the 185th degree of the sphere, corresponding to the nineteenth “decade” and the genius Souchoë under lunar influence. He gives five subsidiary calendar days—25 April, 6 July, 16 September, 27 November, and 7 February—and an invocation time from noon to 12:20.

Lenain says Aniel:

- serves “to obtain victory and to raise the siege of a city”;
- rules sciences and arts;
- reveals secrets of nature;
- inspires wise philosophers in meditation;
- gives those born under the influence fame through talent and enlightenment.

The contrary genius rules perverse spirits, charlatans, and those skilled in deceiving others.

[Lenain, *La science cabalistique*, 1823, p. 69](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1823__lenain___science_cabalistique.pdf)

**[Esoteric correspondence]** Degrees 181–185 correspond to the opening five degrees of Libra if the zodiac begins at 0° Aries. Printed sources sometimes call this 1°–5° Libra because they number ordinal degrees inclusively. Modern notation normally writes **0°00′–4°59′59″ Libra**, or more loosely 0°–5° Libra.

### Lenain’s Psalm

Lenain prescribes:

> **Deus virtutum converte nos: et ostende faciem tuam et salvi erimus.**

“God of hosts, restore us; show your face, and we shall be saved.”

He calls this the eighth verse of Psalm 79 in the Vulgate. It corresponds approximately to **Psalm 80:7** in common modern English numbering.

### The Psalm discrepancy

Other Rudd-derived or occult tables prescribe:

> **Domine Deus virtutum, converte nos: et ostende faciem tuam, et salvi erimus.**

This is the final refrain, Vulgate **Psalm 79:20**, corresponding to modern **Psalm 80:19**.

A Golden Dawn table prints a related refrain as Psalm **80:18**, reflecting another numbering convention or copying error.

**[Disputed transmission]** There is therefore no honest way to give one Psalm number without naming the source:

- Lenain: Vulgate 79:8 / modern 80:7;
- Rudd-derived tables: Vulgate 79:20 / modern 80:19;
- Golden Dawn transcription: Psalm 80:18.

The wording is similar because Psalm 80 repeats the refrain several times.

## Papus, Ambelain, and Haziel

### Papus

**[Documented reception]** Gérard Encausse, “Papus,” reproduced Lenain’s angel notices in the expanded 1903 edition of *La Cabbale*. His work helped move Lenain’s system into the French occult revival. [Papus, *La Cabbale*, 1903](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/pdf/abg405b2732165.pdf)

Papus did not originate Aniel’s “God of virtues,” calendrical days, or scientific-philosophical offices; these were already in Lenain.

### Robert Ambelain

**[Documented reception]** Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (Paris: Niclaus, 1951) adapted the 72-genius system for twentieth-century theurgy. [Bibliographical record](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL22019447M/La_kabbale_pratique)

**[Tradition]** Ambelain’s system adds or regularizes choir, sephirothic, planetary, ritual, and moral correspondences. These should not be retrojected into medieval Jewish Kabbalah or into Rudd.

### Haziel

“Haziel” was the pen name used for a series of French popular books on guardian angels, including *Répertoire des anges* around 1990.

**[Modern esoteric reception]** The Haziel school popularized each Shem name as a personal guardian angel for a consecutive block of civil-calendar birth dates, supplying personality types, life missions, prayers, and psychological “positive” and “negative” qualities.

Those biographies are late twentieth-century expansions. They draw ultimately on Lenain and the French occult tradition, but they are not present in *Sefer Raziel*, Reuchlin, or Harley 6483. Even the dates vary among modern tables: Aniel may be assigned approximately 19–23 September or 24–28 September depending on how authors reconcile fixed civil dates with zodiacal longitude and inclusive counting.

### Crowley’s explicit *777* entry

Columns CLXI and CLXII of *Liber 777* read:

> **Goetic Demons &c. by Night (Ascendant)**  
> **15 37 … Phenex — Child-voiced phœnix.**

[ Crowley, *777*, cols. CLXI–CLXII](https://www.astrumargenteum.org/wp-content/uploads/2015/06/777-by-Aleister-Crowley.pdf)

Key-scale row 15 is Aries. “Ascendant” here means the first or ascending decan of the sign. Consequently:

- zodiac: 0°–10° Aries;
- day/night: night;
- decan planet: Mars;
- tarot: Two of Wands;
- paired day spirit: Bael, no. 1.

Thus each decan carries a daytime and nighttime Goetic spirit in *777*. Phenex is the night counterpart of Bael in the first Aries decan.

### Why Two of Swords also appears

**[Later synthesis]** If one simply lays the 72 Goetic spirits consecutively over 72 five-degree sectors beginning at 0° Aries:

- spirits 1–6 occupy Aries;
- 7–12 Taurus;
- and so forth;
- spirit 37 begins Libra;
- spirit 38 occupies 5°–10° Libra.

Both fall within the first Libra decan, whose Golden Dawn pip is the **Two of Swords**, Moon in Libra.

This is internally coherent, but it is not the explicit arrangement in *777*. Claims that “Crowley assigns Phenex to 0°–5° Libra and the Two of Swords” conflate the serial quinance system with Crowley’s day/night decan columns.

### Planetary layers

Phenex can therefore acquire several planets without contradiction, because they come from different rules:

| Planet | Why it appears |
|---|---|
| Moon | Phenex is a Marquis; marquises use silver and the Moon |
| Mars | The first Aries decan and Two of Wands are Mars in Aries |
| Venus | Libra’s sign ruler in the serial five-degree system; also Anaiel’s planet in one Golden Dawn table |
| Moon again | First Libra decan’s tarot title is Moon in Libra; Lenain also calls Aniel’s associated “decade” lunar |

These are symbolic overlays, not evidence that an early text considered Phenex simultaneously ruled by several planets.

### Enochian overlays

**[Negative documented finding]** Weyer, Scot, the Goetia manuscripts, and Rudd do not give Phenex an Enochian name, governor, aethyr, watchtower square, or angel.

Crowley’s *777* places Goetic, Enochian, Egyptian, tarot, and astrological material in adjacent correspondence tables. That juxtaposition allows later practitioners to construct Enochian-Goetic syntheses, but it does not itself identify a unique Enochian entity as Phenex’s historical counterpart.

Claims assigning Phenex to a particular Enochian governor should therefore be dated to the modern author making the assignment.

## Iconography

## The phoenix image before demonological art

The early image is verbal rather than pictorial:

- *Livre des Esperitz*: “fair form,” with no explicit bird.
- Weyer: phoenix bird with childlike voice.
- Scot: same.
- Goetia: same, followed by an unspecified human shape.

**[Tradition]** Later artists naturally combine classical phoenix imagery—red and gold feathers, flame, solar light, death and rebirth—with the Goetic details of a youthful voice, musical performance, poetry, and the seal.

**[Historical caution]** Fire is not explicitly mentioned in Phenex’s Weyer or Goetia entry. It arrives through the general phoenix myth.

## Collin de Plancy

Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* first appeared in 1818, but its best-known demonological iconography belongs to the substantially revised sixth edition of 1863, illustrated with engravings by Louis Le Breton.

### 1863 entry, p. 525

> **Phénix, grand marquis des enfers. Il paraît sous la forme d’un phénix avec la voix d’un enfant ; avant de se montrer à l’exorciste, il rend des sons mélodieux, il faut au contraire se boucher les oreilles quand on lui commande de prendre la forme humaine. Il répond sur toutes les sciences. C’est un bon poëte, qui satisfait en vers à toutes les demandes. Après mille ans, il espère retourner au septième ordre des Trônes. Vingt légions lui obéissent.**

Translation:

> Phénix, great Marquis of Hell. He appears in the form of a phoenix with the voice of a child. Before showing himself to the exorcist, he produces melodious sounds; one must instead stop one’s ears when commanding him to take human form. He answers concerning all the sciences. He is a good poet who satisfies every request in verse. After a thousand years, he hopes to return to the seventh order of Thrones. Twenty legions obey him.

[De Plancy, sixth edition, p. 525](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.,_1863/Ph%C3%A9nix_%28d%C3%A9mon%29)

### De Plancy’s alterations

Compared with Weyer, de Plancy:

- changes 1,200 years to **1,000**;
- turns “the seventh throne” into “the seventh order of the Thrones”;
- tells the reader explicitly to stop the ears;
- makes Phenex satisfy requests **in verse**;
- calls him a Marquis “of Hell.”

These are de Plancy’s interpretive embellishments or errors, not independent ancient testimony.

### Does Louis Le Breton portray Phenex?

**[Negative finding]** The 1863 Phenex entry is text-only. No dedicated Louis Le Breton portrait appears on p. 525, and Phenex is not among the recognizable indexed Breton demon plates.

The small “image” marker on some Wikisource displays refers to page media or navigation, not to a Phenex portrait. Modern websites that place an illustration beside Phenex and call every such image “a Louis Le Breton engraving” are misattributing later art.

The dictionary’s following entry on the mythological phoenix is separate from the demonological notice.

## Modern visual art

**[Modern invention]** Contemporary Phenex art has no stable canon. Frequent motifs include:

- a humanoid phoenix;
- red, gold, or blue flame;
- a youthful or androgynous face;
- musical notes or a lyre;
- a silver lunar disk, derived from the Marquis correspondence;
- the seal on the breast, halo, or background;
- angelic ruins or seven thrones;
- death-and-rebirth imagery.

None of these details, except the phoenix form, youthful voice, song, poetry, human transformation, and seal, is demanded by the early text.

## Grimoire ancestors

### *Testament of Solomon*

**[Documented absence]** The Greek *Testament of Solomon*, a composite late-antique pseudepigraphon, presents Solomon commanding demons through a divinely empowered ring and compelling them to assist in building the Temple. It is a major legendary ancestor of Solomonic demonology.

Phenex/Fenix does not appear by that name. The *Testament* is an ancestor of the Solomon-controls-demons narrative, not a direct Phenex witness. [*Testament of Solomon*](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Testament_of_Solomon)

### Munich Manual, Clm 849

The fifteenth-century Munich handbook contains spirits resembling later Weyer entries, including names comparable to Barbatos, Gusion, Botis, Purson, Eligor, Gaap, Volac, Gemory, Cimeries, and Amy.

**[Documented absence]** Peterson’s comparison does not identify Fenix or Phenex there. The manuscript demonstrates a medieval catalogue environment but not this spirit’s name. [Bavarian State Library, Clm 849](https://daten.digitale-sammlungen.de/bsb00002157/image_1)

### *Livre des Esperitz*

This is the earliest surviving possible direct witness: Fenix, great Marquis, sweet-voiced, courteous, answering promptly, with 25 legions.

Jean-Patrice Boudet identifies extensive overlap between its catalogue and Weyer’s. Thirty-five of its spirits correspond to Weyer entries, while other names do not. The exact dating and direction of dependence remain contested.

### *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

Weyer says his source was called:

> *Officium spirituum*, or *Liber officiorum spirituum*, or *Liber dictus Empto. Salomonis*.

Trithemius catalogued a similarly titled necromantic book in his *Antipalus maleficiorum* in 1508.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** There was probably a branching family of “Offices of Spirits” catalogues rather than one fixed lost archetype. The *Livre des Esperitz*, Weyer’s source, the Folger *Book of Oberon* materials, and the later Goetia preserve overlapping but non-identical branches.

**[Open textual question]** No surviving manuscript has been proved to be the exact copy Weyer used.

### *Book of Abramelin*

Abramelin’s demon lists belong to the broader early modern environment of ranked spirits and demonic names.

**[Negative finding]** No secure Phenex/Fenix entry was located in the principal Abramelin lists. A general similarity of genre is not evidence of direct attestation.

## Authors, editors, and practitioners

### Johann Weyer

Weyer supplied the earliest securely dated printed Phœnix entry in 1577. He published the catalogue while denouncing ceremonial magicians’ pretensions and arguing that accused witches were deluded or ill rather than deserving execution.

His importance for Phenex is textual, not experiential: no record shows that Weyer personally evoked Phœnix.

### Reginald Scot and probably Abraham Fleming

Scot’s 1584 English version gave the Phenex description the wording later copied by the Goetia. Peterson tentatively attributes the translation to Abraham Fleming.

Scot printed such material to expose the claims of witchmongers and conjurers. He was not advertising a personal Phenex operation.

### The anonymous Goetia compiler and manuscript copyists

The seventeenth-century compiler:

- reorganized the Weyer/Scot catalogue;
- expanded it to 72;
- supplied individual seals;
- placed the material in an explicitly Solomonic ritual book;
- added such phrases as “as he said to Solomon.”

The names of the original compiler and designer of the seals remain unknown.

### Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

The Rudd recension paired each Goetic spirit with a same-numbered Shem angel. Peter Smart preserved the material in Harley 6483.

Phenex’s historical importance in this recension is its pairing with Aniel and the resulting angelic constraint technique.

### Lazare Lenain

Lenain did not write about Phenex directly. His importance is the 1823 elaboration of Aniel’s meaning, zodiacal ray, Psalm, days, time, virtues, and contrary genius.

### Collin de Plancy

De Plancy retold Weyer’s description in 1863, adding the claim that Phenex answers requests in verse and changing 1,200 years to 1,000. No Breton portrait accompanies it.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Aleister Crowley

Mathers prepared the English Goetia text and seals; Crowley edited and published it in 1904 with his “Preliminary Invocation” and interpretive essay.

Crowley’s *777* subsequently gave Phenex the night position in the first Aries decan. No published evidence establishes a personal Crowley evocation of Phenex.

### Frater Achad

**[Negative finding]** No Phenex-specific operation, diary entry, doctrine, or substantial discussion by Frater Achad—Charles Stansfeld Jones—was located. His name is relevant to Crowleyan and qabalah history generally, but not demonstrably to Phenex.

### Poke Runyon

Runyon’s *Book of Solomon’s Magick* presents a modern ritual reconstruction using crystal or dark-mirror methods, astrological sectors, and Shem angels.

His Phenex material combines:

- Goetia no. 37;
- Marquis = Moon/silver;
- Aniel;
- approximately 0°–5° Libra;
- an Egyptian Bennu interpretation.

The Bennu identity and zodiacal synthesis are Runyon’s reconstruction, not early textual facts.

### Lon Milo DuQuette, Christopher Hyatt, and David P. Wilson

*Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* popularized a practical and psychological reading of the spirits. Its Phenex table follows the Crowleyan night-decan scheme: first decan Aries, approximately 21–30 March, Two of Wands, with lunar/silver rank symbolism.

The work is influential modern reception, not an independent historical witness.

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 edition and Esoteric Archives transcriptions made manuscript comparison possible without relying exclusively on Crowley. His work establishes the crucial differences in spelling, numbering, order, and source dependence.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their 2007 *Goetia of Dr Rudd* transcribed and interpreted Harley 6483 and related material, bringing the Aniel–Phenex pairing and double-seal system into modern publication.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s *Encyclopaedia Goetica* and related writings place the Goetia within a much larger history of chthonic religion, necromancy, spirit catalogues, and Greco-Egyptian magic.

**[Scholarly/esoteric qualification]** This broader genealogy may illuminate the genre, but it does not provide direct evidence that Phenex is an ancient deity. No Phenex-specific pre-*Livre des Esperitz* identification has been demonstrated.

### Owen Davies, Jan Veenstra, Julien Véronèse, and Jean-Patrice Boudet

These scholars are valuable for the history of grimoires, learned magic, spirit catalogues, and manuscript culture.

- Davies supplies the social history of grimoires and their transmission.
- Veenstra and Véronèse study medieval learned magic and ritual texts.
- Boudet is particularly relevant to Phenex because of his transcription and comparison of the *Livre des Esperitz*.

None provides evidence that a demon called Phenex was worshipped in antiquity.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Is the *Livre des Esperitz* really pre-Weyer?

**[Disputed]** Possibly, but not with certainty.

- Traditional dating placed Trinity O.8.29 in the fifteenth century.
- Some paleographical assessment permits or favors the sixteenth century.
- Boudet notes that the hand may be no earlier than the mid-sixteenth century.

Consequently:

- **Earliest surviving possible attestation:** *Livre des Esperitz*, Fenix.
- **Earliest securely dated attestation:** Weyer, 1577.

The assertion “Phenex first appears in 1577” is too absolute if it ignores Fenix. The assertion “Phenex is certainly a medieval fifteenth-century demon” is also stronger than the manuscript dating permits.

## 2. Is Fenix definitely the same spirit?

**[Strong scholarly reconstruction]** The identification is highly probable because of the identical name, Marquis rank, sweet voice, similar answering/obedience, location in a cognate catalogue, and proximity to Stolas.

But the descriptions are not identical:

- Fenix appears in a fair form, not explicitly as a bird;
- it has 25 rather than 20 legions;
- no poetry, sciences, human shape, or seventh throne appears.

It is therefore safer to call Fenix the earliest textual ancestor or cognate of Phenex than to claim a perfectly stable entity biography.

## 3. Is Phenex an ancient god?

**[Unsupported claim]** No evidence establishes this.

The name is the phoenix bird’s name. Bennu is a plausible ancestor or analogue of the bird myth, but no source identifies the Goetic spirit as a fallen Bennu cult-god.

## 4. Does the name mean “crimson one”?

**[Disputed]** Greek *phoînix* is associated with red/purple, Phoenicians, palms, and the fabulous bird. “Crimson one” is a possible etymological gloss, not a settled translation of the demon’s proper name.

## 5. Is Phenex a former Throne angel?

**[Legendary inference]** The entry says that Phenex hopes to return to “the seventh throne.” It does not state:

- “Phenex was of the Order of Thrones”;
- that the seventh throne is the seventh angelic choir;
- that Phenex formerly occupied a particular seat.

De Plancy explicitly converts the phrase into “the seventh order of the Thrones.” That interpretive reading first becomes clear in his paraphrase, not in Weyer’s Latin.

## 6. Is Phenex deceived in its hope?

**[Textual error]** Not in Weyer’s Phenex entry, Scot’s entry, Sloane 2731, Sloane 3825, or Mathers–Crowley.

The formula about being “deceived in that hope” belongs to descriptions of other spirits in parts of the tradition and was copied into modern Phenex summaries.

## 7. Is the singing a lethal siren song?

**[Modern embellishment]** The sources say only that the conjurer must not attend or give ear to it. They do not specify the consequence.

“Siren-like” is a useful modern simile but should not be placed in quotation marks as if it came from Weyer.

## 8. Why 25 versus 20 legions?

**[Documented textual change]**

- *Livre des Esperitz*: 25.
- Weyer onward: 20.

No source explains the reduction. It may result from a variant exemplar, a copying error, deliberate redaction, or Weyer’s fuller source being independent of the surviving French wording.

## 9. What is a legion?

Scot appends the note:

> “Note that a legion is 6 6 6 6.”

**[Disputed interpretation]** Whether this means 6,666 individual spirits per legion or is a talismanic/numerological notation is unclear. Multiplying Phenex’s 20 legions by 6,666 produces 133,320, but that precision should not be presented as a consistent bureaucratic census.

## 10. What is the correct zodiacal sector?

There are at least three answers because there are three systems:

1. **Lenain/serial Aniel:** 0°–5° Libra.
2. **Golden Dawn Shem table:** Anaiel at 0°–5° Aquarius.
3. **Crowley *777* Phenex:** night spirit of 0°–10° Aries.

Calling one of these the ancient or original sector is historically false. Weyer and the Goetia give none.

## 11. What is the tarot card?

- Crowley’s explicit night-decan arrangement: **Two of Wands**.
- Consecutive 72 × 5° arrangement: **Two of Swords**.

The second is a later serial synthesis. It is not the placement printed for Phenex in *777*.

## 12. Which Psalm belongs to Aniel?

The refrain is stable; the verse number is not.

- Lenain: Vulgate Psalm 79:8.
- Other Rudd-derived tables: Vulgate Psalm 79:20.
- Modern English numbering: usually Psalm 80:7 or 80:19.
- Golden Dawn transcription: Psalm 80:18.

Differences arise from repeated refrains, Vulgate/Hebrew numbering, inclusive conventions, and copying errors.

## 13. Does de Plancy provide Phenex’s portrait?

**No.** The 1863 entry has no dedicated Louis Le Breton engraving. Claims to the contrary usually assume that every demon in the dictionary received an illustration.

## 14. Is “Phoeniex” historical?

No principal witness examined uses it. It should be labeled a modern spelling until an identifiable early edition or manuscript is supplied.

## 15. Are the twenty legion commanders named?

**[Modern invention]** Websites sometimes list twenty officers—Astrat, Bilgirim, Cirzun, Dirgazon, and others. No such names appear in the *Livre des Esperitz*, Weyer, Scot, the Goetia manuscripts, Rudd, Mathers–Crowley, Peterson, or de Plancy.

At least one modern site acknowledges that these names derive from community-created “Demonic Paradise” material. They are modern apocrypha, not recovered grimoire content.

## 16. Is Phenex “neutral good” or unusually benevolent?

**[Modern invention]** Alignment categories and moral ratings come from role-playing conventions or community encyclopedias. The oldest Fenix is courteous; Weyer’s Phœnix is obedient; neither source supplies an ethical alignment.

## 17. Is the modern enn ancient?

The formula frequently printed as something like:

> *Enn Ef Enay Phenex Ayer*

is part of modern demonolatry.

**[Modern invention/tradition]** It is not in Weyer, Scot, the Goetia manuscripts, Rudd, de Plancy, or Mathers–Crowley. Its wording should not be presented as ancient Hebrew, Latin, or Solomonic material.

## 18. Are jasmine incense, eastern direction, or fixed colors traditional?

Unless an author gives a traceable correspondence chain, these are modern additions. Phenex’s historically grounded rank correspondence is Moon/silver; its fixed incense, color, direction, element, and “best offerings” vary widely across contemporary sites.

## 19. Has Phenex been conflated with Haures/Flauros?

Occasional modern discussions confuse Phenex with Haures because both can be illustrated as fiery or feline-bird hybrids. The texts distinguish them clearly:

- Phenex: Marquis, phoenix, child’s voice, poet and scientist.
- Flauros/Haures: Duke, leopard-like manifestation, fiery eyes, answers concerning divinity and the creation of the world in the Goetia.

Their names, ranks, seals, offices, and numbers differ.

## Open questions

1. **The date of Trinity O.8.29.** A firmer paleographical or codicological date would determine whether Fenix is securely pre-Weyer or merely an approximately contemporary cognate.

2. **Weyer’s exact exemplar.** The manuscript Weyer called *Liber officiorum spirituum* or *Empto. Salomonis* has not been identified. Finding it could explain the bird, child’s voice, poetry, sciences, 1,200 years, and legion change.

3. **The transition from “fair form” to phoenix bird.** Did Weyer or an intermediate scribe infer the bird from the name, or did a fuller source already contain it?

4. **The 1,200-year formula.** Phenex shares a throne-return motif with Marchosias and related Goetic entries. Its theological or chronological source remains unknown. It may be a floating catalogue formula rather than a feature unique to Phenex.

5. **“Seventh throne.”** It is unclear whether the phrase originally meant a numbered throne, the seventh celestial sphere, a choir, or merely a heavenly seat. De Plancy’s “order of Thrones” is an interpretation.

6. **The origin of the seal.** No pre-Goetia witness of Phenex’s distinctive seal has been securely identified. Its designer and the intended meaning of its strokes remain unknown.

7. **Rudd’s authorship.** Harley 6483 is later than Thomas Rudd. Further comparison of Smart’s exemplars and related Rudd papers may clarify how much of the angel–demon pairing can securely be assigned to Rudd himself.

8. **The Psalm archetype.** The repeated refrain in Psalm 80 makes multiple choices possible. It is unclear whether Lenain deliberately chose the middle refrain or inherited a shifted verse number.

9. **The first occurrence of “Pheynix.”** It is prominent in Mathers–Crowley, but an earlier manuscript or Mathers transcription may yet explain whether it was an editorial spelling, a misreading, or copied from another witness.

10. **The origin of “Phoeniex.”** No historical source in the examined chain supports it. Locating its earliest online or printed occurrence would document how a typo became a standard modern alias.

11. **Phenex-specific magical diaries.** Many modern practitioners report experiences, but no substantial, independently authenticated operation record by Crowley, Frater Achad, or another major historical magician was located.

12. **Modern media cataloguing.** Phenex is frequently confused with generic phoenix characters. Reliable cataloguing needs evidence that each creator intended the Ars Goetia spirit rather than merely the mythological bird.
