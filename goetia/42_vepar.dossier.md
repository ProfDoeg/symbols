## The name and its origins

### Earliest securely datable appearance

**[Documented text]** The earliest securely datable printed appearance of the name is Johann Weyer’s *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*, added to the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum* in **1577**. The frequently repeated date **1563** belongs to the first edition of *De praestigiis*; the *Pseudomonarchia* catalogue was not yet included there. Peterson’s transcription places Vepar at printed page marker **[922]** and as entry 33 in the actual sequence.

Weyer begins:

> “Vepar, aliàs Separ, Dux magnus & fortis: similis syreni.”

That establishes two readings at the first attestation:

- **Vepar** — the headword.
- **Separ** — already marked by Weyer as an alternative.
- **Syreni** — not a name but the dative of Latin *siren*, describing its likeness.

**[Documented text]** Weyer attributed the material behind his catalogue to a manuscript work he called *Liber officiorum spirituum*, also described as a book of “Empto. Salomonis.” That testimony makes an earlier manuscript ancestry plausible, but the cited exemplar is not now available in a form that permits Vepar’s name to be dated before 1577.

**Finding:** there is presently **no securely identified pre-1577 textual witness to Vepar or Separ**.

### Is the name derived from an ancient god, hero, place, Greek word, or Latin word?

**[Open finding]** Unlike Baal, Astarte, Amon, Asmodeus/Ashmedai, Belial, or Phenex/Phoenix, Vepar has no securely demonstrated ancient divine, heroic, geographical, Greek, Latin, Hebrew, or Near Eastern antecedent.

Several proposals circulate, but none has a documented chain of transmission:

- **[Disputed] South Slavic *vepar*, “wild boar.”** The word is genuine—Serbo-Croatian *vepar*, from Proto-Slavic *veprь*—but there is no evidence that Weyer or his source borrowed it. Its meaning also offers no evident connection with a siren, waters, ships, or infected wounds.

- **[Disputed] Latin *vepres*, “brambles/thorn bushes.”** This is a modern internet etymology. No manuscript, early commentator, or historical linguist has connected *Vepar* with *vepres*.

- **[Disputed] Latin *separare*, “to separate.”** The variant *Separ* makes this superficially tempting, but no early source provides the derivation and the spirit’s offices do not explain it.

- **[Disputed] A corrupted word for “siren” or “mermaid.”** Weyer treats *similis syreni* as Vepar’s appearance, not its etymology. No intermediate spelling establishes a linguistic derivation.

- **[Disputed] An ancient sea goddess.** Modern devotional and demonolatry pages occasionally treat Vepar as the survival of an unnamed marine divinity. No corresponding ancient name, cult, inscription, or textual equation has been produced.

The most economical historical conclusion is therefore:

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** *Vepar* is an opaque late-medieval or early-modern spirit name, perhaps copied from the lost branch of the *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*. *Separ* may be a genuine variant from that source, a scribal error, or an attempted normalization. No deeper origin is presently reconstructable.

### Siren versus mermaid

Weyer’s Latin says *similis syreni*, “like a siren.” **[Documented text]** Scot renders this as “like a mermaid,” and the Goetia follows that interpretation.

This is not a trivial equivalence:

- Classical Greek Sirens were normally composite women and birds.
- Medieval and Renaissance visual traditions increasingly represented the siren with one or two fish tails.
- English “mermaid” therefore narrows an image that Weyer’s Latin leaves somewhat broader.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Vepar’s standard fish-tailed image descends chiefly from Scot’s translation and later visual convention, not from proof that Weyer intended a modern mermaid specifically.

## Number and place in the catalogues

### Weyer’s position

**[Documented text]** Vepar is the **33rd** spirit in Weyer's actual catalogue order. It follows Gaap and precedes Sabnac.

Joseph Peterson’s useful comparison chart labels it “32” in the Weyer column, but that conflicts with both the full transcription and the chart’s own inclusion of Pruflas at number 4. **[Scholarly reconstruction]** This is an off-by-one editorial/chart error, not an alternative Renaissance numbering.

### Scot’s position

Scot translates Weyer’s order but leaves out **Pruflas**. Consequently, an ordinal count made only from Scot’s printed entries shifts later names by one. This is a frequent source of inconsistent numbers in secondary literature.

### The Goetia’s position

**[Documented text]** The *Ars Goetia* makes Vepar spirit **42** of 72.

The 72-name Goetia is not simply Weyer's 69-name catalogue with three names appended. Its compiler:

- added **Vassago** as Goetia 3;
- omitted Weyer’s **Pruflas**;
- added **Seere**, **Dantalion**, and **Andromalius** as 70–72;
- reorganized much of the intervening order.

Thus four names were added while one was omitted, producing a net increase from 69 to 72.

## The description across the sources

## Johann Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* (1577)

### Text and translation

Weyer’s opening wording is quoted above. The complete entry, paraphrased clause by clause, says:

- Vepar, also Separ, is a great and strong **Duke**.
- He resembles a **siren**.
- He is leader or conductor of waters and of ships laden with **arms**.
- At the master’s command, he makes the sea swell or grow rough and makes it appear filled with ships.
- Against the exorcist’s enemies, he infects people for three days with putrefying wounds that produce worms.
- After the business has been completed, all may be healed.
- He commands **29 legions**.

The Latin *navium armis onustarum* means ships “laden with arms/weapons.” Scot’s “armour” is defensible in sixteenth-century usage, but modern “merchant ships” is not what Weyer wrote.

The pathogenic passage also deserves care. Weyer’s verb is *inficit*, “infects” or “afflicts,” not unambiguously “kills.” It is followed by a healing clause. The more categorical claim that Vepar kills in precisely three days becomes explicit in Scot and the Goetia.

### Context

**[Documented authorial context]** Weyer was a physician and critic of witch persecutions. He printed the catalogue as a supposed “false monarchy” of demons and evidence of the fantasies, deceptions, or deliria circulated among magicians—not as an uncomplicated devotional endorsement of the operations.

That skeptical frame does not make the catalogue fictitious in a simple sense: Weyer was preserving genuine ritual-literary material, but he did so polemically.

## Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584)

Scot’s entry begins:

> “Vepar, alias Separ, a great duke and a strong, he is like a mermaid.”

The remainder says that Vepar:

- guides waters and ships laden with armour;
- makes the sea rough and stormy at the master’s command;
- makes it appear full of ships;
- kills people in three days by putrefying their wounds and producing maggots;
- nevertheless permits them all to be healed “with diligence”;
- rules 29 legions.

The relevant passage occurs in Book XV, chapter 2; in the Brinsley Nicholson reprint it is on **p. 321**.

### Differences from Weyer

| Point | Weyer | Scot |
|---|---|---|
| Appearance | Like a siren | Like a mermaid |
| Ships | Laden with arms | Laden with armour |
| Sea | Made swollen; appears ship-filled | Rough and stormy; appears ship-filled |
| Injury | Infects enemies for three days | Kills people in three days |
| Cure | All healed when the business is finished | All healed with diligence |
| Legions | 29 | 29 |

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Scot intensifies and naturalizes portions of the Latin. “Mermaid,” “stormy,” “kills,” and “maggots” are influential English choices, not exact one-to-one equivalents in every case.

Scot, like Weyer, presented magical catalogues within an overwhelmingly skeptical argument against witchcraft credulity and judicial persecution.

## The manuscript *Ars Goetia*

### Dating and textual relationship

The extant Goetia manuscripts are seventeenth- and early-eighteenth-century witnesses. Sloane 3825 is one of the most important; Sloane 3648, Sloane 2731, Harley 6483, Northwestern MS 65, and Folger V.b.26 preserve related material.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The Goetia catalogue was compiled substantially through Scot’s English version of Weyer, while adding seals and ritual machinery from other magical sources. The recurrent English phrasing makes direct dependence on Scot much more likely than a fresh translation from Weyer’s Latin.

### Sloane 3825

At folio **106v**, the entry begins:

> “The 42d spirit is Named Vepar he is a great & strong duke, & appeareth like a Mairmaid.”

The entry then states that Vepar:

- governs the waters;
- guides ships laden with arms or armour;
- makes the sea rough and stormy at the conjurer’s request;
- makes the sea appear full of ships;
- causes people to die in three days by putrefying wounds or sores and breeding worms in them;
- governs 29 legions;
- has a seal or lamen to be worn before the operator.

The phrase “request of the” is damaged or omitted in Sloane 3825 and restored through comparison with Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, Northwestern 65, and Harley 6483.

### Material change from Weyer and Scot

The Goetia retains Scot’s stronger claim that Vepar kills in three days but drops both earlier healing clauses.

That produces three distinct layers:

1. **Weyer:** infectious, worm-producing injuries lasting three days; healing after the task.
2. **Scot:** death in three days; healing remains possible through diligence.
3. **Goetia:** death in three days; no cure is specified.

Later claims that Vepar specializes in miraculous healing are therefore misleading if presented as part of the Goetia entry. At most, they extrapolate from Weyer's and Scot’s statements that the affliction can be reversed.

### Folger’s “Semper”

**[Documented variant]** Folger V.b.26 reads **Semper** and describes the form as something like a maiden rather than preserving the standard *Vepar/mermaid* wording. Its offices nevertheless identify it as the same textual spirit: waters, ships, marine disturbance, and corrupting wounds.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** *Semper* is probably a copying error or deformation of *Separ/Vepar*, not a separate demon later conflated with Vepar.

## Mathers and Crowley, *The Goetia* (1904)

On printed **p. 50**, the edition begins:

> “The Forty-second Spirit is Vepar, or Vephar. He is a Duke Great and Strong, and appeareth like a Mermaid.”

Mathers and Crowley then give the familiar offices:

- government of waters;
- guidance of ships carrying arms, armour, and ammunition;
- production of a stormy sea apparently filled with ships;
- death in three days through putrefying wounds or sores in which worms breed;
- rule over 29 legions.

A footnote concedes that the manuscripts more simply describe him as guiding waters and ships laden with armour. “Ammunition” is therefore an editorial expansion suited to modern military vocabulary.

This edition contributes three especially influential features:

- **Vephar**, a spelling not supported by the principal manuscripts collated by Peterson;
- two printed seal variants, figures **75–76**;
- Crowley’s mermaid portrait, figure **77**.

---

## Joseph H. Peterson’s editions

Peterson’s online transcription of Sloane 3825 and his 2001 printed *Lesser Key of Solomon* restore a manuscript-centered text and record variants across Sloane, Harley, Northwestern, and Folger witnesses.

The 2001 edition also prints the Weyer/Scot antecedents in an appendix around **p. 242**, allowing the reader to see that:

- the descriptive entry was inherited from Weyer through Scot;
- the seal belongs to the Goetia manuscript layer;
- *Vephar* is Mathers’s reading rather than a major manuscript variant;
- *Semper* belongs to the Folger witness;
- the healing clause disappeared during transmission into the Goetia.

## Rank, planet, metal, timing, and legions

### Duke: Venus and copper

The Goetia’s general “Observations” prescribe metals by rank. **Dukes’ seals are to be made in copper**, corresponding to **Venus**.

This is an internal ritual correspondence, not a historical etymology:

- **[Documented ritual instruction]** rank: Duke;
- **[Esoteric correspondence]** planet: Venus;
- **[Esoteric correspondence]** metal: copper.

The Venus attribution arises from the rank, not from Vepar’s marine form or offices.

### Time of conjuration

The same general rules say dukes may be bound from **sunrise until noon**, in **clear weather**.

No early source supplies a special Vepar hour. Any planetary-hour recommendation is a subsequent application of general Venus magic:

- Friday as Venus’s weekday;
- the planetary hour of Venus;
- waxing or otherwise ritually suitable Moon;
- sunrise-to-noon ducal window.

The Goetia also recommends certain even-numbered lunar ages—2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, and 14 days—for operations generally. These are not Vepar-specific.

### Legions

All principal witnesses agree on **29 legions**. No reliable source explains the number or provides names, ranks, or an organization for those legions.

## Appearance and offices: consolidated findings

### What the sources actually say

**[Documented text]**

- First manifestation: siren or mermaid.
- Second manifestation: none.
- Maritime authority: conductor, guide, or governor of waters.
- Ships: originally armed ships; later “armour and ammunition.”
- Marine phenomenon: a swelling, rough, or stormy sea.
- Fleet phenomenon: the sea is made to **appear** full of ships.
- Hostile operation: putrefying wounds or sores, producing worms or maggots.
- Duration: three days.
- Outcome: infection in Weyer, death in Scot/Goetia; possible healing only in Weyer and Scot.
- Subordinates: 29 legions.

### What the sources do not say

There is no early evidence that Vepar:

- teaches languages, sciences, astronomy, medicine, navigation, or naval strategy;
- reveals treasures;
- obtains love;
- gives familiars;
- grants invisibility;
- transmutes metals;
- answers all questions;
- changes into a human form on demand;
- has a dedicated color, incense, direction, enn, offering, personality, gender identity, or feast day.

Those claims, when encountered, belong to modern occult synthesis or individual visionary practice.

### Is the fleet real or illusory?

Weyer’s *appareat* and Scot’s “shall appear full of ships” favor an apparition or visual phenomenon. The texts do not say that Vepar materially manufactures ships.

**[Tradition]** Ritual practitioners sometimes interpret the office as control of fleets, naval protection, naval warfare, or the transportation of weapons. Those are reasonable extensions of the wording, but they are not explicit historical descriptions.

## The seal

### Absence in Weyer and Scot

**[Documented text]** Neither Weyer’s *Pseudomonarchia* nor Scot’s translated catalogue gives individual demonic seals. Vepar’s sigil therefore cannot be traced to the 1577 printed entry.

### Manuscript Goetia forms

The Goetia manuscripts supply a seal beside or following the description. Although the copies differ in penmanship and internal geometry, the familiar family of forms has:

- a generally circular outer boundary;
- a prominent capital-like **V** at the top;
- a descending internal stem;
- one or more loops or crossings;
- a broad lower curve or enclosure;
- small differences in how the loops, terminal hooks, and circle intersect.

Sloane 3825 and Sloane 3648 preserve distinguishable drawings. Mathers and Crowley consequently printed two versions.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** These variants look like ordinary scribal evolution of a diagram copied by hand, not evidence for two distinct Vepars. No source explains the sigil’s component strokes or gives a linguistic decoding.

### Harley 6483 and the angelic counter-seal

Harley 6483’s “Rudd” recension places demonic seals within an angelically constrained system. The spirit is paired with a Shem angel of the same ordinal number; angelic names, seals, or Psalm material are placed in ritual opposition to the demon.

**[Tradition]** In the edited Rudd operation, the corresponding angel is not a friendly companion assigned to Vepar but a superior divine force used to **restrain, bind, or control** it.

### Printed 1904 seal and Crowley’s picture

Mathers–Crowley p. 50 prints:

- figure 75: one Vepar seal;
- figure 76: an alternative manuscript form;
- figure 77: “Vepar, as drawn by Crowley.”

The first two are sigils. The third is representational art and should not be mistaken for a manuscript seal.

## The angel: Mikael, number 42

## Identity and spelling

| Form | Description |
|---|---|
| מיכ | Three-letter Exodus triad, mem-yod-kaph |
| מיכאל | Mikael/Mikha’el/Michael after adding *-El* |
| Mikael | Common French and occult spelling |
| Michael | Conventional English and biblical spelling |
| Mīkhāʾēl | Scholarly transliteration |

The ordinary Hebrew parsing is:

- *mi* — who?
- *ke/ka* — like/as;
- *El* — God.

Thus **“Who is like God?”**—a rhetorical question implying that no one is comparable to God.

Lenain’s phrases “virtue of God,” “house of God,” and “similar to God” are traditional occult attributes or wordplay, not a sound three-part historical etymology.

### Is Mikael 42 the archangel Michael?

**[Documented name identity]** It is the same Hebrew spelling as Michael.

**[Disputed metaphysical identity]** Some Christian and occult authors identify every occurrence directly with the biblical archangel. Other modern practitioners distinguish the 42nd Shem “Mikael” as a specialized name-force or guardian angel rather than the archangel in his universal office.

Historically, the 72-name construction generates *Mikael* from a specific three-letter column. The text itself does not settle later theological questions about whether this is a distinct being, an aspect, or the familiar archangel.

---

## Derivation from Exodus 14:19–21

**[Documented kabbalistic procedure]** Each of Exodus 14:19, 14:20, and 14:21 contains 72 Hebrew consonantal letters in the traditional text. The construction proceeds by writing:

1. verse 19 in its ordinary direction;
2. verse 20 in reverse;
3. verse 21 in its ordinary direction.

Reading the three rows by columns yields 72 three-letter combinations. Column 42 gives **מיכ**. Christian and practical Kabbalists expanded the triads with endings such as **-El** or **-Yah**, creating pronounceable angelic names.

This technique is later than Exodus as a compositional text. It is a kabbalistic rereading of the verses, not an assertion that 72 angels are explicitly named in the biblical passage.

## History of the 72-fold tradition

### Rabbinic and medieval Jewish background

**[Documented history]** In early rabbinic usage, *Shem ha-Mephorash* denotes the “explicit,” “distinguished,” or fully pronounced divine Name; it did not always mean this particular list of 72 angels.

**[Documented medieval tradition]** A 72-fold divine-name construction from Exodus 14 appears in medieval Jewish esotericism, including *Sefer ha-Bahir* §§109–110 and material transmitted in *Sefer Raziel*. Rashi also knows a 72-letter divine-name construction.

What those medieval sources transmit is principally a **divine-name technique**. The fully developed system of 72 individualized guardian angels, each with five zodiacal degrees, dates, behavioral profiles, moral opposites, and Psalm verses is a much later accretion.

### Reuchlin and Christian Cabala

Johann Reuchlin’s *De Arte Cabalistica* (1517) helped Christian Europe systematize angelic names made from the 72 triads by adding *-El* and *-Yah*. This placed the Hebrew construction within a Christian angelological and magical framework.

Blaise de Vigenère’s *Traicté des chiffres* (1586) reproduced and developed related material. Vigenère is especially relevant to later early-modern magicians because his tables made Hebrew divine-name lore available outside Jewish manuscript settings.

### Kircher

Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–54) presents extensive Christian-Cabalistic diagrams, divine names, alphabets, angelic orders, and 72-fold material.

**Caution:** Kircher did not originate the Exodus technique, and his various lists should not automatically be equated with Lenain’s nineteenth-century zodiac-and-Psalm system. One online Kircher Cabala transcription, for example, contains a 42-name angelic list alongside other schemes; it is not itself the modern “Mikael number 42” table.

### Rudd and Harley 6483

Harley 6483, dated approximately **1712–13**, is associated in its headings and later scholarship with Dr. Thomas Rudd. The surviving compilation appears to have been copied or assembled by **Peter Smart**, long after some of Rudd’s presumed working period.

**[Documented manuscript feature]** It couples the 72 Goetic spirits with 72 angels in matching numerical order. Consequently:

- Vepar = Goetia 42;
- Mikael = angel 42;
- Mikael is invoked as the divine counterforce to Vepar.

**[Disputed attribution]** Thomas Rudd lived approximately 1583–1656. Harley 6483 is not established as his autograph. “The Goetia of Dr Rudd” is therefore a useful designation for the recension, but stronger claims that the surviving volume was personally written or used by Rudd go beyond the evidence.

### Skinner and Rankine

Stephen Skinner and David Rankine’s *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (2007) edited and reconstructed this recension as a working system. It brought the Mikael–Vepar pairing to a wide modern audience and emphasized the double structure of demonic seal and angelic constraint.

Their reconstruction is invaluable for accessing the material but should remain distinguishable from the literal physical state and uncertain authorship history of Harley 6483.

## Lenain’s Mikael

Lazare Républicain Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* (Amiens, 1823), p. 74, gives a developed entry for the 42nd genius, **Mikael**.

### Attributes

**[Documented esoteric correspondence]**

- Number: 42.
- Name: Mikael.
- Qualities: “virtue of God,” “house of God,” and “similar to God.”
- Foreign divine name: **Biub/Biud**, allegedly in the language of “the Canadians.”
- Zodiacal rays: 206th through 210th degrees.
- Decan: the 21st ten-degree sector.
- Decan genius: **Arpien**.
- Daily period: approximately **1:40–2:00**, normally rendered in later tables as 13:40–14:00.
- Psalm: Vulgate Psalm **120:7**.
- Ruled dates: 30 April, 11 July, 21 September, 2 December, and 12 February.

Lenain’s 206th–210th degrees correspond to the final five degrees of Libra, conventionally written **25°–30° Libra**.

### Psalm verse

Lenain’s verse is:

> “Dominus custodit te ab omni malo.”

The complete verse says that the Lord preserves the speaker from all evil and preserves the soul.

Because the Vulgate/Septuagint and Hebrew/Protestant Psalm numberings differ, this is:

- **Psalm 120:7** in the Vulgate;
- **Psalm 121:7** in the Hebrew, KJV, and most modern Protestant numbering.

### Offices

Lenain assigns Mikael:

- safety in journeys;
- influence over monarchs, princes, and nobles;
- maintenance of subjects’ loyalty or obedience;
- discovery of conspiracies and threats;
- aptitude for diplomacy and political observation.

The contrary or adverse genius rules:

- treason;
- malice;
- false reports;
- political deceit.

These are Lenain’s nineteenth-century moral-astrological correspondences; they are not stated in Exodus, *Bahir*, or the medieval *Sefer Raziel*.

### “Biub/Biud in the language of the Canadians”

**[Disputed]** Lenain inherited a Christian-occult habit of assigning each angel a supposedly corresponding divine name from a different nation. No linguistic or ethnographic evidence establishes *Biub/Biud* as a historical Indigenous Canadian name for God. The attribution should be preserved as part of Lenain’s text but not treated as reliable comparative religion.

## Papus, Ambelain, and Haziel

### Papus

Papus—Gérard Encausse—reproduced practical Cabalistic tables and angelological material derived substantially from Lenain. His *La Cabbale* appeared in 1892 and in an enlarged edition in 1903. Papus’s circle also helped revive Lenain’s book in the early twentieth century.

**[Tradition]** Through Papus, Lenain’s angels entered French Martinist, occultist, and practical-Cabala literature.

### Robert Ambelain

Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) repeated and ritualized much of the French 72-angel system. Ambelain helped popularize:

- pentacles and angelic seals;
- Psalm invocations;
- daily twenty-minute angelic periods;
- the morally opposed “contrary genius”;
- operative theurgy based on the angel’s name.

Later editions or associated warnings claim that some published seals were erroneous or dangerous. **[Tradition]** Such warnings reflect operative occult concerns; they are not independent evidence for the antiquity or supernatural efficacy of the diagrams.

### Haziel

Haziel, a twentieth-century French esoteric author, popularized the 72 angels as personal “guardian angels” associated with blocks of civil-calendar birthdays.

In that system Mikael commonly governs **19–23 October**, corresponding approximately to the Sun’s passage through 25°–30° Libra, and has a daily invocation period around **13:40–14:00**.

This differs from Lenain:

- Lenain gives five separated calendar dates marking analogous zodiacal degrees in different seasonal or planetary cycles.
- Haziel and related authors turn the principal zodiacal period into one continuous birthday interval.

**[Modern esoteric system]** The popular claim “Mikael protects people born 19–23 October” is not found in medieval Jewish sources or in Rudd’s Goetia. It is a twentieth-century guardian-angel calendar.

### Crowley’s *777*: the important correction

*Liber 777* first appeared anonymously in **1909**; *777 Revised* appeared posthumously in 1955. Its Goetic tables put Vepar in the Taurus row, marked as a ducal/Venus spirit and assigned to the **night or cadent** half.

Therefore Crowley’s own system yields:

- zodiac: Taurus;
- decan: **20°–30° Taurus**;
- quinance: conventionally the second half, **25°–30° Taurus**;
- card: **Seven of Pentacles/Disks**;
- decan planet: Saturn;
- Golden Dawn title: **“Success Unfulfilled”**;
- Crowley’s Thoth title: **“Failure.”**

The accompanying magical image is a mermaid.

### Which spirit shares the Seven of Pentacles?

In the day/night decanic arrangement, each ten-degree tarot pip carries two five-degree Goetic spirits:

- daytime/succedent quinance: **Valefor**, Goetia 6;
- nighttime/cadent quinance: **Vepar**, Goetia 42.

Thus Vepar’s actual Crowley/*777* pip partner is **Valefor**, not Focalor.

### The ordinal/Rudd Libra assignment

If the 72 angels—and subsequently the 72 demons paired with them—are laid consecutively around the zodiac in five-degree blocks:

- no. 41 occupies 20°–25° Libra;
- no. 42 occupies 25°–30° Libra;
- the encompassing decan is 20°–30° Libra;
- the tarot pip is the **Four of Swords**;
- the decan planet is Jupiter;
- the Golden Dawn title is **“Rest from Strife”**;
- Crowley’s Thoth title is **“Truce.”**

In that construction the Four of Swords carries **Focalor/angel 41** and **Vepar/Mikael 42** in its two quinances.

### Why the two systems conflict

The underlying rules are different:

- **Rudd pairing:** match demon and angel by ordinal number.
- **Lenain zodiac:** map angel 42 to the 206th–210th zodiacal degrees.
- **Crowley’s 777:** distribute Goetic spirits through elemental zodiac rows and day/night decanic categories rather than merely placing spirit 1 at 0° Aries and counting forward.

Modern tables frequently merge all three without indicating the seams. Consequently, “Crowley assigns Vepar to Four of Swords” is false: Crowley’s table assigns Vepar to the Taurus/Seven-of-Disks complex.

### Enochian overlays

No Enochian name, aethyr, governor, tablet square, or angel is attached to Vepar in Weyer, Scot, the basic Goetia, or the original 72-name construction.

**[Modern esoteric synthesis]** Skinner and Rankine discuss broader Ruddian relationships between Goetia and Enochian material. Lon Milo DuQuette’s *Tarot of Ceremonial Magick* also overlays astrology, Enochian magic, tarot, and Goetic spirits.

These systems associate Vepar indirectly with an Enochian sector through a shared tarot decan. They do not establish that Vepar was historically an Enochian being.

## Collin de Plancy

### First edition, 1818

Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* first appeared in 1818. Its demonology drew heavily on Weyer, Scot, later demonological literature, folklore, and the author’s changing religious outlook.

### Sixth edition, 1863

The sixth edition describes:

It then says that Vepar:

- guides merchant ships;
- inflicts poisonous wounds;
- can have those wounds cured only by exorcism;
- commands 29 legions.

This is not an exact copy of Weyer:

| Storm/fleet | Included | Omitted from the short entry |
| Three-day decay | Included | Omitted |
| Worms/maggots | Included | Omitted |

### Does the 1863 edition contain a Louis Le Breton portrait of Vepar?

**Finding:** no dedicated Vepar plate was located in the 1863 entry or the catalogued Breton illustrations examined. The page contains prose but not one of the famous full demon engravings.

This matters because modern compilations often label any antique-looking Vepar image “from the *Dictionnaire Infernal*.” The most widely reproduced early mermaid portrait is actually **Crowley’s 1904 figure 77**, not a Louis Le Breton engraving.

Absence from the examined edition is not proof that no stray plate or later derivative edition ever paired an image with the name, but a canonical Breton Vepar portrait comparable to those of Bael, Astaroth, or Buer was not found.

## Crowley’s 1904 portrait

Crowley depicts Vepar as a female-presenting mermaid in profile:

- long loose hair;
- human torso;
- curling fish tail;
- simplified black-line treatment;
- no ship, weapon, wound, or worm imagery.

**[Modern artistic interpretation, 1904]** The drawing visualizes “mermaid” but does not reproduce a manuscript miniature. The manuscripts give seals, not representational portraits.

## Modern art

Modern artists commonly elaborate Vepar as:

- a crowned or armored mermaid;
- a “Duchess of Hell” rather than a male duke;
- a blue- or green-skinned sea demon;
- a naval commander;
- a healer of wounds;
- a patron of sailors, storms, poison, marine biology, or emotional depth.

**[Modern invention or personal gnosis]** Brown hair, blue aura, leather armor, prescribed shells, saltwater bowls, particular candles, and the enn “On ca Vepar ag na” do not appear in the historical source chain. They belong to modern demonolatry, internet occultism, artistic invention, or practitioner testimony.

The female presentation is understandable because the manifested form is a mermaid, but the early texts use masculine grammar and the masculine rank *dux*. “Duchess Vepar” is a modern reinterpretation rather than Weyer’s wording.

## People and works

### Johann Weyer

**Role:** earliest secure printer of Vepar’s name and description, 1577.

Weyer preserved the spirit catalogue while attacking credulous demonology and witch persecution. There is no evidence that he personally conjured Vepar.

### Reginald Scot and Abraham Fleming

**Role:** English transmission, 1584.

Scot incorporated an English version of the catalogue into *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*. The translation has commonly been associated with Abraham Fleming’s work on the demonological material. Scot’s “mermaid,” “stormy,” and “kills” became decisive for later English Goetia.

No Vepar operation by Scot is documented.

### Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

**Role:** angelic constraint scheme associated with Harley 6483.

The recension attributes to the Goetia a 72-angel counterstructure. Peter Smart appears connected with the surviving early-eighteenth-century copy. Rudd’s precise authorship and degree of personal involvement remain disputed.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers

**Role:** translator/compiler of the 1904 printed Goetia.

Mathers produced the text on which Crowley’s publication was based, incorporated manuscript seal variants, and introduced or propagated *Vephar*. No item-specific Vepar working by Mathers has been established.

### Aleister Crowley

**Role:** editor, publisher, artist, and later tabulator.

Crowley:

- published Mathers’s Goetia in 1904 with his own prefatory material;
- drew the mermaid portrait;
- later assigned Vepar to the Taurus/night complex in *777*.

Crowley practiced Goetic magic generally, but no securely documented operation devoted specifically to Vepar was located.

### Frater Achad

No reliable Vepar-specific treatment or operation by Charles Stansfeld Jones/Frater Achad was located. His broader Qabalistic work does not establish an item-specific tradition.

### Joseph H. Peterson

**Role:** manuscript collation and critical edition.

Peterson’s 2001 edition and online transcriptions are central for separating:

- Weyer and Scot;
- manuscript Goetia readings;
- Folger’s *Semper*;
- Mathers’s *Vephar*;
- the various sigils;
- Ruddian material from the ordinary Goetia.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

**Role:** editors and practical reconstructors of Harley 6483.

Their 2007 *Goetia of Dr Rudd* is the principal modern source for the full angel-against-demon method, including Mikael’s relationship to Vepar.

### Poke Runyon

Runyon’s *Book of Solomon’s Magick* and “Master Mandala” arrange the 72 spirits in a systematic zodiacal sequence. This helped spread the simple ordinal quinance model in which number 42 belongs to the last five degrees of Libra.

**[Modern operative synthesis]** That model is coherent but should not be retroactively called Crowley’s *777* attribution.

### Lon Milo DuQuette

DuQuette’s *Tarot of Ceremonial Magick* combines tarot decans with Goetic and Enochian correspondences. His work exemplifies the late-twentieth-century tendency to make the Golden Dawn’s several tables into one visual ritual system.

No unique ancient Vepar tradition follows from that synthesis.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s *Encyclopaedia Goetica* argues more broadly for reading Goetic spirits against ancient, late-antique, medieval, and early-modern spirit traditions rather than only through Victorian ceremonial magic.

No secure Stratton-Kent identification of Vepar with a particular ancient god was located. His broad pagan-continuity framework should not be treated as evidence that Vepar itself has a demonstrated classical precursor.

### Owen Davies, Jan Veenstra, Julien Véronèse, and Jean-Patrice Boudet

These scholars provide historical context for grimoires, medieval ritual magic, and catalogues such as the *Livre des Esperitz*. Their work supports a history of compilation, adaptation, and manuscript circulation rather than a simple claim that all Goetic names descend intact from antiquity.

No pre-Weyer Vepar supplied by this scholarship was located.

## Grimoire ancestors and parallels

## *Testament of Solomon*

The Greek *Testament of Solomon* is a late-antique pseudepigraphon in which Solomon interrogates and controls demons through a divinely empowered ring.

**[Documented absence]** Vepar does not appear by that name.

It is a conceptual ancestor of Solomonic demon catalogues—especially the interrogation of spirits about form, office, adversary, and constraint—but not a direct attestation of Vepar.

## *Livre des Esperitz*

The fifteenth-century French *Livre des Esperitz*, preserved in Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29, contains a catalogue of spirits with ranks, appearances, and offices. Numerous names and descriptions overlap the later Weyer tradition.

**[Documented absence]** Vepar and Separ do not occur in the extant text.

This is important negative evidence. The work demonstrates that much of Weyer's catalogue has medieval antecedents, but Vepar is not among the currently identified overlaps.

## Munich Handbook, Clm 849

The fifteenth-century Munich manual edited by Richard Kieckhefer as *Forbidden Rites* contains necromantic operations and spirit names.

**[Documented absence]** No Vepar or Separ has been identified in it.

It supplies cultural and ritual context, not a direct genealogy for this spirit.

## *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

Weyer explicitly names a *Liber officiorum spirituum* as a source. Later English “Office of Spirits” manuscripts preserve related catalogues, including Folger’s *Semper*.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Vepar probably entered print through this otherwise incompletely recovered family. Whether the name began in a now-lost medieval witness, was introduced in an early-sixteenth-century copy, or resulted from corruption cannot presently be determined.

## *Book of Abramelin*

The surviving Abramelin textual tradition has extensive lists of spirits, although its manuscripts are later than the work’s purported 1458 narrative date.

**[Documented absence]** Vepar/Separ is not an identified Abramelin name. Abramelin is a parallel source of early-modern demonic cataloguing, not evidence for Vepar before Weyer.

- **Milton:** no Vepar in *Paradise Lost*. Milton uses major biblical, classical, and demonological names, but not this one.

These are findings from text and character-list searches; absence claims remain limited by the possibility of unindexed translations, background cameos, or later releases.

### *The Case of the Toxic Spell Dump*

Harry Turtledove’s 1993 novel uses Vepar in a comic bureaucratic-fantasy setting. A magician calls on Vepar in connection with water used against a fire.

**[Modern literary adaptation]** This draws directly on the water-governing office while removing the spirit from the coercive framework of the historical grimoire.

Vepar appears in the wider *Megami Tensei* franchise, beginning with *Digital Devil Story: Megami Tensei II* (1990), where it belongs to the Element race. It subsequently appears in several related titles, including:

- *Last Bible Special*;
- *Giten Megami Tensei: Tokyo Revelation*, including an aquarium-associated encounter.

**Finding:** Vepar is attested in the broader *Megami Tensei* franchise, but no verified appearance in the **Persona** subseries was located.

### *Bloodstained*

In *Bloodstained: Ritual of the Night* (2019), Vepar is a colossal female sea-demon boss encountered aboard the Galleon Minerva. The design expands the mermaid form into a ship-scale monster.

Vepar also appears in *Bloodstained: Curse of the Moon 2* (2020).

**[Modern invention]** The specific anatomy, combat abilities, and narrative role are game designs, although the oceanic placement and female marine form clearly derive from the Goetia.

### *Welcome to Demon School! Iruma-kun*

Lady Vepar is a mermaid- or siren-like demon associated with water magic and the title “Crying Empress.” She serves in an instructional role.

The character preserves the name and marine iconography while creating a distinct modern personality, social position, and magical repertoire.

### *Megido 72*

The Japanese mobile RPG *Megido 72* and its web animation include a character named Vepar, voiced by Kaori Nazuka. The title’s 72-spirit premise explicitly draws on Goetic enumeration.

### *In Nomine Satanis/Magna Veritas*

The French role-playing setting uses **Vephar** as a demonic prince associated with the oceans. This follows Mathers’s variant spelling and expands the maritime office into an infernal political domain.

### Other modern works

Sean McAnulty’s *Vepar’s Infernal Legions* uses the name and legion motif explicitly in its title. Numerous web novels, tabletop settings, collectible games, fan wikis, and occult-fiction projects also employ Vepar, usually as a female sea demon.

These appearances are best treated individually; name use alone does not establish dependence on Weyer rather than on a modern Goetia dictionary or game adaptation.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Was Vepar in Weyer's 1563 edition?

No. **1577** is the secure date for the *Pseudomonarchia* appendix. “1563” mistakenly transfers the date of Weyer's original *De praestigiis* to a catalogue added in a later edition.

### 2. Is Vepar Weyer number 32 or 33?

The actual sequence makes Vepar **33**. A Peterson comparison chart’s “32” is inconsistent with the primary transcription and probably results from an indexing error involving Pruflas.

### 3. Does the Goetia simply add three spirits to Weyer?

No. It adds four—Vassago, Seere, Dantalion, and Andromalius—while omitting Pruflas, yielding a net addition of three.

### 4. Is *Separ* a different demon?

No evidence supports that. Weyer explicitly says “Vepar, alias Separ.” It is an alternative name from the first secure attestation.

### 5. Is *Semper* a different demon?

Probably not. The Folger entry has the same distinctive offices. It is most economically explained as scribal corruption of *Separ* or *Vepar*.

### 6. Is *Vephar* ancient?

No evidence found. It becomes prominent in Mathers–Crowley 1904. Peterson’s manuscript comparison does not support it as a principal Sloane or Harley reading.

### 7. Is Vepar the same as Zepar?

No.

| Vepar | Zepar |
|---|---|
| Weyer 33; Goetia 42 | Weyer 19; Goetia 16 |
| Siren/mermaid | Soldier or warrior |
| Waters, ships, storms | Love or union between women and men |
| Putrefying wounds | Sterility or barrenness in women |
| 29 legions | 26 legions |

Modern conflation probably results from the similarity of **Separ**, **Vepar**, and **Zepar**. Some French and demonolatry pages merge their aliases, but the earliest catalogues keep their offices and positions distinct.

### 8. Does Vepar kill, infect, poison, or heal?

The answer changes by source:

- Weyer: infects or afflicts with putrefying wounds for three days; healing follows completion.
- Scot: kills in three days; diligent healing remains possible.
- Goetia: kills in three days; no cure mentioned.
- De Plancy: poisonous wounds; exorcism is the cure.
- Modern demonolatry: sometimes recasts Vepar as a healer.

The modern healing office is at best an inversion or extrapolation of the early curability clause.

### 9. Does Vepar guide merchant ships?

Only de Plancy explicitly changes the ships into merchant vessels. Weyer says ships laden with arms; Scot says armour; Mathers expands that to arms, armour, and ammunition.

### 10. Does Vepar summon physical ships?

The earliest wording says the sea is made to **appear** filled with ships. Material manifestation, naval teleportation, and command of literal fleets are possible ritual interpretations but not explicit statements.

### 11. Is the mermaid an ancient identity?

No. It is an appearance. There is no evidence that Vepar descends from a named ancient mermaid goddess or classical Siren.

### 12. Is Vepar male or female?

The early texts use masculine grammatical forms and call the spirit a duke, while assigning a mermaid-like appearance. Modern “Duchess,” “she,” and female characterizations are interpretations, not exact early wording.

### 13. Is Vepar 25°–30° Libra or Taurus?

Both answers exist in different systems:

- **Libra:** ordinal Mikael/Vepar system derived from angel 42.
- **Taurus:** Crowley’s actual Goetic table in *777*.

Calling either one universally “the traditional correspondence” conceals the source conflict.

### 14. Is the tarot card Four of Swords or Seven of Pentacles?

- Four of Swords in the ordinal Rudd–Mikael/Lenain quinance synthesis.
- Seven of Pentacles/Disks in Crowley’s *777*.

### 15. Did the original Golden Dawn pair Mikael with Vepar?

No direct evidence was found that the original Golden Dawn curriculum adopted Rudd’s ordinal demon-versus-angel pairing as a standard doctrine. The Golden Dawn supplied the decan and tarot machinery later authors used to construct such syntheses.

### 16. Is Mikael’s period 19–23 October?

That is the later guardian-angel birthday calendar. Lenain’s original entry instead gives zodiacal degrees 206–210 and five separated ruled dates. The systems are related but not identical.

### 17. Is Mikael’s Psalm 120:7 or 121:7?

Both references identify the same verse under different numbering systems:

- Vulgate/Septuagint: 120:7.
- Hebrew/Protestant: 121:7.

### 18. Is the famous mermaid picture by Louis Le Breton?

The widely reproduced line drawing is Crowley’s 1904 figure 77. No dedicated Breton engraving for Vepar was located in the 1863 *Dictionnaire infernal*.

### 19. Are the “enn,” offerings, colors, and personality ancient?

No. Those belong to modern demonolatry and personal-practice literature. They may be meaningful within those traditions but are not found in Weyer, Scot, the manuscripts, Rudd, de Plancy, or Mathers–Crowley.

## Open questions

1. **What manuscript did Weyer actually use?**  
   His *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* is not securely identified with a surviving complete witness. Finding an earlier copy containing Vepar would change the current first-attestation date.

2. **Which spelling is archetypal?**  
   *Vepar* and *Separ* coexist in the earliest print. There is insufficient evidence to decide whether one is original and the other a corruption.

3. **What produced the name?**  
   No convincing linguistic or mythological derivation exists. The Slavic “wild boar,” Latin “brambles,” and *separare* proposals lack a transmission history.

4. **How early is Folger’s *Semper* reading?**  
   Better codicological dating and comparison with related Office-of-Spirits manuscripts could clarify whether *Semper* is independently inherited or copied from the Weyer–Scot tradition.

5. **Where did the first Vepar seal originate?**  
   No explanatory source bridges Weyer’s seal-less catalogue and the seventeenth-century Goetia drawings.

6. **How much of Harley 6483 is authentically Ruddian?**  
   The relationship among Thomas Rudd, Peter Smart, earlier exemplars, and the surviving compilation remains partly reconstructive.

7. **Was the ordinal angel–demon pairing original to Rudd?**  
   Harley 6483 demonstrates the scheme, but its precise inventor and immediate sources are not decisively known.

8. **When was the day/night quinance language attached to the ordinal Libra scheme?**  
   Lenain gives degrees; Crowley gives a separate Goetic day/night system. The exact point at which later practitioners fused these structures deserves a dedicated table-by-table study.

9. **Did de Plancy ever commission a Vepar illustration outside the canonical 1863 plate series?**  
   None was located, but uncatalogued derivative editions and later illustrated dictionaries warrant examination.

10. **Can Vepar be linked to an earlier unnamed marine spirit by description alone?**  
    Similar spirits undoubtedly existed, but resemblance of office is not enough to prove identity without names, textual borrowing, or an intermediate manuscript.
