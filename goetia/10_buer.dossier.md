# Buer: Research Dossier

> **Scope and method.** Labels used throughout:
>
> - **Documented text** — stated directly in a dated primary source.
> - **Scholarly reconstruction** — a proposed textual or historical relationship supported by comparison but not stated by the sources.
> - **Tradition** — a received attribution repeated within a magical or religious lineage.
> - **Esoteric correspondence** — a deliberate occult assignment rather than a historical or linguistic identity.
> - **Disputed claim** — proposed but unsupported, contradicted, or dependent on uncertain evidence.
> - **Legend** — traditional narrative not independently historical.
> - **Modern invention** — a recent artistic, gaming, devotional, or internet addition.
>
> The early texts are public domain, but complete reproductions of hosted editions cannot be copied here. I therefore give decisive excerpts, page and section references, clause-by-clause comparison, and links to the full texts.

## The name and its origins

### Before Weyer: possible textual ancestors

#### The *Livre des Esperitz*

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The closest known pre-Weyer analogue is **Gemer**, number 13 in the French *Livre des Esperitz*, preserved in Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29. The catalogue is usually dated to the fifteenth or early sixteenth century.

Gemer is called a great king and is assigned the power to:

- teach the virtues of herbs and all sciences;
- cure the sick when commanded;
- make people sick;
- command forty legions.

The office is substantially closer to Buer than the name is. Jean-Patrice Boudet, comparing the demon lists, identifies Gemer and Buer as probable textual equivalents. Julien Véronèse and other scholars of medieval ritual magic place the *Livre des Esperitz* within the broader transmission from medieval spirit catalogues to Renaissance demonologies.

Important differences remain:

| *Livre des Esperitz*: Gemer | Weyer: Buer |
|---|---|
| Great king | Great president |
| Forty legions | Fifty legions |
| Teaches herbs and all sciences | Teaches philosophy, logic, and herbs |
| Cures and causes illness | Cures illness |
| No appearance recorded | Appears in a star-like sign |
| Name Gemer | Name Buer |

The correspondence strongly suggests common ancestry or corruption during copying, but it does not establish that “Gemer” evolved mechanically into “Buer.” No intermediate spelling has been found.

#### “Centu” in MS Coxe 25

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Joseph Peterson notes another close office in a late-fifteenth-century Latin manuscript, Oxford, Bodleian Library, MS Coxe 25, p. 181. Its spirit is read as **Centu**:

> “Centu preses magnus … docet … physicam, ethycam, logycam…”

It is a great president who teaches practical or natural philosophy, ethics, and logic. The office and rank are strikingly close to Weyer’s Buer. Yet “Centu” is no more a simple palaeographical form of “Buer” than Gemer is. Whether Gemer, Centu, and Buer represent one corrupt textual lineage or several spirits whose offices were amalgamated remains unresolved.

#### *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

**Documented text and scholarly reconstruction.** Weyer says his source belonged to a book called the *Officium spirituum* or *Liber officiorum spirituum*, also associated with a *Liber Empto. Salomonis*. Johannes Trithemius mentioned books with similar titles in his *Antipalus maleficiorum* of 1508.

The surviving manuscript family contains spirits described chiefly by their “offices”: teaching sciences, finding treasure, healing, causing love, furnishing familiars, and so forth. This is the immediate genre from which Weyer’s catalogue emerged. Nevertheless, no securely dated pre-1577 witness has yet yielded the exact name **Buer**.

#### Munich handbook, Abramelin, and the *Testament of Solomon*

**Negative findings.**

- No clear Buer occurs in the fifteenth-century Munich necromancer’s handbook, Clm 849.
- No securely identified Buer occurs in the spirit lists attached to the *Book of Abramelin*.
- No Buer occurs in the ancient or late-antique *Testament of Solomon*.

The *Testament of Solomon* is an important remote ancestor of the genre: Solomon interrogates demons about their names, functions, thwarting angels, and astrological affiliations. That structure anticipates later catalogues, but it is not evidence that Buer itself is ancient.

### 1577: Weyer’s first secure attestation

**Documented text.** The exact name first appears securely as **Buer** in Johann Weyer’s *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*, appended to the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum* in 1577.

Weyer’s entry begins:

> “Buer Præses magnus conspicitur in signo ☆. Absolutè docet philosophiam, practicam, ethica item & logica, & herbarum vires…”  
> —Weyer, 1577, p. 914, §7

In English: Buer is a great president, seen in a star-like sign; he teaches practical or natural philosophy, ethics, logic, and herbal powers. The remaining clauses give him excellent familiars, the ability to restore the sick—especially human beings—to health, and command over fifty legions.

Weyer was not presenting the catalogue as a devotional manual. He was a physician and critic of witch prosecution who treated many alleged magical phenomena as deception, illness, or demonic imposture. He also says that he withheld portions of the ritual material so that the text could not readily be used. The catalogue’s publication nevertheless made its names available to later English and ceremonial-magical traditions.

### 1584: Reginald Scot

**Documented text.** Reginald Scot translated or closely paraphrased Weyer in *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (London, 1584), p. 378 in the first edition. Scot’s Buer remains number 7:

> “Buer is a great president, and is seene … in the form of a star; he absolutelie teacheth philosophie morall and naturall…”  
> —Scot, 1584

Scot then gives logic, the virtues of herbs, excellent familiars, healing of diseases—especially those of humans—and fifty legions.

Scot’s wording matters because the seventeenth-century English compiler of the *Goetia* appears to have used an edition of Scot, probably the 1665 edition, rather than translating Weyer independently. Shared English wording and mistakes elsewhere in the list support this conclusion.

### Etymology

#### No established ancient etymology

**Negative finding.** Unlike Bael/Baal, Astaroth/Astarte, Amon/Amun, Asmoday/Ashmedai, Belial/Bĕliyyaʿal, or Phenex/Greek *phoinix*, Buer has no demonstrable derivation from a known ancient god, hero, place, Greek noun, Latin noun, or Hebrew demon.

No medieval myth, cult, inscription, biblical figure, classical literary character, or ancient place-name has been shown to stand behind Buer.

#### Proposed derivations

| Proposal | Assessment |
|---|---|
| German or Low German *Buer/Bauer*, “farmer, peasant” | **Disputed.** A real Germanic word and surname, but no textual bridge connects it with the demon. The agricultural association may have encouraged modern herbal interpretations. |
| Westphalian place-name Buer | **Disputed.** The place-name exists, but no evidence connects it to Weyer’s catalogue. |
| French *buer*, “to wash/launder” | **Disputed.** Phonetic coincidence without contextual support. |
| Hebrew root בער, “burn, consume” | **Disputed.** Frequently repeated on occult websites, but the spelling and transmission are unsubstantiated. No Hebrew Buer tradition precedes the Latin catalogue. |
| Latin or Greek derivation | **Negative finding.** No convincing derivation has been demonstrated. |
| Gemer → Buer by scribal corruption | **Scholarly reconstruction.** Supported by matching office, not by a surviving chain of intermediate forms. |
| Centu → Buer | **Scholarly reconstruction.** Supported by rank and office, but palaeographically uncertain. |
| Heramael/Heneral → Buer | **Disputed reconstruction.** Jake Stratton-Kent and related writers compare healing and herbal spirits across the *Officiorum* family. Functional kinship is plausible; identity is not proven. |

The responsible conclusion is therefore: **Buer’s etymology is unknown**.

### Weyer, 1577

Bibliographical reference: Johann Weyer, *De praestigiis daemonum*, fifth edition, Basel, 1577, *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*, p. 914, §7.

Clause-by-clause:

1. Buer is a **great president**.
2. He is seen **in a sign or figure represented by a star glyph**.
3. He teaches philosophy completely.
4. Weyer’s list distinguishes practical/natural, ethical, and logical learning.
5. He teaches the powers of herbs.
6. He supplies excellent familiars.
7. He knows how to restore the sick to health, especially human beings.
8. He rules fifty legions.

There is no lion head, goat leg, wheel, centaur, archer, human transformation, treasure-finding, romantic office, invisibility, transmutation, prophecy, or sobriety teaching.

### Scot, 1584

Bibliographical reference: Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, London, William Brome, 1584, first-edition p. 378; later editions renumber books and pages.

Scot’s important interpretive contribution is to gloss Weyer’s *in signo* as being seen “in the form of a star.” He otherwise follows Weyer closely:

- “president” remains the rank;
- practical/ethical philosophy becomes moral and natural philosophy;
- logic and herbal virtues remain;
- familiars and human healing remain;
- the number is fifty legions.

### Sloane 2731 and 3825

**Documented text.** Both major *Lemegeton* witnesses place Buer at number 10. Sloane 2731 reads, with irregular spelling, that he appears in the relevant shape “when the sun is in Sagitarius.” Sloane 3825 similarly says:

> “The 10th spirit is Buer, a great president and appereth in [Sagittarius]…”  
> —British Library, Sloane MS 3825

The manuscript entry then assigns:

- moral and natural philosophy;
- the logical arts;
- virtues of herbs and plants;
- healing of all human distempers;
- good familiars;
- fifty legions;
- a “seal of obedience” to be worn when calling him.

The important change is **star → Sagittarius**. The wording is not simply a fuller version of Weyer. It represents an English compiler’s reinterpretation.

### Was Sagittarius a translation error?

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Peterson points to problems in the English compiler’s handling of astrological and pictorial phrases. In Weyer, the neighboring spirit Barbatos appears *in signo sagittarii silvestris*—in the image or likeness of a woodland archer. English transmission could turn an image-description into the zodiac sign Sagittarius.

For Buer, Weyer supplies only *in signo* plus a star-like mark. The later sentence “that is his shape when the Sun is there” looks like an explanatory expansion based on reading the mark as a zodiacal sign.

Three possibilities remain:

1. the lost source really contained a particular astrological sign;
2. Weyer’s typography simplified an unidentified emblem to a star;
3. the *Goetia* compiler reinterpreted Weyer/Scot’s star as Sagittarius.

The third is the best-supported current reconstruction, but no autograph source settles the question.

### Harley 6483 and Rudd

British Library Harley MS 6483 is dated 1712–1713. It contains *Liber malorum Spirituum seu Goetia* and material attributed to Thomas Rudd.

Its Buer description follows the established English *Goetia*. Its principal innovation is ritual and angelic:

- Buer remains number 10;
- Aladiah, angel number 10, is the corresponding “thwarting” or constraining angel;
- an angelic name and psalmic sentence are incorporated into the protective/seal apparatus;
- the demon is approached under a superior divine and angelic authority.

Stephen Skinner and David Rankine argue that Harley 6483 is Peter Smart’s later fair copy of material originally assembled by Thomas Rudd around 1655–1656. Peterson is more cautious because the surviving manuscript is later and contains innovations and derivative readings. “Rudd’s system” is therefore a convenient attribution, not proof that every word was written personally by Rudd.

### Mathers–Crowley, 1904

Bibliographical reference: S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Aleister Crowley, *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King*, Foyers, Inverness, 1904, printed p. 16.

The entry opens:

> “The Tenth Spirit is Buer, a Great President. He appeareth in Sagittary, and that is his shape when the Sun is there.”

It then states that he teaches moral and natural philosophy, logic, and the virtues of herbs and plants; heals human illnesses; gives good familiars; rules fifty legions; and has a character of obedience to be worn during appearance.

This is a polished modernization of the English manuscript, not an independent witness.

A marginal note in the 1904 printing says, “I think he can only be invoked at this time.” Because the note is positioned around several successive entries, its exact grammatical target is not beyond doubt. Later readers regularly assign it to Buer’s Sagittarius sentence.

### Peterson, 2001

Joseph H. Peterson’s *The Lesser Key of Solomon: Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis* was published by Weiser in 2001. Peterson collated Sloane 2731, Sloane 3825, Harley 6483, Sloane 3648, and other witnesses instead of merely reprinting Mathers–Crowley.

For Buer, the critical result is conservative:

- the name remains Buer;
- the number remains 10;
- the rank and fifty legions remain;
- the Sagittarius sentence is identified as the manuscript reading;
- manuscript variations do not support the Breton wheel as an original Goetic image;
- comparison with Weyer and Coxe 25 exposes the earlier star-sign and possible textual antecedent.

## Number, order, and rank

### Weyer’s 69 and the *Goetia*’s 72

Weyer’s catalogue contains **69** entries. The *Goetia* reaches **72** through four additions and one omission:

- added: **Vassago**, number 3;
- added: **Seere**, number 70;
- added: **Dantalion**, number 71;
- added: **Andromalius**, number 72;
- omitted: Weyer’s **Pruflas**, also called **Busas**, number 4.

Thus 69 − 1 + 4 = 72.

Buer consequently shifts from Weyer number 7 to Goetia number 10, but the shift is not produced merely by inserting three names before him. The compiler also rearranged several early spirits; notably, Paimon moves from Weyer’s number 22 to Goetia number 9.

The 72-spirit architecture was evidently attractive because it could be correlated with:

- the seventy-two angelic names;
- zodiacal quinances;
- thirty-six decans divided into day and night halves;
- other schemata based on 72.

Those correlations are clearer in later Rudd and Golden Dawn materials than in Weyer.

### President: Mercury and quicksilver

**Esoteric correspondence.** The general rank table printed in the 1904 *Goetia* assigns:

| Rank | Planet | Metal |
|---|---|---|
| King | Sun | Gold |
| Duke | Venus | Copper |
| Prince or prelate | Jupiter | Tin |
| Marquis | Moon | Silver |
| Knight | Saturn | Lead |
| President | Mercury | Mercury/quicksilver |
| Earl/count | Venus and Moon | Copper and silver |

Buer’s presidency therefore implies **Mercury**, not because the Buer entry itself names Mercury, but because a separate rank rule applies to all presidents.

Mathers noticed that the scheme appears imperfect: Mars/iron lacks a straightforward rank, while earls combine Venus and Moon. He speculated about alternative metallic assignments. That speculation should not be confused with the actual table, where presidents are Mercurial.

Mercury fits Buer’s teaching, logic, and learned arts remarkably well, but that fit is an interpretive harmony rather than proof that the original name or office arose astrologically.

## The seal

### Source history

- **Weyer, 1577:** no Buer seal.
- **Scot, 1584:** no established individual Buer seal in the catalogue.
- **Sloane 2731 and 3825:** a Buer “seal” or “character of obedience.”
- **Sloane 3648:** same basic character, name written **BVER**.
- **Harley 6483:** core character embedded in Rudd’s more elaborate angelic construction.
- **Mathers–Crowley, 1904:** engraved/printed redrawing of the manuscript seal.
- **Modern occult art:** often regularizes the linework, adds perfect symmetry, a surrounding circle, colors, planetary glyphs, or the Breton wheel. Those additions are not all manuscript features.

### Description

No early source supplies a verbal blazon. Any verbal description is consequently an editorial approximation of a drawing.

The standard printed form is an irregular linear sigil built around:

- a dominant vertical or oblique central stroke;
- a short crossing arm near its upper portion;
- a large curved, looped, or open rounded body below and to one side;
- smaller hooked or curled terminals;
- one or more small dot- or loop-like details, depending on the copy.

It is **not** a picture of the five-legged wheel. The seal and the Breton portrait are independent visual traditions.

### Variants

The manuscript variants concern:

- whether curves close into loops;
- the angle and length of the transverse bar;
- whether a terminal is a dot, tiny loop, hook, or copying artifact;
- how the lower sweeping curve joins the central line;
- the orientation and proportion of the whole figure.

The available witnesses do not support radically different “first” and “second” seals for Buer. Modern websites sometimes label redrawn versions as separate seals without identifying a manuscript.

Harley 6483’s major distinction is contextual: the demonic character is placed within circles and divine/angelic protection bearing the corresponding angel and verse. The underlying Buer character remains recognizable.

## The angel: Aladiah

## The seventy-two triplets

### Biblical basis

**Tradition.** Exodus 14:19–21 consists, in the consonantal Hebrew text used by later kabbalists, of three verses of seventy-two letters each. A traditional arrangement writes:

1. verse 19 in its ordinary direction;
2. verse 20 in reverse;
3. verse 21 in its ordinary direction.

Reading vertically produces seventy-two three-letter combinations.

The tenth triplet is:

> **אלד** — Aleph, Lamed, Daleth

Later Christian kabbalists and ceremonial magicians made the triplets pronounceable as angelic names by adding endings such as **-el** or **-yah**. The tenth therefore becomes:

> **אלדיה — Aladiah / Aladiyah**

This is a constructed angelic name. It should not be treated as an ordinary Hebrew lexical item.

### What “Shem ha-Mephorash” originally meant

**Historical qualification.** Rabbinic uses of *Shem ha-Mephorash* generally mean the “explicit,” “distinguished,” or fully expressed divine Name, frequently the Tetragrammaton. Later Jewish magic and Christian Kabbalah applied the term more broadly to expansions of forty-two or seventy-two names.

The seventy-two triplets themselves are discussed in medieval Jewish sources including the *Bahir* and writings or commentaries associated with the *Sefer Raziel* tradition. The fully angelized European list used by Rudd is the product of subsequent Christian-kabbalistic transmission.

### Sefer Raziel

**Tradition with historical qualification.** *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* presents itself as angelic wisdom given to Adam through Raziel. Historically it is a compilation of different Jewish magical and cosmological materials. Parts derive from medieval Ashkenazi traditions; some commentary on the seventy-twofold name appears to be later, possibly fourteenth century. The familiar printed Hebrew edition dates from Amsterdam, 1701.

It transmits seventy-two-name material, but it should not automatically be credited with every later Latin spelling, zodiacal degree, psalm, and moral profile attached to Aladiah.

### Kircher

Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* prints the numbered angelic list in which Haziel is number 9 and Aladiah number 10. Kircher was not documenting an ancient Israelite zodiacal guardian-angel calendar. He was synthesizing Jewish, Christian, Hermetic, linguistic, and antiquarian material within a seventeenth-century Catholic framework.

### Rudd’s pairing

**Documented manuscript tradition.** Harley 6483 pairs the tenth demon with the tenth angel:

> **Buer ↔ Aladiah**

The logic is serial rather than etymological. Buer is not “the dark half of Aladiah,” nor is Aladiah historically derived from Buer. Their connection is created by aligning two numbered lists.

In the Rudd arrangement, the angel functions as a superior or thwarting power. Its name and scriptural verse reinforce the divine authority by which the spirit is constrained.

The verse is:

> *Fiat misericordia tua, Domine, super nos, quemadmodum speravimus in te.*

“Let thy mercy, O Lord, be upon us, according as we have hoped in thee.”

Harley 6483 and its modern editions sometimes omit or vary **Domine** in reproducing the line. It is Vulgate Psalm 32:22, modern Psalm 33:22.

### Lenain’s Aladiah

Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* (Amiens, 1823), printed p. 52, assigns Aladiah the divine attribute:

> “Dieu propice.”

Lenain further gives:

- a zodiacal “ray” printed as the 41st through 50th degrees;
- relationship to a decanal genius named **Viroaso**;
- invocation time from **3:00 to 3:20**;
- Psalm 32:22;
- five calendar dates distributed through the year: 29 March, 9 June, 19 August, 31 October, and 11 January;
- help for a person burdened by a concealed crime and fearful of discovery;
- authority against rabies or rage and plague;
- an influence over healing;
- good health, success in enterprise, esteem, and good society for the person born under the influence;
- under the contrary genius, neglect of health and business.

These are **Lenain’s esoteric attributions**, not contents of Exodus or early Jewish angelology.

#### The degree misprint

The tenth five-degree segment in a 72-part zodiac must be degrees 46–50 measured from 0° Aries:

- degrees 1–30: Aries;
- degrees 31–45: 0°–15° Taurus;
- degrees 46–50: **15°–20° Taurus**.

Lenain’s printed “41 to 50” is ten degrees wide and overlaps the preceding angel. It is almost certainly a compositorial or authorial error. Later tables normalize Aladiah to 15°–20° Taurus.

This correction is mathematical and stemmatic; it is not an alternative ancient reading.

### Papus, Ambelain, and Haziel

**Tradition.**

- **Papus** reproduced and popularized Lenain’s angelic framework. A 1909 edition of Lenain carried a prefatory letter by Papus.
- **Robert Ambelain** incorporated the seventy-two angels into twentieth-century French ceremonial and practical Kabbalah. His tables helped normalize degree ranges and occult functions.
- **Haziel**, the pen name of François Bernard-Termés, transformed the system into popular guardian-angel devotion: five-day birth ranges, petitions, moral qualities, and life guidance.

The familiar statement “Aladiah is the guardian angel of people born 6–10 May” belongs chiefly to these modernized calendrical presentations. It should not be projected back into Rudd, Kircher, *Sefer Raziel*, or Exodus.

### The quinance and tarot card

The zodiac has 360 degrees. Dividing it by 72 gives seventy-two segments of five degrees each. Aladiah, number 10, occupies the tenth segment, conventionally **15°–20° Taurus**.

The Golden Dawn tarot system assigns each ten-degree decan to one pip card. Taurus’s second decan, 10°–20° Taurus, is:

- Moon in Taurus;
- **Six of Pentacles** or **Six of Disks**;
- traditionally styled the Lord of Material Success.

Two five-degree angels inhabit that one decan:

- Haziel: 10°–15° Taurus;
- Aladiah: 15°–20° Taurus.

Thus each pip carries two Shem angels.

### Crowley’s separate decan assignment

Crowley’s *Liber 777* uses a different operation for the Goetic spirits. It distributes 72 demons over the thirty-six ten-degree decans as day and night spirits.

For the first Cancer decan:

- **Buer**, number 10: day;
- **Bifrons**, number 46: night;
- zodiac: 0°–10° Cancer;
- planet: Venus by the Chaldean decan sequence;
- tarot: **Two of Cups**.

This produces two independent locations:

| Entity/system | Zodiac |
|---|---|
| Aladiah in the Shem quinances | 15°–20° Taurus |
| Buer in Crowley’s Goetic decans | 0°–10° Cancer |
| Buer’s textual appearance in the *Goetia* | Sun in Sagittarius |

They are incompatible if treated as one natal address. They coexist only as different historical layers.

### Day and night

In *777*, Buer is the **day** demon of the first Cancer decan. That is probably why modern tables sometimes call him “diurnal.”

The original Weyer entry says nothing about day or night. The seventeenth-century *Goetia*’s general rank rules say presidents may be called during most hours except twilight; at night, an additional condition involving the ruling king applies.

A historically precise account must therefore distinguish:

- presidential hours from the *Goetia*;
- Sagittarius as an appearance condition;
- Cancer/day as Crowley’s decan overlay;
- Taurus and 3:00–3:20 as Aladiah’s Lenain-derived properties.

### Lunar timing

The *Goetia*’s preliminary instructions favor the second, fourth, sixth, eighth, tenth, twelfth, and fourteenth days of the Moon. This is a general prescription, not something found in Buer’s individual entry.

### Enochian overlays

**Negative finding.** The Golden Dawn and *777* place Goetic, Enochian, tarot, astrological, and kabbalistic tables in a single encyclopedic structure. But no early source equates Buer with a specific Enochian king, senior, governor, or angel.

Harley 6483 contains broad claims connecting spirits with Enochian or “Enoch” tables, but no securely demonstrated historical Buer–Enochian identity follows. Specific modern equations should be credited to their individual authors rather than presented as Rudd’s or Dee’s teaching.

## Iconography

### Weyer’s star

The earliest iconographic datum is minimal: Buer is seen *in signo* followed by a star-like glyph. The glyph may mean:

- the figure of a star;
- an unidentified copied sign;
- a typographical substitute for an image in Weyer’s source.

It does not depict a lion or animal legs.

### The Sagittarius image

The seventeenth-century *Goetia* turns the sign into Sagittarius. Mathers retained the word “Sagittary.” Crowley’s *777* accordingly suggests:

> a centaur or archer

That is speculation based on Sagittarius, not a description from Weyer or a manuscript illustration.

### Collin de Plancy, 1863

The *Dictionnaire infernal* was first published in 1818 and underwent major revisions. The securely verified Buer wording and portrait considered here are from the sixth edition, Paris, 1863, p. 123.

De Plancy describes Buer as:

> “démon de seconde classe, président aux enfers … une étoile ou … roue à cinq branches…”

He says Buer:

- has the form of a five-branched star or wheel;
- advances by rolling upon himself;
- teaches philosophy, logic, and medicinal herbs;
- claims to give good servants;
- restores the sick to health;
- commands fifty legions.

This combines Scot’s star with a new literalization: a star can become a wheel, and a wheel can roll.

The 1863 portrait, designed by **Louis Le Breton** and engraved in the edition’s workshop, goes further:

- a large, shaggy lion’s head forms the hub;
- five goat-like legs radiate directly from it;
- each leg bends in the same rotational direction;
- there is no torso;
- the limbs form a biological wheel or pinwheel;
- hooves emphasize the infernal and animal quality;
- the gaze is frontal while the body implies continuous rotation.

The lion head and goat legs are artistic inventions. They are not stated by Weyer, Scot, or the *Goetia* manuscripts.

### Was the wheel already in the 1818 edition?

**Open textual question.** De Plancy’s work began in 1818, but the exact 1863 wording and Breton portrait must not automatically be dated to the first edition. The sixth edition was extensively revised and uniquely famous for its engravings. Without a confirmed matching entry in the 1818 printing, **1863 is the secure date for the canonical wheel description and portrait**.

### Modern art and the meme afterlife

Le Breton’s image has become more culturally influential than Buer’s seal. It has been:

- redrawn in occult encyclopedias;
- animated as a rolling creature;
- used in reaction images and surreal memes;
- turned into tattoos, jewelry, game sprites, sculptures, and 3-D models;
- merged with fiery wheels, biblical ophanim, Japanese *wanyūdō*, or Sagittarius;
- inaccurately described as an image copied from a medieval grimoire.

**Modern invention.** There is no single documented “Buer meme” with an established creator or first posting. The reusable public-domain engraving and inherently absurd locomotion produced several independent meme traditions.

Comparisons with Ixion’s wheel, Chiron, the Wheel of Fortune, Ezekiel’s wheels, or *wanyūdō* can be visually suggestive, but no historical evidence makes any of them the source of Le Breton’s design.

## People and works

### Johann Weyer

Weyer is the first secure witness to the name. His importance is double:

- he preserved material from a now-fragmentary *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* tradition;
- he presented it in a skeptical demonological work and says he removed operational passages.

Weyer did not provide a seal or five-legged portrait.

### Reginald Scot

Scot translated Weyer for an English audience and helped transmit the list to the compiler of the *Goetia*. His skeptical purpose—exposing alleged witchcraft and conjuring—did not prevent later magicians from treating the text as authoritative.

### Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

The Rudd-attributed system pairs every Goetic spirit with a Shem angel. Skinner and Rankine reconstruct Thomas Rudd as the mid-seventeenth-century architect and Peter Smart as the later copyist behind Harley 6483.

The attribution is plausible but not proven by an autograph. The surviving codex dates more than half a century after Rudd’s death.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers

Mathers transcribed and translated Solomonic materials in the British Museum. The 1904 book is based on his work, though Crowley was responsible for publication, editing, prefatory material, and his own magical framing.

### Aleister Crowley

Crowley:

- published the Mathers transcription in 1904;
- attached the “Preliminary Invocation,” adapted from the *Bornless Ritual*;
- inserted notes and magical interpretations;
- later gave Buer a Cancer decan and day assignment in *777*;
- suggested a centaur or archer as the magical image.

Owen Davies reports the familiar interpretation that Crowley published Mathers’s transcription amid their personal rupture. The exact motives remain biographical interpretation rather than a fact stated in the book.

### A. E. Waite

Waite summarized Goetic material in *The Book of Black Magic and of Pacts* (1898). Peterson notes that “Lesser Key of Solomon” is not the ordinary manuscript title and may owe its modern prominence, or perhaps its coinage in this context, to Waite.

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 edition is the essential modern textual resource because it collates multiple manuscripts and prints comparisons with Weyer and Scot. For Buer, it makes visible:

- the Weyer/Goetia numbering difference;
- the star/Sagittarius transformation;
- manuscript name and seal variants;
- the possible Coxe 25 cognate;
- the dependence of the English *Goetia* on Scot.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

*The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (Golden Hoard, 2007; later Llewellyn edition) publishes and interprets the Harley 6483 material, including:

- thwarting angels;
- psalms;
- protective circles;
- variant seals;
- ritual implements;
- the reconstructed Rudd–Smart history.

Its major contribution to Buer studies is not a new demonic description but the restored **Buer–Aladiah** pairing.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent approached the Goetic list through the broader *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* family. He emphasized continuities between Buer, Gemer, and spirits such as Heramael whose offices involve medicine and herbs.

This is a productive comparative hypothesis. It remains a reconstruction from shared function and list transmission, not a demonstrated etymology.

### Lon Milo DuQuette

DuQuette, Christopher S. Hyatt, and David P. Wilson’s *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* reproduces and expands Crowley’s later correspondence system. Its Buer table gives the first Cancer decan, daylight, the Two of Cups, and Mercurial associations. It is a twentieth-century interpretive guide, not an independent manuscript witness.

### Poke Runyon

Runyon contributed to the twentieth-century revival of Solomonic evocation through the Church of the Hermetic Sciences, filmed ritual, and *The Book of Solomon’s Magick*. No uniquely influential Buer-specific doctrine was located in the primary material consulted. His relevance is to modern Goetic practice generally.

### Frater Achad

**Negative finding.** No Buer-specific doctrine by Frater Achad was located. Achad’s Qabalistic work belongs to the wider Crowleyan environment, but the Buer decan table derives from Crowley’s *777*, not from a distinct Achad revelation.

### Scholars

- **Jean-Patrice Boudet** supplies the important Gemer/Buer comparison.
- **Julien Véronèse** has established the broader medieval Solomonic and ritual-magic context, although no unique etymology of Buer is attributable to him.
- **Owen Davies** provides the modern social and publishing history of grimoires.
- **Jan Veenstra** is important for medieval learned magic and demonological transmission, but no Buer-specific identification was found in the material consulted.

**Documented modern reuse.** “Buer” is the demonic or divine-name title associated with Nahida, the Dendro Archon, in *Genshin Impact*. The game explicitly uses other Goetic names for major gods and Archons.

Names or creatures called Buer occur in additional independent games and fan works, frequently using the rolling Breton image. Each requires individual verification; name reuse alone does not imply a shared canon.

Other musicians and occult-themed releases have used the public-domain Buer engraving, but those uses do not create an earlier iconographic pedigree.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. “Buer first appeared in 1563”

**Incorrect.** Weyer’s *De praestigiis daemonum* first appeared in 1563, but the *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* was added in the fifth edition of **1577**. The latter is the secure date for Buer.

### 2. An ancient god behind the name

**Unsupported.** No ancient god, hero, demon, place, or lexical source has been demonstrated. Comparisons with Baal, Amun, Astarte, or Hebrew roots are analogical, not evidence.

### 3. Gemer is unquestionably Buer

**Too strong.** The offices strongly suggest a relationship, and Boudet’s equation is valuable. The names, ranks, legions, and one power differ. “Probable textual counterpart” is more accurate than “original spelling.”

### 4. Centu is unquestionably Buer

**Unresolved.** Centu’s presidency and intellectual offices are compelling, but its name and precise textual relationship remain uncertain.

### 5. The five-legged wheel is in the *Goetia*

**Incorrect.** It belongs to de Plancy’s revised description and Le Breton’s nineteenth-century art. The manuscripts say Sagittarius; Weyer and Scot say star or star-like form.

### 6. Buer is a centaur

**Later correspondence.** Crowley’s “probably a centaur or archer” comes from reading Sagittarius pictorially. It is not Weyer’s description.

### 7. Buer has two forms

**Unsupported.** Unlike spirits whose entries explicitly require a change into human form, Buer’s entry gives no second form.

### 8. Buer rules sobriety

**Modern invention.** Healing is traditional, but alcoholism, sobriety, addiction recovery, and temperance are not specified by Weyer, Scot, the manuscripts, or de Plancy.

### 9. Buer and Bune/Bimé are the same spirit

**Unsupported conflation.** Bune/Bimé is a duke with a different number, office, appearance, rank, and name history. Similar initial letters do not establish identity.

### 10. Buer is number 7 or number 10

Both are correct within different catalogues:

- Weyer and Scot: number 7;
- *Ars Goetia*: number 10.

Calling either universally “the original number” ignores the list history.

### 11. Buer is a Sagittarius spirit

**Textually true only at one layer.** The manuscript *Goetia* says Sagittarius, but Weyer says a star-like sign. Crowley later assigns Buer to Cancer. “Sagittarius spirit” is therefore not a stable correspondence across the tradition.

### 12. Buer is a Taurus spirit

**Conflation.** Taurus belongs to **Aladiah’s** five-degree segment, not to Buer’s description or Crowley’s demonic decan.

### 13. Aladiah literally means “Propitious God”

**Needs qualification.** “Dieu propice” is Lenain’s attributed divine quality. The Hebrew base ALD is a three-letter name generated from Exodus; it is not transparently a Hebrew sentence meaning “God is propitious.”

### 14. The 72 angels are all already present in Exodus

**Theological tradition, not literal textual fact.** Exodus supplies the three verses from which later interpreters extract triplets. It does not print a list of vocalized angels called Aladiah, Haziel, and so forth.

### 15. Rudd wrote Harley 6483 personally

**Disputed.** The manuscript dates to 1712–1713, while Rudd died in 1656. Skinner and Rankine’s Peter Smart/fair-copy reconstruction is plausible but inferential.

### 16. Lenain assigns degrees 41–50 to Aladiah

**Printed but internally corrupt.** The text does print that span. A seventy-twofold five-degree sequence requires degrees 46–50, corresponding to 15°–20° Taurus.

### 17. Buer’s seal has one perfectly authoritative geometry

**Incorrect.** Manuscript sigils are hand-copied drawings. Small differences in stroke, closure, hook, dot, and proportion are normal. Highly symmetrical digital forms are editorial redrafts.

### 18. The seal depicts the wheel demon

**Incorrect.** The sigil predates the Breton portrait, and its strokes do not function as a representational drawing of five legs and a lion head.

### 19. Every pip card carries one demon and two angels in the same way

**Misleading.** The Golden Dawn’s two Shem angels divide each ten-degree decan into two quinances. Crowley’s day/night Goetic assignment places two demons in a decan by a different rule. The systems can be tabulated together but are not genetically identical.

### 20. The meme preserves medieval imagery

**Incorrect.** It preserves nineteenth-century French illustration, usually further modified through animation, games, or internet art.

## Open questions

1. **What exactly stood in Weyer’s immediate manuscript source?**  
   His star glyph could preserve, simplify, or replace another sign.

2. **Are Gemer, Centu, and Buer three stages of one entry?**  
   Their offices make this likely enough to investigate, but no continuous manuscript stemma survives.

3. **How did “star” become Sagittarius?**  
   Scribal misunderstanding is probable, but the lost intermediary has not been found.

4. **Where did Buer’s seal originate?**  
   It is absent from Weyer and securely present in the seventeenth-century *Goetia*. Its source before those manuscripts is unknown.

5. **What is the precise relationship among the surviving seals?**  
   A full answer requires high-resolution palaeographical comparison of every manuscript leaf, not only normalized printed tables.

6. **How much of Harley 6483 is genuinely Rudd’s?**  
   The Smart-copy hypothesis explains the late manuscript date, but an autograph or independent contemporary witness is lacking.

7. **Did de Plancy describe Buer as a wheel before 1863?**  
   Earlier editions require edition-by-edition collation. The 1863 wording is secure; automatic retrojection to 1818 is not.

8. **Who first gave Buer a lion head and goat legs?**  
   Le Breton’s published portrait is the first secure source located. Whether an unpublished sketch or intermediary inspired it is unknown.

9. **What was the first Buer internet meme?**  
   No defensible single origin has been established.

10. **Can Buer’s etymology ever be recovered?**  
    Discovery of another *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* witness could clarify whether Buer is a corrupted personal name, a copied rubric, or a transformation of Gemer/Centu.
