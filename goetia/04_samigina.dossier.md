# Samigina: Research Dossier

## Evidence labels used

- **Documented text** — stated directly in a dated primary source or visible manuscript.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference advanced from textual history, codicology, philology, or comparison.
- **Tradition** — a claim transmitted by ritual or occult writers, whether historically verifiable or not.
- **Esoteric correspondence** — an intentional magical association, not necessarily a historical or linguistic relationship.
- **Disputed** — supported by conflicting sources or uncertain attribution.
- **Legend** — supernatural narrative presented without independent historical evidence.
- **Modern invention** — an attribution that first appears in modern occultism, entertainment, or internet writing.

## The name and its origins

### 2. Is “Samigina” ancient?

**Documented text:** The earliest securely dated printed form is Weyer’s **Gamygyn**, in 1577. The principal seventeenth-century *Lemegeton* manuscripts read **Gamigin**, not *Samigina*. The latter became standard largely because Mathers and Crowley printed “SAMIGINA, or GAMIGIN” in 1904.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** *Samigina* is most plausibly a manuscript reading, editorial normalization, or corruption from the *Gamygyn/Gamigin* family. It is not supported as the original form by the source chain presently available.

**Disputed/unsupported:** No reliable Semitic, Hebrew, Greek, or Latin etymology has been demonstrated. Unlike Baal, Astarte, Amun, Asmodeus/Ashmedai, Belial, or Phoenix, Samigina cannot presently be traced to a known ancient god, hero, ethnic name, or place.

Modern claims that the name:

- occurs in the *Zohar*;
- is a Hebrew divine or demonic word;
- means “the one who withdraws,” “the gathering of souls,” or “red as a rose”;
- derives from an otherwise unknown ancient goddess;

are **modern inventions or disputed occult etymologies** unless accompanied by a manuscript citation. Searches of the standard pre-Weyer material have not produced such a citation.

The apparent Hebrew form in Crowley’s *777* is a transliteration of an already existing European demon-name into Hebrew letters. It is not evidence that the name originated in Hebrew.

### 3. The problem of “Cartagra”

Weyer says the souls dwell in purgatory “which is called **Cartagra**, that is, affliction of souls.” Scot and de Plancy preserve the word.

**Documented text:** *Cartagra* occurs in this explanatory context.

**Open philological problem:** No generally accepted Latin, Greek, Hebrew, Arabic, or geographical derivation has been established. It may be a corruption inherited from Weyer’s lost source, a nonce technical name, or a damaged gloss. It should not be silently converted into an otherwise unattested “region of hell.”

### 4. Number and order

Weyer’s work promises or speaks of 72 spirits, but its extant catalogue contains 69 distinguishable descriptions. The *Ars Goetia* has 72.

The relationship is:

- Weyer’s 69;
- minus **Pruflas/Bufas**, omitted by the *Goetia*;
- plus **Vassago, Seere, Dantalion, and Andromalius**;
- equals the *Goetia*’s 72.

Samigina’s dramatic movement from the middle of Weyer’s catalogue to position 4 supports Peterson’s observation that portions of the source may have been reordered like shuffled leaves.

A counting complication must be preserved:

- In a strict count that treats the embedded description of **Belial** as a separate spirit, Weyer’s Gamygyn is number **47**.
- Older tables frequently number Gamygyn **46**, effectively failing to advance the count at the embedded Belial passage.
- Scot omits Pruflas, so Gamigin actually falls at **46** in Scot’s 68-entry sequence.
- In the *Ars Goetia*, he is unambiguously **4**.

Thus “Weyer 46” is widespread conventional indexing, while 47 better reflects a complete census of Weyer’s descriptions.

## The description across the sources

### 1. Weyer, 1577

Weyer’s fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum* contains the expanded *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*. Gamygyn appears on original page 927. The modern Peterson transcription counts it §47; older electronic editions often label it §46.

> **Gamygyn magnus Marchio: in forma equi parvi visitur: at ubi hominis simulachrum assumit, raucam edit vocem, de omnibus artibus liberalibus differens: efficit quoque, ut coram exorcista conveniant animæ in mari exeuntes, & quæ degunt in purgatorio (quod dicitur Cartagra, id est, afflictio animarum) & corpora aërea suscipiunt, apparentque evidenter, & ad interrogata respondent. Permanet apud exorcistam, donec ipsius votum expleverit. Triginta legiones in sua habet potestate.**

— Johann Weyer, *De praestigiis daemonum*, 5th ed. (Basel, 1577), *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*, p. 927; transcribed at [Esoteric Archives](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

A close gloss is: Gamygyn is a great Marquis, seen as a small horse. When he assumes a human likeness, he speaks with a hoarse voice and discusses all the liberal arts. He causes souls departing in the sea and souls dwelling in purgatory—called Cartagra, “affliction of souls”—to assemble before the exorcist, assume airy bodies, appear plainly, and answer questions. He remains until the exorcist’s wish is fulfilled and commands 30 legions.

Important readings:

- *equi parvi* — “a small/little horse,” not an ass.
- *raucam … vocem* — unequivocally “a hoarse voice.”
- *de omnibus artibus liberalibus differens* — discussing or discoursing on all liberal arts.
- *animae in mari exeuntes* — literally souls “departing in the sea,” interpreted by Scot as those drowned at sea.
- *corpora aërea* — airy or aerial bodies.
- 30 legions.

Weyer said that his catalogue came from a work called *Liber officiorum spirituum*, also associated with the title *Empto. Salomonis*. The actual exemplar has not survived. Weyer deliberately withheld parts of the conjuring apparatus, explaining that he did not wish readers to use it operationally. His skeptical treatment of witch trials does not mean that every demon entry was intended merely as satire.

### 2. Scot, 1584

Reginald Scot incorporated an English version of Weyer’s catalogue into Book XV of *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*. The translation may have involved Abraham Fleming, although the printed book places the material under Scot’s authorship.

> **Gamigin is a great marquesse, and is seene in the forme of a little horsse, when he taketh humane shape he speaketh with a hoarse voice, disputing of all liberall sciences; he bringeth also to passe, that the soules, which are drowned in the sea, or which dwell in purgatorie (which is called Cartagra, that is, affliction of soules) shall take aierie bodies, and evidentlie appeare and answer to interrogatories at the conjurors commandement; he tarrieth with the exorcist, untill he have accomplished his desire, and hath thirtie legions under him.**

— Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (London, 1584), Book XV, p. 224; transcription at [Esoteric Archives](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/scot16.htm), facsimile at [Cornell University Library](https://digital.library.cornell.edu/catalog/witchcraft081).

Scot clarifies Weyer in several places:

- souls “departing in the sea” become souls “drowned in the sea”;
- *raucam* is correctly translated “hoarse”;
- the spirits answer interrogatories “at the conjuror’s commandment”;
- the number remains 30 legions.

This is a translation or close adaptation of Weyer, not independent testimony about an entity.

### 3. The Folger *Book of Oberon*

Folger Shakespeare Library MS V.b.26, compiled around the late sixteenth century, contains two related descriptions.

A short list names “**Cagyne vel Cogin**,” appearing as a pale horse and able to bring a soul not presently under heavenly or infernal power. A longer office describes “**Sogan vel Sogom**,” a great marquis who appears as a pale horse, speaks hoarsely, teaches mathematical science, wisdom, and philosophy, and brings forth souls associated with purgatory, waters, and seas in airy bodies.

The longer entry gives **36 legions**, rather than Weyer’s 30.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** The unusual combination of pale horse, hoarse voice, liberal or mathematical learning, purgatorial souls, waters, and airy embodiment makes these functional cognates of Gamygyn. The radically altered names show either difficult manuscript transmission or dependence on a divergent *Offices of Spirits* recension.

**Limitation:** The Folger manuscript overlaps chronologically with or postdates Weyer. It therefore cannot by itself establish a pre-1577 attestation of Gamygyn.

A full transcription is available from [Esoteric Archives](https://www.esotericarchives.com/folger/v_b_26_transcription.pdf).

### 4. Sloane 3825

Sloane MS 3825, fol. 101r, reads:

> **The 4th spirit is called Gamigin, a great Marquise, he appereth in ye forme of a litle horse or asse and then into humane shape he putteth himself at ye request of ye Master and speaketh wth a horse voice; he teaches all Liberall siences, and giveth an account of ye dead soules of them that dye in sin. & he ruleth over 30 Legions of Inferiours &c. This is his seal, wch is to be worne by the Magician when he Invocateth.**

— British Library, Sloane MS 3825, fol. 101r; transcription in Joseph Peterson’s [*Ars Goetia*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

Changes from Weyer include:

- “horse or ass,” rather than horse alone;
- “horse voice,” almost certainly an error for “hoarse voice”;
- “dead souls of them that die in sin,” replacing the more elaborate sea, purgatory, Cartagra, aerial-body, and interrogation passage;
- “30 legions of inferiors”;
- the addition of a ritual seal to be worn by the magician.

The text’s “Marquise” is an archaic spelling of the title and is followed by masculine pronouns. It is not evidence that the spirit was originally female.

### 5. Other *Lemegeton* witnesses

Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, Harley 6483, Northwestern University MS 65, and Wellcome MS 3203 preserve **Gamigin**.

The manuscript order varies. Wellcome MS 3203 makes Gamigin number 5 because it transposes Gamigin and Marbas. This is evidence of copying and rearrangement, not a separate occult hierarchy.

Harley 6483 restores material close to Weyer and Scot:

> he bringeth also to passe, that the souls which are drowned in the sea shall take airy bodies and evidently appeare and answer to Interrogatories at the request of the Exorcist.

It therefore preserves both the shorter *Goetia* office and part of the older drowned-souls tradition.

### 6. Mathers–Crowley, 1904

> **(4.) SAMIGINA, or GAMIGIN.—The Fourth Spirit is Samigina, a Great Marquis. He appeareth in the form of a little Horse or Ass, and then into Human shape doth he change himself at the Request of the Master. He speaketh with a hoarse voice. He ruleth over 30 Legions of Inferiors. He teaches all Liberal Sciences, and giveth account of Dead Souls that died in sin. And his Seal is this, which is to be worn before the Magician when he is Invocator, etc.**

— S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Aleister Crowley, *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King* (1904), printed p. 13, scan p. 25 in this [facsimile PDF](https://lo2.org/pdf/celepha%C3%AFs/mathers/Mathers-and-Crowley.Goetia.pdf).

This edition:

- makes **Samigina** the headword while retaining Gamigin;
- corrects “horse voice” to “hoarse voice”;
- retains horse or ass, human transformation, 30 legions, liberal sciences, and the dead who died in sin;
- does not restore the drowned-at-sea, Cartagra, aerial-body, or interrogation clauses;
- publishes a polished circular seal.

Mathers prepared the grimoire text. Crowley published and supplemented it with ritual and interpretive material, including the “Preliminary Invocation,” an “Initiated Interpretation,” and Enochian invocations. It is therefore imprecise to describe every word of the 1904 book as Crowley’s translation.

### 7. Peterson, 2001

Joseph H. Peterson used Sloane 3825 as the principal witness while collating other manuscripts and printed editions. Gamigin appears on p. 8 of the 2001 edition, corresponding to Sloane 3825 fol. 101r. Peterson preserves the manuscript’s “horse voice” in the diplomatic text while the notes and parallel witnesses make the correct “hoarse” reading evident.

Peterson’s work is a modern critical edition, not a new revelation or working. It is especially important because it exposes readings that the polished Mathers–Crowley edition silently normalized. See [the publisher’s edition page](https://redwheelweiser.com/book/the-lesser-key-of-solomon-9781578632206/) and Peterson’s [online transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

## The angel: Elemiah

### 1. The 72-fold Name

The angelic scheme begins with Exodus 14:19–21. In the Hebrew text, each of these three verses has 72 letters. Kabbalistic practice writes them in alternating directions—a boustrophedon arrangement—and reads vertical groups of three letters. This produces 72 three-letter divine-name units.

**Documented Jewish tradition:** Medieval Jewish sources recognize a 72-fold divine Name derived from the Exodus verses. The units were not originally a European catalogue of 72 guardian angels with birthdays, personality descriptions, and tarot cards.

**Christian-kabbalistic development:** Writers added the suffixes **-el** or **-yah**, turning the triads into pronounceable angelic names. Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517) is an important early printed witness to this Christian angelological treatment. Kircher subsequently systematized the names in *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–1654).

The fourth triad is עלם. With the suffix יה it becomes עלמיה, conventionally **Elemiah**.

### 2. Meaning

The established Latin gloss is **Deus absconditus**, “the hidden God.”

The consonantal root עלם can bear the sense of hiding, concealment, or being unknown. That makes the gloss linguistically intelligible. Nevertheless:

- **Documented philology:** the consonantal root has associations with concealment.
- **Tradition:** “The Hidden God” is the angel’s conventional title.
- **Caution:** the fully vocalized personal angel Elemiah is a kabbalistic construction, not a biblical person named in Exodus.

### 3. Rudd’s pairing

Harley MS 6483 pairs the 72 Goetic spirits with the 72 angels in matching numerical order. Spirit 4, Gamigin, is therefore paired with angel 4, **Elemiah**.

The angelic side bears the Psalm formula:

> **Convertere Domine, et eripe animam meam, salvum me fac propter misericordiam tuam.**

“Return, O Lord, and deliver my soul; save me for thy mercy’s sake.”

This is Psalm 6:4 in most modern English Bibles and Psalm 6:5 in Vulgate-based numbering.

Rudd’s construction is defensive and coercive. The magician wears a double-sided lamen or uses paired seals: the demon’s sigil on one face and the corresponding divine or angelic name and Psalm on the other. The angelic side is directed toward the magician’s heart. The theory is that a specific celestial power constrains, balances, or “thwarts” the correspondingly numbered spirit.

Stephen Skinner and David Rankine publish the Gamigin–Elemiah material on pp. 104–106 of *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (2007).

**Disputed attribution:** Harley 6483 was copied by Peter Smart in 1712–1713 from papers attributed to Thomas Rudd. Skinner and Rankine argue that Rudd’s original material may date to approximately 1655–1656. Other researchers caution that the compiler’s identity is not conclusively demonstrated. Accordingly:

- the manuscript and its early eighteenth-century copy are documented;
- the underlying system may be seventeenth century;
- personal authorship by Thomas Rudd remains an attribution rather than a certainty.

A recurring internet claim pairs Samigina with **Ieuiah**. That conflicts with the same-number arrangement preserved in the Rudd edition and appears to be a displaced or erroneous table entry. In the Rudd system, number 4 is Elemiah.

### 4. Elemiah in Lenain

Lazare Républicain Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* (Amiens, 1823) gives the later, elaborate angelic profile:

- number 4;
- **16th through 20th degrees of Aries**, meaning the five-degree interval conventionally written 15°–20° Aries;
- invocation time approximately **1:00–1:20**;
- Psalm 6:5 in his numbering;
- divine name **Allah** in the Arabic-language column;
- power against spiritual or mental torment and for discovering traitors;
- governance of journeys, maritime expeditions, and useful discoveries;
- favorable character: industrious, successful, fond of travel;
- contrary genius: harmful education, socially dangerous discoveries, and impediments to enterprise.

Lenain also gives five scattered calendar days—23 March, 3 June, 14 August, 25 October, and 5 January—according to an older method of distributing the 72 names through the year. The familiar continuous assignment around **5–9 April** is a later simplification produced by dividing the solar year into consecutive five-degree portions.

These moral, vocational, and natal qualities are **nineteenth-century angelic astrology**. They are not present in the biblical text, Weyer, Scot, or the early *Goetia* manuscripts.

### 5. Papus, Ambelain, and Haziel

- **Papus/Gérard Encausse:** *La Cabbale* and his endorsement of later Lenain editions helped circulate the French occult treatment of the 72 angels. His work belongs to the French occult revival, not ancient Jewish exegesis.
- **Robert Ambelain:** *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) reproduces and expands the Lenain system. Elemiah is placed among the Seraphim, under Metatron, and supplied with practical invocation material.
- **Haziel:** Under this pseudonym, later twentieth-century books popularized the 72 names as individual “guardian angels,” often teaching that a person has three angels calculated from birth date, time, and related divisions. This is **modern popular angelology**, not Rudd’s coercive demon–angel pairing and not the original Jewish 72-fold Name.

### 6. Golden Dawn transmission

Golden Dawn materials inherited Christian-kabbalistic lists of the 72 names and assigned them to five-degree zodiacal sectors. They also mapped the 36 ten-degree decans to the numbered tarot cards.

Consequently, Elemiah’s 15°–20° Aries quinance falls within:

- Aries 10°–20°;
- Sun in Aries;
- the **Three of Wands**;
- Golden Dawn title: **Lord of Established Strength**.

The first half of that decan, 10°–15° Aries, belongs to angel 3, Sitael; the second half, 15°–20°, to angel 4, Elemiah. Only when this sequential angel scheme is combined with Rudd’s same-number demon pairing does Samigina acquire the Three of Wands.

### The rank planets and metals

The *Goetia*’s “Observations” assign:

| Rank | Planet | Metal |
|---|---|---|
| King | Sun | Gold |
| Marquis | Moon | Silver |
| Duke | Venus | Copper |
| Prince or Prelate | Jupiter | Tin |
| Knight | Saturn | Lead |
| President | Mercury | Mercury |
| Earl/Count | Venus and Moon | Copper and silver |

Later tables sometimes regularize Earls under Mars and iron. That is not the reading of the principal *Goetia* observation. For Samigina the matter is simpler: a Marquis belongs to the **Moon and silver** throughout the usual Golden Dawn/Crowley rank symbolism.

The Moon is the planet of rank. It must not be confused with:

- Mercury in Taurus, the ruler of Crowley’s Five of Pentacles decan;
- the Sun in Aries, ruler of Elemiah’s Three of Wands decan;
- claims that Samigina is “solar” because of the angel’s later zodiacal placement.

These are different layers answering different questions.

### Crowley’s *777*

Crowley’s table CLV gives the fourth demon as approximately:

> **4 — ☽ — Gamigina — Little horse or ass.**

The Moon sign marks the Marquis rank.

Crowley distributes demons 1–36 as the daytime spirits of the 36 decans and demons 37–72 as the corresponding nighttime spirits. Therefore:

- spirit 4 Samigina = daytime demon of decan 4;
- decan 4 = 0°–10° Taurus;
- tarot card = Five of Pentacles/Disks;
- astrological force = Mercury in Taurus;
- spirit 40 Raum = night demon of the same decan.

This is how each tarot pip receives two demons. It is not derived from Rudd’s Elemiah pairing.

### Astrological timing of conjuration

The *Goetia* says a Marquis may be bound or called:

- from 3 p.m. until 9 p.m.;
- from 9 p.m. until sunrise.

Its general lunar observation prefers an even-aged Moon: the 2nd, 4th, 6th, 8th, 10th, 12th, or 14th day.

**Documented ritual prescription:** These rules occur in the *Goetia*’s general observations.

**Later esoteric practice:** A magician might additionally choose:

- the planetary hour of the Moon;
- a waxing Moon;
- 15°–20° Aries for an Elemiah-centered operation;
- 0°–10° Taurus for Crowley’s Samigina decan;
- the Sun’s passage through the relevant zodiacal interval.

No single early manuscript combines all these elections. Stacking them produces a modern synthetic ritual.

### Enochian overlays

Rudd’s broader manuscript complex draws on John Dee’s angelic magic, including the *Tabula Sancta*. Crowley’s 1904 publication adds Enochian calls and the “Preliminary Invocation.”

These are **later ritual overlays**. Neither Weyer’s Gamygyn description nor Scot’s translation associates the spirit with Enochian tablets, Calls, aethyrs, or Dee’s watchtowers.

## The seal

### 1. Origin and function

Weyer and Scot provide no seal for Gamygyn. The seal enters the surviving record through the seventeenth-century *Lemegeton* manuscript tradition.

This distinction matters:

- the *office*—horse, hoarse speech, liberal sciences, and dead souls—comes from the earlier *Offices of Spirits* tradition;
- the familiar Goetic seal is not securely attested with the name before the *Lemegeton* manuscripts.

The manuscript instructs that it is to be worn by the magician when invoking the spirit. In the broader system, the seal is drawn on the metal appropriate to rank: for Samigina, silver.

### 2. Graphic description

In its stable printed form, the seal contains:

- a central upright or stem;
- a small cross-like or forked termination at its top;
- lateral arms spreading from the upper section;
- a broad rounded arch or dome underneath;
- a horizontal bar crossing the lower form;
- several short vertical strokes that divide the arch into narrow compartments, giving it the appearance of a grille or barred gate;
- a small dot at or immediately below the lower centre.

The 1904 Mathers–Crowley version encloses this character in a double circular band and writes **SAMIGINA** around the band.

The significant transmission variants are:

- **Sloane manuscripts:** essentially an unlettered working character, with normal pen-level variation in the curvature, number or length of lower bars, and position of the dot.
- **Harley 6483:** the demonic character is incorporated into a more elaborate paired angel–demon lamen; circles and Hebrew divine or angelic material are supplied.
- **Mathers–Crowley:** a clean, symmetrical, double-circled emblem labeled with the Roman-letter name.
- **Modern redraws:** often regularize the lattice, produce perfect geometric circles, alter the number of bars, omit or enlarge the dot, or add colors, planetary signs, heptagrams, “enns,” and decorative alphabets.

Those modern graphic refinements should not be mistaken for manuscript details.

The common printed seal can be viewed through [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Samigina_Seal.jpg), while Peterson reproduces the manuscript-derived form in [his *Goetia* transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

## Iconography

### 1. Collin de Plancy

Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* first appeared in 1818. The famous sixth edition of 1863 included illustrations attributed principally to Louis Le Breton.

The 1863 entry reads:

> **Gamygyn, grand marquis des enfers. C’est un puissant démon. On le voit sous la forme d’un petit cheval. Mais dès qu’il prend celle d’un homme, il a une voix rauque et discourt sur les arts libéraux. Il fait paraître aussi devant l’exorciste les âmes qui ont péri dans la mer, et celles qui souffrent dans cette partie du purgatoire qui est appelée Cartagra (c’est-à-dire affliction des âmes). Il répond clairement à toutes les questions qu’on lui fait ; il reste auprès de l’exorciste jusqu’à ce qu’il ait exécuté tout ce qu’on lui ordonne ; cependant là-bas, trente légions lui sont soumises.**

— Collin de Plancy, *Dictionnaire infernal*, 6th ed. (Paris, 1863), p. 295, [Wikisource transcription](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.%2C_1863/Gamygyn).

Translation: Gamygyn is a great marquis of hell and a powerful demon. He appears as a little horse; in human form he speaks hoarsely and discusses the liberal arts. He makes visible the souls lost at sea and those suffering in the purgatorial region Cartagra. He answers questions, remains until commanded work is complete, and rules 30 legions.

De Plancy cites Wier/Weyer. This is a French retelling of the inherited description, not an independent nineteenth-century encounter.

### 2. Was there a Le Breton portrait?

No identifiable Louis Le Breton portrait of Gamygyn appears in the standard illustration set of the 1863 edition. The entry is text-only.

Therefore:

- the famous engravings of Bael, Astaroth, Asmodeus, Buer, Stolas, and others should not be reassigned to Samigina;
- online images labeled “de Plancy’s Samigina” require separate verification;
- horse-demons, winged women, skeletal horses, or equine necromancers presented as Samigina are normally modern illustrations.

A complete scan is available through the [Bibliothèque nationale de France](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k5754923d).

### 3. Modern art

Modern artists usually emphasize one or more of four textual motifs:

1. a small, pale, black, or spectral horse;
2. transformation into a human figure;
3. the drowned dead rising in aerial or translucent bodies;
4. a scholar or necromancer surrounded by books.

Female depictions often arise from reading the archaic title “Marquise” as a gender designation. Early texts consistently use masculine pronouns.

Modern demonolatry sometimes depicts Samigina as a winged woman or androgynous teacher and supplies a spoken “enn,” commonly rendered in forms such as “Esta ta et tasa Gamigin.” That formula is **modern invention**: it does not occur in Weyer, Scot, the Sloane manuscripts, Harley 6483, Mathers–Crowley, Lenain, or the medieval Shem tradition.

### Grimoire ancestors

#### *Testament of Solomon*

The *Testament of Solomon* supplies the broad literary paradigm of Solomon compelling demons, learning their functions, and identifying thwarting angels. It includes recognizable ancient figures such as Asmodeus, but no Gamigin/Samigina.

**Scholarly reconstruction:** Rudd’s matching of a demon to a defeating angel resembles the *Testament*’s logic, but a direct textual line from a particular *Testament* passage to Gamigin has not been demonstrated.

#### Munich Handbook, Clm 849

This fifteenth-century handbook contains several names later found in Weyer’s catalogue. Comparative work identifies roughly ten close correspondences, but not Gamygyn.

#### *Livre des Esperitz*

The French *Livre des Esperitz*, preserved in Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29, gives 46 spirit offices; about 35 have recognizable relatives in Weyer. No convincing Gamygyn entry appears in the published comparisons.

This establishes that Weyer’s catalogue belongs to a broader late-medieval *Offices of Spirits* family. It does not establish that every Weyer spirit existed in the surviving *Livre des Esperitz*.

#### *Liber officiorum spirituum*

Trithemius recorded the title *Liber officiorum spirituum* in 1508. Weyer later named a work of this type as his source.

**Documented:** the title and textual genre predate Weyer.

**Unproven:** no surviving pre-1577 copy has yet supplied the exact name Gamygyn. The existence of an older source-book cannot be converted into an attested older occurrence of this particular name.

#### *Abramelin*

No secure Gamygyn/Gamigin/Samigina match has been identified in the commonly published *Abramelin* spirit lists. Because the *Abramelin* manuscript families contain unstable spellings, total exclusion is difficult, but proposed matches require more than a loose resemblance of two or three letters.

### Later practitioners and scholars

- **Aleister Crowley:** Published the 1904 book, added ritual apparatus, and later supplied the *777* decan system. His “Initiated Interpretation” treats spirits psychologically—as portions or faculties of the human mind. That is a modern esoteric interpretation, not the stated ontology of Weyer or the manuscripts.
- **Frater Achad:** No well-attested Samigina-specific operation or distinctive doctrine was located. His relevance is to Crowleyan Qabalah generally.
- **Poke Runyon:** His *Book of Solomon’s Magick* includes the Goetic spirits and develops a revised ceremonial system with directional, elemental, and mirror-based practice. Such assignments are twentieth-century practitioner reconstructions.
- **Lon Milo DuQuette, Christopher Hyatt, and David P. Wilson:** *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* republishes and visually interprets the Goetic material. It is an important modern reception work but not an early textual witness.
- **Stephen Skinner and David Rankine:** Published and interpreted the Rudd materials, making the Gamigin–Elemiah pairing widely accessible.
- **Joseph H. Peterson:** Produced the most useful accessible textual collation of Sloane, Harley, Weyer, Scot, and related witnesses.
- **Jake Stratton-Kent:** Reconstructed the broader spirit-office and grimoire context in the *Encyclopaedia Goetica*. His work is practitioner scholarship and should be distinguished from manuscript cataloguing or academic textual editions.
- **Owen Davies:** Provides broad historical scholarship on grimoires, circulation, print, and popular magic rather than a Samigina-specific thesis.
- **Jan Veenstra, Jean-Patrice Boudet, and Julien Véronèse:** Their work on learned magic, spirit catalogues, and medieval ritual texts contextualizes the *Livre des Esperitz* and related traditions. None supplies a presently accepted ancient etymology for Samigina.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Gamygyn, Gamigin, or Samigina?

**Best-supported historical sequence:** Gamygyn → Gamigin → Samigina/Gamigin.

There is no evidence that *Samigina* is the pristine ancient name and *Gamigin* a later corruption. The reverse is closer to the surviving chronology: *Samigina* emerges securely in 1904.

### 2. Number 46 or 47 in Weyer?

Both numbers occur in modern reference works because of different handling of Belial within Weyer’s sequence.

- Strict census: Gamygyn 47.
- Conventional older table: Gamygyn 46.
- Scot, after omitting Pruflas: Gamigin 46.
- Goetia: Samigina/Gamigin 4.

Any claim that the number changed simply from 46 to 4 omits the editorial counting problem.

### 3. Horse voice versus hoarse voice

- Weyer: *raucam vocem*, “hoarse voice.”
- Scot: “hoarse voice.”
- Sloane 3825: “horse voice.”
- Harley/Rudd: “hoarse voice.”
- Mathers–Crowley: “hoarse voice.”

The Sloane reading is a scribal error or visual/aural pun encouraged by “little horse.” It is not evidence that the spirit literally speaks in a horse’s voice, although modern fiction may use it that way.

### 4. Horse or ass?

Weyer and Scot say only a little horse. The *Goetia* manuscripts add “or ass.” No ancient mythological source for the ass form is known.

### 5. Drowned souls or sinful dead?

The differences form a recoverable transmission:

1. Weyer: souls who die or depart in the sea plus souls in purgatory;
2. Scot: explicitly drowned souls and purgatorial souls;
3. Folger: souls near waters and seas and in purgatorial conditions;
4. Sloane: dead souls of those who die in sin;
5. Harley/Rudd: combines the shorter sinful-dead office with drowned souls and airy bodies;
6. Mathers–Crowley: retains only the sinful-dead formulation.

The claim that Samigina is exclusively the demon of “forgotten dead” or “souls erased from memory” is a modern poetic expansion.

### 6. Is this necromancy?

In the historical sense, producing dead souls to appear and answer questions is necromantic. Nevertheless, the texts present Samigina as an intermediary or coercer of souls, not necessarily as one of the dead.

The airy bodies reflect medieval and early modern theories in which spirits required a subtle or aerial vehicle to become visible. They should not automatically be equated with modern ideas of ectoplasm or astral projection.

### 7. Is Cartagra a real place?

It is real as a word in this textual tradition. It is not independently established as:

- a named region in Catholic doctrine;
- a classical underworld;
- a Hebrew afterlife term;
- an ancient city of the dead.

Calling it a fixed “district of purgatory” goes beyond the evidence.

### 8. Elemiah or Ieuiah?

Rudd’s ordinal method makes angel 4 **Elemiah** the partner of spirit 4. Ieuiah is angel 33 in the conventional Shem list. The Samigina–Ieuiah pairing found on some websites is therefore an error unless the author explicitly announces a different permutation.

### 9. Which zodiac sign and tarot card?

Both common answers have sources, but they belong to different systems:

- **15°–20° Aries / Three of Wands:** Samigina → Rudd’s angel 4 Elemiah → Lenain’s quinance → Golden Dawn tarot decan.
- **0°–10° Taurus / Five of Pentacles:** Crowley’s direct allocation of Goetia spirit 4 as the fourth daytime decan demon.

It is historically misleading to cite the first as “Crowley’s *777* correspondence.”

### 10. Moon or Sun?

- Moon/silver comes from Samigina’s rank as Marquis.
- Sun in Aries governs the Three of Wands decan containing Elemiah’s quinance.
- Mercury in Taurus governs the Five of Pentacles in *777*.

These are simultaneous only in a synthetic modern table. Claims that Samigina’s rank is solar and therefore requires gold contradict the *Goetia*’s Marquis rule.

### 11. Did de Plancy or Le Breton draw Samigina?

De Plancy wrote an entry for Gamygyn. No confirmed Le Breton engraving specifically representing Gamygyn has been identified in the 1863 illustration series. Modern portraits should not be captioned as de Plancy images without a page reference.

### 12. Is Samigina female?

Early texts use masculine pronouns. The feminine interpretation rests principally on:

- misunderstanding “Marquise” as a gendered designation rather than variable spelling of the noble rank;
- modern visionary or demonolatry accounts;
- modern character design.

A female Samigina is a legitimate modern artistic interpretation, but not the documented early description.

### 13. Is there a pre-Weyer attestation?

No secure one is presently known.

The *Liber officiorum spirituum* genre is older. Related functions and corrupted names occur in the Folger manuscript. But:

- the exact name is not found in the known *Livre des Esperitz* list;
- it is not in the Munich manual under a recognizable form;
- it is not in the *Testament of Solomon*;
- no convincing *Abramelin* identification has been shown;
- the Folger manuscript cannot confidently be dated earlier than Weyer’s 1577 printing.

The earliest secure attestation therefore remains **Gamygyn, 1577**.

## Open questions

1. **Weyer’s immediate exemplar:** The lost *Liber officiorum spirituum* or *Empto. Salomonis* manuscript might have contained an older spelling, but it has not been identified.

2. **The Folger names:** Whether Cagyne/Cogin and Sogan/Sogom derive from one badly transmitted name or from two parallel offices remains unresolved.

3. **Cartagra:** No satisfactory etymology or independent doctrinal parallel has been established.

4. **The birth of “Samigina”:** The exact manuscript, editorial judgment, or copying accident from which Mathers obtained the form has not been conclusively demonstrated. It may reflect a witness not represented in Peterson’s principal comparison.

5. **Seal genealogy:** The seal is absent from Weyer and Scot. Its earlier visual ancestor, if any, remains unknown. A stroke-by-stroke stemma would require direct comparison of every surviving manuscript at full resolution.

6. **Rudd authorship:** The early eighteenth-century Peter Smart copy is secure; the attribution and proposed mid-seventeenth-century date of the underlying papers remain debated.

7. **Ancient etymology:** No convincing ancient god, hero, place, or language has yet been connected to the name. Future manuscript discoveries could change this, but resemblance-based internet etymologies do not currently supply evidence.

8. **Modern media:** Game and animation databases change continually. Claims about *Persona*, *Helluva Boss*, *Hellboy*, or other franchises should be checked against official credits, scripts, or databases rather than demon-name compilations.
