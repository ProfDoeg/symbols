# Ipos: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes the kinds of evidence as follows:

- **Documented text** — directly stated in a manuscript, early printed edition, or identifiable modern publication.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — a relationship inferred by editors or historians from textual comparison.
- **Tradition** — a claim transmitted within magical or angelological literature but not independently demonstrable.
- **Esoteric correspondence** — an attribution created by an occult system, such as the Golden Dawn or *777*.
- **Disputed** — readings or historical claims for which sources conflict or the evidence is insufficient.
- **Legend** — narrative presented by a grimoire or practitioner as sacred or supernatural history.
- **Modern invention** — a post-grimoire creative elaboration, including fiction, games, “enns,” personal gnosis and newly devised correspondences.

The principal finding is that **Ipos does have a credible pre-1577 precursor**: *Vipos*, number 19 in the French *Livre des Esperitz*. The surviving manuscript’s date is disputed—variously fifteenth century, late fifteenth/early sixteenth century, and approximately the middle of the sixteenth century—but its text belongs to the same “Offices of Spirits” family from which Weyer drew.

## The name and its origins

### The *Livre des Esperitz*: Vipos

**Documented text, manuscript source; scholarly reconstruction of identity.** Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29, fols. 179r–182v, contains the French *Livre des Esperitz*. Its entry 19 reads:

> **Vipos est un grant conte qui appert en semblance d’un ange, et faict home saige et hardy, et dit verité de ce que on luy demande ; et a XXV legions.**

Translation:

> Vipos is a great count who appears in the likeness of an angel, makes a man wise and bold, and tells the truth concerning what is asked of him; and he has twenty-five legions.

This is the earliest surviving close parallel. The conjunction of name, rank, angelic appearance, production of wisdom and boldness, and position immediately before *Cerbere/Naberius* makes the identification with Weyer’s Ipes unusually strong. Jean-Patrice Boudet prints the entry and aligns it with Ipes in his comparison of the medieval and Renaissance demon catalogues. Joseph Peterson likewise places “Trin. #19: Vipos” beside Weyer’s Ipes and Goetia’s Ipos. [Boudet’s text and study](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/1019), [PDF transcription](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/pdf/1171), [manuscript transcription](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre_des_esperitz/Texte_entier).

**Disputed date.** The manuscript catalogue and Wikisource describe the inserted booklet as mid-fifteenth-century. Elizabeth Butler assigned its hand to the late fifteenth or early sixteenth century; Richard Kieckhefer favored the fifteenth century; Boudet argued that the hand is probably no earlier than the middle of the sixteenth century. Consequently, “pre-Weyer” is secure in the sense of a prior textual stage and probably in the physical witness as well, but a precise copying date cannot be stated with confidence.

The Vipos entry differs from Weyer in four ways:

1. It gives only the rank of count, not prince.
2. It supplies only an angelic likeness, without animal components.
3. It says he answers questions truthfully rather than explicitly knowing past and future.
4. It gives twenty-five rather than thirty-six legions.

These differences make Vipos a **textual ancestor or cognate**, not proof that every later feature already belonged to the earlier form.

### Weyer: Ipes and Ayperos

**Documented text.** Johann Weyer printed the catalogue as the *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*, an appendix to the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum* in 1577. Weyer called his source *Liber officiorum spirituum, seu Liber dictus Empto. Salomonis, de principibus & regibus dæmoniorum*. His Ipes is number 16.

The two forms **Ipes** and **Ayperos** occur together as primary name and alias. Compared with *Vipos*, loss or misreading of initial *V-* readily explains *Ipos/Ipes*. *Ayperos* may preserve an independent alias or a more badly corrupted portion of the manuscript transmission.

### Scot: Ipos and Ayporos

**Documented text.** Reginald Scot incorporated an English translation of Weyer's catalogue into book 16 of *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584). Peterson argues that the translation was probably made by Abraham Fleming. Scot’s text has **Ipos** and **Ayporos**.

This is consequential because the seventeenth-century compiler of the *Ars Goetia* appears to have worked from Scot’s English, not directly from Weyer’s printed Latin. Thus **Ipos**, now the standard spelling, is likely the product of English textual transmission rather than recovery of an older name.

### The seventeenth-century manuscripts

Joseph Peterson’s diplomatic comparison gives the following chain:

| Witness | Reading |

| Sloane MS 3825 | **Ipos** |
| Sloane MS 3648 | **Ipos** |
| Sloane MS 2731 | **Ipos** |

| Mathers–Crowley, 1904 | **Ipos**, otherwise **Ipes, Ayporos, or Aypeos** |

This comparison is available in Peterson’s [*Goetia Spirit Variant Spellings*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia_spirits.pdf).

**Documented text.** Sloane 3825, fol. 103v, calls him Ipos. Sloane 2731 and Harley 6483 agree on the name. Mathers recovered several older variants in his apparatus but introduced **Aypeos**, apparently another transcriptional or editorial form rather than an independently ancient name.

### Does the name derive from an ancient god, hero or place?

**Finding: no established derivation.** Unlike Bael/Baal, Astaroth/Astarte, Asmoday/Ashmedai, Belial/Bĕliyyaʿal or Phoenix, no ancient deity, hero, people or geographical place has been securely identified behind Ipos. Neither Weyer, Scot, the *Goetia*, Rudd nor de Plancy proposes an etymology.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The most economical history is textual:

> *Vipos* → *Ipes/Ipos*  
> with *Ayperos/Ayporos* as an alias preserved and repeatedly altered by copyists.

Boudet and Peterson support the identification of Vipos with Ipes/Ipos through catalogue position and description; neither supplies an ancient mythological derivation.

**Disputed modern claims.**

- An equation with **Anubis/Anpu** circulates in demonolatry and personal-gnosis forums. Its evidence is resemblance between *Ipos* and *Anpu/Inpu*, plus animal-headed imagery. No premodern source makes the identification; Anubis is canine/jackal-headed, whereas Ipos is lion-headed, and the documented name *Vipos* weakens the phonetic argument. This is **modern legend/personal gnosis**, not historical etymology.
- Suggested derivations from Greek *apeiros* (“boundless, infinite”) or *hesperos* (“evening, west”) are **modern folk etymologies**. No source-chain spelling, Greek gloss or contemporary philological argument supports them.
- A recent web claim that *Aiperos* means “one who pierces” or “revealer” in Greek or Coptic is unsupported by a cited lexicon and is **modern invention**.
- Cebuano *ípos/ipis*, “cockroach,” Shasta-derived English *ipos*, an edible tuber, and Yami *ipos*, “tail,” are genuine but unrelated homonyms.

Accordingly, the safest conclusion is: **the etymology is unknown; the name probably survives through scribal mutation within an Offices-of-Spirits catalogue, and there is no demonstrated pagan deity behind it.**

## Order: Weyer’s 69 and the Goetia’s 72

**Documented text and scholarly reconstruction.** Weyer’s surviving catalogue contains 69 numbered spirits. The *Ars Goetia* has 72, but it did not simply append three names.

The *Goetia*:

- added **Vassago** as no. 3;
- added **Seere**, **Dantalion** and **Andromalius** as nos. 70–72;
- omitted Weyer’s no. 4, **Pruflas alias Bufas**;
- extensively rearranged the remaining names.

Thus 69 minus Pruflas plus four additions equals 72. Ipes/Ipos moves from **Weyer no. 16** to **Goetia no. 22**. Scot had also omitted Pruflas, whether accidentally or because the copy available to his translator already lacked it. The *Goetia*’s dependence on Scot is one reason Pruflas never entered its list.

The compiler’s motive for producing exactly 72 is not stated. Peterson considers the numerological prestige of 72, and perhaps the 72-fold divine name, the probable reason. The later Rudd arrangement makes the symmetry explicit by setting 72 Shem angels against the 72 spirits. That explicit one-to-one pairing, however, is not part of Weyer, Scot or the ordinary Sloane *Goetia*.

## The description across the sources

### 1. *Livre des Esperitz*

**Documented text, entry 19:**

> Vipos est un grant conte qui appert en semblance d’un ange, et faict home saige et hardy, et dit verité de ce que on luy demande ; et a XXV legions.

Vipos is a great count, appears like an angel, makes a person wise and bold, answers questions truthfully and has twenty-five legions. There is no seal, prince rank or animal anatomy.

### 2. Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* (1577)

**Documented text, Latin, no. 16, printed p. 916:**

> **Ipes, aliàs Ayperos, est magnus Comes & Princeps, apparens quidem specie angelica, interim leone obscurior & turpis, capite leonis, pedibus anserinis, cauda leporina. Præterita & futura novit: redditque hominem ingeniosum & audacem. Legiones huic obediunt trigintasex.**

Literal translation:

> Ipes, otherwise Ayperos, is a great Count and Prince, appearing indeed in an angelic form, yet meanwhile darker/more obscure and ugly than a lion, with the head of a lion, the feet of a goose and the tail of a hare. He knows past and future things, and renders a person ingenious and bold. Thirty-six legions obey him.

[Weyer’s Latin and the parallel Scot text](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm#par16).

The clause *interim leone obscurior & turpis* is awkward. Scot rendered it “and yet indeed more obscure and filthie than a lion.” It does **not** clearly establish a second lion form. De Plancy later converted the ambiguity into two alternatives—angel or lion—and then shifted the goose anatomy.

### 3. Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584)

**Documented text, book 16, no. 16, p. 380:**

> **Ipos [Ipes], alias Ayporos [Ayperos], is a great earle and a prince, appeering in the shape of an angell, and yet indeed more obscure and filthie than a lion, with a lions head, a gooses feet, and a hares taile: he knoweth things to come and past, he maketh a man wittie, and bold, and hath under his jurisdiction thirtie six legions.**

The bracketed corrections are Peterson’s comparison with Weyer. Scot is the first located witness for the standard **Ipos** spelling and for **Ayporos**.

### 4. Sloane MS 3825, *Ars Goetia*

**Documented text, fol. 103v; Peterson 2001, approximately p. 16:**

> **The 22d spirit is called Ipos—, he is an Earle and a mighty prince, and appeareth in ye forme of an angel, wth a Lions head gooses feet & a haires Taile, he knoweth Things past and to come; he maketh Men witty and bold, and governeth 36 Legions of spirits, his seal or Charecter is This, wch must be worne as a Lamen before you.**

[Peterson’s Sloane 3825 transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm#ipos).

“Haires Taile” is plainly a spelling of **hare’s tail**, not “hair’s tail.” The manuscript follows Scot closely but removes the unfavorable comparison with a lion and adds the lamen instruction and seal.

### 5. Sloane MS 2731 and related manuscripts

**Documented text.** Peterson’s collation records **Ipos** in Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648 and Northwestern MS 65. No substantial alternative office or legion count for Ipos is reported. The wording varies in ordinary scribal matters—capitalization, spelling and punctuation—but not in its central claims.

This matters negatively: several modern lists supply extra powers such as love, treasure finding, invisibility, transmutation, familiars or mastery of all sciences. **None belongs to the Ipos entry in Weyer, Scot or the Sloane Goetia.** Ipos’s documented offices are limited to knowledge of past and future and the gift of wit/ingenuity and boldness.

### 6. Harley MS 6483 and the Rudd recension

**Documented text, surviving manuscript; disputed attribution.** Harley 6483 gives substantially:

> **The 22th Spirit is Ipos, he is an Earl and mighty Prince and appeareth in the form of an Angel with a Lion’s head a goose’s foot and a hare’s tail: he knoweth all things past and to come, he maketh men witty and bold, and he governeth 36 Legions of Spirits. His Seal or Character is this.**

Its distinctive contribution is not a radically different Ipos description but the accompanying Shem angel, seal and psalmic constraint discussed below.

The surviving Harley manuscript is internally dated **1712–1713** and is associated with the copyist Peter Smart. Skinner and Rankine argue that Smart copied older material belonging to Thomas Rudd, who died in 1656. That reconstruction is plausible but not universally accepted as proof that every feature was authored by Rudd himself.

### 7. Mathers–Crowley, *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King* (1904)

**Documented text, pp. 28–29:**

> **(22.) IPOS.—The Twenty-second Spirit is Ipos. He is an Earl, and a Mighty Prince, and appeareth in the form of an Angel with a Lion’s Head, and a Goose’s Foot, and Hare’s Tail. He knoweth all things Past, Present, and to Come. He maketh men witty and bold. He governeth 36 Legions of Spirits. His Seal is this, which thou shalt wear, etc.**

[Sacred Texts facsimile transcription, pp. 28–29](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks05.htm).

The important innovation is **“Past, Present, and to Come.”** Vipos answers questions; Weyer and Scot say past and future; Sloane 3825 says past and future. “Present” is therefore a Mathers expansion, probably introduced to complete a conventional triad. It is now repeatedly quoted as though it were original.

Mathers’s name apparatus gives:

> **Ipos, otherwise Ipes, Ayporos, or Aypeos.**

“Aypeos” is not Weyer’s reading and appears to be an editorial/transcriptional variant.

### 8. Peterson’s 2001 edition

**Documented editorial work.** Joseph H. Peterson’s *The Lesser Key of Solomon: Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis* (Weiser, 2001) returned to Sloane MS 3825 as principal copy-text and collated other manuscripts. For Ipos, Peterson restores the manuscript’s omission of “present,” prints the name as Ipos, and notes Weyer’s **Ipes/Ayperos** and Scot’s **Ipos/Ayporos**.

Peterson’s edition is therefore not another independent version of the spirit; it is a critical presentation of the seventeenth-century manuscript evidence.

## Appearance and requested second form

**Documented text.** Ipos’s appearance is:

- angelic in overall form;
- lion-headed;
- goose-footed;
- hare-tailed.

The wording does not specify wings, sex, clothing, stature, voice, color or weapons.

**Finding concerning a second form.** Unlike Marbas, Sitri, Gamigin, Bifrons and several other spirits, Ipos has **no individually specified second or human form**. No source says that the conjurer may demand that Ipos become human.

The general *Goetia* conjuration commands every spirit to appear:

> “in a fair and humane shape, without any deformity or ugly shew.”

That general ritual demand can be applied to Ipos, but it must not be mistaken for a second form recorded in his catalogue entry.

**Disputed reading.** De Plancy’s statement that Ipès appears as an angel and “sometimes” as a lion probably arose from rereading Weyer’s awkward comparison. It is not a clear feature of the earlier Latin.

## Offices, rank, legions and timing

### Offices

| Office | Source status |
|---|---|
| Makes a man wise | *Livre des Esperitz*: **documented** |
| Makes a man witty/ingenious | Weyer onward: **documented** |
| Makes a man bold/audacious | All early witnesses: **documented** |
| Gives true answers to questions | Vipos: **documented** |
| Knows past and future | Weyer, Scot, Sloane manuscripts: **documented** |
| Knows the present | Mathers 1904: **editorial addition** |
| Love, familiars, invisibility, treasure, languages, sciences, transmutation | **Not in the Ipos entries** |
| Maritime protection, trade, fame | Belongs to the later angel Yeiayel tradition, not Ipos’s early office |
| “Prince of Fools” | D&D, 2006: **modern invention** |
| An “enn” such as *Desa an Ipos Ayer* | Late demonolatry: **modern invention**, absent from all historical grimoires |

### Rank and planetary metal

**Documented manuscript rule.** The *Goetia*’s “Observations” says:

> “The Seals of the 72 Kings are to be made in Metals. The Chief Kings’ in Sol (Gold); Marquises’ in Luna (Silver); Dukes’ in Venus (Copper); Prelacies’ in Jupiter (Tin); Knights’ in Saturn (Lead); Presidents’ in Mercury; Earls’ in Venus (Copper), and Luna (Silver), alike equal.”

Thus, in the manuscript system:

- Ipos as **Earl** implies equal parts copper and silver.
- Ipos as **Prince**, if “prelacy” is the intended category, implies Jupiter and tin.
- The manuscript says nothing about how to treat a spirit holding both ranks. Peterson suggests that an alloy might be inferred but correctly labels that an inference.

**Esoteric correspondence, *777*.** Crowley’s later table regularized the ranks differently:

| Rank | Planet | Metal |
|---|---|---|
| King | Sun | Gold |
| Duke | Venus | Copper |
| Prince | Jupiter | Tin |
| Marquis | Moon | Silver |
| Earl/Count | Mars | Iron |
| President | Mercury | Mercury |
| Knight | Saturn | Lead |

In this later system Ipos combines **Mars/iron** and **Jupiter/tin**. The Mars attribution to earls is not in the Sloane observation—the manuscript notably omits Mars and assigns earls to copper and silver.

### Legions

- *Livre des Esperitz*: **25**.
- Weyer, Scot, Sloane manuscripts, Rudd and Mathers: **36**.

There is no independent way to interpret “legion” numerically. It is a conventional unit of infernal hierarchy, not a census.

### Traditional time of conjuration

**Documented text.** The *Goetia*’s general observations allow:

- Princes or “prelates” at any hour of the day.
- Earls or counts at any hour of the day, provided the place is a wood, an unfrequented location or somewhere without noise.

For Ipos’s double rank, the safest manuscript-based intersection is therefore **daytime in a secluded and quiet location**. This is not a unique hour assigned to Ipos.

The same section recommends workings when the Moon is 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 or 14 days old. Elsewhere, it says to observe the Moon when positioning the triangle. These are general ritual rules, not Ipos-specific astrology. [Goetia “Observations”](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks06.htm).

**Later correspondence.** *777* calls Ipos a **day/Ascendant spirit**, reinforcing daytime, but this is a twentieth-century decanic overlay rather than a reading from Weyer or the manuscripts.

## The seal

### Attestation

**Documented text.**

- *Livre des Esperitz*: no seal for Vipos.
- Weyer: no seal.
- Scot: no seal.
- Sloane *Goetia*: a seal appears with Ipos.
- Harley 6483/Rudd: a seal appears.
- Mathers–Crowley: a redrawn and normalized printed seal.
- Peterson: a modern diplomatic redraw after the manuscripts.

Peterson notes that the familiar 72 Goetic sigils are not found in the earlier known Offices-of-Spirits catalogues. They are therefore probably a development within or immediately before the *Lemegeton* tradition rather than ancient “Solomonic” signs.

### Description in words

**Visual description, not wording from the grimoire.** The standard Mathers–Crowley/Peterson figure consists of:

- a long central upright stroke;
- a closed or nearly closed loop at its upper end;
- a broad cross-stroke whose arms terminate in curling, hook-like forms;
- a lower continuation ending in a small cross or forked mark;
- a separate, smaller three-lobed or trident-like glyph to the right, joined or nearly joined by a fine lateral stroke in some reproductions.

The original catalogue does not explain the components, assign letters to them or state that they encode Ipos’s name.

A public-domain rendering of the 1904 form is available as [Wikimedia Commons’ Ipos seal](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:22-Ipos_seal.png).

### Variants

**Documented but limited.** The principal manuscript differences are ductal—whether a loop is fully closed, the curvature of the lateral hooks, the length of the upright and whether the right-hand three-lobed element visibly touches the main figure. Mathers converted the handwritten character into a clean, symmetrical printed emblem.

Unlike Bune, whose entry explicitly gives two seals, the Ipos entry contains no statement that there are multiple authorized characters. Claims that a particular manuscript gives Ipos a “double circle,” Hebrew letters or a wholly different sigil are not supported by Peterson’s collation and often result from modern graphics being misidentified as manuscript facsimiles.

## The angel: Ieiaiel, Yeiael or Yeiayel

### Name and construction

**Documented esoteric tradition.** The twenty-second triplet extracted from Exodus 14:19–21 is:

> **ייי — Yod, Yod, Yod**

With the divine ending *-El* added, it becomes:

> **יייאל**

Common Romanizations include:

- **Ieiaiel**
- **Yeiael**
- **Yeiayel**
- **Yeyayel**
- **Yiaiel**

The abundance of spellings results from representing yod as *I*, *J* or *Y*, and from inserting vowels into a consonantal sequence. **Ieiaiel** is common in Latin and French tables; **Yeiael** appears in Golden Dawn material; **Yeiayel** is widespread in twentieth-century French-derived angelology.

### The Exodus derivation

**Documented Jewish mystical tradition.** Exodus 14:19, 14:20 and 14:21 each contain 72 Hebrew letters in the received consonantal text. The usual construction writes:

1. verse 19 in its normal right-to-left direction;
2. verse 20 in reverse, left-to-right;
3. verse 21 right-to-left;

and reads the resulting columns as 72 three-letter names.

The twenty-second column yields Y-Y-Y.

**Scholarly context.** The method is not stated in Exodus itself. Rashi’s commentary on Sukkah 45a describes the selection of the first letter of the first verse, last letter of the middle verse and first letter of the third, continuing correspondingly. The *Sefer ha-Bahir*, first known in Provence between approximately 1150 and 1200, is one of the earliest surviving works to connect the Exodus passage to reconstruction of the 72-fold name. The medieval compilatory *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* gives the 72-fold name a central magical and angelological role.

The original triads are divine names or powers; treating all 72 as individual angels by adding *-El* or *-Yah* became especially characteristic of Christian Kabbalah.

### Psalm and meaning

Rudd’s verse is:

> **Dominus custodit te, Dominus protectio tua super manum dexteram tuam.**

Translation:

> The Lord keeps you; the Lord is your protection/shade upon your right hand.

This is **Psalm 120:5** in the Latin Vulgate numbering and **Psalm 121:5** in Hebrew, Protestant and most modern English numbering:

> “The LORD is thy keeper: the LORD is thy shade upon thy right hand.”

The Golden Dawn therefore glosses Yeiael as **“Thy right hand.”** French tables commonly expand this to **La Droite de Dieu**, “the Right Hand of God.”

**Important qualification.** Three repetitions of the Hebrew letter yod do not lexically mean “right hand.” The meaning comes from the selected psalm verse and later interpretive tradition.

### Historical transmission

#### Jewish sources

**Documented tradition.** The 72-fold name belongs to medieval Jewish mystical and magical literature, especially the *Bahir* and *Sefer Raziel*. These texts do not contain Ipos and do not pair YYY/Ieiaiel with a demon. The demon pairing is a Christian magical development.

#### Reuchlin and Agrippa

**Documented Christian Kabbalah.** Johannes Reuchlin discussed the divine names in *De verbo mirifico* (1494) and printed a developed angelic treatment in *De arte cabalistica* (1517). Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia*, book III, chapter 25—written in an earlier form around 1510 and printed in 1533—also presents the 72 names as angels associated with divine verses.

Bernd Roling and collaborators trace how Reuchlin and other early modern Christian writers transformed Jewish name traditions into a Christian angelology. [*72 in His Name*](https://www.academicstudiespress.com/9781644692455/).

#### Blaise de Vigenère

**Documented transmission.** Vigenère reproduced a table of the 72 angelic names in his sixteenth-century Kabbalistic and cryptographic work, following Reuchlin. Skinner and Rankine identify Vigenère’s table as a major source for Rudd’s angelic material and sigils.

#### Athanasius Kircher, 1652

**Documented text.** Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus*, volume II, part I (Rome, 1652), includes an elaborate *Cabala Hebraeorum* with the 72 names, divine attributes and linguistic/national correspondences. It transmits **Ieiaiel** in the twenty-second position. Kircher did not originate the Exodus construction and did not pair Ieiaiel with Ipos. [Kircher’s *Cabala Hebraeorum*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/kircher/cabala.htm).

#### Rudd/Harley 6483

**Documented scheme; disputed authorship and date.** The Rudd recension is the crucial point at which the twenty-second Shem angel is deliberately placed over the twenty-second Goetic spirit:

> **Ieiaiel — Ipos**

The associated verse is Psalm 121:5. In this system, the angel’s name, seal and biblical verse function as the celestial counterforce or “adversarial angel” of the demon. The purpose is coercive and protective: the operator invokes divine and angelic authority to restrict the corresponding spirit rather than approaching angel and demon as equal partners.

**Tradition rather than earlier Goetia.** The ordinary Sloane *Goetia* uses divine names, Michael, circles, triangles and general constraints, but does not supply a one-to-one Shem angel for each spirit. Rudd’s pairing is an overlay upon the numbered list.

Stephen Skinner and David Rankine’s *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (Golden Hoard Press, 2007) publishes and interprets this material. Its subtitle accurately identifies its focus: *The Angels and Demons of Liber Malorum Spirituum seu Goetia Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis*. The editors argue that Peter Smart’s 1712–13 manuscript copies a pre-1656 Rudd exemplar. The surviving manuscript date is certain; the extent of Rudd’s authorship is not.

#### Lenain, 1823

**Documented esoteric correspondence.** Lazare Lenain’s *La science cabalistique, ou l’art de connaître les bons génies qui influent sur la destinée des hommes* (1823) reorganized the 72 angels as “good genii” governing zodiacal sectors, dates, peoples, moral dispositions and practical benefits.

In this Aries-first system, Ieiaiel occupies the twenty-second five-degree sector, conventionally **15°–20° Cancer**. Boundary notation varies: some French writers say **16°–20°**, counting whole numbered degrees rather than mathematical intervals.

Lenain associates Ieiaiel with England/the English and describes a genius governing fortune, renown, diplomacy, commerce, voyages, discoveries and maritime expeditions, with protection against storms and shipwreck. These are **angelic offices introduced in this later current**; they are not offices of Ipos.

[Lenain’s 1823 scan](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_ms05365).

#### Papus

**Documented transmission.** Gérard Encausse, “Papus,” reproduced and popularized Lenain’s 72-genii material in *La Cabbale*, especially through its expanded late-nineteenth/early-twentieth-century editions. Papus is a transmitter of the French angelological system, not the source of Rudd’s Ipos pairing.

[Papus, *La Cabbale*](https://resources.warburg.sas.ac.uk/pdf/abg405b2732165.pdf).

#### Golden Dawn and Mathers

**Documented esoteric correspondence.** A Golden Dawn Shem lecture attributed to S. L. MacGregor Mathers gives:

> **22nd Angel — Yeiael**  
> **Sign:** Scorpio  
> **Planet:** Sun  
> **Degree:** 15°–20°  
> **Meaning:** “Thy right hand”  
> **Psalm 121:5:** “Tetragrammaton Keepeth Thee. Tetragrammaton is Thy shadow upon Thy right hand.”

[Golden Dawn Shem table](https://www.tarrdaniel.com/documents/Thelemagick/gd/publication/english/Schemhamphorash.html).

This table begins angel no. 1 at **0° Leo**, not 0° Aries. Consequently, Yeiael is 15°–20° Scorpio rather than Cancer. This is a genuine system difference, not merely a typo.

#### Ambelain

**Documented twentieth-century practitioner tradition.** Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) systematized the 72 angels into choirs, archangels, planets, psalms, talismans and ritual schedules. Ieiaiel belongs to the third group of eight, conventionally the **Thrones**, under **Zaphkiel/Tzaphkiel**, and is linked to Binah/Saturn at the choir level. This higher hierarchical planet should not be confused with the planet ruling a particular zodiacal five-degree sector.

[Ambelain, *La Kabbale pratique*](https://www.societyofguardians.org/resources/practicalkabbalah1.pdf).

#### Kabaleb and Haziel

**Documented modern angelology.** Kabaleb’s *Les Anges: le livre des génies et des planètes*, translated and promoted in French by Haziel in 1989–90, popularized the guardian-angel calendar. It assigns Yeiayel to:

- 16°–20° Cancer;
- commonly 7–11 July;
- fortune, fame, honorable success, commerce and philanthropic associations;
- maritime and travel protection.

Haziel’s books and calendars transformed a ritual-angel table into a popular birth-angel system. The Bibliothèque nationale de France records Kabaleb/Haziel’s edition in 1989. [BnF catalogue record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb35062108t).

These birth dates are **modern esoteric correspondence**, not Jewish calendrical tradition and not part of Rudd’s manuscript.

### Why one pip carries two spirits

**Esoteric correspondence.** *777* distributes the first 36 Goetic spirits through the 36 zodiacal decans as diurnal or Ascendant spirits and the second 36 through the same decans as nocturnal or Descendant spirits. Each decan—and therefore each numbered tarot pip from Two through Ten—carries two spirits.

For the first decan of Scorpio:

- day: **Ipos, no. 22**;
- night: **Amy, no. 58**;
- tarot: **Five of Cups**;
- decan ruler: **Mars in Scorpio**;
- Golden Dawn title: **Lord of Loss in Pleasure**.

Crowley’s table describes Ipos’s image as:

> “Angel with lion’s head, goose’s feet, horse’s tail.”

“Horse’s tail” is a **documented error in *777***; the source chain consistently has a hare’s tail. [Original *777* table](https://hermetic.com/_media/93beast.fea.st/files/section1/777/liber_777.pdf).

### The quinance controversy

Three statements that are often collapsed into one must remain separate:

1. **Rudd pairs angel no. 22 Ieiaiel with demon no. 22 Ipos.**
2. **The Golden Dawn places angel no. 22 at 15°–20° Scorpio.**
3. ***777* places demon no. 22 across the full first decan of Scorpio, 0°–10°, as its day spirit.**
4. **Lenain and Haziel place angel no. 22 at 15°–20° Cancer.**
5. **Runyon maps both numbered lists from 0° Aries in five-degree units, thereby placing both no. 22 figures in Cancer.**

Only statement 1 belongs to the Rudd pairing proper. Calling Cancer 15°–20° “Rudd’s quinance” is historically misleading unless one explicitly means a later Rudd-inspired synthesis.

### Enochian and other overlays

**Finding.** No Ipos-specific Enochian name, Aethyr, governor or tablet square occurs in Weyer, Scot, the *Goetia* or Harley 6483. John Dee and Edward Kelley’s Enochian records are a separate sixteenth-century angelic system.

Golden Dawn and Thelemic authors studied Enochian, Goetic, tarot and Kabbalistic material side by side, making later synthesis easy. Modern practitioners have consequently:

- translated Goetic conjurations into the Enochian language;
- used Enochian ritual structures for Goetic evocation;
- assigned Goetic spirits to Aethyrs or Enochian squares;
- placed Ipos on Qliphothic paths;
- associated him with modern “enns,” colors, herbs, chakras and incense recipes.

These are **modern overlays** unless a specific author and publication are supplied. No canonical Enochian correspondence for Ipos exists.

## Iconography

### The manuscript seal

The seal is Ipos’s earliest individual iconographic object. Its presence in the Sloane manuscripts separates the *Goetia* from the earlier Offices-of-Spirits catalogues. In the manuscripts it is a practical “character” to be worn as a lamen, not a portrait.

Modern tattoo, jewelry and altar art overwhelmingly reproduces the normalized 1904 seal rather than a specific manuscript hand.

### Collin de Plancy, 1818–1863

#### First edition, 1818

**Documented text.** De Plancy’s first edition already contains the cross-reference:

> **Ayperos, comte de l’empire infernal. C’est le même qu’Ipès. Voy. ce mot.**

Translation:

> Ayperos, count of the infernal empire. He is the same as Ipès. See that word.

The first edition did not contain Louis Le Breton’s familiar demon portraits. [First edition scans](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/1re_%C3%A9dition,_1818).

#### Sixth edition, 1863

**Documented text, p. 366:**

> **Ipès ou Ayperos, prince et comte de l’enfer ; il apparaît sous la forme d’un ange, quelquefois sous celle d’un lion, avec la tête et les pattes d’une oie et une queue de lièvre, ce qui est un peu court ; il connaît le passé et l’avenir, donne du génie et de l’audace aux hommes, et commande trente-six légions.**

Translation:

> Ipès or Ayperos, prince and count of hell; he appears in the form of an angel, sometimes in that of a lion, with the head and feet of a goose and a hare’s tail—which is rather short; he knows the past and future, gives men genius and audacity, and commands thirty-six legions.

[De Plancy’s Ipès entry](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.,_1863/Ip%C3%A8s).

De Plancy makes two notable alterations:

- Weyer’s comparison with a lion becomes an alternative lion form.
- The lion’s head becomes a **goose’s head** in the second form.

#### Louis Le Breton’s portrait

**Documented image.** The 1863 edition includes a Le Breton engraving labeled *Ipès*. It follows de Plancy’s mutated wording rather than the standard Goetic anatomy: a compact lion-like quadruped is given a long-necked bird or goose head, webbed avian feet and a short hare-like tail. It is earthbound and zoological, not recognizably angelic.

This engraving is a major source of modern depictions that give Ipos a goose, ibis or vulture head. It should not be used to “correct” the manuscripts, all of which give the spirit a **lion’s head**. [Public-domain Ipès engraving](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ip%C3%A8s.png), [Library of Congress 1863 edition](https://www.loc.gov/item/63056292/).

### Modern art

**Modern invention and adaptation.**

- David P. Wilson illustrated Ipos for Christopher S. Hyatt and Lon Milo DuQuette’s *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* (1992), combining the traditional monster with *777*’s zodiacal and planetary apparatus.
- Modern occult decks, tattoos and digital paintings generally use either the Mathers seal, Le Breton’s goose-headed creature or a humanoid lion-headed angel.
- D&D’s 2006 image of Ipos as a bald ibis-headed, iron-crowned vestige deliberately rationalizes the goose/vulture confusion as an ibis.
- Claims that Ipos traditionally appears as a vulture derive from these later images, not from Vipos, Weyer, Scot or the Sloane manuscripts.

## People and works

### Johann Weyer

**Documented historical role.** Weyer published the first printed Ipes entry in 1577. His larger work attacked witch prosecutions and argued that many accused witches were deluded or ill rather than genuinely commanding demons. Nevertheless, he considered spirits real and printed the catalogue while deliberately withholding portions of its ritual, stating that he wished to make it unusable.

### Reginald Scot and Abraham Fleming

Scot reproduced the catalogue in an anti-witchcraft-prosecution work. The likely translator, Abraham Fleming, introduced or transmitted several readings that passed into the *Goetia*, including **Ipos/Ayporos**. Scot did not present the catalogue as a personal magical operation.

### The anonymous *Lemegeton* compiler

The seventeenth-century compiler rearranged Scot’s list, expanded it to 72, added seals and combined it with ritual material from the *Heptameron* and other Solomonic sources. This compiler, not Solomon and not Weyer, is responsible for Ipos’s standard position as no. 22.

### Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

The surviving Harley material associates the system with “Dr. Rudd,” normally identified as Thomas Rudd, mathematician, engineer and writer on angel magic. Peter Smart made the surviving early-eighteenth-century copy. Rudd’s exact authorship remains disputed; the **Rudd recension** is a safer expression than treating every line as Thomas Rudd’s autograph work.

### Mathers and Crowley

S. L. MacGregor Mathers prepared the English edition from manuscript materials. Aleister Crowley published it in 1904, adding his “Preliminary Invocation,” magical commentary and provocative framing. Mathers’s Ipos text added “Present” to past and future. Crowley later assigned Ipos to Scorpio, the day half of the first decan and the Five of Cups in *777*.

### A. E. Waite

Waite’s *Book of Ceremonial Magic* (1911), p. 202, summarizes:

> “Ipos, a mighty earl and prince, appearing as an angel with a lion’s head, the webbed feet of a goose and a hare’s tail. He knows the past and future, and imparts wit and courage.”

Waite includes Ipes, Ayporos and Aypeos among the variants but contributes no new early evidence. [Waite’s catalogue](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/bcm/bcm56.htm).

### Frater Achad

**Negative finding.** Charles Stansfeld Jones, Frater Achad, was a significant Thelemic Kabbalist, but no published Achad text located for this dossier contains an Ipos-specific working, etymology or correspondence. Associating him personally with Ipos merely because he belonged to Crowley’s milieu would be unwarranted.

### Poke Runyon

Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* (1996) and accompanying film advocate integrating the 72 angels with the 72 spirits and using a crystal for angelic invocation and dark mirror for spirit evocation. Runyon explicitly begins both sequences at the first five degrees of Aries. This produces a matched **Cancer quinance** for no. 22 and differs from both the Golden Dawn’s Leo-first angel table and *777*’s ten-degree demon table.

Runyon documents an Astaroth operation, not an Ipos operation. His book includes Ipos in its full catalogue but does not establish that he personally evoked Ipos. [Runyon’s description of his system and editions](https://www.pokerunyon.com/books/the-book-of-solomons-magick-second-edition).

### Lon Milo DuQuette and Christopher Hyatt

Their *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* (1992) gives Ipos:

- 1°–10° Scorpio, day;
- October 23–November 1;
- Five of Cups;
- Mars/iron as Earl;
- Jupiter/tin as Prince.

This is a lucid modern presentation of Crowley’s correspondence scheme, not manuscript evidence. [Scanned edition](https://libraryofrickandria.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Aleister-Crowleys-Illustrated-Goetia-Aleister-Crowley-Christopher-S_-Hyatt-Lon-Milo-Duquette-Third-1992-2000-New-Falcon-Publications.pdf).

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 critical edition and continually corrected Esoteric Archives transcription are the principal readily accessible tools for distinguishing Sloane 3825 from Mathers’s alterations. He also identified Vipos in the comparison table and stressed that the seals are absent from earlier Offices catalogues.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their 2007 *Goetia of Dr Rudd* placed the Harley material, 72 angels, demonic catalogue, seals and evocation technique into print. Their reconstruction of a pre-1656 Rudd exemplar is influential but should be distinguished from the physical date of the surviving copy.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s *Pandemonium: A Discordant Concordance of Diverse Spirit Catalogues* compares the *Livre des Esperitz*, Weyer, Scot and later spirit catalogues. His work helped bring Vipos and the wider Offices-of-Spirits family into contemporary practitioner discussion. It is a comparative modern synthesis rather than the first scholarly publication of Vipos; that distinction belongs to Boudet.

### Owen Davies, Jan Veenstra and Julien Véronèse

These scholars are relevant principally to context:

- Owen Davies surveys the social and publishing history of grimoires.
- Jan Veenstra studies the *Almandal* and the intellectual aims of ritual angel magic.
- Julien Véronèse’s scholarship establishes the broader medieval development of Solomonic and learned ritual magic.

None has supplied an Ipos-specific ancient-god etymology.

## Grimoire ancestors and parallels

### *Testament of Solomon*

**Documented broad ancestor; no Ipos.** The late-antique Greek *Testament of Solomon* narrates Solomon’s control of demons by means of a divinely empowered ring and interrogates spirits about their names, forms, activities and thwarting angels. This is an important ancestor of the literary structure in which each demon has an office and celestial opponent.

Ipos, Ipes, Ayperos and Vipos do not appear in its surviving recensions. Rudd’s one-angel-against-one-demon format resembles the *Testament*’s logic, but no direct line from a particular Testament demon to Ipos is demonstrable.

### The Munich handbook, Clm 849

**Documented partial catalogue; no Ipos.** The fifteenth-century necromancer’s handbook edited by Richard Kieckhefer contains names cognate with Barbatos, Gusion, Botis, Purson, Eligor, Gaap, Valac, Gomory, Cimeries and Amy. It supplies evidence for an older Offices-of-Spirits family but no recognized Vipos/Ipos entry.

### *Livre des Esperitz*

This is the first surviving direct cognate: **Vipos**.

### *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Trithemius’s *Antipalus maleficiorum* (1508) lists a *Liber officiorum* among forbidden magical works. Weyer explicitly says his source was a *Liber officiorum spirituum*. Several surviving catalogues—the French *Livre des Esperitz*, the Folger *Book of Oberon* materials, Clm 849 and Weyer—appear to be divergent branches of this textual family.

The lost archetype cannot be reconstructed word for word. It is reasonable to infer that some Vipos/Ipes entry circulated before Weyer, but not that the lost original contained the later Goetic seal or Rudd angel.

### *Book of Abramelin*

Abramelin’s demon lists participate in a related late-medieval environment but contain no secure Ipos/Vipos cognate. Attempts to match any vaguely similar name are speculative.

### *Book of Oberon*

The Folger Shakespeare Library’s MS V.b.26 preserves extensive Offices-of-Spirits material and ritual passages omitted by Weyer. It helps explain the textual world from which the *Goetia* arose, but the published comparison does not establish an independent Ipos entry there.

## Literature, film, games and popular culture

### Classical and canonical literature

**Negative findings.**

- **Dante:** no Ipos. Dante died in 1321, well before the first known Vipos witness, and the *Divine Comedy* contains no name resembling Ipos/Ayperos.
- **Milton:** no Ipos in *Paradise Lost* or *Paradise Regained*. Milton uses major biblical and classical infernal names, not Ipos.
- **Bartimaeus novels:** no attested Ipos character was located in the novels’ indexed or searchable demon lists.
- **Hellboy:** no securely attested named Ipos character was located.
- **Helluva Boss:** no Ipos character is attested in released episodes or official character lists. The series uses other Goetic names, notably Stolas, Andrealphus, Vassago and Paimon.
- No feature film centered on Ipos was identified.

The absence of Ipos from these works is significant because online compilations frequently copy entire “Goetic demons in popular culture” lists without verifying individual appearances.

### *DragonQuest*

**Documented game adaptation.** The fantasy role-playing game *DragonQuest* includes “Ipos, the Prince of Fools” in its demon material. It keeps the angel/lion/goose/hare form and thirty-six subordinate spirits but adds a sweet voice, musical accomplishment, illusion magic and game statistics. These additions are modern game design.

### *Dungeons & Dragons: Tome of Magic* (2006)

**Documented modern invention.** The 3.5 edition *Tome of Magic*, p. 36, makes “Ipos, Prince of Fools” a sixth-level vestige. Its invented legend says that Ipos was once a mortal planar scholar who discovered pact magic, became obsessed with locating the realm of vestiges and eventually became one himself.

Its apparition has:

- the head of a bald ibis;
- a black iron crown;
- layered gray robes;
- long arms ending in lion-like paws and cold-iron claws;
- an iron cane.

The book grants binders cold-iron claws, supernatural perception and enhanced charisma. It explicitly dismisses “vulture or goose” descriptions as mistakes for a bald ibis. This is a clever adaptation of the de Plancy and Goetic iconographic confusion, but the mortal scholar, crown, ibis, vestige cosmology and title “Prince of Fools” are all **2006 inventions**. [Searchable *Tome of Magic* scan](https://anyflip.com/dvabt/ftfj/basic).

### *Megami Tensei*

**Attested but limited.** Ipos appears in the comparatively obscure *Giten Megami Tensei: Tokyo Mokushiroku* as a demon of the Fallen race/Dark-Chaos alignment. Available franchise catalogues do not support the common assertion that Ipos is a recurring demon throughout *Shin Megami Tensei* or the *Persona* series. No standard *Persona* manifestation of Ipos was verified. [Megami Tensei Wiki entry](https://megatenwiki.com/wiki/Ipos).

### *Welcome to Demon School! Iruma-kun*

**Documented modern allusion.** Osamu Nishi’s manga and its anime adaptation include **Ipos Ichou**, a tactics instructor at Babyls. The surname is taken from the Goetic name, as are many names in the series. The character is not a literal adaptation of the angelic lion-goose-hare apparition.

### Other online claims

Statements that Ipos appears in *Hellboy*, *Helluva Boss*, the *Bartimaeus* sequence or multiple *Persona* games could not be substantiated. They should remain unreported or explicitly labeled unverified until a volume, episode, game compendium or credit is produced.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Is Vipos really Ipos?

**Strong scholarly reconstruction, not absolute identity.** The catalogue position and nearly identical office strongly support the relationship. The loss of initial V and change from 25 to 36 legions show that the transmission was fluid. “Vipos is the earliest form of the same entry” is justified; “Vipos proves an unchanged individual demon was known in the fifteenth century” goes beyond the evidence.

### 2. Fifteenth- or sixteenth-century manuscript?

The descriptions “mid-fifteenth century” and “mid-sixteenth century” both circulate. They reflect conflicting palaeographical judgments, not two separate manuscripts. Boudet’s mid-sixteenth-century assessment is the most explicit published argument; manuscript catalogues retain earlier dating.

### 3. Ipes or Ipos?

**Documented variants.** Ipes is Weyer’s printed reading; Ipos is Scot’s. Because the *Goetia* follows Scot, Ipos became canonical. There is no linguistic basis for declaring one the demon’s metaphysically “true” name.

### 4. Ayperos, Ayporos or Aypeos?

- **Ayperos:** Weyer.
- **Ayporos:** Scot.
- **Aypeos:** Mathers’s apparatus.
- **Aiperos/Ayporus:** modern normalizations.

These are variants in one textual chain, not independently attested ancient names.

### 5. Does he have a lion form?

Weyer says Ipes appears angelically yet is *leone obscurior & turpis*, followed by “lion’s head.” Scot translates the comparison literally. De Plancy interprets it as an alternative lion form. The latter reading is possible but secondary and uncertain.

### 6. Lion head or goose head?

All core Goetic witnesses specify a **lion’s head** and goose feet. De Plancy’s 1863 French gives a goose’s head and feet to the optional lion form; Le Breton illustrates that version. Goose-headed, ibis-headed and vulture-headed Ipos images descend from de Plancy and modern art, not the manuscripts.

### 7. Hare’s tail or horse’s tail?

The source chain has a **hare’s tail**. “Horse’s tail” appears in Crowley’s *777* table and is a twentieth-century error.

### 8. Past and future—or past, present and future?

Vipos answers questions truthfully. Weyer, Scot and Sloane 3825 say past and future. Mathers adds “Present.” Modern descriptions that include present follow 1904, not the earlier manuscripts.

### 9. Twenty-five or thirty-six legions?

Vipos has 25; Weyer onward has 36. It is impossible to determine whether Weyer expanded the number, read a different archetype or inherited an already altered entry.

### 10. Earl, Prince or Duke?

The authoritative source chain gives Count/Earl, later Earl and Prince. “Duke” occurs in unsourced modern lists, probably through confusion with neighboring spirits or generic use of “duke” for infernal nobility. No examined primary witness makes Ipos a duke.

### 11. Which planet and metal?

There are two authentic but different occult systems:

- manuscript *Goetia*: Earl = copper and silver; Prince/prelate = Jupiter/tin;
- *777*: Earl = Mars/iron; Prince = Jupiter/tin.

A statement that Ipos is simply “a Mars demon” or simply “a Jupiter demon” suppresses his double rank and the disagreement between systems.

### 12. Cancer or Scorpio?

Both appear in genuine later sources:

- Lenain/Haziel angelic calendar: Yeiayel at 15°–20° Cancer;
- Golden Dawn angelic table: Yeiael at 15°–20° Scorpio;
- *777* demonic table: Ipos at 0°–10° Scorpio;
- Runyon’s matched Aries-first quinances: Ipos and Ieiaiel at 15°–20° Cancer.

There is no single historical quinance shared by every source.

### 13. Five of Cups or Three of Cups?

- *777*’s decanic Ipos is **Five of Cups**.
- Runyon’s five-degree, Aries-first matched sequence places no. 22 in Cancer and can generate **Three of Cups**.

Five of Cups is the correct answer specifically to “Crowley’s *777* tarot pip for spirit 22.” Three of Cups belongs to a later remapping.

### 14. Is Ieiaiel inherently Ipos’s angel?

No. The Exodus triplet and angel name circulated independently for centuries. Ieiaiel became Ipos’s opponent because Rudd’s scheme aligned two numbered lists. Jewish Kabbalah does not teach that the angel’s essential function is to bind Ipos.

### 15. Is Ipos Anubis?

**Modern legend, unsupported.** No source before contemporary practitioner literature makes the equation. There is no historical, linguistic or iconographic chain strong enough to support it.

### 16. Was Ipos a fallen angel?

The catalogue says he appears in angelic form but does not say which angelic order he once occupied or narrate a fall. “Fallen angel,” “former cherub,” “former virtue” and similar biographies are extrapolations.

### 17. Misprints that became doctrine

The Ipos tradition provides several examples:

- Scot’s *Ipos/Ayporos* displaced Weyer’s *Ipes/Ayperos*.
- Mathers’s *Aypeos* entered modern name lists.
- Mathers added “Present.”
- *777* changed hare’s tail to horse’s tail.
- De Plancy shifted the lion’s head to a goose’s head.
- Some modern tables call Cancer 15°–20° “Rudd’s quinance,” although the zodiacal assignment is later.
- A few derivative tables number Ieiaiel as 23 because they begin counting differently or insert an extra name; in the Rudd/standard 72 sequence it is no. 22.

## Open questions

1. **Exact date of Trinity MS O.8.29’s inserted booklet.** A renewed codicological and palaeographical examination could narrow the fifteenth-versus-sixteenth-century range.

2. **The lost Latin antecedent of Vipos.** The French entry is probably an abridged translation. No known manuscript presently supplies its immediate Latin form.

3. **Origin of the alias Ayperos.** It may preserve a genuine alternate reading from the lost Offices tradition, or it may be a corruption. No etymology has been demonstrated.

4. **When the animal anatomy entered the entry.** It is absent from Vipos and fully present in Weyer. An intermediate manuscript could determine whether Weyer inherited or introduced it.

5. **When the seal was created.** It is absent from known earlier catalogues and present in the *Goetia*. No securely dated prototype for the Ipos character has been identified.

6. **Relationship among the principal Sloane seal hands.** A stemmatic study comparing ink, pen movement and copying errors could determine whether later Ipos seals descend from a single exemplar.

7. **Extent of Thomas Rudd’s authorship.** The surviving witness is Peter Smart’s early-eighteenth-century copy. Whether the angel-demon apparatus in precisely its surviving form predates Rudd’s death in 1656 remains an editorial reconstruction.

8. **Who first gave Mathers the spelling Aypeos.** It may come from a manuscript reading, an intermediate transcript or a simple error.

9. **Source of de Plancy’s anatomical reversal.** It may be grammatical reinterpretation of Weyer, a defective French intermediary or deliberate comic elaboration.

10. **No ancient-god identification.** Future discovery of a pre-Weyer Latin witness could change this conclusion, but current evidence favors scribal transmission rather than pagan euhemerism.
