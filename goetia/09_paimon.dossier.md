# Paimon: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes different kinds of claims:

- **[Documented text]**: stated or depicted in a surviving primary source.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]**: a conclusion drawn by modern historians or editors from manuscript relationships, dating, or textual comparison.
- **[Tradition]**: a teaching transmitted within a religious or magical tradition, without implying historical or empirical proof.
- **[Esoteric correspondence]**: an occult system’s deliberate symbolic assignment.
- **[Disputed]**: an attribution or interpretation for which the evidence is conflicting or inadequate.
- **[Legend]**: a narrative presented by magical or religious literature as received lore.
- **[Modern invention]**: a feature introduced by a modern author, occultist, filmmaker, game designer, or fan community.

“Paimon” below therefore means both a textual character that changed over centuries and, where explicitly indicated, the spirit understood by practitioners as an independently existing being.

# The name and its origins

## 1. The earliest recoverable stratum: a western king

### Cecco d’Ascoli, before 1327

**[Documented text]** Francesco Stabili, commonly called Cecco d’Ascoli, died in 1327. His commentary on Sacrobosco’s *Sphaera* names four demonic or aerial kings associated with the cardinal directions: **Oriens, Amaymon, Paymon, and Egim**. The known text survives through manuscript and printed transmission, including a Venice edition of 1499. Paymon is associated with the **west**.

This is currently the earliest securely located occurrence of the name in the European learned-magic chain. It does not yet give the crowned dromedary rider’s complete biography. Paymon is first a directional ruler, not “demon number 9.” The transcribed incunable material is available through the [Grimorium Latinum Cecco d’Ascoli edition](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/cecco-dascoli/).

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** This four-king system belongs to medieval astral and necromantic magic. Its recurring forms—Oriens/Urieus, Amaymon, Paymon and Egyn/Egin—suggest a shared ritual vocabulary rather than an author inventing Paimon in 1577.

### The *Livre des Esperitz*, fifteenth century

**[Documented text]** The French *Livre des Esperitz*, preserved in Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29, is the earliest located source to give Poymon a substantial individual entry. The catalogue dates the manuscript broadly to the second half of the fifteenth century. Its entry reads:

> **Poymon appiert en ſemblance de fẽme coronnee, moult reſplendiſſante et chevaulche ung dramagdonere Celuy qui le contrainct doibt avoir ſon viſaige vers occident et il dict verite de ce q̃ on luy demande et apprant toutes ſciences au maiſtre et manifeſte tout choſes mucees et donne dignites et grandes ſeigneuries et faict venir a mercy touz les malveillantz du maiſtre plainement Et est ſeigner de xxv legions.**

A normalized translation is:

> Poymon appears in the likeness of a crowned woman, very resplendent, and rides a dromedary. Whoever constrains him must face west. He speaks truth concerning what is asked of him, teaches all sciences to the master, manifests all hidden things, gives dignities and great lordships, and fully brings all the master’s ill-wishers to submission. He is lord of twenty-five legions.

The entry can be consulted in the [Wikisource transcription](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre_des_esperitz/Poymon); manuscript metadata is available through the [Biblissima catalogue](https://portail.biblissima.fr/fr/ark%3A/43093/mdata454dde45506284fbdb223f1ab242101b25c2e133).

This supplies several features later found in Weyer:

- the crown;
- the dromedary;
- a markedly feminine appearance;
- facing west;
- instruction in sciences;
- revelation of hidden things;
- dignities and lordships;
- subjection of enemies;
- twenty-five legions.

It does not mention Lucifer, 200 legions, the noisy musical procession, or Bebal and Abalam.

### Florence, Biblioteca Medicea Laurenziana, Plut. 89 sup. 38

**[Documented text]** A magical collection copied by January 1494 contains **Paimon/Paymon** among a series of spirits and includes a character or sigil for Paymon. Joseph Peterson notes this witness in his apparatus to Weyer. Its sigil is not identical to the familiar *Lesser Key* seal. This proves that seals associated with the name existed before the seventeenth-century *Lemegeton*, while also showing that the drawing was not stable.

### Other early directional lists

**[Documented text]** Paimon or Paymon appears repeatedly among the four kings:

- **Poymon** in the *Livre des Esperitz*;
- **Paymon** in Cecco and later lists;
- **Paymon** in Trithemius’s *Polygraphia*;
- **Paymon** in Agrippa;
- **Paymon** in Sloane MS 3853;
- related lists in magical miscellanies.

Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia* III.24, printed in its complete form in 1533, assigns **Paymon to the west**. Agrippa also reports speculative equivalences between the directional rulers and names such as Samael, Azazel, Azael and Mahazael.

**[Tradition/Disputed]** Agrippa’s equivalences are occult identifications, not linguistic derivations. They do not demonstrate that *Paymon* is a corruption of *Azazel*, nor that Paimon originated as a Jewish angel.

## 2. *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* and Weyer’s source

**[Documented text]** Johann Weyer says that his *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* derived from a book titled approximately *Liber officiorum spirituum, seu Liber dictus Empto. Salomonis, de principibus et regibus daemoniorum*. This belongs to the manuscript family conventionally called the *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*, “Book of the Offices of Spirits.”

A related English witness, Folger Shakespeare Library MS V.b.26, copied approximately 1577–1583, says:

> Paymon vocatur … he will speake of the state of the world, & he maye give familiarity, & he maye make all fishes of the sea to be obedient, he appeareth like a kinge with a womans face crowned with a bright crowne, he rydeth upon a dromedary & after him comes a greate companye of spirites with all manner of instruments of melodye, but if he be called alone, then he appeareth with 2 kinges & speakes misticallye for he wold not be understanded, neverthelesse thou mayest commaund him to speake in thine owne language, he hath infinit of spirits under him but 12 of the most mightiest be these.

The [Folger V.b.26 transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/folger/v_b_26_transcription.pdf) preserves a version close to Weyer but with additional material: obedience of fishes, twelve named subordinates, and an explicit command that Paimon speak the operator’s own language.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Weyer did not create the persona. He edited one version of an already circulating offices-of-spirits tradition. The *Livre des Esperitz*, Folger manuscript, and Weyer are cognate or closely related witnesses, though a complete, simple line of descent has not been demonstrated.

## 3. Is the name an ancient god’s name?

No ancient god, hero or locality has been securely demonstrated behind **Paimon**.

### Hebrew *paʿamon*, “bell”

**[Documented etymological gloss]** In his 1898 edition of *The Book of the Sacred Magic of Abramelin*, S. L. MacGregor Mathers connected Paimon with Hebrew **פעמון**, *paʿamon*, “bell” or “small bell.” The Hebrew word is genuine and occurs for the bells on the high priest’s robe in Exodus. A lexical entry is available in [Strong’s Hebrew 6472](https://biblehub.com/hebrew/6472.htm).

**[Disputed]** No medieval source derives Paymon from this word. The similarity may have suggested the explanation to Mathers, especially because Paimon has a musical procession, but phonetic resemblance is not evidence of historical transmission.

### Palaimon/Palaemon

**[Disputed]** Paimon is sometimes linked online to Greek **Παλαίμων**, Palaimon, the sea divinity identified with Melicertes. Greek *palaimōn* means approximately “wrestler” or “one who struggles.” The classical name and its etymology can be checked in the [Perseus lexical record](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn%3Acite2%3Ascaife-viewer%3Adictionary-entries.atlas_v1%3Amiddle-liddell.perseus-eng2-n24163/).

There is no documented manuscript bridge from *Palaimon* to *Paymon*. The absent *l* and the medieval directional context make this at best a speculative resemblance.

### Arabic *Maymūn*

**[Disputed]** Islamic and Arabic magical traditions contain jinn names built on **Maymūn**, “fortunate” or “auspicious,” including Maymūn Abā Nūkh and related directional rulers. Such material may be relevant to the complicated history of **Amaymon/Amaimon**. A direct derivation of **Paymon** from *Maymūn*, however, has not been established.

### A goddess, Amun, Baal or another ancient deity

**[Modern invention/Disputed]** Poke Runyon suggested that Paimon preserved a Middle Eastern goddess and connected the musical imagery to the sistrum and Isis. The medieval female form makes comparison tempting, but no source names Isis or supplies a linguistic route.

Paimon is not demonstrably:

- the Egyptian Amun or Amon;
- Baal;
- Astarte;
- Asmodeus/Ashmedai;
- Belial;
- Phoenix;
- Palaemon.

Several of those Goetic names have plausible ancient or linguistic pedigrees of their own. That does not transfer an ancient pedigree to Paimon. **Amon**, moreover, is a separate Goetic spirit, number 7.

### Best-supported conclusion

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The historically responsible conclusion is modest: *Paymon/Poymon* is attested as the name of a western spirit king by the early fourteenth century, and its deeper linguistic origin remains unknown.

# The order: Weyer’s 69 and the Goetia’s 72

**[Documented text]** In Weyer’s 1577 sequence, Paymon is **number 22**. In the *Ars Goetia*, Paimon is **number 9**.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The *Goetia*’s order follows Reginald Scot more closely than Weyer’s Latin original. Scot rearranged or omitted material in ways inherited by the English compilation. The *Goetia* added four spirits:

1. Vassago, number 3;
2. Seere, number 70;
3. Dantalion, number 71;
4. Andromalius, number 72.

It omitted Weyer’s fourth spirit, **Pruflas**, also written **Bufas**, which Scot had omitted. Thus:

> Weyer’s 69 + four additions − one omission = the *Goetia*’s 72.

The result matched the symbolically important number seventy-two. That numerical fit later allowed the entire list to be paired with the seventy-two names or angels of the Shem HaMephorash. It does not prove that the demon list was originally composed as a dark reflection of those angels. Peterson’s [Weyer introduction and apparatus](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm) documents the differences.

# The description across the sources

## The fifteenth-century nucleus

The *Livre des Esperitz* already contains the most recognizable core:

- splendid crown;
- feminine figure;
- dromedary;
- west;
- truth;
- sciences;
- hidden things;
- dignities and lordship;
- mastery over enemies;
- twenty-five legions.

Weyer considerably expands this with Lucifer, cosmological questions, the musical army, angelic origins, two accompanying kings, sacrifices or libations, and 200 legions.

## Weyer’s Latin, 1577, in full

**[Documented text]** Johann Weyer, *De praestigiis daemonum*, fifth edition, Basel, 1577, *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*, §22, printed pp. 918–919:

> **Paymon obedit magis Lucifero quam alij reges. Lucifer hic intelligendus, qui in profunditate scientiæ suæ demersus, Deo assimilari voluit, & ob hanc arrogantiam in exitium proiectus est. De quo dictum est: Omnis lapis pretiosus operimentum tuum [Ezech. 28]. Paymon autem cogitur virtute divina, ut se sistat coram exorcista: ubi hominis induit simulachrum, insidens dromedario, coronaque insignitus lucidissima, & vultu fœmineo.**
>
> **Hunc præcedit exercitus cum tubis & cimbalis bene sonantibus, atque omnibus instrumentis Musicis, primo cum ingenti clamore & rugitu apparens, sicut in Empto. Salomonis, et arte declaratur. Et si Paymon hic quandoque loquitur, ut minus ab exorcista intelligatur, propterea is non tepescat: sed ubi porrexerit illi primam chartam ut voto suo obsequatur, iubebit quoque ut distinctè & apertè respondeat ad quæsita, & de universa philosophia & prudentia vel scientia, & de cæteris arcanis.**
>
> **Et si voles cognoscere dispositionem mundi, & qualis sit terra, aut quid eam sustineat in aqua, aut aliquid aliud, & quid sit abyssus, & ubi est ventus & unde veniat, abundè te docebit. Accedant & consecrationes tam de libationibus quam alijs. Confert hic dignitates & confirmationes. Resistentes sibi suo vinculo deprimit, & exorcistæ subijcit. Bonos comparat famulos, & artium omnium intellectum.**
>
> **Notandum, quòd in advocando hunc Paymonem, Aquilonem versus exorcistam conspicere oporteat, quæ ibi huius sit hospitium. Accitum verò intrepidè constanterque suscipiat, interroget, & ab eo petat quicquid voluerit, nec dubiè impetrabit. At ne creatorem oblivioni tradat, cavendum exorcistæ, propter ea quæ præmissa fuerunt de Paymone. Sunt qui dicant, eum ex ordine Dominationum fuisse: sed alijs placet, ex ordine Cherubin. Hunc sequuntur legiones ducentæ, partim ex ordine Angelorum, partim Potestatum.**
>
> **Notandum adhęc, si Paymon solus fuerit citatus per aliquam libationem aut sacrificium, duo reges magni comitantur, scilicet Bebal & Abalam, & alij potentes. In huius exercitu sunt vigintiquinque legiones: quia spiritus his subiecti, non semper ipsis adsunt, nisi ut appareant, divina virtute compellantur.**

The Latin source and Peterson’s editorial notes are at [Esoteric Archives: Weyer](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

A close summary is that Paymon obeys Lucifer more than the other kings; is compelled by divine virtue; appears as a crowned man with a feminine face on a dromedary; arrives after trumpets, cymbals and other instruments; roars and speaks obscurely; teaches philosophy, wisdom, science, cosmology and secrets; grants dignity; subdues resisters; supplies servants; and belongs, according to rival reports, either to the Dominations or Cherubim.

### “Most obedient to Lucifer”

Weyer’s actual phrase is **“obedit magis Lucifero quam alij reges”**: Paymon obeys Lucifer more than the other kings do. “Most obedient to Lucifer” is a fair compressed translation, but grammatically the Latin is comparative rather than an absolute superlative.

Weyer then identifies Lucifer through the fallen-prince interpretation of Ezekiel 28. This is a Christian demonological frame placed over earlier spirit-office material.

### Direction: north, northwest or west?

Weyer says **Aquilonem versus**, literally “toward Aquilo,” the north or north wind. Scot renders this as **northwest**. The medieval directional-kings system and Mathers say **west**.

Possible explanations include:

- a copying or translation error;
- two competing directional traditions;
- “Aquilo” being interpreted as a broader northern quarter;
- Mathers restoring the older western attribution.

No surviving source settles the discrepancy.

## Scot’s English, 1584, in full

**[Documented text]** Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, 1584, Book XV, chapter 2, original pp. 219–220:

> **Paimon is more obedient in Lucifer than other kings are.**
>
> **Lucifer is heere to be understood he that was drowned in the depth of his knowledge: he would needs be like God, and for his arrogancie was throwne out into destruction, of whome it is said; Everie pretious stone is thy covering (Ezech. 88.). Paimon is constrained by divine vertue to stand before the exorcist; where he putteth on the likenesse of a man: he sitteth on a beast called a dromedarie, which is a swift runner, and weareth a glorious crowne, and hath an effeminate countenance.**
>
> **There goeth before him an host of men with trumpets and well sounding cymbals, and all musicall instruments. At the first he appeereth with a great crie and roring, as in Circulo Salomonis, and in the art is declared. And if this Paimon speake sometime that the conjuror understand him not, let him not therefore be dismaied.**
>
> **But when he hath delivered him the first obligation to observe his desire, he must bid him also answer him distinctlie and plainelie to the questions he shall aske you, of all philosophie, wisedome, and science, and of all other secret things. And if you will knowe the disposition of the world, and what the earth is, or what holdeth it up in the water, or any other thing, or what is Abyssus, or where the wind is, or from whence it commeth, he will teach you aboundantlie.**
>
> **Consecrations also as well of sacrifices as otherwise may be reckoned. He giveth dignities and confirmations; he bindeth them that resist him in his owne chaines, and subjecteth them to the conjuror; he prepareth good familiars, and hath the understanding of all arts. Note, that at the calling up of him, the exorcist must looke towards the northwest, bicause there is his house.**
>
> **When he is called up, let the exorcist receive him constantlie without feare, let him aske what questions or demands he list, and no doubt he shall obteine the same of him. And the exorcist must beware he forget not the creator, for those things, which have beene rehearsed before of Paimon, some saie he is of the order of dominations; others saie, of the order of cherubim. There follow him two hundred legions, partlie of the order of angels, and partlie of potestates.**
>
> **Note that if Paimon be cited alone by an offering or sacrifice, two kings followe him; to wit, Beball & Abalam, & other potentates: in his host are twentie five legions, bicause the spirits subject to them are not alwaies with them, except they be compelled to appeere by divine vertue.**

The complete text is at [Esoteric Archives: Scot, Book XV](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/scot16.htm).

### Scot’s purpose

**[Documented historical context]** Scot’s book was written largely against witch persecutions and credulous demonological claims. Printing magical texts was part of his exposure of what he considered fraud and superstition, not evidence that he personally worshipped or conjured Paimon.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The translation of Weyer’s catalogue may have been made or assisted by Abraham Fleming. It introduces errors:

- Weyer’s Ezekiel 28 becomes Scot’s nonsensical “Ezech. 88”;
- *Aquilo* becomes “northwest”;
- *Empto. Salomonis* becomes *Circulo Salomonis*;
- some Latin syntax becomes awkward English, including “obedient in Lucifer.”

## The seventeenth-century *Ars Goetia* manuscripts

**[Documented text]** Joseph Peterson’s transcription, principally from Sloane MS 3825 and compared with Sloane 2731 and other witnesses, reads:

> **The 9th spirit in order is Paimon; a great king, & very Obedient to Lucifer, he appeareth in ye forme of a man, sitting one a dromedary, wh a Crowne most glorious on his head. Three [There] goeth before him a host of spirits like men with Trumpets and well sounding Cymballs, and all other sorts of musicall Instruments &c. he hath a great voice, and roareth at his first comming, and his speech [voice] is such as ye Magician cannot well understand, unless he compelleth him.**
>
> **This spirit can teach all arts and siences, and other secret Things; he can discover wt ye Earth is, and wt holdeth it up in ye waters, & wt ye wind is or where it is, or any other Thing yu desire to know, he giveth dignity and confirmeth ye same, he bindeth or maketh a man subject to ye Magician if he desireth it he giveth good familiars, and such as can teach all arts, he is to be observed towards ye North west, he is of ye order of dominions and hath 200 Legions of spirits under him, one part of them is of ye order of Angells & ye other of Potestates [Potentates], If yu call this spirit Paimon alone yu must make some offering to him & there will attend him 2 kings called Bebal & Abalam, & other spirits of ye order of Potestates [Potentates] in his host are 25 Legions because all those spirits wch are subject to him, are not allwayes wth him unlesse ye Magician compelleth them, This is his Character.**

The transcription and manuscript variants appear in [Peterson’s online *Lemegeton*, Part I](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm#paimon).

### What changed from Weyer?

The *Goetia*:

- numbers Paimon ninth;
- removes Weyer’s long moral warning concerning the Creator;
- suppresses the alternative claim that Paimon belonged to the Cherubim;
- usually retains northwest from Scot;
- compresses the cosmological questions;
- keeps the 200/25 legion distinction;
- adds the seal as a required lamen.

The text is therefore heavily dependent on Scot but is not an exact copy.

## Mathers–Crowley, 1904, in full

**[Documented text]** *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King*, translated by S. L. MacGregor Mathers and edited by Aleister Crowley, 1904, printed pp. 24–25:

> **(9.) PAIMON.—The Ninth Spirit in this Order is Paimon, a Great King, and very obedient unto LUCIFER. He appeareth in the form of a Man sitting upon a Dromedary with a Crown most glorious upon his head. There goeth before him also an Host of Spirits, like Men with Trumpets and well sounding Cymbals, and all other sorts of Musical Instruments. He hath a great Voice, and roareth at his first coming, and his speech is such that the Magician cannot well understand it unless he can compel him.**
>
> **This Spirit can teach all Arts and Sciences, and other secret things. He can discover unto thee what the Earth is, and what holdeth it up in the Waters; and what Mind is, and where it is; or any other thing thou mayest desire to know. He giveth Dignity, and confirmeth the same. He bindeth or maketh any man subject unto the Magician if he so desire it. He giveth good Familiars, and such as can teach all Arts. He is to be observed towards the West. He is of the Order of Dominations.**
>
> **He hath under him 200 Legions of Spirits, and part of them are of the Order of Angels, and the other part of Potentates. Now if thou callest this Spirit Paimon alone, thou must make him some offering; and there will attend him two Kings called LABAL and ABALIM, and also other Spirits who be of the Order of Potentates in his Host, and 25 Legions. And those Spirits which be subject unto them are not always with them unless the Magician do compel them. His Character is this which must be worn as a Lamen before thee, etc.**

The entry is reproduced with original pagination at [Sacred Texts](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks05.htm) and in a [Mathers–Crowley facsimile/transcription](https://lo2.org/pdf/celepha%C3%AFs/mathers/Mathers-and-Crowley.Goetia.pdf).

### “Wind” became “Mind”

**[Textual error]** Weyer asks where the **wind** is and whence it comes: *ubi est ventus et unde veniat*. Scot retains “where the wind is, or from whence it commeth.” The manuscripts also read *wind*. Mathers prints **Mind**.

“Paimon teaches the nature and location of Mind” is therefore a famous reading in the 1904 edition, but almost certainly arose from misreading or editorial alteration. It should not be projected back into Weyer or the medieval source.

### Bebal and Abalam versus Labal and Abalim

**[Documented variants]**

- Weyer: **Bebal, Abalam**
- Scot: **Beball, Abalam**
- *Goetia* manuscripts: normally **Bebal, Abalam**
- Mathers–Crowley: **Labal, Abalim**

No independent descriptions of these two kings establish that the Mathers names refer to different beings. They are best treated as transmissional variants.

### The number of legions

The texts say both:

- 200 legions follow or are under Paimon, partly former Angels and partly Powers/Potentates;
- 25 legions are “in this host” when the accompanying kings and their spirits appear.

**[Disputed interpretation]** Modern summaries sometimes report 200, 25, or 225. The grammar suggests nested or situational bodies, not an arithmetic equation. “Paimon commands 200 legions, while the particular attending host of Bebal and Abalam comprises 25” preserves the ambiguity.

## Appearance and the nonexistent “second form”

Paimon does **not** have the standard two-stage appearance found in descriptions such as Marbas, Furfur, Amon or Sitri, where an animal form changes into a human or angelic form at command.

The variants are textual, not sequential transformations:

| Source | Appearance |
|---|---|
| *Livre des Esperitz* | A resplendent crowned **woman** riding a dromedary |
| Folger V.b.26 | A king with a **woman’s face**, crowned, on a dromedary |
| Weyer | A **man**, crowned, with a feminine face, on a dromedary |
| Scot | A man with an effeminate countenance |
| *Ars Goetia* | A crowned man on a dromedary |
| de Plancy | A man with a woman’s face |
| *Hereditary* | A crowned, effeminate male figure; additionally embodied through a male host |

A demand for a “second form” is therefore not part of Paimon’s traditional entry.

# Paimon in the *Book of Abramelin*

**[Documented text]** Paimon appears among eight “sub-princes” under the four principal spirits Lucifer, Leviathan, Satan and Belial. The name is usually **Paimon** in Mathers and **Paymon** in German witnesses.

Mathers’s 1898 version attributed *Abramelin* to the fifteenth century. Georg Dehn and Steven Guth’s later manuscript work, followed by other researchers, places the surviving German composition closer to **1595–1609**, with the oldest complete dated witness from 1608. The [Peterson *Abramelin* resource](https://www.esotericarchives.com/abramelin/abramelin.htm) discusses manuscript dating and variants.

Paimon participates in the collective hierarchy of subordinate princes and is singled out in an operation for causing armed men to appear. Powers assigned to the whole class of spirits should not automatically be rewritten as Paimon’s personal office.

**[Tradition]** Mathers’s note says Paimon is also called Ozazl/Azazel “by the Rabbins” and derives the name from a bell. Neither assertion has been traced to the medieval Paimon sources. They belong to Mathers’s occult commentary.

# The seal

## What survives

**[Documented image]** The *Goetia* manuscripts give Paimon a linear character. Printed Mathers editions reproduce two variants. They can be inspected directly as:

- [Paimon seal, printed variant 1](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:09-Paimon_seal01.png)
- [Paimon seal, printed variant 2](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:09-Paimon_seal02.png)

A verbal description cannot replace an exact drawing, but the recurring printed structure is:

1. a central upright or gate-like figure;
2. a crown-like upper section with four small rounded uprights or loops;
3. a vertical stem descending from the center;
4. short transverse strokes;
5. curved or hooked lower terminals;
6. in Mathers’s lamen, a circular border containing the name **PAIMON**.

The two published versions vary in the width and curvature of the top, the lower curls, the number and position of cross-strokes, the shape of the base, and the way the glyph is fitted to the circle.

## Manuscript variation

- Weyer prints **no seal**.
- The 1494 Florence manuscript has a Paymon character, but it is unlike the standard *Goetia* one.
- Sloane witnesses normally show a free-standing character before or beside the entry.
- Harley 6483 places the demonic character within a more elaborate angelic-counterseal system.
- Mathers regularizes the seal as a circular lamen and prints two forms without providing a sufficiently precise source-by-source stemma.

**[Scholarly caution]** These are copied and recopied diagrams. Small bends, loops or missing strokes may be scribal, engraver’s or typesetter’s variation. It is not historically defensible to designate every pixel-level difference as a distinct revealed seal.

# The angel: Haziel

## Identification

| Field | Haziel |
|---|---|
| Number | 9 of 72 |
| Hebrew triad | **הזי** |
| Expanded angel name | **הזיאל** |
| Latin spellings | Haziel, Hasiel |
| Traditional attribute | *Deus misericors*, “Merciful God” |
| Zodiacal span | 10°–15° Taurus |
| Psalm | Vulgate 24:6 / modern Psalm 25:6 |
| Traditional choir | Cherubim |
| Lenain invocation interval | 2:40–3:00 |
| Lenain’s five annual days | 28 March, 8 June, 19 August, 30 October, 10 January |
| Rudd pairing | Haziel constrains Paimon, spirit 9 |

## The seventy-two names from Exodus

**[Jewish mystical tradition]** Exodus 14:19–21 consists, in its consonantal Hebrew form, of three verses traditionally counted as seventy-two letters each. The first verse is written forward, the second backward, and the third forward. Reading one letter from each line produces seventy-two three-letter combinations.

This method is explained in traditions associated with the *Sefer ha-Bahir* and later *Sefer Raziel*. Joshua Trachtenberg summarizes the arrangement in [*Jewish Magic and Superstition*](https://sacred-texts.com/jud/jms/jms09.htm).

The ninth triad is **הזי**, conventionally transliterated HZY. Christian Kabbalists added endings such as **-el** or **-yah**, producing personal angel names. Thus **HZY + EL → Haziel**.

The three-letter combinations are older than their fully developed Christian-angelic biographies. It is misleading to assume that every modern description of Haziel occurs in early Jewish texts.

## Meaning

**[Tradition]** Lenain gives Haziel’s attribute as **“Dieu de miséricorde,” “God of mercy.”** The Latin formula is *Deus misericors*.

**[Disputed philology]** “Merciful God” is a traditional title derived from the associated Psalm and later angelological system. It is not a transparent literal translation of the consonants H-Z-Y. Alternative modern glosses such as “vision of God” depend on selecting a different Semitic root and are no more securely established.

## Psalm verse

Harley 6483 and later tables associate Haziel with:

> **Reminiscere miserationum tuarum, Domine, et misericordiarum tuarum, quae a saeculo sunt.**

This is Psalm 24:6 in the Vulgate:

> Remember thy compassions, O Lord, and thy mercies, which are from eternity.

In Masoretic and most modern Protestant numbering it is Psalm 25:6:

> “Remember, O LORD, thy tender mercies and thy lovingkindnesses; for they have been ever of old.”

The numbering difference is bibliographical, not evidence for two different verses.

## Haziel’s five degrees

**[Esoteric correspondence]** Lenain assigns Haziel the **41st through 45th degrees** of the zodiac, equivalent in modern notation to **10°00′–15°00′ Taurus**.

Later guardian-angel calendars commonly translate this into approximately **1–5 May**, though exact civil dates vary by epoch and by whether an author uses tropical longitude, idealized five-day blocks or a fixed popular calendar.

Lenain separately says Haziel presides over five days distributed around the year:

- 28 March;
- 8 June;
- 19 August;
- 30 October;
- 10 January.

These are a different scheme from the continuous Taurus birth-date block. Conflating them produces apparently contradictory calendars.

## Historical chain of the angelic system

### *Sefer ha-Bahir* and *Sefer Raziel*

**[Documented tradition]** The seventy-two-fold divine name is part of medieval Jewish mystical speculation. *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh*, a composite medieval magical work printed in the early eighteenth century but drawing on earlier materials, transmits names and angelic operations. It does not supply the complete modern “Haziel personality profile.”

### Reuchlin and Agrippa

**[Christian Kabbalah]** Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517) and Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia* (1533) helped transmit Hebrew divine names into Christian learned magic. Agrippa gives the method of deriving the seventy-two names and relates them to angelic orders and zodiacal divisions.

### Athanasius Kircher

**[Christian Kabbalah]** Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–1654) presented elaborate tables of divine and angelic names. His work helped normalize the seventy-two angel names and their cosmic organization in early modern occult reference culture.

### Rudd/Smart and Harley 6483

**[Documented manuscript practice]** British Library Harley MS 6483, copied by Peter Smart in 1712–1713, pairs the spirits of the *Goetia* with the seventy-two angels by number. Paimon, spirit 9, receives **Haziel, angel 9**.

The manuscript’s procedure places the spirit’s character on one side of a lamen and an angelic name and Scriptural verse on the other. The angel is invoked as divine authority against or over the spirit. This is a ritual technology of constraint, not a statement that Paimon and Haziel had always been cosmic “twins.”

**[Disputed attribution]** The manuscript is conventionally associated with “Dr Rudd,” but the extent to which it descends from the historical engineer and mathematician Thomas Rudd is debated. The physical copy is Peter Smart’s. The simple claim “Thomas Rudd personally wrote Harley 6483” is false.

Stephen Skinner and David Rankine published this material as *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* in 2007; a revised edition followed. Their publisher’s description is available from [Llewellyn](https://www.llewellyn.com/product.php?ean=9780738723556). Dan Harms has discussed the attribution and Smart’s role in his [review of the Rudd edition](https://danharms.wordpress.com/2007/10/27/on-the-shelf-the-goetia-of-dr-rudd-part-1/) and [discussion of Peter Smart](https://danharms.wordpress.com/2007/11/07/on-the-shelf-the-goetia-of-dr-rudd-part-6-or-get-smart/).

### Lenain, 1823

**[Documented esoteric system]** Lazare Républicain Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* gives Haziel:

- number 9;
- *Dieu de miséricorde*;
- 41st–45th zodiacal degrees;
- Moon as the decan’s planetary influence;
- membership among the Cherubim;
- Psalm 24:6;
- invocation from 2:40 to 3:00;
- aid in obtaining divine mercy;
- friendship and favors from powerful people;
- fulfillment of promises;
- good faith and reconciliation.

Its negative or “contrary” side is associated with hatred, hypocrisy and deception.

The original edition is available through [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_ms05365) and [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books?id=BqzyzEEX3MAC).

### Papus

Gérard Encausse, “Papus,” incorporated and republished Lenain’s material in the French occult revival, including *La Cabbale* and a 1909 Lenain edition with Papus’s preface. The bibliographical record is at the [Bibliothèque nationale de France](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb30789329z).

### Robert Ambelain

**[Esoteric correspondence]** Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) further systematized the seventy-two angels through Sephiroth, angelic choirs, archangels, planets, moral qualities and ritual schedules. Haziel is commonly placed among the Cherubim, under Chokmah and Raziel.

Some modern tables assign Uranus to Chokmah. That is a post-discovery modern overlay, not part of Lenain’s 1823 planetary system or early Jewish Kabbalah.

### Golden Dawn and Crowley

The Golden Dawn integrated Shem names, zodiacal degrees, Tarot decans and Enochian materials into a large correspondence system. It did not originate Rudd’s one-to-one numerical pairing.

Crowley’s *777*, first published anonymously in 1909, uses a different astrological allocation for the Goetic demons. It should not be used as proof that Paimon originally ruled Gemini.

### François Bernard Termès, “Haziel”

A late-twentieth-century French writer, François Bernard Termès, published popular angel books under the name **Haziel**. These works spread the now-common five-day “guardian angel” profiles, moral descriptions, prayers and birthday calendars.

**[Modern occult development]** These biographies greatly expand Lenain and earlier tables. They are not translations of a single ancient Kabbalistic book.

## Why two spirits can occupy one pip card

The thirty-six Tarot pip cards from Two through Ten correspond to thirty-six ten-degree decans. Seventy-two five-degree segments fit into those same thirty-six decans, so each pip contains two quinances.

Under the sequential Rudd/Shem-derived scheme:

- **Paimon/Haziel**, 10°–15° Taurus;
- **Buer/Aladiah**, 15°–20° Taurus;

both fall within the Moon-in-Taurus decan and therefore share the **Six of Pentacles or Disks**.

Crowley’s *777* does something different: it assigns two Goetic spirits to each complete decan as **day and night rulers**. There:

- Paimon is the daytime spirit of 20°–30° Gemini;
- Vine is the nighttime spirit;
- the card is the **Ten of Swords**, Sun in Gemini.

The *777* PDF is available from [Astrum Argenteum](https://www.astrumargenteum.org/wp-content/uploads/2016/07/0777-by-Aleister-Crowley.pdf).

## Rank, planet and metal

**[Documented ritual correspondence]** The *Goetia*’s observations associate ranks with planetary metals. A King’s seal is made in:

- **gold**;
- or, by correspondence, solar-colored material.

Thus Paimon’s rank implies the **Sun**, not the Moon. The Moon belongs to Haziel’s Taurus decan in Lenain’s table. Both can appear in a composite scheme because they answer different questions.

## Timing

The printed *Goetia* says kings may be bound:

- from **9 a.m. until noon**;
- and from **3 p.m. until sunset**.

It recommends a waxing or otherwise favorable Moon, specifically lunar ages:

- 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12 and 14 days.

The relevant observations are at [Sacred Texts: magical observations](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks06.htm).

**[Later practice]** Solar planetary magic often favors Sunday or the Sun’s planetary hour for a king. That is a reasonable Golden Dawn or planetary-magic overlay, but the Paimon entry itself does not simply say, “Conjure only on Sunday.”

## Enochian overlays

**[Modern esoteric synthesis]** Crowley’s 1904 volume added an Enochian translation of some conjurations, the “Preliminary Invocation” or “Bornless Ritual,” and his psychological essay on ceremonial magic. Enochian language is not present in Weyer’s Paimon entry or the seventeenth-century textual nucleus.

Later writers and decks—notably Lon Milo DuQuette’s *Tarot of Ceremonial Magick*—combine:

- Goetic spirits;
- Shem angels;
- zodiacal decans;
- Tarot;
- Enochian tablets;
- planetary and elemental correspondences.

Such combinations are usable within their systems, but historically secondary.

# Iconography

## Manuscript image

The earliest Paimon imagery is verbal rather than pictorial:

- feminine or effeminate crowned ruler;
- dromedary;
- brilliant crown;
- musical retinue;
- shouting or roaring voice.

The manuscript seals are abstract linear characters, not portraits.

## Collin de Plancy, 1818 and 1863

Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* first appeared in 1818. Its early editions supplied prose entries but not the celebrated full series of Breton engravings.

The sixth edition of 1863 includes the following entry:

> **Paymon, l’un des rois de l’enfer. S’il se montre aux exorcistes, c’est sous la forme d’un homme à cheval sur un dromadaire, couronné d’un diadème étincelant de pierreries, avec un visage de femme. Deux cents légions, partie de l’ordre des Anges, partie de l’ordre des Puissances, lui obéissent. Si Paymon est évoqué par quelque sacrifice ou libation, il paraît accompagné des deux grands princes Bébal et Abalam.**

Translation:

> Paymon, one of the kings of hell. If he shows himself to exorcists, it is in the form of a man mounted on a dromedary, crowned with a diadem glittering with precious stones, and with a woman’s face. Two hundred legions, partly of the order of Angels and partly of the order of Powers, obey him. If Paymon is evoked through some sacrifice or libation, he appears accompanied by the two great princes Bebal and Abalam.

The entry is explicitly dependent on Weyer and can be read at [French Wikisource](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.,_1863/Paymon).

## Louis Le Breton’s portrait

**[Documented image]** The 1863 edition’s Louis Le Breton engraving depicts:

- a robed, crowned rider;
- a comparatively soft or feminine human face;
- an unmistakable dromedary;
- a procession of attendants;
- a prominent horn or trumpet;
- desert-like ground and a theatrical forward movement.

The image is available as [Louis Le Breton’s Paymon engraving](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ill_dict_infernal_p0537_paymon.jpg).

It is a nineteenth-century interpretation of Weyer’s prose, not an illustration transmitted from Solomon or the Middle Ages.

## Modern art

Modern depictions fall into several types:

1. direct copies of Le Breton;
2. crowned, androgynous desert monarchs;
3. monstrous camel-riders;
4. sigil-centered occult designs;
5. handsome or feminine demon kings in comics and games;
6. the white-robed male monarch and cult iconography of *Hereditary*;
7. avian aristocrats derived from *Helluva Boss*’s fictional “Ars Goetia” family.

**[Modern invention]** Common additions—antlers, black wings, blue skin, peacock imagery, specific eye colors, mandatory feminine pronouns, or a fixed camel-headed anatomy—are artistic choices rather than stable features of the source chain.

# People and works

## Medieval and early modern transmitters

### Cecco d’Ascoli

The earliest currently located writer to name Paymon, as one of four directional rulers. His source is unknown.

### The anonymous compiler of the *Livre des Esperitz*

Preserved the earliest full Poymon description. This work is one of the closest ancestors of the later spirit catalogues.

### Johannes Trithemius and Agrippa

Trithemius transmitted lists and cryptographic or magical classifications; Agrippa placed Paymon in the west and connected directional spirits with Christian-Kabbalistic demonology.

### Johann Weyer

Weyer printed the first widely circulated Latin description in 1577. He included it as an appendix to his larger attack on witch-persecution demonology. The common claim that *Pseudomonarchia* already appeared in Weyer’s 1563 first edition is incorrect: it was added in the **1577 fifth edition**.

### Reginald Scot and possibly Abraham Fleming

Scot’s 1584 English version supplied the wording that the *Lemegeton* compiler most directly inherited. Its errors therefore became part of later occult tradition.

### The *Lemegeton* compilers

Anonymous seventeenth-century compilers reshaped the list into 72 spirits, supplied seals, numbered Paimon ninth and embedded the catalogue in a larger Solomonic ritual manual.

### Peter Smart and “Dr Rudd”

Smart copied Harley 6483 in 1712–1713. The manuscript pairs each demon with an angelic authority. Skinner and Rankine connect its material with a Rudd/Heydon magical lineage; the precise authorship and descent remain contested.

## Nineteenth- and twentieth-century occultists

### Collin de Plancy and Louis Le Breton

De Plancy popularized Weyer’s demonography for a broad French audience; Le Breton created the canonical nineteenth-century portrait in 1863.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers

Mathers edited *Abramelin*, produced the English *Goetia* text used by Crowley, printed two seal variants, changed “wind” to “Mind,” changed Bebal/Abalam to Labal/Abalim and restored the western direction.

### Aleister Crowley

Crowley published Mathers’s *Goetia* in 1904 with his own essay, Enochian material and ritual additions. His *777* later assigned Paimon to 20°–30° Gemini and the Ten of Swords.

No securely documented Paimon-specific Crowley operation was located. A stray or ambiguous working note should not be inflated into a developed Paimon cult.

### Frater Achad

No substantial, securely attested Paimon-specific treatment by Charles Stansfeld Jones, Frater Achad, was located. His importance lies in Thelemic and Qabalistic history generally.

### Papus, Lenain, Ambelain and “Haziel”

These writers constructed the French lineage of highly detailed Shem-angel correspondences. Their work explains much of Haziel’s modern biography, but it postdates Rudd and should not be read back into ancient Judaism.

### Poke Runyon

Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* develops a ritual system placing each Goetic spirit in a five-degree zodiacal segment. In this reconstruction Paimon occupies 10°–15° Taurus, is paired with Haziel, corresponds to the Six of Disks and is situated within an elaborate directional and angelic hierarchy.

Runyon’s Isis/sistrum or “ancient goddess” theory is imaginative occult reconstruction, not demonstrated history.

### Lon Milo DuQuette

DuQuette co-authored *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* and created *Tarot of Ceremonial Magick*, which helps popularize integrated Goetic, Enochian, Tarot and astrological practice. No major, source-backed claim that DuQuette conducted a defining Paimon operation was located.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their *Goetia of Dr Rudd* made Harley 6483’s angelic-counterseal material widely accessible and established the Paimon–Haziel pairing in contemporary practice.

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 *The Lesser Key of Solomon: Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis* and online critical transcriptions provide the clearest accessible comparison of Sloane manuscripts, Weyer and Scot. His work is indispensable for separating manuscript readings from Mathers’s editorial forms.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s *True Grimoire* and *Encyclopaedia Goetica* emphasize the older four-king structure, the relationship between European and Mediterranean spirit traditions and the practical grimoire context. His broader comparative model has been influential among practitioners but does not prove a specific ancient-god etymology for Paimon.

### Historians

- Jean-Patrice Boudet studied Renaissance demonological catalogues and their medieval ancestors.
- Julien Véronèse has worked extensively on medieval ritual magic, Solomonic texts and works such as the *Livre des Esperitz*.
- Jan Veenstra has examined learned magic, spirits and medieval intellectual culture.
- Richard Kieckhefer’s *Forbidden Rites* edits the Munich handbook.
- Owen Davies’s *Grimoires: A History of Magic Books* places such texts in their larger social and print history.

Boudet’s relevant study is [“Les who’s who démonologiques de la Renaissance et leurs ancêtres médiévaux”](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/1171).

# Grimoire ancestors and relatives

## *Testament of Solomon*

**[Documented absence]** The late-antique, composite *Testament of Solomon* tells how Solomon uses a divinely empowered ring to interrogate and bind demons for construction of the Temple. It supplies important ancestors of the Solomonic narrative form.

Paimon does **not** appear by that name. The text therefore supports the broad “Solomon commanding demons” framework, not Paimon’s individual antiquity.

## Munich Handbook, Clm 849

The fifteenth-century Munich manual edited by Richard Kieckhefer contains ritual magic and spirits that anticipate parts of later catalogues.

**[Documented absence]** Paimon is not among its identifiable spirits. The handbook is a contextual ancestor, not a Paimon witness.

## *Livre des Esperitz*

This is the strongest medieval precursor for Paimon’s individual description.

## *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

This textual family supplied the offices-of-spirits tradition used by Weyer and represented by manuscripts such as Folger V.b.26.

## *Abramelin*

Paimon appears as a subordinate prince, but the surviving composition is approximately contemporary with or later than Weyer rather than a secure fifteenth-century precursor.

## No single linear genealogy

A simplified chain—

> *Testament of Solomon* → Munich handbook → *Livre des Esperitz* → Weyer → *Goetia*

—is too neat. The sources represent overlapping literary and ritual families. The first two supply context; the *Livre* and *Liber Officiorum* family give direct Paimon evidence; Weyer and Scot lead most clearly into the *Goetia*.

# Literature, film and games

## Dante and Milton

**[Documented absence]** Paimon does not appear in Dante’s *Divine Comedy* or Milton’s *Paradise Lost*. Their infernal hierarchies shaped later Western demon imagery, but neither is a source for Paimon.

## The *Bartimaeus* novels

Jonathan Stroud’s *Bartimaeus* sequence uses Solomon, magicians, bound spirits and named demons or djinn. No attested appearance of Paimon was located. The series belongs to the broader Solomonic afterlife, not Paimon’s direct reception history.

## *Hellboy*

No securely attested Paimon character was located in the principal *Hellboy* comics or films. *Hellboy* uses many demonological names, including names known from grimoires, but that general resemblance is not evidence for Paimon.

## *Last Shift* (2014)

The horror film uses the cult leader’s name **John Michael Paymon**. This is a transparent name-level allusion or pun, but the film does not present a faithful adaptation of Weyer’s spirit. A synopsis is available at [IMDb](https://www.imdb.com/title/tt2965466/plotsummary/).

## *Hereditary* (2018)

This is the most consequential modern Paimon adaptation.

Ari Aster’s screenplay includes a fictional book titled *Invocations* and describes:

- a crowned, effeminate man on a dromedary;
- musical or ceremonial attendants;
- “King Paimon”;
- a cult seeking a male host;
- possession;
- decapitated bodies and headless worshippers;
- promises of riches, knowledge and rewards;
- the title “God of Mischief.”

The screenplay can be consulted in [The Script Lab’s PDF](https://thescriptlab.com/wp-content/uploads/scripts/Hereditary-by-Ari-Aster-11-30-2016.pdf).

### What the film preserves

- kingly rank;
- the name Paimon;
- crown;
- effeminacy or androgyny;
- dromedary image in the cult book;
- musical procession;
- knowledge and worldly advancement;
- a seal based on the printed Goetic character.

### What the film invents or transforms

- hereditary cultivation of a human vessel;
- a categorical preference or need for a male body;
- decapitation as Paimon’s defining ritual;
- the family’s miniatures as a magical technology;
- the cult’s exact liturgy;
- “God of Mischief”;
- possession as the principal office;
- several details of the displayed seal;
- the coronation scene and cult organization.

Aster explained that he wanted a specific demonological figure rather than a generic devil and chose Paimon through research; see his [GQ interview](https://www.gq.com/story/how-hereditary-director-ari-aster-made-the-most-fucked-up-movie-of-the-year).

**[Reception]** *Hereditary* did not rediscover a forgotten name—the Mathers–Crowley *Goetia*, de Plancy and occult communities had kept it in circulation—but it greatly expanded the name’s visibility in mainstream horror culture. Calling this a “revival” is reasonable as reception history, though no quantitative measure proves how much of subsequent interest came from the film.

## *Shin Megami Tensei* and *Persona*

Paimon appears in Atlus’s demon-compendium tradition, with an early appearance in *Megami Ibunroku Persona/Revelations: Persona* in 1996. These games generally reinterpret figures from mythology and grimoires as collectible or negotiable entities. A series record is maintained by the [Megami Tensei Wiki](https://megatenwiki.com/wiki/Paimon); a later official appearance is documented by [Sega’s *Dx2 Shin Megami Tensei* announcement](https://d2-megaten-l.sega.com/en/news/detail/104185.html).

The visual designs and combat abilities are game mechanics, not historical magical attributes.

## *Genshin Impact*

The companion character Paimon shares the Goetic name. Other important characters in the game also bear demonological names, making deliberate borrowing highly likely.

**[Scholarly inference about fiction]** Unless supported by a direct developer statement, one should distinguish “the name comes from the same demonological catalogue” from claims that the cheerful game character is secretly identical with or possessed by the grimoire spirit. The latter are fan theories.

## *Helluva Boss*

In “The Circus,” Paimon is represented as Stolas’s father and a royal patriarch within the show’s fictional Ars Goetia. He is an avian, shape-changing aristocrat rather than the dromedary rider. The episode is listed by [Apple TV](https://tv.apple.com/jo/episode/the-circus/umc.cmc.6jqrmmw17kqwmvdhdlsiu7nc2).

The royal rank and Goetic family name derive from the grimoire; the genealogy, personality, appearance and relationship to Stolas are modern inventions.

## Other recent uses

Paimon or Paymon appears in additional games, comics and horror fiction, including *Amanda the Adventurer*, the 2024 *Silent Hill 2* remake’s occult puzzle imagery, and later independent comics. In many cases the borrowing is confined to a seal or name. Those appearances should be verified title by title rather than treated as a continuous esoteric tradition.

# Controversies and disputes

## 1. Was Paimon invented by Weyer?

No.

**Finding:** The name predates Weyer by more than two centuries in Cecco d’Ascoli, while the dromedary-riding persona occurs in the fifteenth-century *Livre des Esperitz*.

Weyer remains the first widely printed full Latin version and the principal source for the later *Goetia*.

## 2. Does Paimon have an ancient pagan origin?

Possibly in some remote, unrecoverable sense—but none is documented.

No reliable source connects the name historically to Isis, Amun, Baal, Astarte, Palaimon or a named Arabian deity. The earliest evidence already belongs to medieval European ritual magic.

## 3. Is Paimon male, female or androgynous?

The textual record changes:

- woman;
- king with woman’s face;
- man with feminine face;
- simply man.

“Male king with an effeminate countenance” best describes Weyer. “Originally female” overstates a single early witness; “always male” ignores that witness.

## 4. Does Paimon rule 25, 200 or 225 legions?

- 25 in the *Livre des Esperitz*;
- 200 under Paimon in Weyer and the *Goetia*;
- 25 in the specially attending host.

The sources never plainly total them as 225.

## 5. Is Paimon west, north or northwest?

All three occur:

- west is earliest;
- north is Weyer;
- northwest is Scot and much manuscript *Goetia*;
- west returns in Mathers.

The discrepancy is unresolved.

## 6. Does Paimon teach “Mind”?

Only in Mathers’s influential 1904 reading. Earlier texts say **wind**. The philosophical “Mind” interpretation is a productive occult development founded on a probable textual error.

## 7. Are Labal and Abalim distinct from Bebal and Abalam?

There is no evidence that they are distinct spirits. They are spelling or copying variants.

## 8. Is the Haziel pairing ancient?

No. The seventy-two divine names have medieval Jewish antecedents, but pairing angel 9 with demon 9 depends on the completed 72-spirit *Goetia*. It appears in the early-eighteenth-century Rudd/Smart material.

## 9. Is 10°–15° Taurus Crowley’s assignment?

No.

- It follows the Haziel/numerical five-degree system and was explicitly applied to Paimon by later occultists such as Runyon.
- Crowley’s *777* gives Paimon 20°–30° Gemini and the Ten of Swords.

## 10. Is “Paimon’s enn” ancient?

No known medieval or early modern source contains modern formulas such as **“Linan tasa jedan Paimon.”** Such enns belong to recent demonolatry currents and personal or received ritual traditions. Their spiritual validity is a matter of belief; their late documentary date is not.

## 11. Do modern offerings reproduce Weyer?

Weyer mentions a libation or sacrifice when Paimon is called alone. The text does not prescribe the now-common inventories of wine, incense, coins, music, tobacco, sandalwood, yellow candles or particular foods. Those are later ritual systems, analogical correspondences or practitioner reports.

## 12. Is Paimon uniquely dangerous or benevolent?

The sources present coercive powers and submission of opponents, but also learning, truth, dignities and familiars. Modern moral classifications—benevolent teacher, sinister possessor, neutral intelligence—are interpretive or experiential claims rather than settled features of the textual record.

## 13. Did Paimon inspire a real historical cult?

No premodern Paimon cult has been documented. The sources are manuals and catalogues for conjuration, not records of temples, priesthoods or popular worship.

## 14. Did Solomon write the *Ars Goetia*?

No historical evidence supports Solomonic authorship. The text is a seventeenth-century English compilation drawing on sixteenth-century printed and manuscript sources. “Solomon” is pseudepigraphic authority.

# Open questions

1. **The ultimate origin of the name:** No accepted etymology explains *Paymon* before Cecco d’Ascoli.

2. **Cecco’s immediate source:** His four directional kings may derive from older Arabic, Jewish, Latin astral or necromantic material, but the precise intermediary remains unidentified.

3. **Relationship between *Poymon* and the four kings:** The *Livre des Esperitz* combines an individual office with the western directional role. Whether these began as separate traditions is unknown.

4. **The earliest exact form of the dromedary motif:** It is present by the fifteenth century, but its symbolism—speed, exotic royal travel, Arabic influence or visual pun—has not been established.

5. **The Florence sigil’s relationship to the Goetic seal:** Both are attached to Paymon, but their graphical discontinuity suggests either independent characters or substantial replacement.

6. **Why Weyer changed west to north:** This could be Weyer’s source, a scribal error or a different directional scheme.

7. **The precise manuscript ancestry of Mathers’s two seals:** Mathers did not document his sources at a standard that permits every line to be assigned confidently.

8. **The Rudd attribution:** Harley 6483 is Peter Smart’s copy; how much derives from a Thomas Rudd, and which Rudd, remains debated.

9. **Bebal and Abalam:** They have no securely recovered independent medieval biographies sufficient to explain their relationship to Paimon.

10. **The significance of gender:** The early feminine appearance may reflect iconography, grammar, scribal inheritance or a now-lost source. Claims of a suppressed goddess remain unproven.

11. **Transmission from Arabic magic:** Comparative research may eventually clarify Paymon’s relationship to Arabic directional-jinn traditions, but resemblance is not yet a demonstrated genealogy.

# Conclusions

Paimon is neither an ancient deity with a securely recoverable pagan biography nor a creation of the 1904 *Goetia*. The recoverable historical figure begins as **Paymon, western king**, in early-fourteenth-century Latin learned magic. By the fifteenth century, **Poymon** is a resplendent crowned woman riding a dromedary, teaching sciences, revealing hidden things and commanding twenty-five legions.

The *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* tradition expanded this figure. Weyer’s 1577 printing produced the decisive version: a crowned man with a feminine face; a dromedary; trumpets, cymbals and roaring; obedience to Lucifer; cosmological instruction; dignities; familiars; 200 legions; and Bebal and Abalam with a 25-legion host. Scot translated it in 1584, introducing errors that passed into the seventeenth-century *Ars Goetia*.

The *Goetia* renumbered Paimon from Weyer’s 22 to **9**, supplied the familiar seal and made possible the later numerical pairing with **Haziel, angel 9**. Harley 6483 uses Haziel’s name and Psalm verse as divine authority constraining Paimon. Lenain and later French occultists furnished Haziel’s “God of mercy” title and 10°–15° Taurus quinance.

Crowley’s *777* belongs to a different scheme: Paimon rules the daytime half of the third Gemini decan with Vine at night, under the **Ten of Swords**. Runyon and later practitioners instead align Paimon with Haziel in Taurus and the **Six of Disks**. Both are real esoteric correspondences; neither is the medieval spirit’s original astrology.

Louis Le Breton’s 1863 engraving fixed Paimon’s visual form for modern readers. *Hereditary* transformed it again in 2018, preserving the crown, dromedary, music and effeminacy while inventing the male host, hereditary possession cult, decapitation complex and “God of Mischief.” The film brought Paimon into mainstream horror, games and popular culture, where the name now bears several layers that must be kept distinct from the historical texts.
