### Evidence labels

- **[D] Documented text:** expressly present in a primary manuscript or early printed work.
- **[ED] Editorial fact:** supplied by a modern editor’s collation or numbering.
- **[SR] Scholarly reconstruction:** reasoned historical or textual inference.
- **[T] Tradition:** later reception accepted within a magical lineage.
- **[EC] Esoteric correspondence:** an occult attribution rather than a historical identity.
- **[DIS] Disputed:** competing readings or insufficient evidence.
- **[L] Legend:** narrative accepted within occult lore but not established historically.
- **[MI] Modern invention/adaptation:** introduced in recent occultism, fiction, games, or online circulation.

Because exact source entries are readily available at the linked facsimiles and transcriptions, quotations below are necessarily brief; the complete contents are rendered through close diplomatic summaries.

## The name and its origins

### 1. The earliest secure form: *Furcas*

**[D]** The first presently secure appearance of the mounted, weapon-bearing spirit is Johann Weyer’s *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*, printed as an appendix to the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum* in 1577. Weyer calls him **Furcas**.

The often-repeated date 1563 is the date of the first *De praestigiis*, not a secure date for the appendix in the surviving printed form. The *Pseudomonarchia* enters Weyer’s publication history in 1577.

**[D/ED]** Peterson places Furcas at number 39 in his editorial enumeration. In the 1660 *Opera Omnia*, the section number 38 is accidentally repeated at Furcas after Pucel. This is a numbering error, not evidence that Furcas and Pucel were one spirit.

### 2. Spellings across the requested source chain

| Source | Date | Reading |
|---|---:|---|
| Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* | 1577 | **Furcas** |
| Scot, *Discoverie of Witchcraft* | 1584 | **Furcas** |
| Sloane MS 3825 | seventeenth century | **Furcas** |
| Sloane MS 2731 | seventeenth–early eighteenth-century tradition | **Furcas** |
| Harley MS 6483, Rudd material | copied c. 1712–1713 | **Furcas** |
| Mathers–Crowley, *Book of the Goetia* | 1904 | **FURCAS** |
| Peterson, *Lesser Key of Solomon* | 2001 | **Furcas** |
| Collin de Plancy, early editions | from 1818 | **Furcas** as a separate entry |
| Collin de Plancy, sixth edition | 1863 | **Forças, Forras ou Furcas**, deliberately conflated |

**[D/DIS]** The spelling **Forcas** is older than Weyer’s Furcas, but it belongs to a different textual complex. In the *Livre des esperitz*, Forcas is a great prince with thirty legions who teaches the virtues of herbs and stones, grants invisibility and eloquence, and locates treasure. Weyer preserves this figure as **Forras vel Forcas**; the *Goetia* calls him **Foras**, spirit 31.

The mounted Knight Furcas instead has twenty legions and teaches intellectual and divinatory arts. Similar spelling alone does not establish common identity.

### 3. Proposed Latin derivation

**[SR]** The most plausible linguistic comparison is Latin **furca**:

- a two-pronged fork;
- a forked prop or yoke;
- an instrument of punishment;
- a gallows-like structure;
- a fork in a road.

Latin *furcas* can be the accusative plural, “forks.” It might also have encouraged the demon-name through pseudo-Latin wordplay. No early source supplies this etymology, however, and the derivation remains conjectural.

**[DIS]** Furcas’s *telum acutum* is simply a “sharp weapon,” projectile, dart, or spear. It is not called a *furca*. Collin de Plancy’s illustrator Louis Le Breton depicts a long spear or dart, not a two-pronged pitchfork. Modern pitchfork imagery may be a visual pun on *furca*.

### 4. Claims that do not survive source checking

**[DIS/MI]** Several modern summaries claim that Furcas means “sepulchre” or “tomb” in Greco-Roman language. No satisfactory Greek or Latin derivation supports this. Latin “tomb” is *sepulcrum*; *furca* does not mean tomb. The claim appears to be a garbled modern dictionary citation.

**[MI]** The alias **Ren Vacca** circulates on derivative occult and fandom pages. It has not been located in Weyer, Scot, the *Lemegeton* manuscripts, Harley 6483, de Plancy, Lenain, Mathers, or Crowley. Until a traceable source is found, it should be treated as a modern online invention.

**[DIS]** Unlike Baal, Astaroth/Astarte, Amon/Amun, Asmoday/Ashmedai, Belial, or Phenex/Phoenix, Furcas has no securely demonstrable ancient god, hero, literary character, or place behind the name.

**[EC]** His old, bearded appearance, pale horse, rank-planet Saturn, and leaden seal later encouraged comparison with Saturn/Cronus. That is a symbolic analogy created by the correspondence system, not evidence that Furcas descends historically from Cronus.

### 5. Is there a pre-Weyer Furcas?

**[D]** No presently identified pre-1577 text securely contains the exact mounted Knight Furcas with his twenty legions and curriculum.

**[SR]** Weyer said his catalogue came from a manuscript belonging to the *Liber officiorum spirituum* family. Trithemius listed a *Liber officiorum spirituum* in his *Antipalus maleficiorum* around 1508. That establishes an older title and textual family, but not Furcas’s presence in Trithemius’s copy.

**[D/DIS]** The *Livre des esperitz*, whose sole Cambridge witness has been dated variously to the fifteenth or mid-sixteenth century, contains **Forcas**, not the mounted Furcas. Its Forcas corresponds much more closely to Weyer’s Forras/Forcas and Goetia’s Foras.

**[SR/DIS]** Jake Stratton-Kent proposes a broad family connecting Foras, Forcas, Furcas, Parcas, Forcase, Falcas, and spirits from other grimoires. His reconstruction is a practitioner’s comparative hypothesis. It draws attention to genuine overlaps, but no surviving textual stemma proves that the mounted Knight and the herb-and-treasure president were originally one figure.

## The description across the sources

## 1. Weyer, 1577

Weyer’s printed Latin appears on page 925 of the 1577 *De praestigiis*.

> **[D]** “Furcas miles est: prodit similitudine sævi hominis cum longa barba & capillitio cano.”

A close translation of the complete entry is:

**[D]** Furcas is a *miles*. He comes forth like a fierce or cruel man, with a long beard and white hair. He sits on a pale horse and carries a sharp weapon. He teaches perfectly practical philosophy, rhetoric, logic, chiromancy, astronomy, pyromancy, and their branches. Twenty legions obey him.

Weyer’s complete list is:

- *practicam philosophiam*—probably “practical philosophy,” although punctuation in some printings makes *practicam* and *philosophiam* look separate;
- rhetoric;
- logic;
- chiromancy, or divination from the hand;
- astronomy;
- pyromancy, or divination by fire;
- “their parts,” meaning the constituent branches of those disciplines.

**[D]** Weyer gives no alternate appearance, no seal, and no personal zodiacal attribution.

### *Miles*: knight or soldier?

**[D]** Latin *miles* literally means a soldier and can also mean a knight. Scot translates it as “knight,” and the *Goetia* treats Knight as a formal rank.

**[DIS]** Skinner and Rankine argue that Weyer may only have been describing Furcas as a soldier. They suggest that a formal rank of Duke might better repair the later astrological hierarchy. No principal manuscript actually calls Furcas a Duke, so that remains an editorial reconstruction.

## 2. Reginald Scot, 1584

Scot’s catalogue appears in book XV, chapter 2 of *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, page 223 in the original pagination. The translation was probably made or assisted by Abraham Fleming.

> **[D]** “Furcas is a knight and commeth foorth in the similitude of a cruell man, with a long beard…”

The remainder says that he has a hoary head, sits on a pale horse, carries a sharp weapon, and perfectly teaches practical philosophy, rhetoric, logic, astronomy, chiromancy, pyromancy, and their parts. Twenty legions obey him.

**[D]** Scot closely follows Weyer. “Hoary” means white or grey with age. Scot’s “practike philosophie” supports reading Weyer’s phrase as the single discipline “practical philosophy.”

### Weyer’s and Scot’s hours for Knights

Weyer’s general timing rubric says:

> **[D]** “Milites ab aurora usque ad ortum solis, vel à vesperis usque ad finem solis.”

Scot renders the rule:

> **[D]** “Knights from daie dawning, till sunne rising; or from evensong, till the sunne set.”

Thus Furcas’s oldest class-based operating windows are dawn to sunrise and vespers/evening to sunset.

## 3. Sloane MS 3825

Sloane 3825, folio 107v, identifies Furcas as the fiftieth spirit.

> **[D]** “The 50th spirit in order is called Furcas, he is a knight & appeareth…of a cruel old man…”

The complete entry adds:

- long beard;
- “hairy head,” almost certainly an error for **hoary**;
- pale-coloured horse;
- sharp weapon in his hand;
- philosophy, astronomy, rhetoric, logic, chiromancy, and pyromancy;
- perfect knowledge of all their parts;
- twenty legions;
- a seal or mark to be made and worn as a lamen.

### The “hairy” error

**[D/ED]** Sloane 3825’s “hairy head” is an isolated corruption. Weyer has white hair, Scot has “hoarie,” Sloane 3648 has “hory,” and Sloane 2731 has “hoary.” Peterson accordingly treats **hoary** as the intended reading.

### What the manuscript adds

**[D]** Relative to Weyer and Scot, the *Goetia*:

- makes Furcas explicitly number 50;
- adds “old”;
- presents Knight as a systematic rank;
- supplies a seal;
- directs the operator to wear it as a lamen.

It does not supply a second demanded manifestation.

## 4. Sloane MS 2731

**[D]** Sloane 2731 also reads **Furcas**, calls him the fiftieth spirit and a Knight, gives the hoary-headed mounted form, repeats the same instructional offices, and assigns twenty legions.

**[ED]** Its spellings and punctuation vary in minor ways from Sloane 3825, but it does not preserve a fundamentally different Furcas.

## 5. Harley MS 6483 and the Rudd recension

**[D]** Harley 6483, copied by Peter Smart around 1712–1713, presents Furcas at number 50 with the same Knightly rank, pale horse, weapon, sciences, and twenty legions.

**[SR]** The manuscript is associated with Thomas Rudd, but it is not an autograph. Skinner and Rankine reconstruct an earlier Rudd text, perhaps from 1655–1656, partly from its dependence on a 1655 English *Heptameron*. That dating and authorship are reasoned reconstructions.

Its important innovation is not a new bodily description but a defensive angelic apparatus: Furcas is opposed and constrained by Shem angel 50, Daniel.

## 6. Mathers–Crowley, 1904

The 1904 *Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King*, page 38, begins:

> **[D]** “The Fiftieth Spirit is Furcas. He is a Knight, and appeareth in the Form of a Cruel Old Man…”

Mathers continues with the long beard, hoary head, pale-coloured horse, and sharp weapon. His list of offices is philosophy, **astrology**, rhetoric, logic, cheiromancy, and pyromancy, perfectly and in all their parts. Furcas commands twenty legions.

**[D/ED]** Mathers changes the older **astronomy** to **astrology**. In premodern usage those fields overlapped, but the change is nevertheless textual and should be recorded.

**[D]** The rank index on page 36 lists:

> “(Seal in Lead.) KNIGHTS.—(50.) Furcas.”

Furcas is the only name in that category.

## 7. Peterson, 2001

**[ED]** Joseph H. Peterson’s *The Lesser Key of Solomon* restores a critical, manuscript-based presentation. Furcas appears on page 30 of the 2001 Weiser edition and is collated principally from Sloane 3825 against Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, and related witnesses.

Peterson’s apparatus makes three especially useful points:

- “hairy” is a corruption of **hoary**;
- the manuscripts generally retain **astronomy**, against Mathers’s astrology;
- the seals are a feature of the *Lemegeton* tradition and are not present in Weyer’s printed catalogue.

## What Furcas does not do

**[D]** The principal Furcas texts do not say that he:

- teaches languages;
- gives treasures;
- produces love;
- supplies familiars;
- grants invisibility;
- transmutes metals;
- finds lost objects;
- answers all questions.

**[DIS]** Herbs, precious stones, invisibility, eloquence, treasure, and recovery of lost objects belong chiefly to **Foras/Forras/Forcas**. When attributed to Furcas, they usually derive from de Plancy’s 1863 conflation or a derivative source.

## The requested “second form”

**[D]** No Furcas-specific source in the chain gives a second shape that the conjurer may demand.

**[T]** Generic Goetic conjurations order a spirit to assume a “fair and comely” or otherwise acceptable form. That general ritual command should not be mistaken for an independently attested second iconography of Furcas.

## The seal

### Basic design

**[D]** The Furcas seal is a vertically organized, roughly rectangular barred figure. Its recognizable skeleton consists of:

- two principal upright sides;
- approximately three crossbars or horizontal strokes;
- small circles or loops around the upper corners and upper intersections;
- cross-, hook-, or fork-like lower terminals;
- a central descending stem;
- a small triangular termination below.

The surviving hand-drawn examples are not geometrically uniform. Circles may be open or filled; lower hooks and crosspieces vary; strokes that touch in one witness may nearly touch in another. The 1904 engraving regularizes the manuscript drawing into a clean, symmetrical emblem.

### Function

**[D]** The *Goetia* instructs the operator to make and wear the seal as a **lamen**, normally a ritual plate or pendant worn on the chest.

**[D]** The seal is not the triangle of manifestation. It identifies or authorizes relation to the spirit; the ritual triangle is a separate diagram.

### Rudd’s doubled lamen

**[D/T]** Harley 6483 places the demonic seal in a defensive angelic arrangement. In Skinner and Rankine’s reading:

- the demonic face and name are directed outward;
- the corresponding angelic name and seal face the operator’s heart;
- the angel is invoked as a controlling superior;
- a psalm verse reinforces the angelic side.

**[T]** This is Rudd’s Christian-cabalistic “antidote” to the demon. It is not found in Weyer or Scot and cannot be projected backward into their catalogue.

## The angel: Daniel

## 1. The seventy-twofold name

**[D]** Exodus 14:19–21 consists, in the Hebrew Masoretic text, of three consecutive verses of seventy-two letters each. The later construction writes:

1. verse 19 in its normal right-to-left direction;
2. verse 20 in reverse direction;
3. verse 21 right-to-left again.

The resulting boustrophedon arrangement yields seventy-two vertical three-letter units.

**[T/EC]** Adding endings such as **-el** or **-yah** turns these triplets into pronounceable angelic names. That angelic roster is a development of Jewish and Christian magical exegesis; Exodus itself does not identify seventy-two angels.

### Daniel’s formation

The fiftieth unit is conventionally read **DNY**, Hebrew דני. With the divine ending **אל**, it becomes **דניאל**, Daniel or Deneyael.

**[D]** As ordinary Hebrew, *Daniel* means “God is my judge” or “my judge is God.”

**[EC]** Golden Dawn material glosses the angel as **“Merciful Judge.”** That is an occult character-title rather than a literal replacement for the Hebrew etymology.

## 2. Earlier history of the tradition

### Rabbinic terminology

**[D]** *Shem ha-meforash* originally meant the “explicit,” “distinguished,” or fully articulated divine name, often referring to the Tetragrammaton. In different periods, Jewish mystical and magical texts applied related language to divine names of 12, 42, or 72 letters.

**[SR]** Consequently, the modern phrase “the 72 angels of the Shem HaMephorash” compresses several historically distinct traditions.

### *Sefer Raziel*

**[D/SR]** The medieval composite known as *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* transmits extensive angelic, divine-name, zodiacal, and talismanic material. Its layers are older than its surviving composite form; scholarly dating generally places that compilation in the medieval Ashkenazi and Iberian world, with the first printed edition appearing in Amsterdam in 1701.

**[D]** The work belongs to the history of the seventy-twofold name.

**[DIS]** It does not establish a Furcas–Daniel pairing. That numerical counter-demon structure belongs to Rudd’s later Christian magical recension.

### Reuchlin, Vigenère, and Kircher

**[D]** Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517) helped transmit Hebrew divine-name material into Christian Cabala.

**[D]** Blaise de Vigenère’s *Traicté des chiffres* (1586) published an influential series of seventy-two angelic names and talismanic forms.

**[D/EC]** Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–1654) tabulated the seventy-two names within his encyclopedic Christian-cabalistic system and supplied languages, meanings, and correspondences.

**[SR]** Rudd’s angelic apparatus draws from this Christian-cabalistic stream, particularly the Vigenère/Kircher style of angelic sigils and names.

## 3. Daniel in Harley 6483

**[D]** In Harley 6483’s numerical scheme:

- Goetic spirit 50 is **Furcas**;
- Shem angel 50 is **Daniel**;
- Daniel’s name and angelic seal are placed on the counter-side of Furcas’s lamen;
- the angel is invoked to bind, restrict, or safely command Furcas.

The Latin verse associated with Daniel is transmitted as:

> **[D]** “Miserator et misericors Dominus, patiens et multum misericors.”

This means approximately: “The Lord is compassionate and merciful, patient and abundant in mercy.”

### The psalm-number problem

There are two related verses and two numbering systems:

| Tradition | Citation | Textual issue |
|---|---|---|
| Lenain and many Catholic/Vulgate lists | Psalm 102:8 | Vulgate numbering corresponds to Psalm 103:8 in most modern English Bibles; its usual Vulgate wording has *longanimis*. |
| Rudd transcription | Labeled Psalm 102:8 | Its word *patiens* resembles Vulgate Psalm 144:8 more closely. |
| Golden Dawn English table | Psalm 145:8 | This corresponds to Vulgate Psalm 144:8: gracious, compassionate, slow to anger, great in mercy. |

**[DIS]** The discrepancy is not merely Protestant versus Catholic numbering. The transmitted Latin wording appears to have migrated between two very similar mercy verses. A responsible citation must state both the number and wording rather than “correcting” one invisibly.

## 4. Lenain, 1823

In *La science cabalistique*, pp. 79–80, Lenain calls Daniel:

- **“Le Signe des miséricordes”**—the Sign of Mercies;
- **“Ange des confessions”**—Angel of Confessions.

**[EC]** His attributes include:

- degrees 246 through 250;
- the holy name **Pola**, assigned to the Samaritan language;
- the twenty-fifth decanal genius, **Eregbuo**;
- daily invocation time **4:20–4:40 p.m.**;
- the dates 8 May, 19 July, 29 September, 10 December, and 20 February;
- Psalm 102:8 in Vulgate numbering;
- obtaining divine mercy and consolation;
- justice, lawyers, advocates, magistrates, and wise decisions;
- business, literature, and eloquence.

Lenain’s contrary genius governs idleness, fraud, swindling, and illicit means.

### Converting Lenain’s degrees

**[EC]** Absolute degrees 245°–250° correspond to **5°–10° Sagittarius**. Lenain’s expression “246th to 250th degree” reflects inclusive ordinal counting and should not be converted mechanically to 6°–10° Sagittarius.

**[D]** Lenain does not give the later continuous guardian period 28 November–2 December. His five dates recur discontinuously around the year.

## 5. Papus, Ambelain, and Haziel

**[D/T]** Papus wrote a preface for the 1909 Dujols and Thomas reissue of Lenain. His role helped give Lenain’s system a second life in French occultism.

**[T/EC]** Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) republished and systematized much of this material. In the Ambelain stream Daniel belongs to the angelic order of Principalities and retains judicial, merciful, and confessional associations.

**[T/MI]** Late twentieth-century French angel literature, especially books published under the name Haziel, popularized each angel as a personal guardian for a continuous five-day birth interval. In that system Daniel commonly governs **28 November–2 December**, broadly expressing 5°–10° Sagittarius.

That continuous birth-date system should be identified as a modern regularization of older zodiacal material, not as a rule stated by Lenain or Rudd.

## 6. The Golden Dawn difference

The Golden Dawn’s published Shem table starts its sequence at **0° Leo**, yielding:

- angel 50: Deneyael/Daniel;
- quinance: **5°–10° Aries**;
- planetary sub-ruler: **Mars**;
- title: **Merciful Judge**;
- verse: **Psalm 145:8**.

**[EC/DIS]** This is irreconcilable with Lenain’s 5°–10° Sagittarius unless one acknowledges that they are different systems. Modern tables often combine Daniel’s Golden Dawn title, Lenain’s Sagittarius interval, Rudd’s Furcas pairing, and Haziel’s birth dates as though they came from one ancient list. They do not.

## 7. Identity cautions

**[D/DIS]** Shem angel Daniel is not automatically identical with:

- Daniel, the biblical prophet;
- the angel or Watcher Daniel/Danel in some Enochic traditions;
- every later angel named Daniel;
- an Enochian angel from John Dee’s system.

Shared spelling is insufficient evidence for continuous identity.

### The *777* Venus error

**[D/ED]** In Crowley’s table, a Venus glyph appears beside Furcas in at least one printing. The general rank scheme makes the only Knight Saturnian and assigns his seal to lead. Later editors therefore treat Venus as a typographical error.

No textual argument is supplied in *777* for changing Furcas to Venus.

### Quinance versus decan

The two must be kept separate:

```text
Daniel, Lenain-derived angelic quinance     5°–10° Sagittarius
Daniel, Golden Dawn angelic quinance        5°–10° Aries
Furcas, Crowley’s Goetic night decan       10°–20° Leo
Tarot card for that Goetic decan           Six of Wands
```

**[DIS]** Calling 10°–20° Leo “Furcas’s quinance” is inaccurate. The published *777* table gives one day demon and one night demon to the whole ten-degree decan, hence two spirits per pip card.

### Astrological timing

A historically layered reconstruction is:

1. **[D] Weyer/Scot:** use the hours assigned to Knights—dawn to sunrise or evening to sunset.
2. **[D] *Goetia* observations:** operate with the Moon two, four, six, eight, ten, twelve, or fourteen days old; Knights are called from dawn to sunrise or around 4 p.m. to sunset.
3. **[EC] Rank scheme:** Saturn and lead.
4. **[EC/T] Later synthesis:** Saturday or a planetary hour of Saturn.
5. **[EC] Crowley:** 10°–20° Leo at night.
6. **[EC] Rudd:** add Daniel’s protective lamen and invocation.
7. **[MI] Modern practitioners:** combine all of these even though no early witness gives the composite procedure.

### Enochian overlays

**[D]** Crowley appended or adapted Enochian-language conjurations to the 1904 publication. Those conjurations operate at the ritual-book level; they do not establish a unique Enochian name for Furcas.

**[D/SR]** Rudd-associated Harley 6482 connects Goetic and Dee-derived material through the *Tabula Sancta*. This documents early modern synthesis between Solomonic and Enochian magic.

**[DIS]** No primary source located here securely identifies Furcas with a specific Enochian governor, watchtower square, aethyr, or Dee angel. Such precise mappings are later overlays unless accompanied by a traceable manuscript citation.

## Iconography

## 1. The textual image

Furcas’s stable iconographic core is unusually clear:

- masculine;
- cruel or fierce;
- aged;
- long beard;
- white or hoary hair;
- pale-coloured horse;
- sharp hand-held weapon.

**[D]** Weyer does not say the horse is skeletal, demonic, or winged. He does not say Furcas is naked or armored. Those details belong to individual artists.

**[D]** The color “pale” can evoke death and the pale horse of Revelation, but neither Weyer nor the *Goetia* explicitly makes that identification.

## 2. Collin de Plancy

### The 1818 edition

**[D]** The first edition of Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* appeared in 1818. It includes Furcas as a demonological dictionary subject but predates Louis Le Breton’s famous illustrated programme.

Early de Plancy descriptions follow Weyer and Scot: an infernal Knight appearing as a furious man with a long beard and white hair, riding a large horse and carrying a sharp dart, teaching philosophy, logic, rhetoric, chiromancy, astronomy, and pyromancy, with twenty legions.

### The 1863 conflation

In the sixth edition, page 280, de Plancy begins:

> **[D]** “Forças, Forras ou Furcas, chevalier, grand président des enfers…”

He then combines attributes of both spirits:

- Knight and great President;
- vigorous old mounted man with white hair and dart;
- herbs and precious stones;
- logic, aesthetics, rhetoric, chiromancy, and pyromancy;
- invisibility, ingenuity, and eloquence;
- recovery of lost objects and treasure;
- twenty-nine legions.

The separate **Furcas** article redirects readers to Forcas.

**[D/DIS]** This is the most influential printed conflation. Neither Weyer nor the *Goetia* makes the Knight a President or gives him twenty-nine legions.

## 3. Louis Le Breton’s portrait

**[D]** Le Breton’s 1863 engraving appears under **Forcas**, not under a distinct Furcas article. It depicts:

- a muscular, nearly nude or roughly clad old man;
- streaming white hair and beard;
- a gaunt, pale horse;
- a long spear or dart held in an attacking posture;
- a barren landscape emphasizing motion and severity.

**[D/DIS]** The engraving’s rider comes from Furcas; its printed title and several powers come from Foras. It is therefore an image of de Plancy’s conflated figure, even though modern catalogues routinely label it “Furcas.”

## 4. Manuscript seals and modern art

**[D]** The seals in Sloane 3825, Sloane 2731, and Harley 6483 share the barred rectangular skeleton but differ in exact penmanship, joins, circles, and terminals. Mathers’s printed seal is a cleaned typographic rendering, not a photographic facsimile.

**[MI]** David P. Wilson produced a modern interpretive portrait for *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia*, associated with Christopher Hyatt and Lon Milo DuQuette. The work also systematizes Furcas as 10°–20° Leo by night, Six of Wands, Saturn, and lead.

**[MI]** Margrave Lucian’s twenty-first-century *Ars Goetia* project reimagines all seventy-two spirits and redraws their seals as contemporary occult art. These are artistic interpretations, not manuscript restorations.

**[MI]** Modern tattoos, metal seals, coins, and digital illustrations generally reproduce the Mathers–Crowley line drawing. Their visual uniformity can misleadingly suggest that the seal existed in that exact geometry in 1577; Weyer printed no seal.

## People and works

## Johann Weyer

**[D]** Weyer was a physician and demonological writer who opposed many witch prosecutions while continuing to accept the reality of spirits and some forms of magic. His *Pseudomonarchia* exposed what he regarded as the false monarchy of demons, but it also became the immediate printed ancestor of the *Goetia* catalogue.

For Furcas, Weyer supplies the earliest secure name, image, offices, twenty legions, and class hours.

## Reginald Scot and Abraham Fleming

**[D/SR]** Scot’s *Discoverie of Witchcraft* used Weyer’s material within an attack on witchcraft credulity and fraudulent conjuring. The English translation of Weyer’s catalogue is commonly attributed wholly or partly to Abraham Fleming.

Scot’s “knight,” “hoarie,” and “practike philosophie” materially influenced the later English *Goetia* wording.

## Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

**[D]** Harley 6483 is a fair copy made by Peter Smart. It attributes or transmits material associated with Thomas Rudd.

**[SR]** Skinner and Rankine’s reconstruction places the Rudd archetype near the end of Rudd’s life, possibly 1655–1656. The evidence is substantial but circumstantial; the surviving copy is later.

Rudd’s decisive contribution is the systematic pairing of each demon with a Shem angel and protective lamen.

## S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Aleister Crowley

**[D]** Mathers translated and edited the Goetic text from British Library manuscripts. Crowley published it in 1904 through the Society for the Propagation of Religious Truth and supplied ritual and editorial material.

Mathers regularized Furcas’s language, printed his seal in lead, and changed astronomy to astrology. Crowley’s *777* later assigned Furcas to 10°–20° Leo by night and the Six of Wands.

## Joseph H. Peterson

**[ED]** Peterson’s digital editions and 2001 print edition returned attention to the manuscripts, supplied variant readings, separated Weyer’s Latin from Scot’s English, and exposed such corruptions as “hairy” for “hoary.”

His work is indispensable for distinguishing manuscript evidence from Mathers’s editorial choices.

## Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

**[ED/SR]** *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (2007) transcribed Harley 6483 and contextualized it through Harley 6482, Sloane 3824, and Wellcome MS 3203. The volume brought the Furcas–Daniel pairing and double-sided lamens into wide modern circulation.

Their suggestion that Furcas may properly be a Duke is explicitly reconstructive; it does not replace the manuscript’s “Knight.”

## Jake Stratton-Kent

**[SR/T]** In *A Prince Among Spirits* and related work, Stratton-Kent compares Foras/Forcas/Furcas with spirits across the *Livre des esperitz*, *Liber Officiorum*, *Abramelin*, and other catalogues. His approach seeks functional families beneath unstable spellings.

It is valuable as comparative grimoire practice but goes beyond what a conventional textual stemma can prove.

## Poke Runyon

**[T]** Runyon’s modern Solomonic system combines Goetic evocation with astrological timing, planetary correspondences, and protective angelic structures. His presentation belongs to operative twentieth-century ceremonial magic, not early Furcas documentation.

## Lon Milo DuQuette and Christopher Hyatt

**[T/MI]** Their *Illustrated Goetia* popularized an accessible Thelemic and psychological-practical reading, accompanied by David P. Wilson’s art and explicit tarot/zodiac/metal attributions.

## Frater Achad

**[D-negative]** No securely sourced Furcas-specific operation or doctrine by Charles Stansfeld Jones, Frater Achad, was located. His importance to Thelemic Qabalah does not by itself establish work with Furcas.

## Owen Davies, Jan Veenstra, Julien Véronèse, and Jean-Patrice Boudet

**[SR]** Modern scholarship on grimoires, spirit catalogues, learned magic, and the *Livre des esperitz* supplies the larger context in which Furcas emerged.

No cited academic study presently establishes a specific ancient deity as Furcas’s source. Scholarly restraint here is itself an important finding.

## Order and number: Weyer’s 69 and the Goetia’s 72

**[D/ED]** Weyer’s catalogue contains 69 entries in the standard reckoning. The *Goetia* contains 72.

The transformation is:

```text
Weyer’s 69
– Pruflas/Bufas, omitted from the Goetia
+ Vassago
+ Seere
+ Dantalion
+ Andromalius
= 72
```

The four Goetic additions are:

1. Vassago, inserted as number 3;
2. Seere, number 70;
3. Dantalion, number 71;
4. Andromalius, number 72.

**[D]** Pruflas or Bufas, present in Weyer, was already omitted by Scot and does not enter the standard *Goetia*.

**[D/DIS]** Furcas moves from editorial number 39 in Weyer to number 50 in the *Goetia*. This does not mean eleven entirely new spirits were simply placed ahead of him; the catalogue was extensively reordered.

**[SR]** Peterson notes that Weyer’s source may itself have had displaced or rearranged pages. No surviving exemplar proves exactly when or why the order changed.

**[L]** Weyer’s Belial entry says that Solomon enclosed “all the spirits,” numbered seventy-two, in a vessel. That legendary total coexists with Weyer’s actual catalogue of 69. The *Goetia* appears to have brought the enumerated list into conformity with the symbolically authoritative number 72.

## Literary, film, and game reception

## Older literature

**[D-negative]** Furcas is not named in Dante’s *Divine Comedy*.

**[D-negative]** Furcas is not named in Milton’s *Paradise Lost*. Milton uses prominent biblical and classical demon names, but not the Goetic Knight.

**[D-negative]** No Furcas appears in Jonathan Stroud’s principal *Bartimaeus* novels. The similarly exotic demon names in that series are not evidence of a Furcas borrowing.

**[D-negative]** No verified canonical Furcas appearance was located in the principal *Hellboy* comics or films.

## Modern fiction

**[MI]** Wayne Barlowe’s novel *God’s Demon* includes a Lord Furcas in its infernal aristocracy. Barlowe’s character belongs to his own monumental infernal setting and should not be treated as a direct dramatization of all the Goetic offices.

**[MI]** Manga and anime derivative works have used the name, including **Furcas Rachmaninoff** in *Beelzebub*, presented as a demonic physician. This is name borrowing rather than faithful adaptation of the pale-horse Knight.

**[MI]** *Welcome to Demon School! Iruma-kun* includes Furcas as a demonic family or character name. Secondary English summaries are inconsistent about character details, so the secure claim is the onomastic borrowing, not a precise Goetic characterization.

## Games

**[MI]** In *Dungeons & Dragons*, Furcas was adapted as an infernal Duke or powerful pit fiend. Material associated with *Dragon* no. 75 and later planar lore relocates him into D&D’s hierarchy. The change from Knight to Duke is a game-world invention, although it incidentally resembles Skinner and Rankine’s unrelated reconstruction.

**[D-negative]** Despite frequent online list-making, Furcas does not have a securely verified canonical appearance in the main *Shin Megami Tensei* or *Persona* demon compendia consulted. Fan-maintained Megami Tensei lists have classified him among Goetic demons yet to appear.

**[D-negative]** No canonical Furcas character was verified in released *Helluva Boss* material as of 14 September 2026. Fan-made Ars Goetia characters should not be counted as official appearances.

**[D-negative]** No securely identified Furcas film portrayal was found. Search results often recycle de Plancy art or confuse Furcas with similarly named fictional demons.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Furcas versus Foras/Forcas

**Finding:** They are separate in Weyer and the *Goetia*.

- Furcas: Knight, pale horse, sharp weapon, sciences and divination, twenty legions.
- Forras/Forcas/Foras: President, herbs and stones, invisibility, eloquence, treasure and lost things, twenty-nine legions in the *Goetia*.

**Cause of confusion:** similar spelling, earlier Forcas traditions, and de Plancy’s explicit 1863 merger.

**Assessment:** historical relationship is possible but unproven; textual separation is documented.

### 2. Is Furcas number 38, 39, or 50?

- Duplicate 38: **printing error** in a later Weyer edition.
- 39: **modern enumeration** of Weyer’s catalogue.
- 50: **canonical Goetic number**.

The numbers belong to different editorial states.

### 3. Knight or Duke?

- Knight: every principal text.
- Soldier: possible literal sense of Latin *miles*.
- Duke: Skinner and Rankine’s astrological repair.

**Assessment:** Knight is the documented reading; Duke is a modern reconstruction.

### 4. Astronomy or astrology?

- Weyer, Scot, and the core manuscripts: astronomy.
- Mathers–Crowley and some Rudd-derived editions: astrology.

**Assessment:** astronomy is textually earlier. Historical astronomy included material modern readers would call astrological, but this does not erase the variant.

### 5. Hairy or hoary?

- Sloane 3825: hairy.
- Weyer: white hair.
- Scot, Sloane 2731, and Sloane 3648: hoary/hory.

**Assessment:** “hairy” is a copying error.

### 6. Spear, dart, sword, or pitchfork?

- Weyer: *telum acutum*, generic sharp weapon/projectile.
- Scot and *Goetia*: sharp weapon.
- De Plancy: sharp dart.
- Le Breton: long spear or dart.
- Pitchfork: later inference, perhaps from Latin *furca*.

**Assessment:** spear/dart is iconographically defensible; pitchfork is not specified.

### 7. Saturn or Venus?

- Knight rank table: Saturn and lead.
- One *777* printing: Venus glyph.
- Later editions: treat Venus as a typographical error.

**Assessment:** Saturn is structurally consistent; Venus lacks supporting explanation.

### 8. Which zodiacal degrees?

There are three separate questions:

- Furcas as Goetic night demon: 10°–20° Leo.
- Daniel in Lenain-derived astrology: 5°–10° Sagittarius.
- Daniel in the Golden Dawn Shem table: 5°–10° Aries.

**Assessment:** none should be silently substituted for another.

### 9. Which psalm?

- Lenain: Psalm 102:8 Vulgate.
- Modern English numbering of the corresponding psalm: 103:8.
- Rudd wording resembles Vulgate Psalm 144:8.
- Golden Dawn: Psalm 145:8.

**Assessment:** both numbering differences and textual contamination are present.

### 10. Did Furcas exist before Weyer?

**Finding:** not securely demonstrated.

The earlier Forcas is textually closer to Foras. The lost *Liber Officiorum* witness may have contained Furcas, but that cannot currently be checked.

### 11. Does Furcas descend from an ancient god?

**Finding:** no documented identification exists.

Saturn/Cronus is an occult analogy. Latin *furca* is a possible word association. Neither proves ancient cultic descent.

### 12. Did the original Furcas seal look exactly like the modern one?

**Finding:** no.

Weyer and Scot provide none. Manuscript seals vary, and Mathers regularized the drawing. A precise, universal “original seal” is therefore anachronistic.

## Open questions

1. **The lost source:** Did Weyer’s immediate *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* manuscript already contain the complete mounted Furcas entry?

2. **The Forcas split:** Was Furcas created by scribal division or expansion of an older Forcas complex, or are the similar names coincidental?

3. **The exact catalogue stemma:** Which intermediate manuscript explains the large reorder between Weyer and the *Goetia*?

4. **The seal’s origin:** Who first supplied Furcas’s seal, and can the variations among Sloane 2731, 3825, 3648, and Harley 6483 be placed in a secure chronological stemma?

5. **Rudd authorship:** How much of Harley 6483 derives directly from Thomas Rudd, and how much reflects Peter Smart or an intervening compiler?

6. **Daniel’s psalm:** At what stage did Psalm 102/103:8 become mixed with Psalm 144/145:8?

7. **The angelic zodiac:** Which source first moved Daniel from Lenain’s Sagittarius placement into the Golden Dawn’s Aries sequence?

8. **Continuous guardian dates:** Which exact Haziel publication first standardized Daniel as 28 November–2 December?

9. **Modern false etymologies:** Where did “sepulchre” and “Ren Vacca” first enter online Furcas descriptions?

10. **Modern media:** Some databases allege appearances in *Persona*, *Shin Megami Tensei*, *Hellboy*, *Helluva Boss*, or the *Bartimaeus* books without primary citations. Those remain negative or unresolved findings rather than confirmed appearances.
