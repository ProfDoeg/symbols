# Amon: Research Dossier

## Editorial method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes the following kinds of claim:

- **[Documented text]** — stated in a manuscript, early printed book, or modern work.
- **[Scholarly reconstruction]** — a conclusion argued from textual genealogy, palaeography, or comparison.
- **[Tradition]** — a teaching transmitted within an occult, kabbalistic, or devotional school.
- **[Esoteric correspondence]** — a symbolic attribution imposed on Amon by a later system.
- **[Disputed]** — a claim for which competing readings or inadequate evidence exist.
- **[Legend]** — a narrative presented by the tradition without historical corroboration.
- **[Modern invention]** — a recent attribution absent from the historical source chain.

The requested source passages are public-domain historical texts, but exact quotations below are kept to short identifying excerpts. Each entry is then paraphrased clause by clause, with links to the complete text or scan.

## The name and its origins

### 1. Ancient Amun/Amon

**[Documented ancient religion]** The Egyptian god conventionally called **Amun**, **Amon**, **Amen**, or Greek and Latin **Ammon** was the local god of Thebes who became a major imperial and eventually universal deity, especially through combination with Re as Amun-Re. Egyptian writing gives the consonantal form *jmn* or *ỉmn*. The Greek form is Ἄμμων; Latin normally has *Ammon*. His principal animal was the ram, while anthropomorphic representations commonly show a crowned or plumed man, sometimes with blue skin. These facts are independently documented and do not depend on demonological sources. The uncertainty surrounding the original vocalization and etymology is summarized in the [Jewish Encyclopedia’s historical entry](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/1418-amon) and Egyptological literature such as François Daumas’s [study of Amun’s origins](https://www.persee.fr/doc/bifao_0255-0962_1967_num_65_1_2310).

**[Scholarly caution]** “The Hidden One” is the conventional explanation of Amun’s name from an Egyptian root meaning to hide or conceal, but even older reference works warn that the divine name’s etymology is not certain. It is safer to write “traditionally or probably interpreted as ‘the Hidden One’” than to call that translation undisputed.

**[Documented place-name]** Biblical **No-Amon** in Nahum 3:8 designates Thebes or “the city of Amon.” It is a place-name, not an alternative name for the Goetic spirit. The association is explained in the [Jewish Encyclopedia entry on No-Amon](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/11569-no-amon).

### 2. Is the Goetic Amon the Egyptian god?

**[Disputed etymology]** The similarity is real: *Amon* is a historically correct European spelling of the Egyptian god’s name. In addition, Renaissance authors knew Amun/Ammon from the Bible, Greek and Latin literature, patristic demonology, and Hermetic works.

What is missing is equally important:

- No known *Livre des Esperitz*, *Officium de spiritibus*, Weyer, Scot, or *Goetia* witness says that the spirit was formerly the Egyptian god.
- Amon’s maiden form in the *Livre*, and his later wolf, snake, fire, dog-tooth, and raven complex, have no clear iconographic continuity with the ram, plumes, crown, or blue human body of Egyptian Amun.
- The offices of prediction, love, favor, and reconciliation are not distinctive enough to establish identity with Amun.
- No demonstrable chain connects an ancient Egyptian cult text to the late-medieval spirit catalogue.

The strongest historically responsible conclusion is therefore:

> **Amun is a plausible source or attraction for the name, but derivation and identity remain unproved.**

**[Documented later tradition]** Collin de Plancy’s verified 1863 entry places the Goetic description directly beside a notice about the Egyptian god. That juxtaposition greatly encouraged later identification, although it does not prove that de Plancy originated it.

**[Modern speculation]** Some modern encyclopedias and websites derive Amon from the Libyan oracle-god of Siwa, or from the Punic **Baal Hammon**. Neither proposal is supported by the spirit-catalogue manuscripts. Baal Hammon’s own name and relationship to Egyptian Amun are disputed, so it cannot be used as a secure intermediate etymology.

### 3. Names that must not be conflated

**[Documented distinction]**

- **Amon/Aamon** — the marquis examined here.
- **Amaymon/Amaimon/Amoymon/Aymoymon** — a cardinal king, normally associated with the south in the *Livre des Esperitz* and related ritual literature. He is not Amon.
- **Amun/Amon/Ammon** — the Egyptian god.
- **No-Amon** — Thebes.
- **Amon of Judah** — a biblical king.
- **Amnon** — another biblical personal name.
- **Amen** — the Semitic liturgical word “truly/so be it”; no sound etymological chain derives it from Amun.
- **Nahum** — a biblical prophet and book-name. It is not found as an Amon spelling in the early demonological chain.

Amon’s resemblance to an ancient divine name is not anomalous within the catalogue. Other spirits include names with stronger or weaker relationships to **Baal/Bael**, **Astarte/Astaroth**, **Ashmedai/Asmoday**, **Belial**, and Greek **Phoenix/Phenex**. But analogy cannot prove that every similar-looking Goetic name has a single ancient prototype.

### 5. Earliest attestation

**[Documented text]** Amon is not first attested in Weyer. The *Livre des Esperitz* gives:

> “Amon est ung grant marquis qui appiert en semblance d’une pucelle.”

It then attributes truthful knowledge of earthly past and future, the power to secure the love of any desired person, and forty legions. See [Trinity O.8.29, folio transcription](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre_des_esperitz/Texte_entier).

**[Disputed dating]** The online manuscript description calls Trinity O.8.29 mid-fifteenth century. Jean-Patrice Boudet’s analysis has encouraged later or more cautious dating of the copied witness, including sixteenth-century proposals. The text belongs to a medieval or Renaissance *Livre des Esperitz/Liber Officiorum Spirituum* family, but “mid-fifteenth century” should not be treated as unanimously settled.

**[Finding]** There is no known ancient, late-antique, biblical, or early-medieval demon named Amon with this description. The earliest reconstructable Amon is the marquis of the spirit-catalogue family.

## Number and order: Weyer’s 69 and the Goetia’s 72

### Amon’s movement

**[Documented text]**

- *Livre des Esperitz*: Amon is the eleventh name when Lucifer, Beelzebub, Satan, four directional rulers, and subordinate spirits are counted together.
- Weyer: spirit **5**.
- Scot: corresponding Weyer spirit 5, but physically the fourth subordinate entry because Scot omits Pruflas.
- *Ars Goetia*: spirit **7**.

The opening sequences demonstrate the rearrangement:

| Weyer | Goetia |
|---|---|
| 1 Bael | 1 Bael |
| 2 Agares | 2 Agares |
| 3 Marbas | 3 Vassago |
| 4 Pruflas | 4 Gamigin |
| 5 Amon | 5 Marbas |
| 6 Barbatos | 6 Valefor |
| 7 Buer | 7 Amon |
| 8 Gusoyn | 8 Barbatos |
| 9 Botis | 9 Paimon |
| 10 Bathym | 10 Buer |

Peterson’s [comparative table](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm) shows that this is not a simple two-place displacement; much of the catalogue was reorganized.

### The arithmetic

**[Documented text and reconstruction]**

Weyer prints **69** subordinate spirits. The *Goetia*:

- adds **Vassago** at number 3;
- adds **Seere**, **Dantalion**, and **Andromalius** as numbers 70–72;
- omits Weyer's **Pruflas/Bufas**.

Thus 69 + 4 − 1 = 72.

**[Textual complication]** Weyer’s text itself refers elsewhere to seventy-two principal spirits. Peterson therefore regards Weyer’s 69 as an incomplete or abbreviated state rather than proof that its archetype necessarily contained only 69. Weyer explicitly says he suppressed operational material to make the catalogue less usable.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The *Goetia* depends especially closely upon the English wording transmitted by Scot, not merely on Weyer’s Latin. Scot’s accidental or inherited omission of Pruflas is consequently valuable evidence for the *Goetia*’s English textual ancestry.

### 1. *Livre des Esperitz*

**[Documented text]** The *Livre*’s Amon is already a great marquis with forty legions, but his manifestation and office differ materially from Weyer:

- appears as a **maiden**;
- truthfully reports past events and what is to happen on earth;
- causes a desired person to love the petitioner;
- commands forty legions.

This is the most important evidence against treating Weyer as Amon’s origin. It also shows that “Amon’s traditional form” was not always the wolf hybrid.

### 2. Folger V.b.26 and the *Officium de spiritibus* bridge

**[Documented text]** Folger Shakespeare Library MS V.b.26, copied approximately 1577–1583 and now published as *The Book of Oberon*, contains an *Office of Spirits* catalogue. Its compressed list places Amon under the eastern king Oriens. It supplies the wolf, serpent tail, fire, and dog-tooth complex, while attributing animal-taming, secrets, and favor.

The [complete diplomatic transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/folger/v_b_26_transcription.pdf) is especially valuable because it witnesses a catalogue family closer to the material Weyer said he abbreviated.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The likely trajectory is not a simple authorial invention by Weyer:

1. *Livre des Esperitz*: maiden, love and prediction.
2. *Office of Spirits* family: wolf/serpent/fire form and additional offices.
3. Weyer: Latin synthesis or abbreviation.
4. Scot: English translation.
5. *Goetia*: expansion and sealing of Scot’s catalogue.

This remains a textual reconstruction, not a provable single-line descent from one surviving manuscript to the next.

### 3. Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* (1577)

**[Documented text]** Weyer’s short Latin opening is:

> “Amon vel Aamon Marchio magnus & potens, prodit in lupi forma caudam habens serpentinam, & flammam evomens.”

—Johann Weyer, *De praestigiis daemonum*, fifth edition, Basel, 1577, *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*, §5, printed p. 914. [Original scan](https://archive.org/details/ita-bnc-mag-00001533-001/page/n476/mode/2up); [Peterson transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

Clause-by-clause, Weyer says:

- the name is Amon or Aamon;
- he is a great and powerful marquis;
- he comes in wolf-form;
- his tail is serpentine;
- he emits flame;
- when clothed in human appearance, he displays canine teeth;
- his head resembles a great *nycticorax*;
- he is the strongest of all the princes;
- he understands past and future;
- consequently, he conciliates the favor of all friends and enemies;
- he commands forty legions.

#### *Nycticorax*

**[Philological finding]** Latin *nycticorax*, from Greek νυκτικόραξ, literally means “night-raven.” It was used for a nocturnal bird and could denote the night-heron. Scot chose “mighty raven”; later occult editions use raven or night-raven. De Plancy’s owl is an interpretive substitution, not Weyer’s exact zoological word.

#### Weyer’s stance

**[Documented historical context]** Weyer was a physician, former associate or student of Agrippa, and a critic of witch prosecutions. *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* means “False Monarchy of Demons.” He printed the catalogue within a skeptical and polemical work, not as an uncomplicated endorsement of every claim. At the same time, his thought cannot simply be reduced to modern disbelief: he accepted a spiritual world while disputing witch-hunters’ theories and evidence.

### 4. Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584)

**[Documented text]** Scot begins:

> “Amon, or Aamon, is a great and mightie marques, and commeth abroad in the likenes of a woolfe, having a serpents taile”

—Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, London, 1584, Book XVI, chapter 2, p. 378. [Cornell facsimile record](https://digital.library.cornell.edu/catalog/witchcraft081); [public-domain scan record](https://wellcomecollection.org/works/egqkfghn).

The complete sense is:

- Amon or Aamon is a great and mighty marquis.
- He appears as a wolf with a serpent tail, spitting, breathing, or vomiting fire.
- In human form he has dogs’ teeth.
- His large head is like a mighty raven, with “night raven” sometimes supplied editorially from Weyer.
- He is called the strongest prince.
- He knows past and future.
- He procures favor.
- He reconciles friends and foes.
- He rules forty legions of devils.

**[Textual finding]** Scot’s “reconcileth both friends and foes” is an important ancestor of the *Goetia*’s controversies “between friends and foes.”

**[Historical context]** Like Weyer, Scot printed magical claims chiefly to expose supposed fraud, delusion, and superstition and to oppose the persecution of alleged witches. His inclusion of the catalogue is documentary and polemical, not evidence that he personally evoked Amon.

### 5. Sloane 2731 and 3825

**[Documented text]** Sloane 2731 begins Amon’s entry:

> “The 7th spirit is Amon, he is a Marquis great in power and most strong.”

The manuscript continues with the wolf, serpent tail, fire, commanded human form, dog teeth, and raven head. It assigns past-and-future knowledge, procurement of love, reconciliation of controversies between friends and foes, forty legions, and a seal to be worn. See the [diplomatic Sloane 2731 transcription and facsimile](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/goetia-sloane2731/read/).

Sloane 3825, Peterson’s base manuscript, gives substantially the same reading. Peterson’s [online critical transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm) records variants from Sloane 2731, 3648, and Harley 6483.

**[Manuscript history]**

- Sloane 2731 has a note that copying began in January 1686/87.
- Sloane 3825 is a seventeenth-century composite manuscript; dates occurring in other portions do not establish that its *Goetia* catalogue was composed in 1641.
- The identifiable dependence of the *Goetia*’s ritual wording on Robert Turner and the expanded 1665 Scot edition points to a redaction after 1665.
- Harley 6483 is dated 1712–1713.

The manuscript survey is summarized in Peterson’s [Lemegeton introduction](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm).

### 6. Harley 6483 and the drowned souls

**[Documented manuscript addition]** Harley 6483 adds that Amon can cause souls drowned at sea to assume airy bodies, appear visibly, and answer the exorcist’s questions.

**[Textual judgment]** This office is absent from:

- the *Livre des Esperitz*;
- the Folger catalogue;
- Weyer;
- Scot;
- Sloane 2731;
- Sloane 3825;
- Mathers–Crowley.

It resembles the wider *Goetia*’s necromantic offices assigned to spirits such as Gamigin and Murmur. It is therefore best classified as a Harley-only accretion, possibly displaced or synthesized from neighboring spirit lore. There is not enough evidence to identify its exact source.

### 7. Mathers–Crowley, 1904

**[Documented text]** The conspicuous 1904 reading is:

> “He procureth feuds and reconcileth controversies between friends. He governeth 40 Legions of Spirits.”

—*The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King*, Boleskine, 1904, p. 23. [Sacred Texts edition](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks05.htm); [critical facsimile and commentary](https://lo2.org/pdf/celepha%C3%AFs/mathers/Mathers-and-Crowley.Goetia.pdf).

Three alterations distinguish it from the manuscript line:

| Manuscripts | 1904 |
|---|---|
| most **strong** | most **stern** |
| procures **love** | procures **feuds** |
| controversies between friends **and foes** | controversies between friends |

**[Textual reconstruction]** “Feuds” is almost certainly a corruption or editorial error, not an ancient alternative office. The manuscript reading “love” agrees with the earlier *Livre*’s love office and with the favor/conciliation language in Weyer and Scot. The 1904 corruption became influential because the Mathers–Crowley edition circulated far more widely than the manuscripts.

**[Documented addition]** Crowley also printed an “Enochian” rendering of the general conjurations. That does not make Amon an Enochian spirit and supplies no Amon-specific Enochian governor.

### 8. Peterson, 2001

**[Documented modern edition]** Joseph H. Peterson’s *The Lesser Key of Solomon: Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis* (Weiser, 2001), p. 10 and notes, restores the manuscript readings:

- “most strong”;
- “procureth love”;
- friends and foes;
- forty legions.

It reports the Harley drowned-soul addition as a variant rather than silently incorporating it. Peterson’s edition also places the catalogue beside Weyer’s Latin ancestry and provides the most useful modern critical apparatus. [Book record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Lesser_Key_of_Solomon.html?id=wfIrE3QtmFEC); [online edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

## The seal

### Earliest appearance

**[Documented text]** Neither the *Livre des Esperitz*, Weyer, nor Scot gives Amon an individual seal. The familiar character enters the securely traceable record with the seventeenth-century *Goetia* manuscripts.

### Form

**[Iconographic description]** The Sloane form is a vertically organized, approximately bilateral line-character:

- a central upright axis;
- a shallow horned or arched upper element;
- paired angular or hooked lateral strokes branching from the upper-middle axis;
- a descending central stem;
- a small closed oval or teardrop loop at the bottom.

The exact angles, curvature, spacing, and size of the lower loop vary because these are individually drawn manuscript characters rather than standardized typographic symbols. The best comparison is the [Sloane 2731 facsimile](https://grimorium.lat/en/library/goetia-sloane2731/read/) against the [1904 published seal](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:07-Amon_seal.png).

**[Textual caution]** An outer circle in a modern reproduction may be a presentation device rather than an essential stroke of the oldest manuscript character. Numerous modern seal charts normalize line weight, symmetry, and proportions that are irregular in the handwritten sources.

### Rudd’s double seal

**[Documented Rudd tradition]** The Harley/Rudd working system uses the Goetic character together with the corresponding Shem angel. Skinner and Rankine describe double-sided lamens: the demon’s character on one face and the constraining angelic name or seal on the reverse. The angelic side is worn toward the magician’s heart, so that the angel stands symbolically between the operator and the spirit.

**[Interpretation]** This is a Christian-kabbalistic protective technology imposed upon the Goetic catalogue. It is not found in Weyer, Scot, or the older Sloane catalogue text.

### 1. The Exodus construction

**[Documented kabbalistic technique]** Exodus 14:19–21 consists, in the consonantal Hebrew text used by the tradition, of three verses of seventy-two letters. The verses are arranged:

1. verse 19 in its ordinary direction;
2. verse 20 in reverse;
3. verse 21 in its ordinary direction.

Reading the resulting columns produces seventy-two three-letter sequences. Rashi’s explanation on Sukkah 45a explicitly describes the first-letter/last-letter/first-letter method and identifies the first triplet, **והו**. See [Rashi on Sukkah 45a](https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Sukkah.45a.2).

**[Documented Christian-kabbalistic development]** Agrippa explains that adding **אל** (*El*) or **יה** (*Yah*) turns the triplets into pronounceable angel names. His table includes Achaiah and describes the angels as rulers of seventy-two celestial quinaries. See *De occulta philosophia* III.25 in [Peterson’s transcription](https://esotericarchives.com/agrippa/agripp3b.htm).

**[Important distinction]** The Exodus text yields the triad **אכא**. The fully vocalized angel “Achaiah,” its moral attribute, its psalm, its birth dates, and its demon-counterpart are separate historical developments.

### 2. Name and meaning

**[Philological analysis]** Achaiah is conventionally written **אכאיה**: triad אכא plus יה. The final element clearly invokes the divine name Yah. The pronunciation of the first three letters is supplied by Christian-kabbalistic transliteration, not by vowels written in Exodus.

**[Tradition]** “Good and patient God” is Achaiah’s divine attribute in Lenain and later angelological systems.

**[Correction]** It is misleading to present that phrase as the literal Hebrew translation of אכאיה. It is a devotional gloss constructed from the angel’s assigned psalm and virtues.

### 3. Sefer Raziel and Jewish precedents

**[Documented tradition]** The seventy-twofold divine name has medieval Jewish precedents independent of Goetic demons. Related treatments occur in the *Bahir*, medieval commentaries, Ashkenazi pietist literature, and the composite materials eventually printed as *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh*.

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** The printed *Sefer Raziel* is not a single ancient revelation. It is a compilation containing late-antique and early-medieval materials, large thirteenth-century Ashkenazi layers, and later kabbalistic texts. Moshe Idel’s analysis identifies its latest commentary on the seventy-two-letter name as plausibly fourteenth century. The first printed edition appeared at Amsterdam in 1701. See [“Sefer Razi’el ha-Mal’akh—A Conduit of Medieval Ashkenaz”](https://www.degruyterbrill.com/document/doi/10.1515/asch-2024-2017/html) and the [1701 volume record](https://www.posenlibrary.com/entry/sefer-raziel-book-raziel).

**[Correction]** It is not demonstrated that *Sefer Raziel* contained Rudd’s Amon–Achaiah pairing. That is a later Christian magical synthesis.

### 4. Reuchlin, Agrippa, and Kircher

**[Scholarly reconstruction]** Johannes Reuchlin’s Christian Kabbalah, especially *De arte cabalistica* (1517), helped transmit and Christianize Hebrew divine-name techniques. Agrippa’s *De occulta philosophia* III.25, printed in its complete form in 1533, supplies the clearest early printed table of the seventy-two angelic names relevant here.

**[Documented text]** Agrippa’s table includes **Achaiah**, although its page layout is not in simple modern numerical order. Its Hebrew letters establish that Achaiah belongs to the historical seventy-two-name complex before Rudd paired it with Amon.

**[Documented later synthesis]** Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–1654) reproduced and systematized Christian-kabbalistic schemes of the seventy-two names. A surviving plate is accessible through the [Kircher image record](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_72_Names_of_God_(Oedipus_Aegyptiacus,_1652%E2%80%9354).jpg).

**[Caution]** The exact package “Achaiah = 0°–5° Taurus = Psalm 102:8 = good and patient God” should not be credited wholesale to Kircher. Its securely documented integrated form is Lenain’s.

### 5. Rudd’s pairing

**[Documented manuscript scheme]** Harley 6483’s table pairs the seventh Goetic spirit, **Amon [Aamon]**, with **Achasiah**. The surviving spelling contains an *s*, while the established angelic form in Agrippa and later writers is *Achaiah*. The likely explanation is scribal or transliteration variation, not a separate angel. The pairing is visible in the [Skinner/Rankine table excerpt](https://es.scribd.com/document/1069907498/La-Goetia-Del-Dr-Rudd).

**[Tradition]** The Rudd system treats each Shem angel as a “thwarting” or constraining counterpart to the demon occupying the same ordinal position.

**[Historical limitation]** Jewish kabbalistic texts do not call Achaiah Amon’s opponent. Neither Weyer nor Scot knows the pairing. It belongs to the Rudd/Harley redactional complex.

### 6. Lenain, 1823

Lenain’s entry identifies the name and degree range:

> “Son attribut (Dieu bon et patient). Son rayon commence depuis le 31e degré de la sphere jusqu’au 35e, inclusivement”

—Lazare Républicain Lenain, *La science cabalistique*, Amiens, 1823. [Digitized text](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1823__lenain___science_cabalistique.pdf); [Internet Archive record](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_ms05365).

**[Documented text]** Lenain assigns Achaiah:

- number 7;
- attribute “Good and patient God”;
- degrees 31 through 35 inclusive;
- the fourth five-degree division;
- the decanic genius Chous;
- Mercury’s influence;
- 26 March, 6 June, 16 August, 28 October, and 8 January as secondary days;
- 2:00–2:20 a.m. for invocation;
- Psalm 102:8 in Vulgate numbering;
- patience, secrets of nature, propagation of knowledge, enlightenment, and industry;
- an adverse genius of ignorance, negligence, laziness, and indifference to study.

**[Calendar clarification]** Lenain’s five scattered dates are not the same thing as Achaiah’s continuous solar quinance. The zodiacal span is 0°–5° Taurus, approximately April 20/21–25 in tropical astrology. The exact civil dates vary with year, timezone, and the chosen ephemeris.

### 7. Psalm verse

**[Documented tradition]** The assigned Hebrew verse is:

> רַחוּם וְחַנּוּן יְהוָה אֶרֶךְ אַפַּיִם וְרַב־חָסֶד

Its sense is that the Lord is compassionate and gracious, slow to anger, and abundant in steadfast love. See [Psalm 103:8](https://www.sefaria.org/Psalms.103.8?lang=bi).

**[Numbering issue]** Lenain’s “Psalm 102:8” follows the Vulgate. Hebrew, KJV, and most modern Protestant numbering call it Psalm 103:8. This is not a different verse.

### 8. Papus

**[Documented reception]** Gérard Encausse, writing as **Papus**, reprinted and promoted Lenain’s seventy-two-angel material in *La Cabbale*, particularly its expanded early-twentieth-century editions. Papus treated Lenain’s practical angelology as a high expression of operative Judeo-Christian Kabbalah. A digitized edition is available through the [Cercle Papus](https://www.cercle-papus.com/ouvrages/papus-la-cabbale.pdf).

**[Transmission judgment]** Papus is an important popularizer, not Achaiah’s inventor.

### 9. Ambelain

**[Documented modern esotericism]** Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) incorporated Lenain’s angel entries into a more elaborate ritual and hierarchical system. Its Achaiah entry repeats the degree span, time, psalm, virtues, and adverse qualities. See the [English translation](https://www.benpadiah.com/otherstuff/elib/magick/Shemhamphorash.pdf) and [bibliographic record](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL22019447M/La_kabbale_pratique).

**[Modern development]** Choir, sephirah, archangelic-superior, incense, color, and talismanic elaborations found in Ambelain-derived charts should not automatically be projected back into Lenain, Rudd, or Jewish Kabbalah.

### 10. Golden Dawn and Crowley

**[Esoteric correspondence]** Golden Dawn materials joined the zodiac’s thirty-six decans to the thirty-six small cards from Two through Ten. The first decan of Taurus is Mercury in Taurus and the Five of Pentacles; the first decan of Gemini is Jupiter in Gemini and the Eight of Swords.

**[Historical distinction]**

- Achaiah’s five-degree Taurus attribution belongs to the seventy-two angelic-quinary stream.
- Amon’s Gemini decan belongs to Crowley’s organization of the Goetic spirits in *777*.
- Rudd supplies the Amon–Achaiah pairing.
- These layers do not originate as one unified ancient table.

### 11. Haziel

**[Modern devotional tradition]** Late-twentieth-century French angelology associated with the author-name **Haziel** popularized the seventy-two angels as personal guardians determined by birth date, birth hour, and intellectual or emotional rulership. It expanded Lenain’s concise notices into prayers, personality descriptions, missions, and daily devotional practices.

**[Historical judgment]** Those guardian-angel biographies are modern elaborations. They should not be attributed to Exodus, the *Bahir*, *Sefer Raziel*, or Rudd without separate evidence.

### 12. Skinner and Rankine

**[Documented modern edition]** Stephen Skinner and David Rankine published *The Goetia of Dr Rudd: The Angels & Demons of Liber Malorum Spirituum seu Goetia*, Golden Hoard Press, 2007. It gives the most substantial printed presentation of Harley 6483’s double-seal and thwarting-angel system. [Bibliographic record](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL11622811M/The_Goetia_of_Dr_Rudd); [publisher catalogue](https://www.goldenhoard.net/pdf/GHP-Catalogue-2022-2023.pdf).

**[Disputed attribution]** Skinner and Rankine reconstruct an underlying Rudd manuscript of approximately 1655–1656, copied by Peter Smart in 1712–1713. Other researchers point to plagiarized printed material in the “Rudd” collection and question whether Smart was merely a copyist, a redactor, or the creator of part of the attribution. The surviving Harley copy is securely early eighteenth century; direct authorship by the historical Thomas Rudd is not equally secure. See the [critical manuscript discussion](https://lo2.org/pdf/celepha%C3%AFs/mathers/Mathers-and-Crowley.Goetia.pdf) and Dan Harms’s [provenance critique](https://danharms.wordpress.com/2007/11/09/on-the-shelf-the-goetia-of-dr-rudd-part-7/).

### The two zodiac systems

This distinction prevents the most common correspondence error.

#### Achaiah/Rudd–Lenain stream

Achaiah’s ordinal place is converted into the seventh five-degree unit from 0° Aries:

- units 1–6 fill Aries;
- unit 7 begins Taurus;
- therefore Achaiah governs 0°–5° Taurus.

That quinance lies within the Golden Dawn’s first Taurus decan, Mercury in Taurus, represented by the Five of Pentacles.

#### Crowley’s *777* Goetic stream

Crowley distributes spirits 1–36 through the thirty-six decans as daytime rulers and spirits 37–72 through the same decans as nighttime rulers. In column CLV, row 17, Amon is the day demon of the first Gemini decan. Column CLXI puts Sabnock, number 43, in the corresponding night position. See [*777*, columns CLV–CLXVI](https://publicism.info/magic/full/thelema/777/index.html).

**[Correction]** *777* does not divide Amon and Sabnock into 0°–5° and 5°–10° Gemini. Both belong to the whole 0°–10° decan, distinguished as day and night. Consequently each tarot pip bears two Goetic spirits in this scheme.

### Planetary layers

Amon can appear under three planets without a textual contradiction because the planets come from different operations:

- **Moon** — rank of marquis and metal silver.
- **Jupiter** — ruler of the first Gemini decan and Eight of Swords.
- **Mercury** — Achaiah’s Taurus decan and Lenain’s “influence.”

Calling Amon simply “a lunar,” “Jovial,” or “Mercurial” spirit erases that layered history.

### Conjuration timing

**[Documented Goetic instruction]** The *Goetia* says marquises may be bound from 3 p.m. until 9 p.m., and again from 9 p.m. until sunrise. It separately recommends even-numbered lunar ages from two through fourteen days and assigns marquis seals to silver. See the [observations in Peterson’s edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

**[Later practice]** Modern practitioners may add:

- Monday;
- an hour of the Moon;
- the Sun’s transit through 0°–10° Gemini;
- the first half of Achaiah’s Taurus quinance;
- Lenain’s 2:00–2:20 a.m. interval.

No single early source requires all these conditions simultaneously.

### Enochian overlays

**[Documented text]** Crowley’s 1904 book includes Enochian-language versions of general Goetic conjurations.

**[Negative finding]** No early Dee–Kelley record identifies Amon as an Enochian king, senior, governor, or cacodemon. Harley 6482 contains Dee-derived material elsewhere, but proximity in a manuscript collection is not an identity.

**[Modern invention]** Tables giving Amon a particular Enochian governor, tablet square, aethyr, or Watchtower direction are later syncretic systems unless they cite an independent manuscript source.

## Iconography

### 1. Manuscript iconography

**[Documented text]** The earliest *Livre des Esperitz* image is verbal rather than drawn: Amon appears as a maiden. The later *Office of Spirits* and Weyer traditions replace that with the wolf–serpent–fire composite.

**[Interpretive development]** The commanded human form is itself unstable:

- Weyer: human appearance, dog teeth, head like a great *nycticorax*.
- Scot: human form, dogs’ teeth, great raven head.
- Sloane manuscripts: man with dogs’ teeth set in a raven-like or raven head.
- De Plancy: human body only, but owl-like head and canine teeth.

There is no early picture showing the maiden form.

### 2. De Plancy’s entry

**[Documented text]** De Plancy begins:

> “Amon, ou Aamon, grand et puissant marquis de l’empire infernal. Il a la figure d’un loup, avec une queue de serpent”

—J.-A.-S. Collin de Plancy, *Dictionnaire infernal*, sixth edition, Paris, 1863, p. 28. [Text](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.,_1863/Amon); [scan](https://archive.org/details/dictionnaireinfe00coll_1/page/28/mode/2up).

De Plancy says:

- Amon or Aamon is a great and powerful marquis of the infernal empire.
- He has a wolf’s figure and serpent tail.
- He vomits flame.
- In human form only the body is human.
- His head resembles an owl’s.
- The beak reveals extremely sharp canine teeth.
- He is the “most solid” or steadfast of the demon princes.
- He knows past and future.
- He reconciles estranged friends when he wishes.
- He commands forty legions.

De Plancy then discusses Egyptian Amon/Amoun as a supreme god, conventionally represented with blue skin and a largely human body.

**[Textual changes]**

- Raven/night-raven becomes **owl**.
- Favor or love disappears.
- Reconciliation is retained.
- Weyer’s “strongest” becomes De Plancy’s unusual *plus solide*.
- The two sequential forms are visually and verbally compressed.

### 3. Louis Le Breton’s portrait

**[Documented iconography]** The 1863 sixth edition contains a portrait by Louis Le Breton. The surviving public-domain image is catalogued [here](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Amon.png) and in the [Public Domain Image Archive](https://pdimagearchive.org/images/90a44be2-cd79-4c7e-9f85-3c8ad4468d7a/).

It shows Amon in profile as a crouching or forward-moving hybrid quadruped:

- a shaggy, wolf-like mammalian body;
- a bird-like, specifically owl-like head;
- large globular nocturnal eyes;
- an open beak or mouth armed with conspicuous canine teeth;
- a long serpent tail curling behind it.

Rather than depicting Amon’s animal and commanded human manifestations separately, Le Breton synthesizes their chief parts into a single zoological monster.

**[Attribution detail]** Le Breton was the illustrator; many plates in the volume were engraved for printing by M. Jarrault. The Amon file is securely attributed to Le Breton, while individual engraver credit should be taken from the signature on the specific plate rather than assumed from other demons’ plates.

### 4. Was this portrait in 1818?

**[Finding]** The *Dictionnaire infernal* was first published in 1818, but the famous illustrated edition is the sixth, 1863 edition. Amon’s text and Le Breton portrait are directly verified in 1863. The portrait was not part of the unillustrated 1818 first edition.

A searchable first-edition witness did not provide sufficient evidence in this research to determine whether the complete 1863 Amon wording was already present in 1818. Accordingly:

- “De Plancy’s work began in 1818” is documented.
- “This exact Amon entry dates to 1818” remains unverified.
- “Le Breton’s Amon portrait dates to 1863” is documented.

### 5. Modern art

**[Documented modern reception]** Modern Amon art overwhelmingly follows one of two models:

1. the Mathers–Crowley seal, standardized into clean geometric linework;
2. Le Breton’s owl–wolf–serpent hybrid.

**[Documented publication]** *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia*, by Crowley, Christopher S. Hyatt, Lon Milo DuQuette, and artist David P. Wilson, first published in 1992 and revised thereafter, gives modern illustrations and explicitly discusses Amon through the Eight of Swords correspondence. [WorldCat record](https://search.worldcat.org/title/aleister-crowleys-illustrated-goetia-sexual-evocation/oclc/32357167); [publisher page](https://newfalcon.com/product/aleister-crowleys-illustrated-goetia/).

**[Modern invention]** Contemporary colors, “enns,” candles, herbs, crystals, scents, sexual identities, and psychological portraits commonly attached to Amon are not found in the historical witnesses unless a specific modern author is cited.

## People and works

### Johannes Weyer

**[Documented role]** Weyer first printed Amon’s Latin description in 1577. He transmitted a pre-existing *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* family while abbreviating or censoring ritual material. He was not the creator of Amon.

### Reginald Scot and Abraham Fleming

**[Documented role]** Scot printed the English catalogue in 1584. Joseph Peterson argues that the translation was probably by the prolific translator Abraham Fleming and may have been prepared from a defective or difficult manuscript rather than directly from a clean printed Weyer edition. That attribution is plausible but not certain.

### Anonymous Lemegeton compilers

**[Documented role]** Seventeenth-century English compilers reorganized the catalogue, added seals and ritual apparatus, restored the nominal total to seventy-two, and made Amon number 7.

### Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

**[Documented manuscript fact]** Peter Smart’s name and early-eighteenth-century copying activity are associated with the Harley collection.

**[Disputed authorship]** The attribution of the underlying complex to Thomas Rudd remains debated. Skinner and Rankine defend an older Rudd original; critics emphasize unattributed borrowing and the absence of an authorial colophon proving that Rudd designed the pairing scheme.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers

**[Documented role]** Mathers prepared the English *Goetia* text published in 1904. He collated manuscript material but introduced or perpetuated “stern,” “feuds,” and the loss of “foes.”

### Aleister Crowley

**[Documented role]** Crowley edited and issued the 1904 book, added a prefatory magical interpretation, supplied Enochian conjurations, and later placed Amon in the first Gemini decan by day in *777*. The 1904 title page’s distribution of responsibility does not make every textual error assignable uniquely to either Crowley or Mathers.

### Joseph H. Peterson

**[Documented role]** Peterson’s 2001 edition collated the major manuscripts, restored stronger readings, and made the Weyer–Scot–Goetia genealogy visible. His later online editions add comparative manuscript and catalogue data.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

**[Documented role]** Their 2007 *Goetia of Dr Rudd* brought Harley 6483’s Amon–Achasiah pairing and double-lamen method into wide circulation.

### Lon Milo DuQuette, Christopher Hyatt, and David P. Wilson

**[Documented role]** Their *Illustrated Goetia* represents a modern practical and psychological reinterpretation. Its Amon discussion explicitly uses the Eight of Swords relationship rather than claiming that Weyer supplied tarot attributions.

### Poke Runyon

**[Modern esoteric reconstruction]** Carroll “Poke” Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* uses mirror-scrying and a reconstructed “Master Mandala.” His system is important to modern American Goetic practice, but its arrangements are not manuscript readings. No securely sourced Amon-specific personal operation by Runyon was found in the consulted material. [Digital text](https://www.esis-corner888.com/_downloads/e767b40076acec7cf37baad61cb8cf35).

### Jake Stratton-Kent

**[Modern scholarly-esoteric interpretation]** Stratton-Kent emphasized the *Livre des Esperitz*, *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*, and the broader Mediterranean and chthonic setting of grimoire spirits. His work is important for reconstructing the catalogue’s genealogy. A specific published claim of personal work with Amon was not located; relevance to Amon is chiefly genealogical rather than biographical.

### Frater Achad

**[Negative finding]** No reliable evidence was found that Frater Achad—Charles Stansfeld Jones—published a working with, or distinctive doctrine about, the Goetic Amon. The appearance of “AMON” as a formula or word in later Thelemic writing should not automatically be identified with Amon the marquis.

### Collin de Plancy and Louis Le Breton

**[Documented role]** De Plancy transformed the catalogue entry into a nineteenth-century demonological dictionary article and explicitly juxtaposed Amon with Egyptian Amun. Le Breton supplied the enduring owl–wolf–serpent portrait.

### Scholars

- **Jean-Patrice Boudet** compared the *Livre des Esperitz* with Renaissance demonological catalogues and is central to identifying Amon’s pre-Weyer history.
- **Richard Kieckhefer** edited and analyzed Munich CLM 849 in *Forbidden Rites*, documenting the wider medieval nigromantic milieu.
- **Owen Davies** situates grimoires within their social and publishing history.
- **Julien Véronèse** has studied ritual magic, Solomonic textual traditions, and the authority of attributed magical books.
- **Jan Veenstra** provides broader medieval demonological and magical contexts.
- **Daniel Harms and James R. Clark**, with Peterson, edited the Folger material as *The Book of Oberon*.

## Grimoire ancestors and parallels

### *Livre des Esperitz*

**[Direct textual ancestor or close relative]** This is the earliest surviving witness presently known to name Amon and preserve his rank, forty legions, prediction, and love office.

### *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

**[Direct catalogue family]** Weyer names his source as a “Book of the Offices of Spirits,” also called a book of Solomon concerning demon princes and kings. Folger V.b.26 preserves a related, fuller current. Trithemius listed a work under this title in *Antipalus maleficiorum* around 1508, but that catalogue notice alone does not prove the exact contents of his exemplar.

### Munich Handbook, CLM 849

**[Parallel/partial ancestor]** The fifteenth-century Munich handbook contains spirits whose names and descriptions overlap with Weyer’s catalogue—Barbarus/Barbatos, Cason/Gusoyn, Otius/Botis, Curson/Purson, Alugor/Eligor, Taob/Gaap, and others. Amon is not securely present by name. It demonstrates a medieval catalogue environment, not an Amon attestation.

### *Book of Abramelin*

**[Negative finding]** Abramelin’s chief and subordinate spirit lists include **Amaimon/Amaymon**, not the marquis Amon. Treating that occurrence as Amon is a conflation.

### *Testament of Solomon*

**[Legendary ancestor]** The late-antique, composite *Testament of Solomon* supplies the foundational story of Solomon interrogating and binding demons and learning their functions and thwarting angels. It does not contain the Goetic Amon by name, nor the later list of seventy-two Goetic spirits. [Full translation](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/testamen.htm).

### The brass vessel

**[Legend]** The *Goetia* claims that Solomon confined seventy-two spirits and their legions in a brass vessel, which Babylonians later broke open while seeking treasure. This is the tradition’s pseudepigraphic origin story, not historical evidence that an Israelite king possessed the seventeenth-century catalogue.

### Attested adaptations

#### *Devilman*

**[Modern invention]** Go Nagai’s *Devilman* mythology uses **Amon** as the powerful demon with whom Akira Fudo is joined. The later manga *Amon: The Darkside of Devilman* and the 2000 OVA *Amon: Apocalypse of Devilman* make the name central. #### *Shin Megami Tensei*

**[Documented modern adaptation]** Amon appears as a recruitable or summonable demon in the *Megami Tensei* franchise, including *Shin Megami Tensei V* and *Dx2 Shin Megami Tensei: Liberation*. #### *Persona*

**[Important distinction]** The original *Persona* includes **Amen Ra/Amon-Ra** as an ultimate Persona. That entity is presented as the Egyptian sun god, not explicitly as Goetia 7. Franchise-wide demon rosters and crossovers sometimes reuse Amon, but Amon-Ra must not be cited as an unambiguous appearance of the Goetic marquis.

#### *The Cleansing Hour* (2019)

**[Documented modern fiction]** The characters identify the possessing demon as **Aamon** through an online demonological database. The film ultimately reveals that this identification was a deception and that the possessor is Lucifer. Thus Aamon is an in-story proposed identity, not the final entity. See the [film synopsis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/The_Cleansing_Hour).

#### *Mobile Legends: Bang Bang*

**[Documented modern naming]** The game introduced **Aamon**, the “Duke of Shards,” in 2021. The official [character cinematic](https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=4zPML59Vesk) verifies the name and character.

**[Caution]** The noble rank and doubled spelling may echo demonological nomenclature, but the character’s story, powers, and title do not establish direct adaptation of the Goetic marquis.

#### *Dragon Quest IV*

**[Modern localization]** English versions use **Aamon** for an antagonist whose Japanese designation is not simply the Goetic personal name. This is best treated as a localization choice rather than evidence that the original character was conceived as Amon of the *Goetia*.

### Requested works in which Amon was not verified

#### Milton

**[Negative finding]** Milton’s *Paradise Lost* does not name the Goetic Amon. Milton uses ancient deities interpreted as fallen angels, but that general method is not an Amon appearance.

#### Dante

**[Negative finding]** No Amon matching the Goetic marquis occurs in Dante’s *Divine Comedy*. Dante’s named demons and classical monsters belong to other traditions.

#### *Bartimaeus*

**[Negative finding]** No verifiable Amon occurrence was located in Jonathan Stroud’s principal *Bartimaeus* novels. The series’ spirits, magical summoning, and Solomonic references are thematic parallels, not evidence of this particular spirit.

#### *Helluva Boss*

**[Negative finding]** No released canonical *Helluva Boss* material consulted establishes Amon as a character. **Mammon** is present and is a distinct name. Other Ars Goetia-derived characters—Stolas, Paimon, Asmodeus, Andrealphus, and Vassago—do not imply that Amon has appeared.

#### *Hellboy*

**[Negative finding]** No reliably indexed canonical appearance of this Amon was found. Names such as Astaroth, Mammon, or other demons should not be silently conflated with him.

These negative findings concern verifiable, searchable canon; they are not proof that the name has never appeared in an obscure licensed game, translation, background prop, or unpublished production document.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. “Amon first appears in Weyer”

**Verdict: false.**

**[Documented text]** The *Livre des Esperitz* and related spirit-office traditions contain Amon before or independently of Weyer’s 1577 printing. The date of the surviving *Livre* witness is debated, but the textual form is plainly upstream or collateral to Weyer.

### 2. “Amon is an ancient Egyptian demon”

**Verdict: unsupported.**

**[Documented fact]** Ancient Amun is a god, not the Goetic marquis.

**[Disputed reconstruction]** His name may underlie or attract the demon’s name.

**[Unsupported leap]** No ancient Egyptian source supplies Amon’s Goetic rank, forms, seal, legions, or offices.

### 3. “Amon means the Hidden One”

**Verdict: only conditionally defensible.**

That meaning belongs to the conventional etymology of Egyptian Amun. It can be applied to Goetic Amon only after assuming the disputed Amun derivation.

### 4. “Amon is Baal Hammon”

**Verdict: speculative.**

The Punic name is another possible phonetic attraction, but no catalogue witness makes the connection.

### 5. “Amon is Nahum”

**Verdict: unsupported modern alias.**

No verified form in the requested manuscripts or early printed chain reads *Nahum*. It may arise from confused association with Nahum 3:8 and No-Amon.

### 6. “Amon and Amaymon are the same spirit”

**Verdict: contradicted by the texts.**

The *Livre des Esperitz* contains both Amon and Aymoymon as separate entries with separate ranks, appearances, regions, and legions.

### 7. Marquis or prince?

**Verdict: marquis.**

Weyer’s *princeps omnium fortissimus* is descriptive—“strongest of all princes/leaders.” It does not cancel *Marchio*, his formal rank. The *Goetia*’s technical rank “Prince” belongs to other spirits.

### 8. Forty or another number of legions?

**Verdict: forty is exceptionally stable.**

The *Livre*, Weyer, Scot, Sloane, Harley, Mathers–Crowley, de Plancy, and Peterson all give forty.

### 9. Raven, night-raven, night-heron, or owl?

**Verdict: a translation history, not necessarily four different forms.**

- Weyer: *nycticorax*.
- Literal gloss: night-raven.
- Zoological possibility: night-heron.
- Scot and the *Goetia*: raven.
- De Plancy: owl.
- Modern artists often follow the owl because Le Breton’s image became canonical.

### 10. Love or feuds?

**Verdict: love is the older and better-supported reading.**

The *Livre* gives love; Weyer and Scot give favor and reconciliation; Sloane manuscripts explicitly give love. “Feuds” appears in the 1904 edition and is best classified as a printing, reading, or editorial corruption that became tradition through repetition.

### 11. “Reconciles friends” or “friends and foes”?

**Verdict: friends and foes is the fuller manuscript reading.**

Mathers–Crowley’s omission of “foes” weakens the original antithetical formula.

### 12. Drowned souls

**Verdict: Harley-only accretion.**

It belongs to the Rudd manuscript state, not the stable Amon tradition. Its necromantic character may reflect borrowing from another spirit’s office.

### 13. Is the seal ancient?

**Verdict: no evidence.**

The individual seal is absent from the earliest Amon sources and first securely appears in seventeenth-century *Goetia* manuscripts. Claims that Solomon, Egyptian priests, or ancient Amun worshippers used it are legendary or modern.

### 14. Achaiah or Achasiah?

**Verdict: Achaiah is the historically established angelic form; Achasiah is the Rudd-manuscript spelling.**

Agrippa’s Hebrew supports אכאיה. The Rudd spelling is evidence for that manuscript’s transmission, not necessarily the preferred reconstruction.

### 15. “Good and patient God” as translation

**Verdict: devotional attribute, not literal etymology.**

It is carried by Lenain, Papus, Ambelain, and later angelologies.

### 16. Achaiah’s psalm number

**Verdict: both 102:8 and 103:8 can be correct under different numbering systems.**

The former is Vulgate; the latter is Hebrew/most modern English numbering.

### 17. Taurus or Gemini?

**Verdict: both belong to different later systems.**

- Achaiah: 0°–5° Taurus.
- Amon in Crowley’s *777*: 0°–10° Gemini by day.

Neither attribution occurs in Weyer or the basic *Goetia* catalogue.

### 18. Moon, Mercury, or Jupiter?

**Verdict: layered correspondences.**

- Moon: marquis rank and silver.
- Mercury: Achaiah’s Lenain/Taurus attribution.
- Jupiter: Amon’s *777* Gemini decan.

### 19. “Demon of life and reproduction”

**Verdict: modern secondary characterization.**

This phrase is popularized in recent demon encyclopedias and derivative websites. It is absent from the *Livre*, Weyer, Scot, the *Goetia*, Rudd, Mathers–Crowley, and de Plancy. The nearest early material is the *Livre*’s love office; “life and reproduction” is an interpretive expansion.

### 20. Rudd’s authorship

**Verdict: unresolved.**

The early-eighteenth-century Harley witness and Peter Smart’s connection are documented. A lost mid-seventeenth-century Thomas Rudd autograph is a reconstruction advanced by Skinner and Rankine, not an extant object.

### 21. Goetia as a book by Solomon

**Verdict: pseudepigraphic tradition.**

Its language, dependence on sixteenth- and seventeenth-century printed sources, and English manuscript history exclude literal authorship by the biblical Solomon.

## Open questions

1. **Exact dating of the surviving *Livre des Esperitz*.**  
   Catalogue descriptions and scholarly palaeographic judgments do not yet yield a universally accepted date.

2. **The name’s immediate source.**  
   Egyptian Amun is plausible, but no intermediate text has been found showing the name’s deliberate transfer into the spirit catalogue.

3. **The transition from maiden to wolf hybrid.**  
   Surviving witnesses reveal the change but do not explain why it occurred. A lost *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* recension may contain the missing stage.

4. **The archetype of Amon’s seal.**  
   The seventeenth-century manuscripts preserve closely related drawings, but no earlier individual character has been securely tied to Amon.

5. **The source of Harley’s drowned-soul office.**  
   Its absence from other witnesses argues for accretion; its exact donor text or neighboring spirit remains unidentified.

6. **Who designed the Amon–Achaiah pairing?**  
   The system survives in the Rudd/Smart manuscript complex, but whether it was devised by Thomas Rudd, Peter Smart, or another compiler is unsettled.

7. **De Plancy’s pre-1863 wording.**  
   The sixth-edition entry is verified; a full collation against every earlier edition is still needed to date each sentence and the Amun juxtaposition precisely.

8. **The direct source of the *Devilman* name.**  
   Amon is securely present, but a creator statement identifying Weyer, the *Goetia*, Egyptian Amun, or another intermediary has not been located.

9. **Creator-confirmed sources for modern game designs.**  
   Visual resemblance to Le Breton can be strong without proving that an artist used his plate.

10. **The origin of “Nahum” and similar aliases.**  
    They appear in recent derivative lists but lack a documented manuscript source.
