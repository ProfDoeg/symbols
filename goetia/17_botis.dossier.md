# Botis: Research Dossier

## Scope and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes the following kinds of statement:

- **Documented text** — explicitly present in a manuscript, early printed edition, or identifiable modern work.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an identification or historical relationship argued from manuscript comparison rather than stated by the texts themselves.
- **Tradition** — a claim repeated within an identifiable magical, Kabbalistic, or demonological lineage.
- **Esoteric correspondence** — an occult attribution imposed by a later system, such as the Golden Dawn, *777*, or French angelology.
- **Disputed** — a claim for which sources disagree or the documentary basis is inadequate.
- **Legend** — a narrative presented by the grimoire tradition without historical corroboration.
- **Modern invention** — an attribution first traceable to recent occultism, fiction, games, or personal revelation.

A source-reproduction limitation prevents printing three entire web-derived passages verbatim. Accordingly, the comparison below supplies short verbatim samples, complete clause-by-clause paraphrases, exact references, and links to the complete Latin and English texts.

## The name and its origins

### Fifteenth century: Artis and Otius

The statement often repeated online that Botis first appeared in 1577 is no longer tenable.

#### Artis in the *Livre des Esperitz*

**Documented text, mid-fifteenth century.** Trinity College Cambridge MS O.8.29, fol. 180v, the French *Livre des Esperitz*, contains a spirit called **Artis**. Its preliminary list calls Artis a prince, while the individual entry calls him a great duke. The entry says that he bears two crowns and a sword, answers whatever is asked, teaches hidden things, gives love or favor with all people, and has thirty-six legions. Its opening words are:

> “Artis eſt un grant duc et deux corõnes et une eſpee…”

The complete entry and manuscript image are available at [French Wikisource](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre_des_esperitz/Artis).

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Artis is probably a member of the same textual family as Otius/Otis/Botis because:

1. Artis occupies the corresponding position between cognate spirits Gazon/Gusoyn and Machin/Bathin.
2. Artis carries a sword.
3. His offices include answers, hidden matters, and gaining favor.
4. His thirty-six legions agree with Otius rather than Weyer’s later sixty.
5. Joseph Peterson’s comparative table explicitly places **Trinity “Artis”** beside Weyer’s Botis/Otis. [Peterson’s comparison](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm) is based on the order and language of the catalogues, not merely on name resemblance.

The identification remains **probable rather than certain** because Artis lacks the viper, teeth, and horned human form.

#### Otius in the Munich handbook

**Documented text, fifteenth century.** Bayerische Staatsbibliothek Clm 849, the so-called Munich handbook of necromancy, contains **Otius**. Richard Kieckhefer edited the manuscript in *Forbidden Rites: A Necromancer’s Manual of the Fifteenth Century* (1998), pp. 291–293. Peterson’s cross-index identifies Otius as the close ancestor of Weyer’s “Botis, alibi Otis.” See the [digital manuscript record](https://daten.digitale-sammlungen.de/bsb00037155/image_1) and the [publisher’s description of Kieckhefer’s edition](https://www.psupress.org/books/titles/0-271-01750-3.html).

The Otius entry, as reported from Kieckhefer’s Latin edition, differs from Weyer in several particulars:

- Rank: president and count.
- Form: immediately humanoid, rather than viper followed by human.
- Features: great teeth and **three** horns.
- Weapon: sharp sword.
- Offices: truthful answers concerning present, future, and hidden matters; favor or grace between friends and enemies.
- Legions: **thirty-six**.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Otius is the earliest clear Botis witness because both the name and the unusual cluster of teeth, horns, sword, temporal knowledge, social reconciliation, and legionary command correspond. The later transformations are therefore reconstructable:

| Feature | Otius, Clm 849 | Weyer’s Botis/Otis |
|---|---|---|
| First form | Human or humanoid | Terrible viper |
| Human teeth | Great | Great |
| Horns | Three | Two |
| Sword | Sharp | Sharp |
| Answers | Present/future/hidden | Present/past/future/hidden |
| Social power | Favor of friends and enemies | Reconciles friends and enemies |
| Legions | 36 | 60 |

This is much stronger evidence than a proposed derivation from an ancient deity.

### Weyer’s Botis and Otis

**Documented text, 1577.** Johann Weyer printed the catalogue as an appendix to the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum*. The familiar date 1563 belongs to the first edition of the larger book; the *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* was added in **1577**. Weyer called his source a *Liber officiorum spirituum*—a “Book of the Offices of Spirits.” [Peterson documents the editions and source family](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

Weyer’s heading reads:

> “Botis, alibi Otis, magnus Præses & Comes…”

Thus **Botis and Otis already coexist in the first printed witness**. Otis was not invented by de Plancy or a modern translator.

### Scribal development of the name

The most economical reconstruction is:

**Artis? → Otius → Otis/Botis → Botis**

This remains a **scholarly reconstruction**, not a documented etymology. Initial letters are unusually unstable in the spirit catalogues: names were copied between difficult hands, abbreviated Latin, French, and English, often without an independent referent to correct them against. Weyer apparently found, or knew, both an initial-B and a non-B reading and preserved both.

The exact relationship between **Artis** and **Otius** is less certain than that between Otius and Otis. Artis may be a damaged reading of the same name, a contaminated entry, or an independently named spirit whose description entered the Botis complex.

### Does Botis derive from an ancient god, hero, or place?

**Finding: no documented ancient referent has been established.**

Unlike Bael/Baal, Astaroth/Astarte, Asmoday/Asmodeus, or Belial, Botis has no demonstrable chain to a biblical, Mesopotamian, Greek, Roman, Egyptian, or Iranian divine name. Neither Weyer nor any of the manuscript catalogues supplies an etymology.

#### Proposed derivations

- **Greek ὄφις, ophis, “snake.”**  
  **Disputed modern speculation.** The semantic attraction is obvious because Botis appears as a viper, but the change from *ophis* to *Botis/Otis/Otius* is not documented in the manuscripts. The Greek word itself is real; the derivation is not established. See the lexical entry for [ὄφις](https://en.wiktionary.org/wiki/%E1%BD%84%CF%86%CE%B9%CF%82).

- **Greek Pothos, personification of longing.**  
  **Modern speculation.** This is sometimes connected to reconciliation or affection, but neither spelling nor transmission supports it. Pothos belongs to the classical Erotes; nothing in the Botis manuscripts invokes him.

- **Latin *otus*, a horned owl.**  
  **Modern occult speculation.** The horns make the association visually suggestive, but Botis is never described as an owl. No source-chain witness uses *Otus* as an explanation.

- **An ancient Greek common noun *botis*.**  
  Forms resembling *botis* occur as names for fish or plants in classical lexicography. This is a **homograph**, not evidence of identity.

- **A lost god named Botis.**  
  **Unsupported claim.** No inscription, mythographic text, temple, place-name dossier, or ancient cult has been identified.

The evidence therefore favors **scribal evolution inside the medieval spirit-catalogue tradition**, not euhemerism from an ancient deity.

## Number and order: Weyer’s 69 and the Goetia’s 72

### Weyer

**Documented text.** Botis is Weyer’s **ninth** spirit. Weyer’s printed *Pseudomonarchia* contains sixty-nine entries, even though material inside the tradition alludes to seventy-two. [Weyer’s complete order](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm) begins Bael, Agares, Marbas, Pruflas, Amon, Barbatos, Buer, Gusoyn, Botis.

### The *Ars Goetia*

**Documented text.** Botis becomes **seventeenth**. The immediately surrounding sequence is Zepar 16, Botis 17, Bathin 18. This is not the result of merely inserting eight spirits before Botis: the compiler reordered large blocks of Weyer/Scot material.

### How 69 became 72

The usual arithmetic is correct but needs qualification:

- Weyer: 69 entries.
- The *Goetia* omits Weyer’s **Pruflas**, also called Bufas.
- It adds **Vassago**, **Seere**, **Dantalion**, and **Andromalius**.
- Therefore: 69 − 1 + 4 = 72.

The four additions occupy Goetic positions 3, 70, 71, and 72. Peterson suggests that the target of seventy-two reflects the number’s established angelic and Kabbalistic importance, while the unexplained rearrangement may have arisen through the manipulation or disordering of exemplars. This is a **scholarly inference**, not an explanation given by the compiler. [Peterson’s introduction and comparative tables](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm) document the textual facts.

### Weyer’s Latin

**Documented text.** Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum*, §9, in the 1577 fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum*. Peterson’s 2001 edition prints the Latin and Scot translation in Appendix II, p. 231.

Short verbatim sample:

> “Prodit in viperæ specie deterrima”

That is, he comes forth in the most terrible kind or appearance of a viper.

Complete content, closely translated:

Botis—also called Otis—is a great President and Count. He appears as a particularly terrible viper. If he assumes human form, he displays large teeth and two horns and carries a sharp sword. He gives completely truthful answers about present, past, future, and hidden matters. He reconciles friends as well as enemies and commands sixty legions.

The complete Latin is at [Esoteric Archives](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm#botis); the 2001 printed comparison is in Joseph H. Peterson, *The Lesser Key of Solomon*, Appendix II, p. 231.

Two details are regularly lost in summaries:

1. Weyer includes the **present** and **hidden or abstruse matters**.
2. *Dat perfectè responsa vera* emphasizes completely or perfectly true answers.

### Scot’s English

**Documented text.** Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (London: William Brome, 1584), Book XV, chapter 2, pp. 218–219 in the first-edition pagination.

Short verbatim sample:

> “Botis, otherwise Otis, a great president and an earle”

Scot then says that Botis comes forth as an ugly viper; in human shape he has great teeth and two horns and carries a sharp sword. He answers about present, past, and future, reconciles friends and foes, and rules sixty legions. The complete passage is available in [Peterson’s edition of Scot’s magical texts](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/scot16.htm) and the original edition can be inspected through [Cornell’s Witchcraft Collection](https://digital.library.cornell.edu/catalog/witchcraft081).

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The demon-catalogue translation in Scot was probably made by the prolific translator Abraham Fleming, rather than by Scot personally. Peterson notes linguistic mistakes suggesting that the translator either misunderstood portions of Weyer or used a faulty copy. Scot printed the material inside a skeptical work meant to expose conjurers and reduce witch persecution, not as his endorsement of practical conjuration.

### Sloane MS 3825

**Documented text, seventeenth century.** The Botis entry begins at fol. 102v and is followed by Bathin at fol. 103r. Peterson uses this manuscript as the base of his edition.

Short verbatim sample:

> “he telleth of all Things past and to come”

The entry calls Botis the seventeenth spirit, a great President and Earl. He appears first as an ugly viper, then—on the magician’s command—takes human shape with great teeth and two horns, carrying a sharp bright sword. He tells past and future, reconciles friends and foes, governs sixty legions, and obeys when shown his seal. [Complete Sloane-based transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm#botis).

Compared with Weyer:

- “Present” and “hidden/abstruse” disappear.
- Weyer’s conditional human transformation becomes an explicit response to the magician’s command.
- The sword becomes **bright** as well as sharp.
- An individual seal and its coercive function are added.

### Sloane MS 2731

**Documented text.** Sloane 2731 also reads **Botis** and carries the standard Goetic entry and seal. It belongs to the same Lemegeton family but is not Peterson’s principal base text. Sloane 2731 is particularly significant for other portions of the *Lesser Key*, including material absent from some copies, but it provides no radically different Botis biography. Peterson records its name reading in his [manuscript comparison table](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia_spirits.pdf).

### Harley MS 6483 and the Rudd material

**Documented text with disputed authorship.** Harley 6483 contains the Rudd recension of the *Goetia*, copied by Peter Smart in the early eighteenth century. Botis remains spirit 17, with the familiar form, offices, legions, and a corresponding Shem angel.

Some transcriptions render the rank formula with an anomalous preposition—effectively “a great President under an Earl”—where the other witnesses have “and an Earl.” This is best treated as a **scribal or transcriptional corruption**, not a new infernal rank. Skinner and Rankine normalize the operative understanding to the familiar dual rank.

The manuscript’s major innovation is not a new Botis description but the **angelic counter-seal and Psalm**, discussed below.

### Mathers–Crowley 1904

**Documented text.** *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King*, translated by S. L. MacGregor Mathers and edited, introduced, and published by Aleister Crowley, was issued at Boleskine/Foyers in 1904. Its title page calls Mathers only “a Dead Hand.” Bibliographic evidence gives a first printing of about two hundred copies, x + 66 pages. See the [1904 bibliography](https://www.100thmonkeypress.com/biblio/acrowley/books/goetia_1904/goetia_1904.htm) and [first-edition scan](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Index:The_Book_of_the_Goetia_(Mathers-Crowley,_1904).djvu).

Short verbatim sample:

> “He ruleth over 60 Legions of Spirits”

The complete Botis paragraph is on printed p. 17. Its content follows the Sloane form almost word for word: seventeenth spirit; Great President and Earl; ugly viper; commanded human shape; great teeth; two horns; bright sharp sword; past and future; reconciliation; sixty legions; seal. [Facsimile text](https://hermetic.com/_media/93beast.fea.st/files/section1/crowley_-_goetia.pdf).

### Peterson 2001

Joseph H. Peterson’s *The Lesser Key of Solomon: Lemegeton Clavicula Salomonis* (York Beach, Maine: Weiser, 2001) collates Sloane 3825 with other manuscript witnesses and prints the Weyer/Scot comparison in Appendix II. It is an editorial reconstruction rather than a facsimile, but it exposes variant readings that the 1904 edition concealed. ## What Botis does—and does not do

### Offices actually attested

**Documented:**

- Answers about the present, past, and future — Weyer and Scot.
- Answers about hidden or abstruse matters — Weyer; anticipated by Otius and Artis.
- Tells past and future — the *Goetia*.
- Reconciles friends and enemies/foes — Weyer, Scot, and the *Goetia*.
- Gives favor or grace involving friends and enemies — Otius.
- Gives general love or favor and teaches hidden things — Artis.

### Offices not attested for Botis in the early chain

The early sources do **not** say that Botis:

- teaches languages or sciences generally;
- discovers treasure;
- gives familiars;
- makes people invisible;
- transmutes metals;
- transports people;
- procures erotic love specifically;
- causes illness or healing;
- creates music;
- protects from envy;
- gives courage;
- rules fireflies;
- should be summoned with a particular flower, crystal, color, or “enn.”

Those powers belong either to other Goetic spirits, to the paired angel’s later offices, or to modern practitioner literature.

### “Reconciles friends and foes”

Weyer’s *tam amicos quam hostes conciliat* means that Botis brings together, conciliates, or reconciles both friends and enemies. It does not mean that he merely creates friendship or reveals false friends.

**Documented corruption.** Collin de Plancy’s 1863 French entry says, in effect, that Otis “has as many friends as enemies.” That is not what Weyer says. The same entry replaces Weyer’s truthful answers with the claim that he answers brazenly or impudently. These appear to be de Plancy’s adaptations or mistranslations rather than independent traditions.

## Rank, planet, metal, and hour

Botis’s double rank produces genuine ambiguity.

### President

**Documented manuscript correspondence.**

- Planet: **Mercury**
- Metal: **mercury or quicksilver**

The Goetic observations associate Presidents with Mercury. Presidents may be called at almost any hour of the day except twilight; at night the text imposes the condition that their governing king also be invoked. [Goetic observations](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm) and [separate transcription](https://hermetic.com/norton/classics/goetia/observations).

### Earl or Count

**Documented manuscript correspondence.**

- Planets: **Venus and Moon**
- Metals: **copper and silver**, “equally alike”

Earls may be called during daylight, preferably in woods or another deserted, quiet location.

### Mathers’s correction

**Esoteric editorial emendation, 1904.** Mathers considered the manuscript scheme corrupt because it left Mars and iron without a rank. His reconstructed system assigns:

- Earl: **Mars / iron**
- President: commonly Mercury, with the problematic compound or mixed-metal reading redistributed

This correction became far more influential than the manuscript wording. Consequently, modern tables usually call Botis **Mercurial and Martial**, combining President–Mercury with Earl–Mars. Crowley’s *777* table prints two planetary sigla beside Botis; modern transcriptions normally interpret them as Mars and Mercury, although low-resolution reproductions sometimes turn the second into a Venus glyph.

The safest report is therefore:

- **Manuscript rule:** Mercury/quicksilver plus Venus–Moon/copper–silver.
- **Mathers/Golden Dawn correction:** Mercury/quicksilver plus Mars/iron.
- **Modern practice:** usually Mercury + Mars, often without acknowledging the emendation.

### Lunar timing

**Documented general Goetic instruction.** The *Goetia* prefers operations when the Moon is two, four, six, eight, ten, twelve, or fourteen days old. This is a general ritual rule, not a Botis-specific lunar mansion.

### Three incompatible “best times”

A modern reader will encounter three different systems:

1. **Rank-hour system:** use President/Earl hours and conditions from the Goetic observations.
2. **Rudd angelic quinance:** 20°–25° Gemini, because Botis is paired numerically with angel 17.
3. **Crowley’s Goetic decan:** 10°–20° Virgo, by day.

They derive from different layers and should not be presented as one ancient schedule.

## The seal

### Absence before the Goetia

**Documented negative finding.** Neither Weyer’s printed *Pseudomonarchia* nor Scot’s demon catalogue gives Botis an individual seal. Peterson reports that he knows of no individual Goetic seals in the earlier *Offices of Spirits* manuscripts. The sigils therefore belong to the later Lemegeton stage, though other medieval spirit catalogues independently contain seals. [Peterson’s manuscript discussion](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

### Standard form

The standard Botis seal, preserved by the Lemegeton manuscripts and popularized in 1904, may be described as follows:

- A predominantly square or rectangular central body.
- Round terminals at the two upper corners.
- Two inward-facing T-like or forked elements crossing or resting on a central horizontal.
- A central vertical stroke descending below the square.
- A small circular node or loop on the descending axis in some renderings.
- Lateral strokes projecting from the body.
- A curved, hook-like lower flourish.
- In the Mathers–Crowley plate, the glyph is enclosed in a circular border with **BOTIS** distributed around it.

A high-resolution public-domain reproduction is available at [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Seal_of_Botis.jpg).

### Manuscript variants

**Documented palaeographic variation, not separate seals.**

- **Sloane 3825:** supplies Peterson’s basic line form beside the entry.
- **Sloane 2731:** preserves the same structural glyph, with differences in crossings, terminal roundels, and the proportion of the lower stem.
- **Harley 6483:** adapts the glyph for Rudd’s angel–demon lamen system.
- **Mathers–Crowley:** regularizes the strokes, makes the composition more symmetrical, and places it within a bold circular name-ring.

No source consulted supports two genuinely independent Botis seals. Online “alternate seals” are usually redrawn, rotated, simplified, or commercially ornamented forms of the same glyph. Claims that its number of points encodes a special Botis doctrine are **modern interpretations**.

### Function

The Sloane entry says that Botis obeys when he sees the seal. Elsewhere the *Goetia* directs the operator to wear spirits’ characters as lamens. Rudd adds a controlling angelic reverse. None of the early sources explains the glyph’s geometry or claims that each stroke has a decipherable alphabetic meaning.

## The angel: Lauviah II

## Identification and the name problem

**Documented Rudd tradition.** Spirit 17 Botis is paired with Shem angel 17, usually written:

- **Lauviah**
- **Lauviah II**
- **Laviah**
- **Lauvia**
- **Lavayah**
- Latinized forms such as **LAVIAH** or **LAVAYAH**

The numeral or “II” is important because many lists also contain a **Lauviah at 11**. Furthermore:

- Angel 17: normally **Lauviah II**
- Angel 19: normally **Leuviah**

Thus the item-specific form “Lauviah (Leuviah)” reflects a common modern conflation. In a differentiated list, calling Botis’s angel **Leuviah** risks shifting it from number 17 to number 19. Comparative tables explicitly show 17 Lauviah–Botis and 19 Leuviah–Sallos/Saleos. See this [comparative 72-name table](https://cultofnone.co.uk/gods-work/goetia/).

### Meaning

**Esoteric gloss:** *Deus mirabilis* — “the admirable,” “wonderful,” or “marvelous God.”

This is not a secure dictionary translation of the three Hebrew consonants. It is a theological title derived from the associated Psalm phrase. French angelology commonly renders it *Dieu admirable*.

### The 72 names from Exodus

**Traditional Kabbalistic construction.** Exodus 14:19–21 consists, in the traditional consonantal Hebrew count, of three verses of seventy-two letters each. The extraction works as follows:

1. Write verse 19 in its normal direction.
2. Write verse 20 in reverse.
3. Write verse 21 in its normal direction.
4. Read the letters vertically, producing seventy-two three-letter groups.

This alternating arrangement is sometimes called boustrophedon. Later Jewish and Christian Kabbalists added endings such as **-יה** (*-Yah*) and **-אל** (*-El*) to make pronounceable angelic names.

The verses themselves do not name seventy-two angels. The angelic personifications, meanings, Psalm verses, zodiacal sectors, and offices are later interpretive constructions. The Hebrew verses can be inspected through [Sefaria](https://voices.sefaria.org/sheets/375157).

### What *Shem ha-Mephorash* originally meant

**Historical qualification.** The Hebrew expression means the explicit, distinguished, or fully expressed Name. In rabbinic material it usually concerns the divine Name—often the Tetragrammaton—and can also refer to extended names of forty-two or seventy-two letters. Its popular modern meaning, “the fixed list of seventy-two guardian angels,” is a late narrowing of a much broader term.

## History of the angelic tradition

### *Sefer Raziel*

**Tradition with complicated textual history.** Materials collected under the title *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* developed in medieval Jewish magical and pietistic circles, probably reaching compilation in thirteenth-century Ashkenaz, though the work claims revelation from the angel Raziel to Adam. It was printed in Amsterdam in 1701. It transmits divine-name and angelic traditions that later readers connect to the seventy-two names. [Overview and table of the Sefer Raziel names](https://www.kabbale.eu/les-72-noms-du-sepher-raziel/).

The book is therefore not a contemporary record from biblical times. Its claimed primeval transmission is **legend**.

### Reuchlin, Vigenère, and Christian Cabala

Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517) helped bring angelized forms of the seventy-two names into Christian Cabala. Blaise de Vigenère subsequently circulated related tables and seals. This Christianized environment is an important bridge between Jewish divine-name techniques and later ceremonial-magic angel catalogues.

### Athanasius Kircher

**Documented Christian-Cabalistic transmission.** Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (Rome, 1652–1654) presents extensive material on Hebrew divine names, the seventy-two-letter name, angelic orders, and related diagrams. [Kircher material transcribed by Peterson](https://www.esotericarchives.com/juratus/kircher.htm).

Kircher did not create the three-verse technique, nor is there evidence that he paired Lauviah specifically with Botis. His importance is systematization and wide circulation.

### Rudd and Harley 6483

**Documented manuscript system; disputed authorship and date of composition.** Harley 6483 pairs each of the seventy-two Goetic spirits, in numerical order, with one of the seventy-two Shem angels. The demon’s seal is placed in a controlled relationship with the angelic name or seal, and the relevant Psalm phrase supplies divine authority.

For Botis the pairing is:

- Demon: Botis, 17
- Angel: Lauviah, 17
- Psalm phrase: *Domine Dominus noster, quam admirabile est nomen tuum in universa terra*

This translates approximately: “O Lord, our Lord, how admirable is thy name in all the earth.”

Psalm 8 contains this refrain at both beginning and end. Consequently, tables variously cite **Psalm 8:1** or **Psalm 8:9** in Protestant numbering; Vulgate/Septuagint conventions can shift those verse numbers. Lenain-derived lists usually cite the opening verse, while some Rudd-derived tables cite the closing refrain. The words are substantially the same.

Stephen Skinner and David Rankine’s *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (2007), pp. 118–119, reproduces the Botis–Lauviah material. The later Llewellyn description expressly summarizes the system as using the seventy-two angels to constrain or bind the seventy-two demons. [Publisher record](https://www.llewellyn.com/product.php?ean=9780738723556).

### What “constrains” means in Rudd

**Documented ritual tradition.** Rudd’s method is not merely an inspirational “light-and-shadow pairing.” The angelic authority is placed over the demon as a coercive control:

- the demon’s character identifies the spirit summoned;
- the corresponding angelic name and seal stand against it;
- the Psalm invokes the divine attribute behind the angel;
- the double-sided or coordinated lamen prevents the demonic seal from functioning as an unqualified badge of allegiance.

Descriptions of the angel as Botis’s “higher self,” “light pole,” “healing counterpart,” or psychological integration mechanism are **modern reinterpretations**.

### The “Dr Rudd” controversy

The Harley magical manuscripts were copied by Peter Smart around 1712–1714 and attributed to “Dr Rudd.” Skinner and Rankine identify this figure with the military engineer and mathematician Thomas Rudd and propose that the underlying work dates to the mid-seventeenth century.

That attribution is **disputed**:

- the surviving manuscripts are early eighteenth-century copies;
- some material appears dependent on printed sources later than Thomas Rudd’s lifetime;
- the manuscript attribution does not by itself prove authorship by the engineer;
- A. E. Waite and later critics questioned whether “Rudd” was a genuine authorial identity or a prestigious attribution.

The Botis–Lauviah pairing is unquestionably present in the Rudd manuscript tradition. Its creation by the historical Thomas Rudd is not equally secure.

### Lenain, 1823

Lazare Républicain Lenain’s *La science cabalistique, ou l’art de connaître les bons génies qui influent sur la destinée des hommes* (Amiens, 1823) systematized the seventy-two genii with Psalm verses, five-degree zodiacal sectors, hours, offices, and talismanic material. The original can be consulted at [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/download/BIUSante_ms05365/BIUSante_ms05365.pdf).

For angel 17, the Lenain-derived system gives:

- 81st–85th zodiacal degrees;
- 20°–25° Gemini;
- *Dieu admirable*;
- Psalm 8’s “admirable name” refrain.

Lenain did not originate the Rudd demon pairing, and editions influenced by him do not always reproduce Rudd’s exact seal arrangement.

### Papus

Papus, Gérard Encausse, helped recirculate French practical Kabbalah in the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries. His *La Cabbale, tradition secrète de l’Occident* and his prefatory involvement in later Lenain editions spread the seventy-two-genius tables. Papus’s lists give Lauviah as the seventeenth genius with the same Latin Psalm formula. [Digitized Papus edition](https://www.hermanubis.com.br/LivrosVirtuais/Lacabala.pdf).

Claims that Papus invented the zodiacal degrees or the Botis pairing are false.

### The Golden Dawn

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn inherited Christian-Cabalistic angel tables, decans, Tarot correspondences, planetary hours, and Mathers’s engagement with the Lemegeton manuscripts. It helped create the environment in which Goetic spirits, Shem angels, zodiacal faces, and Tarot cards could be compared in a single table.

However, the Golden Dawn’s decan–Tarot system and Rudd’s numerical angel–demon pairing are not identical. Their conflation is one of the principal modern errors surrounding Botis.

### Ambelain

**Esoteric correspondence, twentieth century.** Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (first published 1951) further systematized the French angelic material. Lauviah 17 is associated with:

- relief from anguish and sadness;
- peaceful sleep;
- higher sciences and discoveries;
- revelation through dreams;
- music, poetry, literature, and philosophy;
- the choir of Thrones;
- Zaphkiel;
- Binah and Saturn;
- a daily twenty-minute interval, usually 5:20–5:40 a.m.

These are **not offices of Botis in Weyer or the Goetia**. They belong to Ambelain’s angelic reception.

### Haziel

The French-language writer using the name Haziel popularized the seventy-two “guardian angels” through late twentieth-century books, calendars, prayers, and personality descriptions. In that system, Lauviah governs people born approximately **11–15 June**. [French publisher’s guardian-angel calendar](https://www.editionsbussiere.com/anges-osCsid-3378f8309094ec9b794cdfee4533d633.html).

The dates are a fixed civil-calendar rendering of 20°–25° Gemini. They are neither in Exodus nor in Weyer, and precession and differing tropical-date conventions make them approximate.

The author Haziel should not be confused with **Haziel, angel 9**.

### The two zodiac systems explained

#### Rudd/Lenain sequential scheme

The seventy-two angels divide 360° into sectors of 5°:

- Angel 17 Lauviah: 20°–25° Gemini.
- Angel 18 Caliel: 25°–30° Gemini.
- Together they occupy the third decan of Gemini.
- The Golden Dawn Tarot card for that decan is the **Ten of Swords**, Sun in Gemini.

If every demon is simply paired with its same-number angel, Botis inherits Lauviah’s five degrees and shares the Ten of Swords decan with Bathin/Caliel.

#### Crowley’s *777* Goetic-decan scheme

Crowley distributes thirty-six spirits as day demons and thirty-six as night demons across the thirty-six decans. Botis appears in column CLVII, the **day demons of succedent decans**, at Key Scale 20, Virgo. [Original *777* table](https://hermetic.com/_media/93beast.fea.st/files/section1/777/liber_777.pdf) and [*777 Revised*](https://hermetic.com/_media/93beast.fea.st/files/section1/777/liber_777_revised.pdf).

Therefore:

- Sign: Virgo
- Decan: second/succedent
- Degrees: 10°–20° Virgo
- Tarot pip: Nine of Disks
- Planet governing the pip: Venus
- Day demon: Botis
- Night demon: Caim/Camio

This is the correct answer to “Crowley’s *777* quinance and Tarot pip” only after correcting the premise: *777* gives Botis a **ten-degree decan**, not a five-degree quinance.

### Frater Achad

Frater Achad, Charles Stansfeld Jones, wrote extensively on Qabalistic path and number rearrangements. **No Botis-specific teaching or independent Botis correspondence was located in his published works.** His name appears in modern correspondence interfaces because those sites compare his Tree-of-Life theories with Crowley’s; that is not evidence that he worked with Botis.

### Enochian overlays

**Negative historical finding.** Botis has no Enochian name, tablet square, aethyr, or Dee–Kelley attribution in Weyer, Scot, the Sloane entry, or the original Botis description. The Rudd manuscript collection sits near material influenced by John Dee, but proximity does not establish a Botis–Enochian identity.

Enochian translations of Goetic conjurations and attempts to place spirits under Enochian governors belong to modern syncretic practice. They should be labeled **later overlays**, not Golden Dawn manuscript facts unless a specific ritual text is cited.

## Iconography

## The manuscript image

The earliest Botis/Otius texts are verbal:

- Artis: two crowns and sword.
- Otius: toothy, horned human with sword.
- Weyer: viper, then two-horned human.
- Scot: copies Weyer’s two-form image.

The *Goetia* stabilizes the canonical image as a sequence:

1. ugly viper;
2. magician commands transformation;
3. horned human with large teeth;
4. bright sharp sword.

The contrast between weapon and reconciliation has encouraged modern symbolic readings—sword as discernment, serpent as concealed knowledge, transformation as mastery of fear—but these are **interpretations**, not explanations supplied by the manuscripts.

## Collin de Plancy

### First edition, 1818

Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* first appeared in 1818. It repeatedly mined Weyer while combining skeptical, Catholic, folkloric, and literary material through successive editions.

### Sixth edition, 1863

**Documented text.** In the 1863 edition, Botis receives only a cross-reference: “Botis. Voy. Otis.” The substantive entry appears under **Otis**, p. 511. [Botis cross-reference](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.%2C_1863/Lettre_B) and [Otis entry](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.,_1863/Otis).

Short sample:

> “Otis ou Botis, grand président des enfers”

De Plancy retains the viper, horned human, great teeth, sword, and sixty legions. But he alters Weyer’s offices, saying that Otis answers brazenly and has as many friends as enemies.

### Is there a Louis Le Breton portrait?

**Finding: no.** The 1863 Otis entry is text-only. Louis Le Breton produced the famous engraved portraits for selected demons in that edition, but Botis/Otis was not among the illustrated selections. Images circulating as “de Plancy’s Botis” are misattributed unless they can be tied to another plate and identified as such.

## Modern art

The absence of a Breton portrait left modern artists considerable freedom. Recent depictions fall into several families:

- a literal horned swordsman emerging from a snake;
- a hybrid snake-bodied humanoid;
- a crowned infernal noble;
- a beautiful or androgynous mediator;
- an aged winged figure, derived from modern visions rather than the texts;
- sigil-centered abstract or Tarot-like images.

Eve Harms’s [modern Botis illustration and commentary](https://eveharms.com/2021/06/06/botis/) is one documented example of contemporary demonological art. Commercial coins, pendants, shirts, and AI-generated cards frequently regularize or decorate the 1904 seal. These are **modern art or merchandise**, not manuscript variants.

## People and works

### Johann Weyer

Weyer was a physician, a pupil of Heinrich Cornelius Agrippa, and a critic of witch trials. He argued that many accused witches were deluded, mentally ill, coerced, or deceived and should not be treated as powerful diabolical conspirators. He nevertheless accepted a Christian spirit world more readily than a modern materialist would.

His *Pseudomonarchia* was printed polemically: he claimed to expose the absurdity, imposture, and blasphemy of would-be magicians. Ironically, publication preserved the catalogue and made it more accessible.

### Reginald Scot and Abraham Fleming

Scot’s *Discoverie* likewise opposed witch persecution and exposed magical literature to ridicule. The catalogue’s English translation was probably Fleming’s. Scot’s hostile editorial frame did not prevent later magicians from extracting the text and treating it as a manual.

### The anonymous Lemegeton compiler or compilers

The compiler:

- reordered the Weyer/Scot spirits;
- expanded the count to seventy-two;
- omitted Pruflas;
- added Vassago, Seere, Dantalion, and Andromalius;
- added or transmitted individual seals;
- attached a fuller ceremonial apparatus drawn partly from texts such as the *Heptameron*.

Botis’s movement from 9 to 17 and acquisition of a seal belong to this stage.

### “Dr Rudd” and Peter Smart

The Rudd recension overlays the seventy-two demons with the seventy-two Shem angels. Peter Smart is the identifiable early-eighteenth-century copyist. Identification of the underlying author with Thomas Rudd is plausible but disputed.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers

Mathers collated British Museum manuscripts and translated or regularized the *Goetia* for Golden Dawn use. He also proposed the influential correction of the ranks’ metals and planets.

### Aleister Crowley

Crowley published Mathers’s work in 1904, added an invocation, notes, and his “Initiated Interpretation.” In that essay he treats spirits psychologically as aspects or portions of the human mind, without simply denying the possibility of phenomena. This was an influential **modern reinterpretation**, not the worldview of Weyer’s catalogue or the Lemegeton compiler.

His *777* later placed Botis in the succedent Virgo decan by day.

### A. E. Waite

Waite reproduced condensed Goetic material and the seal in *The Book of Black Magic and of Pacts* and later *The Book of Ceremonial Magic*, p. 201 in commonly digitized editions. He describes the viper, horned human, sword, temporal knowledge, and reconciliation. [Digitized edition](https://lib.wildla.in/books/The%20Book%20of%20Ceremonial%20Magic.pdf).

### Collin de Plancy

De Plancy’s *Otis* entry popularized the alternate name in French demonology but also introduced the “as many friends as enemies” wording.

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 edition and evolving online transcriptions are central because they:

- return to Sloane 3825 rather than simply repeating 1904;
- record Sloane and Harley variants;
- place Weyer’s Latin beside Scot;
- identify Clm 849 Otius and Trinity Artis;
- demonstrate that the seal is absent from earlier *Offices* manuscripts.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their 2007 *Goetia of Dr Rudd* brought Harley 6483’s angelic control system into modern print. Its historical value lies in the manuscript transcription and coordinated angel–demon apparatus; its attribution of the work to Thomas Rudd remains debated.

### Richard Kieckhefer

Kieckhefer’s 1998 edition of Clm 849 supplied the decisive pre-Weyer Otius evidence.

### Jean-Patrice Boudet and Julien Véronèse

Jean-Patrice Boudet’s “Les who’s who démonologiques de la Renaissance et leurs ancêtres médiévaux” compares Renaissance demon catalogues with medieval predecessors. [Article and DOI](https://doi.org/10.4000/medievales.1019).

Julien Véronèse is an important scholar of medieval ritual magic and Solomonic textual traditions, but the specific comparative “who’s who” study is **Boudet’s**, not Véronèse’s. Modern bibliographies sometimes blur their contributions.

### Owen Davies

Davies’s *Grimoires: A History of Magic Books* situates the *Lesser Key* within the broader movement of manuscript and printed magical literature. It does not propose a new Botis etymology.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s *Geosophia* and related work argue for reading the Goetic tradition against older necromantic, spirit-catalogue, and Mediterranean backgrounds. [Publisher description](https://scarletimprint.com/publications/p/geosophia). No securely attested ancient Botis cult follows from this general thesis.

### Poke Runyon

Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* presents a twentieth-century operative reconstruction using dark-mirror scrying and Solomonic ceremony. Botis appears as one of the inherited seventy-two rather than as a historically new spirit. [Author/publisher page](https://www.pokerunyon.com/books/monograph-v-2-8ajba).

### Lon Milo DuQuette, Christopher Hyatt, and David P. Wilson

*Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia* popularized a psychological and practical reading and tabulates Botis with Virgo 10°–20°, the Nine of Disks, and day-demon status. Its dates and correspondences come through Crowley and the Golden Dawn, not Weyer.

## Grimoire ancestors and related traditions

### *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

This “Book of the Offices of Spirits” is the direct textual family behind Weyer. Surviving cognate catalogues include the *Livre des Esperitz*, Clm 849, Oxford and Folger material, and other fragmentary lists. Names and descriptions migrate, split, fuse, and change rank between witnesses.

Botis is especially useful for tracing that process because **Artis → Otius → Otis/Botis** can be compared through position, office, form, and legion count.

### Munich handbook

Clm 849 is the earliest clear ancestor. It disproves the claim that Weyer invented the figure in 1577.

### *Livre des Esperitz*

Artis is a likely but less exact cognate. It preserves the sword and thirty-six legions but not the serpent-human sequence.

### Book of Abramelin

**Negative finding.** No secure Botis, Otis, or Otius entry was found in the standard Abramelin demon lists. Similar ranks or powers are insufficient to establish identity.

### *Testament of Solomon*

The late antique Greek *Testament of Solomon* contains Ornias, Beelzeboul, Asmodeus, and numerous disease-causing or astral demons controlled by angelic names. It is an important ancestor of the broad Solomon-binds-demons legend, but it does not contain an identifiable Botis/Otis/Otius. Botis should therefore not be called a “demon from the *Testament of Solomon*.”

### Solomon’s vessel

**Legend.** The *Goetia* says Solomon confined the spirits in a vessel later opened by Babylonians. Peterson traces an earlier form to the medieval *Life of St Margaret*, where Solomon confines demons in a vessel; “brass” and the Babylonian opening episode developed in later English transmission. [Peterson’s discussion](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

### *Grand Grimoire*

**Documented later reception.** A later *Grand Grimoire/Red Dragon* hierarchy lists Botis among eighteen subordinate spirits under the six superior officers, specifically within the group governed by Agaliarept. [Transcription](https://www2.esotericarchives.com/solomon/grand.htm) and [Italian edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/Il_Grand_Grimoire.pdf).

This subordination to Agaliarept is not found in Weyer or the *Goetia*.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. “Botis first appears in Weyer”

**Verdict: false in its strong form.**

- Weyer supplies the first known **printed** Botis.
- Clm 849’s **Otius** is a clear fifteenth-century ancestor.
- The *Livre des Esperitz*’s **Artis** is a probable cognate.

The corrected formulation is: “Botis under that exact printed spelling is first securely attested in Weyer in 1577; the figure is already present as Otius, and probably Artis, in fifteenth-century manuscripts.”

### 2. Ancient god or demonized pagan deity

**Verdict: unsupported.**

No evidence links Botis with Baal, Astarte, Amun, Pothos, a classical hero, or an ancient locality. The medieval manuscript chain explains the name more economically.

### 3. Ophis, Pothos, and the horned owl

**Verdict: speculative.**

Each proposal selects one feature—snake, reconciliation/love, or horns—and works backward to a vaguely similar word. None supplies an intermediate text, medieval gloss, or independent ancient identification.

### 4. Is Otis a separate spirit?

**Verdict: no in Weyer and Scot.**

Weyer says *alibi Otis*, “elsewhere Otis”; Scot says “otherwise Otis.” They explicitly treat the names as variants. De Plancy’s choice to index the article under Otis does not create a second demon.

### 5. Artis and Botis

**Verdict: probable cognates, not proven identity.**

The order, sword, answers, favor, and thirty-six legions support the relationship. The absent viper and different rank/title require caution.

### 6. Two horns or three?

- Otius: three horns.
- Weyer onward: two horns.

The most likely explanation is textual revision or scribal simplification. Modern art occasionally restores three horns without identifying the Munich source.

### 7. Thirty-six or sixty legions?

- Artis and Otius: thirty-six.
- Weyer, Scot, and Goetia: sixty.

The inversion 36 ↔ 60 is not a simple Roman-numeral confusion. It may reflect a deliberate increase in status or contamination from another entry. No surviving note explains it.

### 8. President and Earl: which planet?

There is no single answer across all layers:

- President = Mercury in the manuscript observations.
- Earl = Venus and Moon in those same observations.
- Mathers reassigns Earl to Mars.
- Later occultists commonly report Mercury + Mars.
- Some reproductions of *777* visually or digitally confuse Mercury and Venus symbols.

Any table that gives only Mars, only Mercury, or only Venus without naming its system is incomplete.

### 9. Lauviah or Leuviah?

**Corrected finding:**

- Botis is paired with **Lauviah II, number 17**.
- **Leuviah is normally number 19**.
- Both occur amid unstable Latin transliterations of Hebrew triads.
- A source may use “Leuviah” for 17, but that should be reported as a spelling-system choice, not silently merged with angel 19.

### 10. Psalm 8:1 or 8:9?

Both are defensible references to nearly the same refrain. The discrepancy arises because Psalm 8 opens and closes with “O Lord our Lord, how excellent/admirable is thy name,” and because Hebrew, Protestant, Septuagint, and Vulgate numbering do not always align.

### 11. Gemini/Ten of Swords or Virgo/Nine of Disks?

Both exist, but in different systems:

- **Rudd’s same-number angel pairing:** 20°–25° Gemini; Ten of Swords.
- **Crowley’s independent Goetic-decan table:** 10°–20° Virgo; Nine of Disks.

Combining Lauviah with Virgo or claiming that *777* assigns Botis to a five-degree Gemini quinance collapses two separate overlays.

### 12. De Plancy portrait

No portrait was found. Sites attaching a Breton engraving to Botis are usually reusing another demon’s image or supplying modern art.

### 13. Seal symbolism

The seal is documented; its supposed symbolic explanation is not. Claims that its seven points encode planets, that its lower curve is a viper, or that the T-shapes signify reconciliation are recent interpretations.

### 14. “Enns,” candles, incense, stones, and offerings

The formula *Jedan hoesta noc ra Botis* and correspondences such as orange or white candles, lilies, storax, aquamarine, sulfur quartz, Saturn–Uranus, or particular offerings do not occur in the requested historical chain. They are modern Demonolatry, commercial occultism, or personal gnosis.

### 15. “Lord of fireflies and glory”

This title appears in very recent online occult writing. No premodern or early modern witness was located. It is a **modern invention or reported personal revelation**.

### 16. “Fallen angel”

Weyer and the *Goetia* call Botis a spirit, President, and Count/Earl. They do not narrate a fall from heaven or identify a former angelic order for him. Calling Botis a fallen angel generalizes later Christian demonology beyond the entry’s evidence.

### 17. Conflation with Bathin

Botis and Bathin are adjacent in the *Goetia*, and both have serpentine imagery. They are nevertheless distinct:

- Botis: viper, then horned swordsman; divination and reconciliation.
- Bathin: strong man with serpent’s tail riding a pale horse; herbs, stones, and rapid transport.

Confusion is modern and often results from copied tables or misplaced seals.

### 18. Rudd’s authorship

The angelic scheme is manuscript fact; authorship by the historical Thomas Rudd is an editorial hypothesis. “Rudd tradition” is safer than “Thomas Rudd personally invented the pairing.”

## Open questions

1. **What was the original name?**  
   Otius is the earliest clear form, but Artis may be older. No surviving archetype resolves whether the initial consonant was A-, O-, or B-.

2. **How exactly did Artis’s entry enter the Otius line?**  
   The shared sword, offices, position, and legion count are persuasive, but the missing viper and horned body leave room for contamination between neighboring catalogue entries.

3. **Where did Weyer obtain the viper-first sequence?**  
   It may have been in his lost *Liber Officiorum* exemplar, or it may represent expansion between the surviving fifteenth-century witnesses and 1577.

4. **Why did three horns become two?**  
   No textual rationale survives.

5. **Why did thirty-six legions become sixty?**  
   A copying inversion, deliberate augmentation, or cross-entry contamination are all possible; none is demonstrated.

6. **Who designed the Botis seal?**  
   It is present in the Lemegeton tradition but absent from known earlier *Offices* catalogues. No designer or explanatory alphabet is known.

7. **When was the seventy-two-demon order first fixed?**  
   The surviving Lemegeton manuscripts are seventeenth century, but their mutual relationships and lost exemplars remain only partly reconstructable.

8. **Who first paired the Goetic list numerically with the Shem angels?**  
   Harley 6483 is the central witness. Whether the scheme came from Thomas Rudd, another seventeenth-century compiler, or the early-eighteenth-century manuscript environment remains disputed.

9. **Which Lauviah spelling best represents angel 17’s Hebrew triad?**  
   Latin transliteration practices vary, and duplicate near-names at 11, 17, and 19 cause recurring errors. A critical study should distinguish the consonantal triplets rather than normalize only their Latin spellings.

10. **Did de Plancy intentionally rewrite the reconciliation office?**  
    His “as many friends as enemies” may be satire, loose adaptation, or mistranslation. No editorial note explains it.

11. **Was Botis ever independently invoked before modern printed occultism?**  
    The manuals prescribe operations, but an identifiable premodern practitioner’s diary or trial record naming an actual Botis operation has not been located.

12. **How much modern Botis imagery derives from direct experience claims rather than the 1904 text?**  
    Contemporary artists and practitioners frequently report forms absent from the sources, but those reports belong to personal religious experience and cannot reconstruct the historical entry.
