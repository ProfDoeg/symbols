# Valefor: Research Dossier

> **Method and evidentiary labels**
>
> - **Documented text** — stated by a named primary source.
> - **Manuscript reading** — found in a particular manuscript witness.
> - **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference based on textual comparison or historical research.
> - **Esoteric correspondence** — a relationship asserted within an occult system, not a historical or scientific fact.
> - **Tradition** — a claim transmitted by practitioners or later compilers.
> - **Disputed** — competing readings or interpretations exist.
> - **Legend** — supernatural narrative reported as part of the tradition.
> - **Modern invention/adaptation** — a recent addition not found in the early sources.
> - **Negative finding** — a specifically investigated claim for which no attesting source was located.
>
> The primary texts are public domain. Because editions and online transcriptions differ, quotations below identify the witness and page or folio wherever recoverable. Links lead to complete texts.

The basic textual record is unusually narrow. Unlike spirits that teach sciences, reveal treasure, grant love, answer questions, alter bodies, or confer invisibility, Valefor’s securely inherited office concerns familiarity and theft alone. Almost every broader modern portfolio is an extrapolation.

## The name and its origins

### 1. No demonstrated ancient god behind Valefor

**Negative finding.** No securely identified ancient deity, hero, place, Greek daimōn, biblical demon, or classical proper name lies behind *Valefar/Malaphar*. This distinguishes Valefor from transparent or plausible cases such as Bael/Baal, Astaroth/Astarte, Amon/Amun, Asmoday/Ashmedai, Belial/*bĕliyyaʿal*, Phoenix, or names constructed from recognizable Greek or Latin vocabulary.

No pre-Weyer source examined here contains a securely identifiable Valefor under this name or description:

- the *Testament of Solomon* contains Ornias, Beelzeboul, Asmodeus and thirty-six decan spirits, but not Valefor;
- the Munich necromancer’s handbook, Clm 849, contains several names or ritual types ancestral to later spirit catalogues, but no established Valefor;
- the *Livre des esperitz* contains numerous close precursors to Weyer’s spirits but no Valefor/Malaphar;
- the surviving Abramelin lists contain many corrupt spirit names, but no generally accepted Valefor identification.

Therefore the earliest **presently verifiable printed attestation** is Weyer’s 1577 *Pseudomonarchia*. A lost or unlocated manuscript source may have preceded it, because Weyer says he used a work called *Liber officiorum spirituum*, *Officium spirituum*, or *Liber dictus Empto. Salomonis*. That possibility is not the same as a surviving attestation. [Weyer’s text and editorial introduction](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

### 2. The source-chain spellings

Joseph H. Peterson’s comparative spelling table gives the following transmission:

| Witness | Reading |
|---|---|

| Scot, 1584 | **Valefar, alias Malephar** |
| Sloane 3825 | **Valefar** |
| Sloane 3648 | **Valefor** |
| Sloane 2731 | **Valefor / Valefar** |
| Harley 6483 | **Valefar** |

This table is available in Peterson’s [comparative Goetic-spirit index](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia_spirits.pdf). Peterson’s 2001 critical text uses **Valefar** as its editorial headword while recording *VALEFOR* and the Weyer/Scot aliases in the apparatus. [Peterson, *The Lesser Key of Solomon*, p. 9](https://jamesparsonslakebucket.s3-us-west-1.amazonaws.com/text/Peterson_Joseph_H_The_Lesser_Key_of_Solomon.pdf)

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The changes *-far/-for* and *Malaphar/Malephar* are compatible with ordinary scribal and editorial instability. They do not demonstrate that the spellings once denoted separate spirits. No early witness treats Valefar and Malaphar as two beings; Weyer expressly gives the latter as an alias.

### 3. Proposed etymologies

#### Latin *valere* plus *fur*

**Disputed modern proposal.** Internet etymologies frequently divide the name into Latin *valēre*—“to be strong, be capable”—and *fur*, “thief,” producing “strong thief.” A related jeu de mots uses *vale*, “farewell,” and *fur*, “thief”: “farewell, thief,” supposedly anticipating the gallows.

The proposals are attractive because Weyer calls the spirit *Dux fortis* and associates him with thieves. But they lack:

- an early source offering that derivation;
- a regular historical formation explaining *Valefar*;
- an intermediate spelling showing that *fur* was intended;
- independent evidence that the name was coined in Latin.

They should therefore be labeled **modern folk etymology**, not recovered ancient meaning.

#### *Malaphar* from “evil,” Arabic, Hebrew, or Greek

**Disputed modern proposals.** Modern sites variously derive *Mala-* from Latin *malus*, propose unattested Arabic expressions, or compare Hebrew words for teaching or speech. No philologically documented derivation was located. These explanations appear after the grimoire tradition and generally begin with Valefor’s reported office, then invent a matching linguistic origin.

#### Baal or another ancient deity

**Unsupported claim.** Occasional modern pages propose that Valefor is a Christianized Baal or another ancient Near Eastern deity. No historical name-series, cult title, inscription, or scholarly argument connecting *Valefar/Malaphar* to Baal has been identified. Similarity to the general history of other Goetic names is not evidence for this particular name.

### 4. The meaning of *goetia*

The title does have a classical etymology even though Valefor’s name does not. Latin *goētia* continues Greek γοητεία, “sorcery” or “enchantment,” from γόης, an enchanter or sorcerer; ancient explanations associated the family with *goaō*, lamenting or wailing. That is the origin of the genre-name, not of Valefor. [Etymonline’s source summary](https://www.etymonline.com/word/goetia)

## The description across the sources

## 1. Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* (1577)

### Date and context

**Documented history.** Weyer’s *De praestigiis daemonum* first appeared in 1563, but the *Pseudomonarchia Daemonum* was added to the **fifth edition of 1577**. The widespread statement “Valefor first appeared in 1563” confuses the first edition of the parent book with the later appendix.

Weyer presented the catalogue within a polemic against witch trials and demonic imposture, not as an endorsement of every claim it contained. He also said he had omitted or displaced ritual material so that the text could not readily be used. His stated source was a *Book of the Offices of Spirits*. [Peterson’s Latin text, introduction and variants](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

### Number

Valefar is **14th** in Weyer’s actual sequence:

1. Bael  
2. Agares  
3. Marbas  
4. Pruflas  
5. Aamon  
6. Barbatos  
7. Buer  
8. Gusion  
9. Botis  
10. Bathin  
11. Purson  
12. Eligor  
13. Leraje  
14. Valefar

The later *Goetia* inserts Vassago at number 3, omits Pruflas, and rearranges parts of the list, placing Valefor at number 6.

### Latin, 1577 folio edition, p. 915

> *Valefar, aliàs Malaphar, Dux est fortis, forma leonis prodiens & capite latronis. Familiaritatem parit suis, donec laqueo suspendantur. Decem præsidet legionibus.*

Literal gloss: “Valefar, otherwise Malaphar, is a strong duke, coming forth in the form of a lion and with the head of a robber. He produces familiarity with his people until they are suspended by a noose. He presides over ten legions.” [Latin text at the 1577 pagination](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

### What Weyer does and does not say

**Documented text.**

- Rank: strong duke.
- Appearance: lion-form with *caput latronis*.
- Office: establishes a familiar relationship but brings the persons involved to hanging.
- Legions: ten.
- Seal: none.
- Second form: none.
- Teaching, treasure, love, prophecy, invisibility or transmutation: none.
- Specific hour: none in Valefar’s entry.

### *Capite latronis*

**Disputed reading.** The straightforward meaning of *latro, latronis* is robber, brigand or thief. Peterson notes a possible semantic association with barking, which helps explain the later *lowring/lowing/bellowing* chain. Nevertheless, Weyer’s phrase most naturally means “with the head of a thief.” [Peterson’s critical note, p. 9](https://jamesparsonslakebucket.s3-us-west-1.amazonaws.com/text/Peterson_Joseph_H_The_Lesser_Key_of_Solomon.pdf)

## 2. Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft* (1584)

### Context

**Documented history.** Scot incorporated an English version of Weyer’s catalogue in Book XVI of *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*. Peterson notes that the translation may have been made or assisted by Abraham Fleming. Like Weyer, Scot published in a skeptical and anti-persecution context rather than as an uncomplicated manual of demonic devotion. [Scot transcription alongside Weyer](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

### English, first edition, Book XVI, chapter 2, p. 379

The entry opens:

> “Valefar, alias Malephar, is a strong duke, comming foorth in the shape of a lion, and the head of a theefe…”

Scot then says that Valefar remains very familiar with those he meets until he has brought them to the gallows, and that he rules ten legions. Peterson’s transcription adds a bracketed alternative, “or barking,” reflecting the textual problem behind the later *lowring/bellowing* readings. [Complete Scot entry](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

### Comparison with Weyer

**Documented text.**

- *Malaphar* becomes **Malephar**.
- *capite latronis* is understood primarily as “head of a thief.”
- Weyer’s compressed statement about familiarity and hanging becomes explicit: the spirit is familiar until he brings the associates to the gallows.
- Rank and ten legions remain unchanged.

The later shorthand “familiar to thieves” is defensible as a summary, but neither Weyer nor Scot says simply that Valefor protects thieves. Their point is darker: the friendship facilitates criminality and ultimately ruin.

## 3. The *Ars Goetia* manuscripts

### Sloane MS 3825

Peterson’s transcription is based principally on Sloane 3825. The Valefar entry is at **folio 101v** and dates from the late seventeenth or early eighteenth-century Lemegeton transmission:

> “The 6th spirit is Valefar—he is a Mighty Duke, & appeareth in ye form of a Lion wh a mans head Lowring…”

It continues that he is a good familiar but tempts those familiar with him to steal, governs ten legions, and that his seal is to be worn constantly if his familiarity is obtained—“Else not.” [Peterson’s manuscript-based *Goetia*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm)

**Manuscript reading.**

- Number: 6.
- Rank: mighty duke.
- Appearance: lion with a man’s head, “lowring.”
- Office: good familiar; tempts familiars to steal.
- Legions: ten.
- Seal rule: wear it constantly if maintaining his familiarity; otherwise do not.
- No commanded second form.

“Lowring” means scowling, threatening or raging. Peterson’s edition notes that the reading may stand somewhere between Weyer’s robber-head and the later idea of an animal lowing.

### Sloane MS 2731

**Manuscript reading.** Sloane 2731, dated 1686/1687, preserves both **Valefor** and **Valefar** in its transmission. Its difficult appearance reading is reported as “a Mansy heade Lowring,” with a marginal alternative from another copy approximately “with Amases head Lowing.” These are probably corrupt attempts to recover an already obscure exemplar, not evidence for a distinct zoological form. [Peterson, critical apparatus, p. 9](https://jamesparsonslakebucket.s3-us-west-1.amazonaws.com/text/Peterson_Joseph_H_The_Lesser_Key_of_Solomon.pdf)

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The sequence may have developed approximately as follows:

```text
Weyer: capite latronis
        │
        ├── Scot: head of a thief
        │
        └── damaged/cursive exemplar
              ├── man's head lowring
              ├── “Mansy/Amases” head lowing
              └── ass's head, bellowing
```

This is a reconstruction, not a demonstrable stemma. It does, however, explain the coexistence of thief, man, lowering/lowing and ass-head traditions more economically than supposing several independent revelations.

### Harley MS 6483 and the Rudd material

Harley 6483 is dated **1712–1713** and entitled *Liber malorum spirituum, seu Goetia*. It is the latest major manuscript witness used in Peterson’s survey. It contains additions and working arrangements associated with “Dr Rudd,” but it is not securely an autograph written by Thomas Rudd himself. It is better called **Rudd material copied in Harley 6483**. [Peterson’s Lemegeton manuscript description](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm)

The spirit is **Valefar**, number 6. Its distinguishing innovation is not a new office or appearance but the matching of every Goetic spirit with one of the seventy-two Shem angels. For Valefar that angel is **Lelahel**.

### The seal in the manuscripts

**Documented object.** Weyer and Scot provide no spirit seals. The seals appear in the Lemegeton manuscript tradition, and Peterson remarks that he knows no corresponding spirit-sigil series in the earlier *Office of Spirits* manuscripts. [Peterson’s *Goetia* introduction](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm)

The Valefor seal is best described topologically:

- a short horizontal headbar;
- a dominant vertical descending stroke;
- paired lateral strokes curling or hooking away from the center;
- small changes of curvature, closure and line length among copies;
- no pictorial lion, ass, thief or gallows;
- no intrinsic outer circle in most manuscript forms—the surrounding circular frame familiar from printed occult art is often an editorial presentation device.

**Textual limitation.** The manuscripts themselves do not describe the geometry in words; they draw it. Consequently any prose description is an *ekphrasis*, not manuscript wording. Peterson’s apparatus does not identify a second, independently authorized Valefor seal. The published notice that Mathers supplied two forms for eight spirits does not include Valefor among the securely doubled seals.

The 1904 form can be viewed in this [public-domain vector rendering](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:06-valefor-sigilo-ars-goetia.svg). A. E. Waite’s 1911 printed form is reproduced [here](https://www.fromoldbooks.org/AEWaite-CeremonialMagic/pages/197c-seal-of-valefor/).

## 4. Mathers–Crowley, *The Goetia* (1904)

### Edition

*The Goetia: The Lesser Key of Solomon the King* was issued at Boleskine in 1904 by the Society for the Propagation of Religious Truth. S. L. MacGregor Mathers supplied the translation or editorial text; Aleister Crowley edited, introduced and supplemented it.

### Text, p. 23

The printed entry begins:

> “VALEFOR.—The Sixth Spirit is Valefor. He is a mighty Duke, and appeareth in the shape of a Lion with an Ass’s Head…”

It adds “bellowing,” calls him a good familiar who tempts his familiars to steal, gives ten legions, and—unlike Sloane 3825’s “else not”—says the seal is to be worn whether or not the operator wants him as a familiar. [Complete 1904 entry](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks05.htm)

### The important alterations

| Feature | Sloane 3825 | Mathers–Crowley |
|---|---|---|
| Name | Valefar | Valefor |
| Head | man’s head | ass’s head |
| Vocal/action word | lowring | bellowing |
| Seal instruction | wear constantly if familiar; otherwise not | wear whether a familiar is desired or not |
| Legions | 10 | 10 |

**Disputed textual development.** The famous “lion with an ass’s head, bellowing” is not Weyer's description. It is the 1904 printed resolution of an unstable manuscript phrase. It may arise from interpreting “Amases/Mansy head lowing” as “ass’s head, bellowing.” The ass-headed iconography is therefore late, though it has become the dominant modern image.

### Peterson’s 2001 edition

Peterson restores **Valefar** as the editorial headword and prints the Sloane 3825 “man’s head Lowring” reading. His note lays out the principal alternatives:

- Sloane witness: *VALEFOR*;
- Weyer: *Valefar, alias Malaphar*;
- Scot: *Valefar, alias Malephar*;
- Sloane 2731: corrupt “Mansy/Amases” readings;
- Mathers/Crowley: “bellowing”;
- Scot/Weyer: thief’s head.

That makes Peterson’s edition the most useful single textual checkpoint, although it does not settle what the lost immediate exemplar originally said. [Peterson 2001, p. 9](https://jamesparsonslakebucket.s3-us-west-1.amazonaws.com/text/Peterson_Joseph_H_The_Lesser_Key_of_Solomon.pdf)

## Number and order: Weyer’s 69 and the Goetia’s 72

**Documented comparison.** Weyer has 69 spirits. The *Goetia* has 72 because it:

- adds **Vassago** as number 3;
- adds **Seere** as number 70;
- adds **Dantalion** as number 71;
- adds **Andromalius** as number 72;
- omits Weyer’s **Pruflas/Bufas**.

Thus \(69 + 4 - 1 = 72\). Peterson’s parallel table documents the changes. [Weyer–Goetia comparison](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm)

Some copies or summaries call Weyer’s total 68 because a printer assigned the same number to two consecutive spirits. That is a numbering error, not evidence for a distinct 68-spirit recension.

Valefor’s movement from Weyer 14 to Goetia 6 is not produced by the four final additions. It results from the *Goetia*’s early reordering and its omission of Pruflas.

## Rank, metal, planet and operating time

The *Goetia*’s “Observations” prescribe metals by rank:

| Rank | Planet | Metal |
|---|---|---|
| King | Sun | Gold |
| Marquis | Moon | Silver |
| Duke | Venus | Copper |
| Prelate or prince in this table | Jupiter | Tin |
| Knight | Saturn | Lead |
| President | Mercury | Mercury |
| Earl/count | Moon and Venus | Silver and copper |

Valefor is therefore **Venus/copper**, not Jupiter/tin. The latter occasionally appears in unsourced web tables through confusion between dukes and princes.

The same observations say dukes are to be bound **from sunrise until noon in clear weather**. The text does not assign Valefor a unique minute, weekday or calendar date. [Goetia’s rank metals and operating periods](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm)

**Later esoteric inference.** Friday and the planetary hour of Venus are reasonable additions within Renaissance planetary magic, but they are not stated in Valefor’s entry. Likewise, green, copper-colored candles, roses, specific incense, cardinal directions and modern “enns” are later systems.

## The angel: Lelahel

## 1. Formation from Exodus 14:19–21

**Documented textual construction.** Exodus 14:19, 14:20 and 14:21 each contain seventy-two Hebrew letters in the form used by this tradition. The verses are written in three rows:

1. verse 19 in its normal direction;
2. verse 20 reversed;
3. verse 21 in its normal direction.

Reading vertically produces seventy-two triads of three letters. This alternating direction is often called boustrophedon. Rashi’s commentary on *Sukkah* 45a describes the construction: first letter of the first verse, last letter of the middle verse, and first letter of the third, then onward. [Rashi on *Sukkah* 45a](https://www.sefaria.org/Rashi_on_Sukkah.45); [Jewish Encyclopedia, “Names of God”](https://www.jewishencyclopedia.com/articles/11305-names-)

The sixth triad is:

> **ל־ל־ה — LLH**

Christian Kabbalistic authors added **אל**, *El*, producing:

> **ללהאל — Lelahel / Lelaël**

### Important historical distinction

**Documented/disputed terminology.** *Shem ha-meforash*—“the explicit, distinguished or fully expressed Name”—does not always mean this particular list of seventy-two angels. Jewish sources use the expression in connection with the Tetragrammaton and also discuss names of twelve, forty-two or seventy-two letters. The seventy-two **triads** are Jewish; treating all seventy-two as individually personified angels with *-el/-yah* suffixes is particularly characteristic of Christian Kabbalah and later Hermetic practice.

Ibn Ezra also records a different explanation of a “seventy-two-letter name”: the expanded spelling of the letters of YHWH whose numerical value totals seventy-two. The Exodus-triad construction is therefore important but not the only historical meaning of the term.

## 2. From *Sefer Raziel* to Christian Kabbalah

### *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh*

**Documented tradition.** The composite medieval Hebrew magical work known as *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* gives great importance to the seventy-twofold divine name. The work circulated in manuscript before its first printed edition at Amsterdam in 1701. Its legendary frame attributes revelation to the angel Raziel and ultimately to Adam, but this is **legendary authorship**, not a historical date of composition.

### Rashi and medieval Jewish transmission

Rashi, who died in 1105, provides an early explicit description of constructing the names from Exodus. The *Bahir*, the Zoharic tradition and later magical compilations helped transmit the scheme. These witnesses concern divine letter-combinations; they do not yet establish every later angelic meaning, zodiacal degree, Psalm and personality trait.

### Johann Reuchlin, 1517

**Christian-Kabbalistic development.** In *De arte cabalistica* (1517), Johann Reuchlin treated the triads as angelic names by adding *El* or *Yah*. This is a major bridge from Jewish name-mysticism to the individualized “seventy-two angels” of Western ceremonial magic.

### Blaise de Vigenère

Vigenère reproduced and developed the seventy-two angelic names in the later sixteenth century. His tables and associated characters were used by later Christian Kabbalists and, according to Skinner and Rankine, lie behind parts of Rudd’s arrangement.

### Athanasius Kircher, 1652–1654

Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus*, especially the *Cabala Hebraeorum*, presented the seventy-two names within an enormous Christian universalizing scheme. His table linked divine names, angels and seventy-two supposed national or linguistic forms of the name of God. [Kircher, *Cabala Hebraeorum*](https://www.esotericarchives.com/kircher/cabala.htm)

Kircher is an important source for the later association of Lelahel with an allegedly Ethiopian divine name, **Abgd**. That is a seventeenth-century Christian antiquarian claim, not accepted Ethiopian linguistics.

## 3. Rudd’s controlling pair

**Esoteric correspondence, documented in Harley 6483.** Rudd’s arrangement places angel 1 against demon 1, angel 2 against demon 2, and so forth. Consequently:

> **Lelahel 6 constrains Valefar 6.**

The operator constructs a double-sided or paired talisman: the demon’s character on one face, and on the reverse the controlling angel’s character, name and Psalm verse. Angelic names also enter the breastplate and brass-vessel arrangements. The underlying principle is *ligatio*, binding: the higher or divine power constrains the corresponding lower spirit.

This sequential pairing is absent from Weyer, Scot and the ordinary Sloane *Goetia*. It is a Rudd-system addition, not an original biblical pairing and not demonstrably part of the lost *Liber Officiorum*. Skinner and Rankine’s 2007 edition explicitly presents the matching angels as the practical key to binding the demons. [Publisher’s description and bibliography](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Goetia_of_Dr_Rudd.html?id=AFx3ngEACAAJ); [British bibliographic record](https://obnb.uk/p13944751-the-goetia-of-dr-rudd-the-angels-demons-of-liber-malorum-spirituum-seu-goetia-lemegeton-clavicula-salomonis-with-a-study-of-the-techniques-of-evocation-in-the-context-of-the-angel-magic-traditio)

## 4. Lenain’s Lelahel, 1823

Lazare Lenain’s *La science cabalistique*, published at Amiens in 1823, gives the fullest influential nineteenth-century notice. The entry begins on p. 49.

### Name and meaning

> “6e. Lelahel. Son attribut: Dieu louable.”

“Sixth: Lelahel. His attribute: Praiseworthy God.” [Lenain scan, p. 49](https://iapsop.com/ssoc/1823__lenain___science_cabalistique.pdf)

**Tradition, not philological translation.** *Dieu louable* is Lenain’s attributed divine quality. The triad LLH does not transparently translate as that English phrase.

### Zodiacal arc

Lenain says the ray begins at the **26th degree and continues through the 30th inclusive**. In modern zero-based notation this is normally written:

> **25°00′–29°59′ Aries**

It is the second five-degree sector of Aries’s third decan.

### Decan genius and planet

Lenain associates the third decan with the genius **Asentacer** and the influence of **Venus**. That Venus is the ruler of the third decan of Aries in the Chaldean decan sequence. It happens also to be the planet assigned to a Goetic duke, but the two routes to Venus are historically independent.

### Five annual days

Lenain gives:

- 25 March
- 5 June
- 15 August
- 27 October
- 7 January

Later editions and derivative “guardian angel” calendars shift some dates by a day because they convert degrees into civil dates differently or attempt to adjust the tropical equinox.

### Daily period

> **1:40 a.m.–2:00 a.m.**

This belongs to a scheme dividing the twenty-four-hour day into seventy-two periods of twenty minutes. Later authors sometimes reckon the intervals from local sunrise instead of civil midnight; that change must be specified.

### Psalm

Lenain designates the “11th verse of Psalm 9” and prints:

> *Psallite Domino, qui habitat in Sion: annuntiate inter gentes studia ejus.*

The sense is: “Sing praises to the Lord who dwells in Zion; proclaim his deeds among the peoples.”

The same sentence is numbered Psalm 9:11 in the KJV and Psalm 9:12 in several Catholic/Vulgate presentations. The wording, rather than an isolated verse number, is the reliable identifier.

### Offices

**Esoteric tradition documented by Lenain.**

- invoked to acquire “lights”—illumination or understanding;
- invoked to heal illnesses;
- rules love;
- rules renown;
- rules sciences and arts;
- rules fortune;
- those born under the influence love being spoken of and acquire celebrity through talents and deeds;
- the contrary genius rules excessive ambition and the desire to rise above others, especially by illicit fortune-seeking.

These angelic offices should not be retrojected into Valefor’s early description.

## 5. Golden Dawn, Papus, Ambelain and Haziel

### Golden Dawn and Mathers

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn incorporated the Shem angels into its late nineteenth-century correspondence system. Two angels occupy each ten-degree zodiacal decan, and each decan corresponds to a minor-arcana pip card. Lelahel, at 25°–30° Aries, therefore occupies the latter half of the **Four of Wands** decan.

Mathers’s *Kabbalah Unveiled* explains that adding *El* or *Yah* to the triliteral names generates the seventy-two angelic names. The Golden Dawn use does not, by itself, prove that the same angel-demon opposition was practiced throughout Jewish Kabbalah.

### Papus

Papus—Gérard Encausse—reproduced Lenain-derived material in *La Cabbale* (1903). In 1909, Dujols and Thomas issued a corrected reprint of Lenain with a letter-preface by Papus under the auspices of the Ordre Kabbalistique de la Rose-Croix. [Bibliothèque nationale de France record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb30789329z)

Papus was therefore principally a **transmitter and legitimizer** of Lenain’s system, not the originator of Lelahel’s 1823 meanings.

### Robert Ambelain

Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* appeared in Paris in 1951. [BnF catalogue record](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb317160320)

Ambelain repeats Lenain’s Lelahel entry—*Praiseworthy God*, degrees 26–30, 1:40–2:00, healing, illumination, love, renown, arts, sciences and fortune—and adds or systematizes practical talismanic material. [English translation of Ambelain’s Lelahel section](https://www.magicgatebg.com/Books/INDEXII/The%20Shemhamphorash.pdf)

Ambelain also criticized Lenain for running the names mechanically around the zodiac without fully respecting elemental groupings. His corrections and angelic seals are twentieth-century Hermetic elaborations, not recovered biblical instructions.

### Haziel

The French author writing as Haziel popularized the seventy-two beings as personal guardian angels, often assigning three angels to each person—physical, emotional and intellectual—according to birth date and hour. *Le grand livre des invocations et des exhortations* appeared in 1990 and presents prayers to the seventy-two “Anges Servants.” [Bibliographic description](https://export.side.fr/fr/9782850900051-le-grand-livre-des-invocations-et-des-exhortations)

**Modern esoteric development.** The familiar claim “Lelahel is the guardian angel of everyone born 15–20 April” belongs chiefly to this modern calendrical angelology. It is downstream of Lenain and related French occultism, not a statement in Exodus or Weyer.

### Crowley’s *777*

Crowley’s correspondence tables place Goetic spirit 6 in the **cadent decan of Taurus**. The table’s magical image is the already Mathers-derived lion with an ass’s head, bellowing. [Crowley, *Liber 777*, Goetic demon columns](https://hermetic.com/_media/93beast.fea.st/files/section1/777/liber_777.pdf)

The corresponding Golden Dawn minor card is:

> **Seven of Pentacles/Disks — Saturn in Taurus — 20°–30° Taurus**

The first thirty-six demons serve as the day series and spirits 37–72 repeat the thirty-six decans as a night series. Thus each pip/decan receives two Goetic spirits:

> **Valefor 6 by day; Vepar 42 by night.**

This is separate from Rudd’s angel-demon numbering. Crowley did not derive Valefor’s Taurus placement from Lelahel’s Aries quinance.

### Four of Wands versus Seven of Disks

Both cards can appear in responsible Valefor studies, but for different reasons:

- **Four of Wands:** Lelahel’s 25°–30° Aries quinance belongs to the card’s 20°–30° Aries decan.
- **Seven of Disks:** Crowley’s independent Goetic demon table places Valefor in 20°–30° Taurus.

A source that gives Valefor “25°–30° Aries, Four of Wands” is normally using **Rudd’s same-number angelic opposition**. A source that gives “20°–30° Taurus, Seven of Disks” is using **Crowley/Golden Dawn demon decans**.

### Enochian overlays

**Documented context, negative item-specific finding.** Harley 6483 contains Rudd-associated material concerning Dee-derived tables, and the Skinner–Rankine edition discusses the *Tabula Sancta cum Tabulis Enochi*. That does not make Valefor an Enochian spirit.

Crowley’s *777* allows virtually every row to be compared with Enochian, elemental and tarot columns; later authors consequently map Valefor into Enochian tablet subangles or aethyric systems. No unique Valefor–Enochian identification was located in Weyer, Scot, Sloane 2731, Sloane 3825 or the basic Rudd spirit entry. Such identifications should be labeled **later synthetic overlays**.

### Other modern overlays

The following are common but post-Goetic:

- a personal “enn” or mantra;
- fixed colors beyond copper/Venus symbolism;
- a unique cardinal direction;
- qliphothic spheres;
- chakras;
- stones, herbs and perfumes assigned directly to Valefor;
- “shadow-work” psychologies;
- a gendered personal identity;
- claims that Valefor teaches occult wisdom, forces confessions, drains enemies, or protects sleepers.

These may be genuine components of particular modern practices, but they are not recoverable from the early textual record.

## Iconography

## 1. Before the *Goetia*

**Negative finding.** Weyer prints no portrait or individual seal for Valefar. Scot likewise provides no portrait. Since no securely identified pre-Weyer Valefor survives, there is no ancient statue, cult image, coin, vase painting or medieval bestiary figure that can responsibly be called an image of Valefor.

## 2. The manuscript seal

The earliest secure iconographic object is the abstract *Goetia* character. It is an identifier or “signature,” not a representation of the spirit’s body.

Across manuscript and printed copies, the principal variations are graphic:

- thickness and angle of the top bar;
- length of the central downstroke;
- open versus tighter terminal curls;
- slight asymmetry in the lateral hooks;
- compression or stretching when later printers fit the seal into a circle.

No manuscript evidence establishes that these small copyist differences represent separate theological forms.

## 3. Collin de Plancy

### First edition, 1818

Collin de Plancy’s *Dictionnaire infernal* first appeared in two volumes in 1818. The work underwent major ideological and textual revision over six editions. The first edition is documented in library records, but the exact Valafar wording is more readily verifiable in the later full-text editions. [1818 volume record](https://books.google.it/books?id=TcY5AAAAcAAJ); [edition index](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/1re_%C3%A9dition,_1818)

### Sixth edition, 1863, p. 676

De Plancy’s entry begins:

> “Valafar ou Malafar, grand et puissant duc de l’empire infernal.”

It then claims that he appears as an angel or as a lion with the head and feet of a goose and a hare’s tail; knows past and future; gives genius and audacity; and commands thirty-six legions. [Complete French entry](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.%2C_1863/Valafar)

### The Ipes conflation

**Documented textual error / scholarly reconstruction.** Nearly every substantive feature after the name is Weyer’s description of **Ipes/Ipos**, not Valefar:

| De Plancy’s “Valafar” | Weyer/Goetia Ipes |
|---|---|
| angelic form | appears as an angel |
| lion form | lion or lion-headed |
| goose features | goose’s feet |
| hare’s tail | hare’s tail |
| knows past and future | knows past, present and future |
| gives genius/audacity | makes men witty and bold |
| 36 legions | 36 legions |

Weyer’s real Valefar has ten legions and the thief-headed lion. De Plancy’s footnote nevertheless attributes the corrupt account to Wierus. This is one of the clearest demonstrable conflations in the spirit’s source history. Compare [Weyer’s adjacent Valefar and Ipes entries](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm) with [de Plancy’s Valafar](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.%2C_1863/Valafar).

### Louis Le Breton portrait

**Negative finding.** The 1863 edition contains hundreds of woodcuts, including seventy-two celebrated demon portraits drawn by Louis Le Breton. However, the illustrated index contains an **Ipos** portrait and no securely labeled, dedicated Valafar/Valefor portrait. [Commons category of the 1863 demon drawings](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/Category:Drawings_of_demons_from_Dictionnaire_Infernal,_6th_edition)

Online images sometimes attach the Ipos engraving—or unrelated modern lion-ass art—to Valefor because de Plancy’s Valafar text is itself copied from Ipos. That is a modern consequence of the conflation, not evidence that Le Breton designed a separate Valefor portrait.

## 4. Modern art

The dominant modern figure—a muscular lion-bodied creature with a donkey’s head—derives from Mathers–Crowley’s 1904 reading, not Weyer. Other artists combine:

- a human or masked thief’s face;
- gallows or rope;
- coins and stolen treasure;
- Venusian copper or green;
- the manuscript seal;
- hybrid wings inspired by *Final Fantasy X*.

These are artistic interpretations. No early text gives Valefor wings, treasure, coins, horns, weapons or multiple heads.

A. E. Waite’s claim that Valefor appears as a “many-headed lion” is another late alteration; his note concedes that Weyer gives a hunter’s or thief’s head. [Waite, *Book of Ceremonial Magic*, pp. 197–198](https://www.fromoldbooks.org/AEWaite-CeremonialMagic/pages/197c-seal-of-valefor/)

## People and works

## Chronological chain

### Ancient and medieval Solomonic background

- **Testament of Solomon** — supplies the broad legend of Solomon commanding demons with a divinely authorized ring. It does not name Valefor.
- **Munich handbook, Clm 849** — a fifteenth-century necromantic manual preserving rituals and spirits comparable to the later grimoire world. No Valefor is identified.
- **Livre des esperitz** — the oldest surviving substantial French spirit catalogue, preserved in Cambridge, Trinity College O.8.29. Many of its spirits anticipate Weyer, but Valefor is absent. [French text](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre_des_esperitz/Texte_entier)
- **Liber Officiorum Spirituum** — the source-family Weyer says he used. Its precise exemplar is lost, and surviving Office-of-Spirits manuscripts do not establish an earlier Valefor.
- **Book of Abramelin** — supplies extensive hierarchical name lists, but no secure Valefor ancestor.

These works demonstrate a long Solomonic and spirit-catalogue background. They do not prove that Valefor himself is ancient.

### Johann Weyer

Weyer is the first verifiable recorder of **Valefar alias Malaphar**. His 1577 catalogue fixed the rank, lion-form, thief/robber head, fatal familiarity and ten legions.

### Reginald Scot

Scot transmitted the name into English in 1584 and made the thief-and-gallows reading explicit. His book greatly increased the accessibility of Weyer’s catalogue to later English compilers.

### Anonymous Lemegeton compilers

Seventeenth-century compilers reorganized the Weyer–Scot catalogue, supplied individual seals and ritual machinery, changed Valefor to number 6 and altered the narrative from “brings them to the gallows” to “good familiar who tempts them to steal.”

### Thomas Rudd and Harley 6483

The Rudd tradition added the sequential Shem-angels as constraining powers. Whether Thomas Rudd personally wrote every relevant note is uncertain because the surviving Harley manuscript is a later copy.

### Collin de Plancy

De Plancy included Valafar from 1818 onward but, by the securely accessible 1863 text, had conflated the entry with Ipes. His account created the anomalous thirty-six-legion, goose-footed, hare-tailed Valafar.

### S. L. MacGregor Mathers and Aleister Crowley

Their 1904 edition established the now-famous spelling **Valefor** and the lion with an ass’s head, bellowing. Crowley’s *777*, first published in 1909, added the day-demon/cadent-Taurus/Seven-of-Disks complex.

### A. E. Waite

Waite reproduced the seal and abbreviated the description in *The Book of Ceremonial Magic* (1911), calling Valefor a many-headed lion while acknowledging Weyer’s thief-headed reading in a note.

### Papus

Papus transmitted Lenain’s angelology through *La Cabbale* and his preface to the 1909 Lenain reprint.

### Robert Ambelain

Ambelain’s 1951 *La Kabbale pratique* repeated and operationalized the French Shem tradition, including Lelahel’s degrees, verse, hour and offices.

### Haziel

Haziel’s late twentieth-century books popularized Lelahel as a personal guardian angel of particular birthdays and built longer prayers and psychological profiles around Lenain’s brief notice.

### Poke Runyon

Runyon’s *The Book of Solomon’s Magick* reproduces the angelic name and Psalm series, including Lelahel’s *Psallite Domino…* verse. His work helped restore the Rudd-style “controlling angel” concept in modern ritual practice. [Runyon text, angelic Psalm table](https://www.esis-corner888.com/_downloads/e767b40076acec7cf37baad61cb8cf35)

No independently documented, Valefor-specific visionary record by Runyon was located; his importance is primarily systemic.

### Lon Milo DuQuette

DuQuette co-edited or coauthored modern practical presentations of the *Goetia*, notably *Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia*. He helped popularize psychological and ceremonial interpretations of the catalogue, but no uniquely Valefor-centered operation or historical discovery was located.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* (Golden Hoard Press, 2007; 448 pp.) transcribed Harley 6483 with related Harley 6482, Sloane 3824 and Wellcome 3203 material. Its major contribution is documenting the paired angelic seals and binding theory rather than treating “Shemhamphorash” as an unexplained word. [Book record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Goetia_of_Dr_Rudd.html?id=AFx3ngEACAAJ)

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 Weiser edition remains the principal accessible critical comparison of the Lemegeton manuscripts. For Valefor it preserves the decisive “man’s head Lowring” reading and lays out the thief/lowing/bellowing variants.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent argued more generally for continuity between early modern goetic practice and older Mediterranean traditions of daimones, the dead and chthonic ritual. That approach is relevant to the genre. No sourced Stratton-Kent derivation of Valefor from a particular ancient god was located; his broader thesis should not be converted into an item-specific etymology.

### Frater Achad

**Negative finding.** No reliable Valefor-specific working, doctrine or correspondence by Frater Achad was located. His relevance is general Thelemic and Qabalistic history, not demonstrable authorship of this spirit’s attributes.

### Historians and scholars

- **Jean-Patrice Boudet and Julien Véronèse** illuminate medieval spirit catalogues and texts such as the *Livre des esperitz*.
- **Richard Kieckhefer** edited and studied the Munich necromancer’s handbook.
- **Jan R. Veenstra** has examined learned magic, spirits and medieval intellectual contexts.
- **Owen Davies** provides the broader history of grimoires, print and circulation.
- **Joseph Peterson** supplies the item-level textual comparison most useful here.

Their work supports a history of textual assembly and transmission rather than an unbroken archive of ancient demon biographies.

## Controversies and disputes

## 1. Was Valefor in Weyer’s 1563 edition?

**No.** *De praestigiis daemonum* dates to 1563, but the appended *Pseudomonarchia* containing Valefar is first verified in the 1577 fifth edition. “1563” is a common bibliographical shortcut that became a factual error.

## 2. Is Valefor Weyer 14 or Goetia 6?

Both, in different catalogues:

- Weyer: 14th in the sequence;
- *Goetia*: 6th.

Tables that call Valefor “Weyer 6” are aligning Weyer’s corresponding entry with Goetia number 6 rather than reporting Weyer’s actual sequence number.

## 3. Does Weyer have 68 or 69 spirits?

The intended catalogue contains **69**. The “68” total derives from duplicated printed numbering in some copies or modern counts, not the disappearance of another named spirit.

## 4. Thief’s head, man’s head, or ass’s head?

| Reading | Earliest secure witness | Status |
|---|---|---|
| thief/robber’s head | Weyer, 1577 | Earliest |
| thief’s head | Scot, 1584 | Direct translation |
| man’s head, lowering | Sloane 3825 | Principal manuscript reading |
| “Mansy/Amases” head, lowing | Sloane 2731 apparatus | Corrupt manuscript alternatives |
| ass’s head, bellowing | Mathers–Crowley, 1904 | Late editorial resolution |
| many-headed lion | Waite, 1911 | Late alteration |
| goose-headed/footed lion with hare tail | de Plancy | Ipes conflation |

No evidence establishes that Valefor has two successive forms. The multiple images result from transmission, not from a command to change shape.

## 5. “Good familiar” versus destroyer of thieves

Weyer says the familiarity lasts until the subjects hang. The *Goetia* calls him a “good familiar” but says he tempts familiars to steal. Modern demonolatry sometimes turns this into protection, solidarity or honorable loyalty among thieves. That favorable interpretation is possible as modern theology but omits the early gallows conclusion.

## 6. Ten or thirty-six legions?

Ten is unanimous in the Weyer–Scot–Goetia chain. Thirty-six belongs to de Plancy’s accidental importation of Ipes’s entry.

## 7. Does he know past and future or give audacity?

Not in Weyer, Scot or the standard *Goetia*. Those are Ipes’s powers, transmitted under Valafar’s name by de Plancy.

## 8. Is “ass’s head” a mere misprint?

It is more likely an **editorial interpretation of corrupt wording** than a one-letter typographical slip. The chain includes *latronis*, thief, “lowring,” “lowing,” “Mansy/Amases,” and finally “ass’s head, bellowing.” The exact transformation cannot be proven without the lost exemplars.

## 9. Is the seal ancient or Solomonic?

The legend attributes the system to Solomon. Historically, the individual Valefor seal is first attested in early modern Lemegeton manuscripts. No ancient Israelite, Greek, Roman or medieval Valefor seal is known.

## 10. Is Lelahel inherently Valefor’s opposite?

Only within the **Rudd sequential pairing and systems derived from it**. The Exodus triad existed independently; Reuchlin and others angelicized the triads; Rudd then aligned the resulting seventy-two angels with the seventy-two Goetic spirits. The pairing is elegant because both lists contain seventy-two entries, but it is not stated in Exodus, Rashi, Weyer or Scot.

## 11. Which Psalm belongs to Lelahel?

The historically identifiable Lenain verse is the sentence beginning *Psallite Domino, qui habitat in Sion*. It is called Psalm 9:11 by Lenain and KJV-derived numbering but Psalm 9:12 in several Catholic and modern presentations.

Modern systems sometimes give Valefor–Lelahel **Psalm 86:3**. That belongs to newer ritual systems such as *Demons of Magick*, not Lenain’s or Runyon’s inherited Lelahel table.

## 12. Which zodiacal sign belongs to Valefor?

There is no single answer unless the system is named:

- no zodiac in Weyer or the spirit entry;
- Aries by Rudd’s angel-number pairing with Lelahel;
- Taurus by Crowley’s independent Goetic decan table.

Claims that one “corrects” the other confuse two different constructions.

## 13. Is Venus his planet?

Yes within the *Goetia*’s **rank-metal** correspondence: duke → copper → Venus.

But that does not prove that Weyer conceived Valefar astrologically as a Venus spirit. Weyer merely calls him a duke. The explicit metal table belongs to the later Goetic ritual framework.

## 14. Is Valefor an ancient demonized god?

No evidence presently supports that conclusion. The name remains opaque, and its earliest attestation is early modern. General theories about Christian demonization of pagan gods cannot substitute for an item-specific name and source chain.

## 15. Were the Rudd papers written by Thomas Rudd?

**Disputed provenance.** Harley 6483 preserves material attributed to or associated with Dr Thomas Rudd, but the physical manuscript dates to 1712–1713, decades after Rudd’s death. “Rudd’s system” is useful shorthand; “Rudd’s autograph manuscript” is not established.

## Open questions

1. **The lost immediate source.** Did Weyer’s exemplar of the *Liber Officiorum Spirituum* already contain *Valefar alias Malaphar*, or did Weyer or an intermediate compiler add it?

2. **The original appearance phrase.** Was Weyer’s *capite latronis* itself copied correctly? Could an earlier word have generated both “thief” and “barking/lowing” readings?

3. **The manuscript stemma.** Which exemplar introduced “man’s head lowring,” and which one produced the “Mansy/Amases” readings preserved in Sloane 2731?

4. **The ass-headed form.** Did Mathers consult a now-unidentified manuscript that clearly read “ass,” or did he normalize an obviously corrupt phrase? The surviving published apparatus favors editorial normalization, but does not prove it.

5. **Seal genealogy.** Which surviving Lemegeton witness contains the earliest Valefor character, and how much of its geometry was inherited rather than newly designed? Peterson’s observation that the earlier Office-of-Spirits manuscripts lack the familiar seal series leaves the question open.

6. **De Plancy’s first-edition wording.** The Valafar headword is associated with the 1818 work, but the exact point at which the complete Ipes description migrated into the entry deserves a page-by-page collation of all six editions.

7. **Rudd authorship.** Which portions of Harley 6483 descend from Thomas Rudd, which from Peter Smart or another copyist, and which were added during the early eighteenth century?

8. **Earliest use of “Valefor.”** The *-for* spelling occurs in seventeenth-century manuscripts and dominates after 1904. A full manuscript collation might identify its first exact dated occurrence more narrowly.

9. **Modern adaptations.** In works such as *Final Fantasy X*, creator statements would be required to distinguish deliberate borrowing from the grimoire from adoption of a name already circulating in fantasy reference books.

## Conclusions

The recoverable history of Valefor begins not in antiquity but in an early modern spirit catalogue. In 1577 Weyer printed **Valefar alias Malaphar**, a strong duke who appears as a lion with a robber’s head, creates a fatal familiarity and presides over ten legions. Scot translated him in 1584 as a thief-headed lion whose associates end at the gallows.

The seventeenth-century *Goetia* moved him to number 6, supplied a seal and softened the description into a “good familiar” who tempts theft. Its manuscript phrase “a man’s head Lowring” became the ass-headed, bellowing lion in Mathers and Crowley’s 1904 edition. That late reading now controls modern art.

The Lelahel pairing is a separate historical layer. The Hebrew triad LLH comes from the seventy-twofold Exodus construction; Christian Kabbalists turned the triads into angels; the Rudd system aligned angel 6 with demon 6 as a binding power. Lenain subsequently supplied the influential meaning “Praiseworthy God,” the 25°–30° Aries quinance, Psalm *Psallite Domino…*, the twenty-minute hour and the offices of illumination, healing, love, fame, sciences, arts and fortune.

Crowley’s *777* then gave Valefor a second, incompatible astrological location: the daylight spirit of 20°–30° Taurus, paired with Vepar by night and represented by the Seven of Disks. The difference is not a minor computational error. It records two separate occult taxonomies.

Finally, de Plancy’s supposed Valafar biography is not an independent revelation about the spirit. It is an unusually clear textual conflation with Ipes, creating the anomalous angelic or goose-footed lion, prophetic powers and thirty-six legions. No dedicated Louis Le Breton Valefor portrait has been verified.

The evidence therefore supports a modest historical conclusion: Valefor is an opaque early modern name whose biography grew through translation, scribal damage, editorial normalization, systematic angelic pairing, occult correspondence and modern fantasy adaptation. No ancient god, pre-Weyer Valefor, unique Enochian identity or securely recoverable etymology has yet been demonstrated.
