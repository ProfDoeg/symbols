# Furfur: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes six kinds of statement:

- **Documented text** — stated in a named primary source or visible in a manuscript/early printed witness.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an argument about textual descent, dating, or historical context made by an editor or historian.
- **Tradition** — a later claim transmitted within an occult or religious lineage, without implying historical or empirical verification.
- **Esoteric correspondence** — an intentionally constructed symbolic assignment, such as planet, decan, tarot card, angel, or Enochian analogue.
- **Disputed claim** — a reading for which sources or scholars conflict.
- **Legend / modern invention** — folklore, fiction, artistic elaboration, or recent occult teaching not found in the early source chain.

The investigation found a probable pre-Weyer attestation in the French *Livre des esperitz*. The common assertion that Furfur first appeared in 1577 therefore requires qualification.

## The name and its origins

### 1. The earliest surviving witness: *Livre des esperitz*

**Documented text.** Cambridge, Trinity College MS O.8.29, fol. 182r, contains:

> **Furfur eſt ung grãt cõte q̃ aꝑt en guyſe dung ange et faict avoir lamor de toutes gens et faict home ſaige en aſtronomie et phỹie.**

Expanded and translated:

> “Furfur is a great count who appears in the guise of an angel, causes [a person] to have the love of all people, and makes a man wise in astronomy and *phỹie*.”

The final abbreviation has been expanded variously. It most probably denotes *physique*—natural philosophy or physics—rather than a modern medical discipline. The text is available in a manuscript-linked [Wikisource transcription](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre_des_esperitz/Furfur) and [facsimile](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Livre_des_esperitz_-_MS_O.8.29.djvu).

This entry already has three durable features:

1. the spelling **Furfur**;
2. the rank of **count**;
3. an **angelic appearance** and an office involving love.

It does **not** yet mention:

- a hart;
- a fiery tail;
- lying;
- the triangle;
- a hoarse voice;
- weather;
- secret and divine answers;
- 26 legions.

Those elements therefore cannot safely be projected back into the earliest surviving Furfur entry.

### 2. How early is that manuscript?

**Disputed dating.** The witness is sometimes described as a fifteenth-century manuscript. Cataloguing descriptions range from the later fifteenth century to the early sixteenth. Jean-Patrice Boudet reviewed earlier estimates by Richard Kieckhefer and others but considered the hand potentially no earlier than the middle of the sixteenth century.

The safest formulation is:

- The *Livre des esperitz* represents an older French catalogue tradition.
- Its surviving Furfur witness is palaeographically dated somewhere between the later fifteenth and middle sixteenth centuries.
- It is probably earlier than Weyer’s 1577 printing, but it has no colophon proving an exact year.
- Arguments that parts of the *Livre* tradition descend from thirteenth-century materials do **not** prove that the name Furfur itself was present in the thirteenth century.

Boudet’s study is [“Les who’s who démonologiques de la Renaissance et leurs ancêtres médiévaux”](https://doi.org/10.4000/medievales.1019), *Médiévales* 44 (2003), pp. 117–140.

### 3. Latin *furfur*

**Documented lexical fact.** Latin *furfur, furfuris* means bran, chaff or grain husks; transferred senses include scurf, scales or dandruff. The plural is *furfures*. See the [Lewis and Short entry](https://atlas.perseus.tufts.edu/dictionaries/entry/urn:cite2:scaife-viewer:dictionary-entries.atlas_v1:lat.ls.perseus-eng2-n19093/).

The exact coincidence between this ordinary Latin noun and the earliest spelling is stronger evidence than proposed names requiring several undocumented changes. What remains unexplained is why a spirit would be named “bran” or “scurf.” Possibilities include:

- a degraded or comic name;
- a corruption whose result happened to be a Latin word;
- an allusion now lost;
- an originally meaningful name in another language altered by copyists.

No early source explains it.

### 4. *Furcifer*

**Disputed etymology.** Modern dictionaries and occult encyclopedias often propose a corruption of Latin *furcifer*: literally a bearer of a *furca*, the fork-shaped punishment yoke, and hence an abusive word for a rogue, rascal or scoundrel.

This is semantically attractive because Furfur is called a liar. It is nevertheless unsupported by the source chain:

- no witness reads *Furcifer*;
- no early commentator derives Furfur from it;
- the French *Livre des esperitz* already reads the exact Latin-looking form *Furfur*;
- reducing *furcifer* to *furfur* requires phonetic and scribal changes for which no intermediate form has been identified.

It should therefore be presented as a modern conjecture, not a recovered ancient etymology.

### 5. Ancient god, hero, or place

**Negative finding.** No ancient god, hero, city, biblical demon, or classical geographical name can presently be identified behind Furfur.

Unlike:

- Bael/Baal;
- Astaroth/Astarte;
- Ammon/Amun;
- Asmoday/Ashmedai;
- Belial/*bĕliyyaʿal*;
- Phenex/Phoenix;

Furfur has no demonstrable ancient cultic pedigree. Searches for identifications with Cernunnos, a Celtic stag god, Demeter, Pazuzu, storm gods, reindeer constellations, or Mesopotamian deer figures lead to modern syncretic interpretations. Neither the *Livre des esperitz*, Weyer, Scot, nor the manuscript *Goetia* makes such an identification.

### 6. Distinct names, not conflations

**Documented catalog structure.** Furfur is distinct from:

- **Furcas/Foras**, a president or knight-like figure associated with philosophy and herbs;
- **Focalor**, a duke associated with winds and seas;
- **Furtiel**, an *Ars Theurgia-Goetia* name;
- **Fursiel**, another later theurgic name.

Their proximity in modern indexes is orthographic. No primary witness says they are forms of the same spirit.

## Chronological source chain

### Before the Furfur entry: catalogues and ancestors

#### *Testament of Solomon*

**Negative finding.** The late antique *Testament of Solomon* supplies the foundational legend of Solomon questioning and binding demons, but it contains no Furfur. Its thirty-fourth demon is not Furfur, and there is no persuasive continuity of name, form, or office. The text can be consulted in [Peterson’s edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/testamen.htm).

#### Munich Handbook, Clm 849

**Scholarly context.** The fifteenth-century Munich magical handbook edited by Richard Kieckhefer as *Forbidden Rites* contains individual spirit names and offices with parallels to later catalogues. Furfur has not been located in its published index or transcribed spirit material. It is an ancestor of the broader textual environment, not a securely attested Furfur witness.

#### *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Weyer named his source:

> *Liber officiorum spirituum, seu Liber dictus Empto. Salomonis, de principibus et regibus daemoniorum.*

That is, “Book of the Offices of Spirits, or the book called *Empto. Solomonis*, concerning the princes and kings of demons.”

Johannes Trithemius’s 1508 *Antipalus maleficiorum* catalogues a *Liber officiorum spirituum*, proving that books under this title circulated before Weyer. Surviving “Office of Spirits” manuscripts share many figures with Weyer and the *Goetia*. The [Folger V.b.26 transcription](https://www.esotericarchives.com/folger/v_b_26_transcription.pdf) is an important related witness, but Furfur has not been found in the searchable transcription.

This supports a **family of catalogues**, not a single recoverable archetype in which every later demon necessarily appeared.

#### *Book of Abramelin*

**Negative finding.** No secure Furfur occurs in the principal Abramelin demon lists. Modern sites that call every Goetic name “Abramelinic” are usually merging independent catalogs.

### *Livre des esperitz*

As shown above, this is the earliest surviving Furfur witness. Its compact entry seems closer to a catalogue of offices than to a full evocation manual.

A useful comparison is:

| Feature | *Livre des esperitz* | Weyer and later |
|---|---:|---:|
| Name Furfur | Yes | Yes |
| Count/earl | Yes | Yes |
| Angel form | Yes, initial appearance | Yes, commanded form |
| Love | Love of everyone | Love between man and woman |
| Astronomy/natural philosophy | Yes | No |
| Hart with fiery tail | No | Yes |
| Compulsory triangle | No | Yes |
| Lying | No | Yes |
| Weather | No | Yes |
| Secret/divine answers | No | Yes |
| 26 legions | No | Yes |
| Seal | No | *Goetia* only |

The change from “appears as an angel” to “appears as a hart but can be forced into angelic form” is especially significant. It could represent expansion, conflation with another description, or a deliberately dramatized revision. No surviving intermediate copy shows exactly how the transformation happened.

## The description across the sources

### Johann Weyer, 1577

Weyer first appended the *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* to the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum* in 1577. Furfur is §30, on printed p. 922 of the Basel edition. Peterson’s online edition preserves the page marker and parallel Scot text. [Primary text and editorial notes](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

#### Latin, verbatim

> **Furfur Comes est magnus, apparens ut cervus cauda flammea. In omnibus mentitur, nisi in triangulum intro ducatur. Iussus angelicam assumit imaginem. Rauca loquitur voce: amorem inter virum & mulierem libenter conciliat: novit & concitare fulgura, coruscationes & tonitrua in ijs partibus ubi iussum fuerit. De occultis & divinis rebus bene respondet. Imperat legionibus vigintisex.**

Literal translation:

> “Furfur is a great count, appearing as a stag with a flaming tail. He lies in everything unless he is led into a triangle. When commanded, he assumes an angelic image. He speaks with a hoarse voice. He willingly brings about love between man and woman. He knows how to stir up lightning, flashes and thunder in those regions where he is commanded. He answers well concerning occult and divine matters. He commands twenty-six legions.”

#### Context

**Documented historical context.** Weyer was not presenting the catalogue as an endorsement of demonic conjuration. His larger work argued against witch prosecutions and treated many magical claims as fraud, delusion, illness, or diabolical deception. He also admitted suppressing operational material to make his source less usable. The paradox is that his attempt to expose or neutralize the literature became one of its principal channels of survival.

### Reginald Scot, 1584

Scot translated much of Weyer into Book XV, chapter 2 of *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*. The entry is numbered [30] in Peterson’s parallel transcription. In reset reprints the pagination varies; the stable reference is Book XV, ch. 2, corresponding to Weyer §30. A complete first-edition copy is available from [Cornell](https://digital.library.cornell.edu/catalog/witchcraft081), with an [1886 Nicholson facsimile/reprint](https://archive.org/details/b21720307).

#### English, verbatim

> **Furfur is a great earle, appearing as an hart, with a firie taile, he lieth in everie thing, except he be brought up within a triangle; being bidden, he taketh angelicall forme, he speaketh with a hoarse voice, and willinglie maketh love betweene man and wife; he raiseth thunders and lightnings, and blasts. Where he is commanded, he answereth well, both of secret and also of divine things, and hath rule and dominion over six and twentie legions.**

Peterson notes that “wife” represents Latin *mulier*, simply “woman.” The change is small linguistically but consequential in later interpretation.

**Documented context.** Scot, like Weyer, printed magical material inside a skeptical attack on witchcraft belief, imposture, and wrongful prosecution. Later practitioners extracted Book XV from that polemical frame and used it as an operative source.

### Numbering in Weyer and Scot

**Documented text with editorial complication.**

- Weyer’s printed catalogue places Furfur at **§30**.
- Scot omits Pruflas, Weyer’s fourth spirit. A fresh count of Scot’s surviving entries can consequently place Furfur one slot earlier.
- Some comparison charts label the Weyer correspondence as 29 because they normalize against a Pruflas-less sequence or confuse the preceding Foras row.
- The primary printed section number is unambiguous: Furfur is Weyer **§30**.
- In the *Goetia*, Furfur is **34**.

### The *Ars Goetia* manuscripts

#### Date and construction

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The *Lemegeton* is a seventeenth-century English compilation. Its first book, the *Ars Goetia*, draws heavily on Scot rather than independently translating Weyer. Peterson notes propagated Scot errors and an order closer to Scot’s text. The work probably cannot predate the particular Scot recension on which it relies.

The principal manuscript witnesses used by Peterson include:

- British Library Sloane MS 3825;
- Sloane MS 2731;
- Sloane MS 3648;
- British Library Harley MS 6483;
- later copies such as Wellcome MS 3203 and Northwestern MS 65.

#### Sloane 3825, fols. 105r–105v

Sloane 3825 uniquely heads the entry **Furtur**, almost certainly a copying error. Peterson’s 2001 edition prints it on pp. 22–23 and restores *Furfur* in brackets. [Peterson’s 2001 edition scan](https://jamesparsonslakebucket.s3-us-west-1.amazonaws.com/text/Peterson_Joseph_H_The_Lesser_Key_of_Solomon.pdf).

#### Verbatim manuscript-based text

> **The 34th spirit is called Furtur [Furfur], he is a great & mighty Earle, appearing in ye forme of an hart wth a firey Taile; he never speaket Truth, except he be compelled or brought up wthin a Triangle, being compelled therein, he will take upon himselfe ye forme of an angel being bidden; he speaketh with a hoarse voice, & will willingly make love between man & wife he can raise Thunder, Lightnings, Blasts and great Tempestuous stormes &c he giveth true answares both of secreet and devine Things if commanded, and ruleth over 26 Legions of spirits, his seal is this which is to be worne as a Lamin &c.**

Orthography and punctuation differ slightly among transcriptions because the manuscript is compressed and irregular. The substantive additions beyond Scot are:

- “great & mighty”;
- “great Tempestuous stormes”;
- the seal and lamen instruction.

The *Goetia* does not retain the *Livre des esperitz* office of teaching astronomy and natural philosophy.

#### Sloane 2731 and other variants

Peterson’s collation reports:

- Sloane 2731: **Furfur**;
- Sloane 3648: **Furfur**;
- Harley 6483: **Furfur**;
- Northwestern MS 65: **Furfur**;
- Sloane 3825: **Furtur**.

Thus **Furtur is a one-witness error**, not the dominant manuscript reading. It nevertheless became an “alternative name” because Peterson faithfully printed the base manuscript’s form before supplying the correction.

### Mathers–Crowley, 1904

S. L. MacGregor Mathers prepared the English text; Aleister Crowley edited, introduced, and published it as *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King* in 1904. The Furfur entry is no. 34, around printed p. 33 in the original edition; the complete scan is available through [Wikisource](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Index:The_Book_of_the_Goetia_(Mathers-Crowley,_1904).djvu), and the text through [Sacred Texts](https://sacred-texts.com/grim/lks/lks05.htm).

#### Verbatim 1904 text

> **(34.) FURFUR.—The Thirty-fourth Spirit is Furfur. He is a Great and Mighty Earl, appearing in the Form of an Hart with a Fiery Tail. He never speaketh truth unless he be compelled, or brought up within a triangle, △. Being therein, he will take upon himself the Form of an Angel. Being bidden, he speaketh with a hoarse voice. Also he will wittingly urge Love between Man and Woman. He can raise Lightnings and Thunders, Blasts, and Great Tempestuous Storms. And he giveth True Answers both of Things Secret and Divine, if commanded. He ruleth over 26 Legions of Spirits. And his Seal is this, etc.**

Mathers returns from Scot’s “man and wife” to **“Man and Woman,”** closer to Weyer’s *virum et mulierem*. He prints “wittingly,” whereas the manuscript tradition and Latin sense are “willingly.”

### Peterson, 2001

Joseph H. Peterson’s *The Lesser Key of Solomon* was the first widely available modern edition to collate multiple manuscripts systematically rather than merely reproduce Mathers–Crowley. Its main text follows Sloane 3825 but records readings from Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, Harley 6483, and other witnesses.

For Furfur, its most important editorial contributions are:

- preserving the base-manuscript error **Furtur**;
- correcting it from all the other principal witnesses;
- identifying the manuscript folios;
- exposing the direct relationship to Weyer and Scot;
- distinguishing original manuscript instructions from Mathers’s later planetary corrections;
- supplying a cross-table of the demon orders and spellings. See Peterson’s [comparison table](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia_spirits.pdf).

## Close comparison of the offices

### Hart with a fiery tail

**Documented text, first secure appearance 1577.** Weyer’s *cervus cauda flammea* is exact: a stag or deer with a flaming tail. Scot uses “hart,” traditionally an adult male deer.

No early source says:

- the hart is winged;
- the body is human;
- the antlers burn;
- the entire animal is aflame.

Those details arise in art and modern retellings.

### The liar and the triangle

**Documented text.** Weyer says Furfur lies “in everything” unless led into a triangle. Scot and the *Goetia* preserve this. In the ritual architecture of the *Goetia*, the Triangle of Art is a separate manifestation space outside the magician’s protective circle.

The text does not explain why the triangle changes Furfur’s truthfulness. Later interpretations include:

- **Tradition:** the divine names surrounding the triangle constrain the spirit;
- **Psychological modern interpretation:** disciplined ritual structure contains self-deception;
- **Modern demonolatry:** the triangle is sometimes treated as optional or disrespectful.

Only the first proposition reflects the coercive logic of the manuscript ritual; the psychological and anti-coercive readings are modern.

### Angel form

The textual development is striking:

- *Livre des esperitz*: Furfur initially appears “in the guise of an angel.”
- Weyer: he initially appears as the fiery-tailed stag and, **when commanded**, assumes an angelic image.
- Scot: the same.
- *Goetia*: the angelic form is explicitly associated with confinement in the triangle.

This “angelic form” is not Lehahiah. Lehahiah is an entirely separate controlling angel introduced by the Rudd system. The wording in Weyer precedes that pairing.

### Love

**Documented text.**

- *Livre des esperitz*: wins “the love of all people.”
- Weyer: *amorem inter virum & mulierem … conciliat* — “brings about love between man and woman.”
- Scot: “maketh love betweene man and wife.”
- Mathers: “urge Love between Man and Woman.”

**Disputed interpretation.** Modern descriptions that restrict Furfur to reconciliation within an existing marriage depend mainly on Scot’s “wife.” The Latin does not contain a word specifically meaning wife, and Mathers did not retain the restriction. Conversely, modern descriptions of Furfur as a general demon of lust are broader than every early text: the operative word is love, not lust.

### Weather

**Documented text.**

Weyer names:

- *fulgura* — lightning bolts;
- *coruscationes* — flashes or sheet lightning;
- *tonitrua* — thunder.

Scot condenses this to thunder, lightning and blasts. The *Goetia* adds “great Tempestuous Storms.”

Earthquakes do not appear in Furfur’s primary entries. Modern references to tremors probably arise through confusion with Agares, whose Weyer/Scot office includes making the earth move, or through general expansion of Furfur’s storm powers.

### Secret and divine matters

**Documented text.** Weyer’s *de occultis et divinis rebus* means occult/hidden and divine matters. “Occult” here first means concealed, not necessarily a modern curriculum called occultism.

The sources promise answers, not systematic teaching of every science. The only source that gives Furfur an instructional office is the earlier *Livre des esperitz*, with astronomy and natural philosophy.

### Legions

**Documented text.** The number is uniformly **26** from Weyer onward. Claims of 29 or 36 legions are modern errors. Twenty-nine belongs to several neighboring spirits; 36 is likely accidental contamination or copying from another entry.

## The order and the number problem

### Weyer’s 69 and the *Goetia*’s 72

Weyer’s extant catalogue has 69 spirits. The *Goetia* has 72. The arithmetic is:

- Four spirits added by the *Goetia*: **Vassago**, **Seere**, **Dantalion**, **Andromalius**.
- One Weyer spirit omitted: **Pruflas/Bufas**.
- 69 + 4 − 1 = 72.

Peterson calls the changed order so irregular that it resembles a shuffled stack of cards. There is no accepted symbolic explanation for every movement. [His introduction and comparison](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm) document the additions and omission.

Furfur moves from Weyer §30 to *Goetia* no. 34 because:

- Pruflas disappears;
- Vassago enters at no. 3;
- many names are substantially reordered;
- Gaap, Asmoday and others shift around the Furfur sequence.

The number 34 is therefore a feature of the seventeenth-century *Goetia*, not Furfur’s earliest intrinsic number.

### Why 72?

**Tradition.** The *Goetia* links its spirits to the Solomonic brass vessel containing 72 kings. Weyer himself recounts a vessel containing 72 kings in his Belial entry despite preserving only 69 catalogued spirits.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The final adjustment to 72 may have been encouraged by several prestigious symbolic sets:

- the 72 nations;
- the 72 elders;
- the 72 disciples in some Gospel manuscripts;
- the 72 divisions of the zodiac into five-degree sectors;
- the 72-fold divine Name.

That does not prove the compiler originally designed the demon list to match the Shem angels. The ordinary *Goetia* supplies no one-to-one angel list. The explicit opposition is the Rudd recension’s intervention.

## The seal

### Earliest appearance

**Documented text.** Neither the *Livre des esperitz*, Weyer nor Scot gives Furfur a seal. The seal enters with the seventeenth-century *Goetia* manuscripts.

### Verbal description

The standard Furfur seal can be described as:

- a long horizontal axis;
- curled or spiral-like terminals at both ends;
- short oblique strokes crossing or descending from the horizontal arms;
- a central vertical junction;
- a small downward-pointing triangular, spearhead, or open angular form below the center.

It is approximately bilaterally balanced but not perfectly geometrical.

A clear reproduction of the 1904 form is available from [From Old Books](https://www.fromoldbooks.org/Mathers-Goetia/pages/034-Seal-of-Furfur/).

### Manuscript variation

**Documented paleographic variation.** Across Sloane 3825, Sloane 2731, Harley 6483 and later copies, the recognizable structure remains stable, but details vary:

- the degree of curl in the two terminals;
- whether terminal curves close into loops;
- the number, length and angle of cross-strokes;
- whether the descending center looks like an open triangle, closed triangle, fork, or arrowhead;
- line thickness and symmetry.

These are normal hand-copying variations. No manuscript evidence establishes that every difference encodes a separate magical meaning.

### Encircled modern seal

Some modern talismans place the emblem inside a circle and write the letters **F-U-R-F-U-R** around the rim. This is a modern presentational elaboration or ritual adaptation. It is not the core seal printed in the 1904 *Goetia*, and it should not be represented as the unique manuscript original.

### Lamen and metal

The entry says the seal is to be worn “as a Lamin”—a lamen or breast emblem.

The manuscript “Observations” give rank metals:

- kings: gold;
- marquises: silver;
- dukes: copper;
- prelates: tin;
- knights: lead;
- presidents: mercury;
- earls: “Venus and Luna equally alike,” normally interpreted as copper and silver.

Mathers regarded this as corrupt or internally inconsistent and reassigned the seven ranks across seven planets. In his system an **earl corresponds to Mars and iron**. Consequently:

- **Copper and silver** are the documented manuscript prescription.
- **Mars and iron** are the better-known Golden Dawn/Mathers correction.
- Calling iron Furfur’s timeless or medieval metal erases that textual history.

### What the Hebrew name is

**Documented textual procedure.** Exodus 14:19–21 consists, in the Masoretic Hebrew text, of three verses traditionally counted as 72 letters each. The letters are arranged boustrophedon:

1. verse 19 in its normal direction;
2. verse 20 in reverse;
3. verse 21 in its normal direction.

Reading vertical columns produces 72 triplets. The thirty-fourth is **להח**.

Adding a divine ending—normally **־אל** (*-el*) or **־יה** (*-yah/iah*)—turns the triplets into angelic names. Thus להח becomes Lehahiah. This procedure is historical Christian and Hermetic Cabala; it is not a claim that Exodus originally named an angel called Lehahiah.

### Meaning

**Tradition.** Lenain glosses Lehahiah’s attribute as **Dieu clément**. “Clement God,” “Gracious God,” or “Merciful God” are reasonable translations of that French occult title.

**Philological caution.** The consonants להח do not constitute an ordinary Hebrew phrase meaning “God is clement.” The meaning is a meditative divine-attribute gloss assigned to the extracted triplet, not a literal dictionary translation.

### Psalm verse

The traditional verse is:

> **Speret Israel in Domino, ex hoc nunc et usque in saeculum.**

Or in the Vulgate form commonly printed:

> **Expectet Israel Dominum ex hoc nunc et usque in aeternum.**

English:

> “Let Israel hope in the Lord from henceforth and for ever.”

Numbering differs:

- Hebrew/Masoretic and many English Bibles: **Psalm 131:3**;
- Vulgate: **Psalm 130:3**;
- Lenain calls it the “fifth verse,” apparently counting the superscription and liturgical divisions.

Online tables that present “Psalm 130:5” as a modern English Bible reference are therefore liable to send readers to a different sentence.

## History of the 72-fold Name and Lehahiah

### Jewish antecedents

#### Early meanings of *Shem ha-Mephorash*

**Scholarly fact.** In rabbinic literature *shem ha-meforash* can mean the explicitly pronounced or distinguished divine name, often the Tetragrammaton. It did not always mean a list of 72 angels.

Later Jewish esoteric sources speak of names of 12, 42 or 72 letters. Conflating every historical use into the exact modern “72 guardian angels” table is anachronistic.

#### *Sefer ha-Bahir*

**Documented tradition.** Sections 109–110 of the *Bahir*, compiled in southern France in approximately the late twelfth century, discuss a divine name derived from three verses, each containing 72 letters. A translated text is available at [Hekhal](https://hekhal.org/texts/sefer-ha-bahir-109-110-targum/).

The *Bahir* supports the 72-letter derivation. It does not pair Lehahiah with Furfur.

#### *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh*

**Tradition and textual history.** *Sefer Raziel* is a medieval Hebrew magical compilation attributed pseudepigraphically to the angel Raziel and printed in Amsterdam in 1701. It contains 72-name materials and helped transmit angelic divine-name traditions.

It is misleading to say that the complete Lenain/Haziel biography of Lehahiah appears unchanged in the ancient *Sefer Raziel*. The text has multiple recensions, and the later dates, psychological characteristics, planetary sequence and Goetic opponent belong to later Western constructions.

### Christian Cabala

#### Giustiniani, 1513

Agostino Giustiniani’s *Precatio pietatis plena* arranged the 72 triplets with biblical or Psalm material in a Christian devotional setting. Saverio Campanini analyzes this development in [“The Seventy-Two Names of God in the Jewish and Christian Kabbalah”](https://cris.unibo.it/handle/11585/844735).

#### Reuchlin and Blaise de Vigenère

Johannes Reuchlin helped integrate Hebrew divine names into Christian Cabala. Blaise de Vigenère’s sixteenth-century writings developed and transmitted the angelized forms and associated verses. Skinner and Rankine identify de Vigenère as an especially important source behind the angelic material later incorporated into Harley 6483.

### Kircher, 1652–1654

Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* printed elaborate 72-name diagrams and Christian-Cabalistic interpretations. The work was published at Rome in three large volumes between 1652 and 1654; digital copies are indexed by the [Online Books Page](https://onlinebooks.library.upenn.edu/webbin/book/lookupid?key=olbp67641).

Kircher’s famous diagram can be viewed on [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:The_72_Names_of_God_(Oedipus_Aegyptiacus,_1652%E2%80%9354).jpg). Kircher was often hostile to magical applications even while reproducing their materials. His treatment shows that printing an occult diagram does not necessarily signal practical endorsement.

### Rudd, Peter Smart, and Harley 6483

#### The manuscript

British Library Harley MS 6483 belongs to a set copied by Peter Smart around 1712–1713. Some sections are headed or described as papers of “Dr Rudd,” traditionally identified with Thomas Rudd, c. 1583–1656.

#### The pairing

**Documented manuscript tradition.** In the Harley/Rudd arrangement, number 34 is:

> **Lehahiah — Furfur**

The procedure pairs the *n*th Shem angel with the *n*th Goetic spirit. The angel is not merely a friendly devotional supplement: it supplies a divine or angelic authority capable of binding, checking, or balancing the corresponding evil spirit. The names and seals are incorporated into the ritual and lamen apparatus rather than being treated as alternative names of the demons.

Thus Lehahiah is “set over” Furfur by **numerical coordination**, not because the earlier Furfur text mentions an angel named Lehahiah.

#### Disputed authorship

**Disputed claim.** Stephen Skinner and David Rankine argue that much of the material derives from Thomas Rudd’s papers, perhaps c. 1655–1656. Their dating partly depends on identifiable printed sources available shortly before Rudd’s death.

Other researchers stress:

- the extant manuscript is Peter Smart’s early-eighteenth-century copy;
- some material in the Harley group derives from books printed after Thomas Rudd’s death;
- “Dr Rudd” attribution may apply only to certain exemplars or may have been used to lend authority;
- Arthur Edward Waite already questioned whether the attributed Dr Rudd was a securely identifiable author.

The appropriate description is therefore **the Rudd/Smart recension preserved in Harley 6483**, not an unquestioned Thomas Rudd autograph.

#### Skinner and Rankine, 2007

Stephen Skinner and David Rankine published *The Goetia of Dr Rudd: The Angels and Demons of Liber Malorum Spirituum seu Goetia* with Golden Hoard Press in 2007; a later Llewellyn edition followed. Its central contribution was making the Harley material and the one-to-one angelic control scheme readily accessible. The publisher summarizes the method as using the 72 angels to constrain the 72 demons: [Llewellyn edition page](https://www.llewellyn.com/product.php?ean=9780738723556).

The editors’ reconstruction is influential but should be separated from what the surviving codex physically proves.

### Lenain, 1823

Lazare Républicain Lenain’s *La science cabalistique* systematized the 72 “good genii” into the form widely reproduced today. The original is available through [Internet Archive](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_ms05365) and [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:La_science_cabalistique_ou_lart_de_conna%C3%AEtre_les_bons_g%C3%A9nies_qui_influent_sur_la_destin%C3%A9e_des_hommes_(IA_BIUSante_ms05365).pdf).

Lenain gives Lehahiah:

- no. 34;
- attribute: **Dieu clément**;
- holy name **Aneb**, said to belong to the language of the people of Congo;
- degrees 166–170 of the zodiacal circle;
- the seventeenth decade and secondary genius **Thopitus**;
- five recurring annual days: April 22, July 3, September 13, November 24, February 4;
- invocation from 11:00 to 11:20 a.m.;
- the Psalm verse discussed above;
- protection against anger;
- governance of crowned heads, princes and nobles;
- maintenance of harmony, understanding and peace among rulers;
- influence over the obedience of subjects;
- favor earned by devotion, fidelity and service;
- contrary influence of discord, war, treason and national ruin.

“Degrees 166–170,” when reckoned from the beginning of Aries, correspond to **15°–20° Virgo**. Modern date lists differ by one day because some treat degrees and calendar days inclusively, some start at 0°, and some round the tropical year into equal five-day units.

Lenain does not derive these biographical offices from Exodus. They are part of his nineteenth-century occult synthesis.

### Papus

Papus—Gérard Encausse—reprinted and popularized Lenain-like angelic tables in *La Cabbale* and related works at the turn of the twentieth century. His versions transmit:

- Lehahiah as “Dieu clément”;
- the Aneb correspondence;
- 166°–170°;
- the Psalm;
- the anti-anger and obedience offices.

Papus is principally a transmitter here, not the inventor of Rudd’s Furfur pairing.

### Golden Dawn

The Hermetic Order of the Golden Dawn integrated the 72 angelic names with:

- the 36 zodiacal decans;
- five-degree quinances;
- the minor tarot cards;
- planetary decan rulers;
- Hebrew and Enochian tables.

Each decan contains two five-degree Shem angels. Each numbered pip from Two through Ten covers one decan, so each pip bears two angelic names.

Lehahiah covers 15°–20° Virgo and therefore occupies the latter half of the **Nine of Pentacles**, whose full decan is 10°–20° Virgo, Venus in Virgo.

The Golden Dawn’s scheme does not prove that the medieval compilers understood Lehahiah astrologically. It is a late-nineteenth-century correspondence system.

### Ambelain

Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique*—first published in the mid-twentieth century and repeatedly reissued—carried the 72 names, angel seals and practical invocations into French popular occultism. A bibliographic record is available through [Open Library](https://openlibrary.org/books/OL22019447M/La_kabbale_pratique).

Ambelain’s work is important for later practice, but its individualized seals and ritual directions are not evidence for the medieval form of the name. In later editorial remarks, Ambelain reportedly warned that some reproduced seals had been wrongly attributed; editions need to be checked separately rather than treated as a uniform text.

### Haziel

The French occult author writing as Haziel popularized the 72 angels as personal guardian angels, birth-date intelligences and subjects of accessible prayer, meditation and card systems. Publisher material survives for his [angel card publications](https://www.editions-tredaniel.com/haziel-les-cartes-des-anges-p-4053.html).

The Haziel literature belongs to the late-twentieth-century popular angel movement. It spreads Lehahiah’s traits—obedience, peace, authority, fidelity and protection from anger—but does not constitute evidence for:

- an ancient Jewish guardian angel assigned to September birthdays;
- a biblical personality called Lehahiah;
- an original Exodus pairing with Furfur.

### Crowley’s *777*

Crowley’s *Liber 777*, compiled from Golden Dawn tables and published in 1909, lists Goetic demons by the 36 decans, one for the day and one for the night. Furfur occupies the day column for the first decan of Pisces. The relevant electronic scan is [Liber 777](https://magicgatebg.com/Books/Liber%20777.pdf).

The assignment yields:

- Pisces 0°–10°;
- approximately February 19–28 in conventional modern calendars;
- Eight of Cups;
- Saturn in Pisces;
- day/ascending demon Furfur;
- night/descending demon Seere.

The appearance of “29” beside Furfur in some *777* extractions refers to a table row or scale key, not to “the twenty-ninth zodiacal decan” and not to the twenty-ninth path of the Tree of Life. Modern sites that convert that row number into Moon in Aquarius are misreading the table.

### Why each pip carries two spirits

There are:

- 36 decans × 10°;
- two Goetic spirits per decan = 72;
- two five-degree Shem angels per decan = 72.

Thus the Eight of Cups can carry:

- two Shem angels covering 0°–5° and 5°–10° Pisces;
- a day Goetic spirit and night Goetic spirit for the whole decan.

This is a deliberately constructed Golden Dawn/Thelemic symmetry. It is not found in Weyer.

### Enochian overlay

Golden Dawn and later ceremonial-magical tarot systems map the same minor card to:

- zodiacal decan;
- planet in sign;
- Hebrew divine names;
- Shem angels;
- Goetic spirits;
- Enochian elemental-tablet forces.

Lon Milo DuQuette’s *Tarot of Ceremonial Magick* makes this synthesis especially visible. The deck’s own description emphasizes its combination of astrology, Qabalah, Enochian magic and Goetia: [deck description](https://www.learntarot.org/cmdesc.htm).

No surviving Enochian diary of John Dee or Edward Kelley contains a Furfur–Lehahiah pairing. The connection is a later table-based overlay.

## Astrological timing of conjuration

### What the *Goetia* actually says

**Documented text.** The general “Observations” say that earls or counts may be bound:

> “at any hour of the day, provided it be in woods, or in any other places where men resort not, or where no noise is.”

The same instructions commend operations when the Moon is:

- 2;
- 4;
- 6;
- 8;
- 10;
- 12;
- 14 days old.

These rules are general by rank, not Furfur-specific.

### Later timing

**Esoteric correspondence.** Once Furfur is classified as an earl of Mars, later practitioners infer:

- Tuesday;
- the planetary hour of Mars;
- Mars dignified and not afflicted;
- sometimes 0°–10° Pisces because of *777*.

These are later composite rules. The early Furfur entry gives no weekday, hour, zodiac sign or lunar mansion.

### Lehahiah’s time

Lenain gives 11:00–11:20 a.m., corresponding to the thirty-fourth twenty-minute segment after midnight if the 72 intelligences divide a 24-hour day. Later sources frequently print 11:01–11:20. The one-minute difference is editorial, not a separate revelation.

## Iconography

### The manuscripts

The earliest *Livre des esperitz* and Weyer–Scot witnesses supply no portrait. Their imagery exists only in words.

The *Goetia* manuscripts add Furfur’s linear seal but no naturalistic drawing of the hart. Copyists’ seals vary modestly, as described above.

### Collin de Plancy, 1818–1863

Jacques-Albin-Simon Collin de Plancy first published the *Dictionnaire infernal* in 1818. The work underwent extensive revision through six editions. The first edition can be read at [French Wikisource](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/1re_%C3%A9dition,_1818/Tome_1/Texte_entier).

The famous illustrated Furfur belongs to the **sixth edition of 1863**, not to the original 1818 printing. On p. 290 de Plancy writes:

> **Furfur, comte aux enfers. Il se fait voir sous la forme d’un cerf avec une queue enflammée ; il ne dit que des mensonges, à moins qu’il ne soit enfermé dans un triangle. Il prend souvent la figure d’un ange, parle d’une voix rauque et entretient l’union entre les maris et les femmes. Il fait tomber la foudre, luire les éclairs et gronder le tonnerre dans les lieux où il en reçoit l’ordre. Il répond sur les choses abstraites. Vingt-six légions sont sous ses ordres.**

Translation:

> “Furfur, a count in hell. He shows himself in the form of a stag with a flaming tail; he tells nothing but lies unless enclosed in a triangle. He often assumes the figure of an angel, speaks in a hoarse voice, and maintains union between husbands and wives. He causes lightning to fall, flashes to shine and thunder to rumble in places where he receives the order. He answers concerning abstract matters. Twenty-six legions are under his command.”

The text is available on [Wikisource, 1863, p. 290](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.,_1863/Furfur), with the whole edition on [Gallica](https://gallica.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/bpt6k5754923d).

De Plancy explicitly depends on Wierus/Weyer. His changes include:

- “maintains the union” of married couples rather than merely causing love;
- “abstract matters” for occult and divine things;
- “often” takes angelic form.

### Louis Le Breton’s portrait

The 1863 engraving attributed to Louis Le Breton depicts a flying hybrid:

- a stag or deer head with antlers;
- a partly human chest and forelimbs/arms;
- cervine hindquarters;
- large bat-like wings;
- an airborne, diagonally posed body.

A public-domain reproduction is available at [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:Ill_dict_infernal_p0306-290_furfur_demon.jpg).

The wings and hybrid human anatomy are **Le Breton’s artistic elaboration**. Weyer says simply *cervus*, a stag; he does not describe wings or a simultaneous half-angel form. The fiery tail is more legible in the text than in many reproductions of the engraving.

Some plates are described as “by Le Breton” while the physical engraving work may have involved another engraver translating his design. “Le Breton design” is safer than assuming he cut every printing block personally.

### Crowley-associated art

The original 1904 edition primarily supplies the seal, not a canonical painted portrait. Later “illustrated Goetia” editions add artists’ interpretations.

*Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia*, edited by Lon Milo DuQuette and Christopher S. Hyatt with illustrations by David P. Wilson, represents Furfur as a more explicitly monstrous or anthropomorphic deer figure. These images are late-twentieth-century occult art, not manuscript evidence.

### Modern art and commodities

Furfur now appears in:

- sigil jewelry;
- tarot and oracle decks;
- demon encyclopedias;
- tattoo designs;
- fan art;
- role-playing bestiaries;
- Lera Auerbach’s large-scale musical/visual *Goetia* project.

These productions frequently merge:

- the manuscript seal;
- Le Breton’s wings;
- a burning tail;
- modern antlered “wendigo” imagery;
- red/Martian color schemes.

That composite is aesthetically influential but historically layered.

## People and works

### Johann Weyer

Weyer is the first securely dated printer of the expanded fiery-hart description. His medical and theological project opposed witch persecution, while conceding a Christian demonology. Furfur entered European print culture through a text intended partly to expose and disable conjuring literature.

### Reginald Scot

Scot gave Furfur its enduring English vocabulary: “great earle,” “hart,” “firie taile,” “man and wife,” and “hoarse voice.” Because later English compilers copied Scot, small translation choices became features of the magical tradition.

### The anonymous *Lemegeton* compiler

The compiler elevated Furfur to no. 34, added a seal and made the weather office more dramatic. There is no evidence that King Solomon wrote this text. “Spirit of Solomon” is the work’s pseudepigraphic frame.

### Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

The Rudd/Smart recension paired Furfur with Lehahiah and made the Shem angel part of a technique of constraint. The precise authorship layers remain disputed.

### Mathers and Crowley

Mathers normalized the prose and seals; Crowley brought the work into the early Thelemic publishing program. Their 1904 text became the source for innumerable twentieth-century reprints.

Crowley’s *777* subsequently gave Furfur the Pisces/Eight of Cups decan correspondence. That astrological identity is therefore later than the Goetic description itself.

### Arthur Edward Waite

Waite summarized Furfur in *The Book of Ceremonial Magic* and helped transmit the catalogue to a wider twentieth-century readership, usually with a more skeptical or critical rhetorical posture than operative occultists.

### Collin de Plancy and Louis Le Breton

De Plancy moved the name from grimoire catalogues into an illustrated demonological dictionary. Le Breton’s 1863 image permanently changed the popular visual conception by adding a winged hybrid body.

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 edition restored the manuscript basis of the text, documented *Furtur* as a base-manuscript error, and distinguished Sloane and Harley readings. His online editions also made Weyer’s Latin and Scot’s English directly comparable.

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their 2007 edition made Harley 6483’s angel–demon scheme central to contemporary “Rudd Goetia.” Their historical reconstruction is indispensable but not universally accepted in every detail of attribution and dating.

### Lon Milo DuQuette and Christopher Hyatt

Their *Illustrated Goetia* helped establish the modern psychological and Thelemic reading of individual Goetic spirits. DuQuette’s tarot work visibly combines Goetic, Shem, astrological and Enochian systems.

### Poke Runyon

Runyon’s *Book of Solomon’s Magick* and Church of the Hermetic Sciences presentations are significant in the revival of ceremonial evocation and mirror/scrying methods. No reliable published record located in this research establishes a uniquely important Furfur operation by Runyon; he belongs to the modern Goetic reception history generally.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent emphasized the necromantic, spirit-catalogue and late-antique backgrounds of Western goetia, challenging purely psychological interpretations. His historical framework is relevant, but no source located gives him a distinctive Furfur etymology or operation. It would be misleading to attribute the *furcifer* theory to him without a precise citation.

### Frater Achad

No Furfur-specific working or scholarly treatment by Charles Stansfeld Jones/Frater Achad was located. His association with Crowley’s milieu is insufficient evidence. He should not be listed as a Furfur practitioner merely because he was a Thelemite.

### Owen Davies, Richard Kieckhefer, Jean-Patrice Boudet, Jan Veenstra and Julien Véronèse

These scholars illuminate the history of grimoires, medieval ritual magic, demon catalogues and learned magic. For Furfur specifically:

- Boudet is central to dating and contextualizing the *Livre des esperitz*.
- Kieckhefer supplies the Munich handbook context.
- Davies explains the social and publication history of grimoires.
- Veenstra and Véronèse provide broader scholarship on medieval learned magic and spirit traditions.
- None should be cited as proving an ancient god behind Furfur.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Did Furfur first appear in 1577?

No, not probably.

- **Older common claim:** Weyer, 1577, is the first appearance.
- **Evidence against it:** Cambridge MS O.8.29 contains a Furfur entry in the *Livre des esperitz*.
- **Qualification:** the manuscript’s exact date is disputed, but its hand is normally placed no later than the middle sixteenth century.
- **Conclusion:** Weyer is the first securely dated **printed** attestation and the first attestation of the fiery hart; the *Livre* is the earliest surviving name witness.

### 2. Is Furfur Weyer no. 29 or 30?

Weyer’s printed text labels Furfur **§30**. The confusion results from:

- Scot’s omission of Pruflas;
- normalized comparison charts;
- accidental row shifting near Foras;
- people counting only surviving Scot entries.

For source citation, “Weyer §30” is preferable.

### 3. Is the name Furtur?

**Documented scribal error.** Only Sloane 3825 among Peterson’s principal witnesses reads *Furtur*. Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, Harley 6483, Weyer, Scot and the older French witness read *Furfur*. *Furtur* is historically real as a manuscript reading but almost certainly secondary.

### 4. Does the name mean “scoundrel”?

Only if the speculative *furcifer* derivation is accepted. The directly attested Latin word *furfur* means bran or scurf. No early explanation chooses between deliberate Latin wordplay and corruption.

### 5. Is Furfur an ancient stag god?

No documentary evidence supports that claim. The stag first appears in Weyer, not the oldest French entry. Cernunnos and comparable identifications are modern syncretism.

### 6. Does Furfur govern marriage or heterosexuality specifically?

Scot says “man and wife,” but Weyer says *virum et mulierem*, “man and woman,” and Mathers follows the broader sense. The tradition is gendered and heteronormative in its historical language, but claims that Furfur exclusively protects legal marriage depend on one translation choice.

### 7. Does Furfur lie even inside the triangle?

The grammar says the opposite: he lies in everything **unless** brought into the triangle. Modern claims that he remains wholly unreliable inside it contradict the literal promise of the entry, although the surrounding ritual tradition still treats spirits as requiring compulsion and testing.

### 8. Is the angelic form Lehahiah?

No. Furfur’s own “angelic form” is part of the Weyer description. Lehahiah is a separate angel later assigned by number in the Rudd scheme.

### 9. Is Lehahiah a Jewish angel opposed to Furfur?

Only with qualification.

- The root triplet comes from Jewish 72-name exegesis.
- The angelized name belongs to Christian/Hermetic Cabala.
- The direct Furfur opposition is the Rudd/Smart magical system.
- It is not stated in Exodus, the *Bahir*, or the standard *Ars Goetia*.

### 10. Is Furfur Virgo/Nine of Pentacles or Pisces/Eight of Cups?

Both associations occur, but they describe different constructions:

- **Lehahiah:** 15°–20° Virgo; Nine of Pentacles.
- **Furfur in *777*:** 0°–10° Pisces; Eight of Cups.

Assigning Furfur directly to Lehahiah’s Virgo quinance is a later fusion of Rudd’s numerical pairing with the angelic zodiac.

### 11. Is the earl’s metal iron?

- Manuscript prescription: copper and silver.
- Mathers/Golden Dawn rationalization: Mars and iron.

Both are historically attested, but at different stages.

### 12. Are 29 or 36 legions valid variants?

No early Furfur source supports them. Twenty-six is stable from 1577 onward.

### 13. Does Furfur cause earthquakes?

Not in the principal sources. This probably reflects confusion with other spirits or modern elemental amplification.

### 14. Is the winged hart ancient?

No. The early text says stag; Le Breton’s 1863 engraving adds wings and hybrid anatomy. Modern artists then made the winged hart look canonical.

### 15. Does Furfur appear in the *Sworn Book of Honorius*?

No Furfur entry was located in the *Liber juratus*/*Sworn Book*. Websites asserting this without a book, chapter, folio or variant spelling should not be treated as source evidence.

### 16. Are modern enns and offerings old Goetic material?

No. “Ganen menach tasa Furfur,” cypress, dragon’s blood, fixed candle colors, gender declarations, preferred liquor, electrical-device phenomena and similar material belong to recent demonolatry or individual practice. They are legitimate evidence for modern reception, but not for Weyer or the manuscripts.

## Open questions

1. **What did the name mean to the compiler of the *Livre des esperitz*?**  
   The Latin noun is visible, but no medieval gloss explains why it became a spirit name.

2. **How did the angelic count acquire a fiery stag form?**  
   No surviving intermediate text bridges the French description and Weyer’s expanded one.

3. **Was Weyer copying a lost version of the *Livre des esperitz*, a separate *Liber officiorum*, or a conflation?**  
   Shared ordering and offices show relationship, but the exact stemma remains incomplete.

4. **How old is Trinity O.8.29?**  
   Cataloguing and paleographic judgments differ by as much as a century. Scientific analysis or a new codicological study might narrow the range.

5. **What exactly does abbreviated Old French *phỹie* represent?**  
   “Physics/natural philosophy” is plausible, but a full examination of the hand, abbreviations and parallel entries is desirable.

6. **Who designed the Goetic seals?**  
   They do not occur in Weyer or Scot, and no source names an artist or supplies a construction rule for Furfur’s emblem.

7. **Which parts of Harley 6483 are genuinely Thomas Rudd’s?**  
   Peter Smart’s copying is secure; the authorship and dating of his exemplars remain debated.

8. **When was *Furfur → furcifer* first proposed?**  
   It is widely repeated but rarely credited. No early printed derivation has yet been identified.

9. **When did *Furtur* become treated as a separate “alias” rather than a Sloane error?**  
   Peterson’s scrupulous reproduction made the form visible, but the precise popularization route deserves bibliographical tracing.

10. **Can every modern game appearance be confirmed from primary game data?**  
    *Devil Children: White Book* is attested through specialist indexes, but an official Atlus demon compendium or game-text extraction would be preferable to a fan-maintained wiki.
