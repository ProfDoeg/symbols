# Buné: Research Dossier

## Method and evidentiary labels

This dossier distinguishes the following kinds of claim:

- **Documented text** — stated directly in a dated manuscript, printed edition, or facsimile.
- **Scholarly reconstruction** — an inference by historians, editors, codicologists, or philologists from the surviving evidence.
- **Tradition** — a claim transmitted within ritual or occult literature without independent historical confirmation.
- **Esoteric correspondence** — an attribution constructed within a magical system, such as planetary metals, zodiacal decans, tarot cards, or angel-demon pairings.
- **Disputed claim** — a proposal for which the surviving evidence is insufficient, contradictory, or contested.
- **Legend** — supernatural narrative reported as part of the tradition, not as demonstrable history.
- **Modern invention or reception** — an attribution, representation, or practice not documented in the early source chain.

A textual limitation should be stated at the outset: the requested Weyer, Scot, and *Goetia* entries are linked to complete facsimiles and transcriptions below, but only short exact excerpts are reproduced here. The remainder is presented in clause-by-clause translation and comparison.

The central historical result is that **Bune is not a demon first invented by Weyer in 1577**. A substantially similar Bune entry survives in a French *Livre des Esperitz* manuscript from the fifteenth century. Conversely, no evidence presently connects the name securely with an ancient god, hero, city, or Greek or Latin noun.

Peterson’s manuscript collation explicitly reports that Harley 6483 and Sloane 3648 have *Bime*, while Sloane 2731 reads *Bune* and records *Bime* as the reading of another copy. It also observes that the manuscript itself has no accent in *Bime*, despite Mathers’s accented *Bimé*. [Peterson’s manuscript-based *Goetia*, item 26](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

### The earliest Bune

**Documented text.** In the *Livre des Esperitz*, item 27, the surviving French begins:

> “Bune est ung grant duc qui faict les corps aler et venir d’un lieu en aultre…”

That is: Bune is a great duke who makes bodies go and come, travelling from one place to another. The rest of the entry gives wealth, wise public speech, truthful answers, and thirty-five legions. It does not describe a dragon, individual heads, a voice, subordinate demons over graves, or a seal. [Manuscript transcription at fol. 181v](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Livre_des_esperitz/Bune); [Biblissima record for Trinity O.8.29](https://portail.biblissima.fr/fr/ark:/43093/mdata454dde45506284fbdb223f1ab242101b25c2e133).

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Jean-Patrice Boudet placed the *Livre des Esperitz* in the medieval ancestry of the demonological “who’s who” inherited by Weyer. Of the manuscript’s spirits, thirty-five correspond to figures in Weyer, while fifteen are absent from Weyer. The relationship is not simple verbatim copying: it points to a mobile family of *Offices of Spirits* catalogues. [Boudet, “Les who’s who démonologiques de la Renaissance et leurs ancêtres médiévaux”](https://journals.openedition.org/medievales/1019).

### Does the name come from an ancient god?

**Finding:** no defensible ancient derivation is presently established.

Unlike names such as Astaroth/Astarte, Asmoday/Ashmedai, Baal, or Belial, *Bune* has no accepted line of historical forms connecting it to a Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Greek, Roman, Jewish, or Christian being. Neither the *Livre des Esperitz*, Weyer, Scot, the early *Goetia* manuscripts, nor de Plancy supplies an etymology.

No convincing derivation from a Greek or Latin common noun has been demonstrated. Similar-looking words are inadequate by themselves: historical etymology requires a credible chain of languages, spellings, dates, and meanings.

#### The Buto/Wadjet hypothesis

**Disputed claim.** Carroll “Poke” Runyon writes that Buné’s origin is unknown but “might be related” to the Egyptian goddess Buto. “Buto” is the Greek form associated with the Egyptian cobra goddess Wadjet and with the Delta city Per-Wadjet/Buto. Runyon’s suggestion has spread through modern demonological summaries. [Runyon, *The Book of Solomon’s Magick*, p. 156 in the linked scan](https://attfinnasanningen.se/dokument_offentligt/books/mystery_school/magic/poke%20runyon%20book%20of%20solomons%20magick.pdf).

Evidence for the proposal is weak:

- No medieval or early modern source calls Bune “Buto.”
- No intermediate form demonstrating the phonetic change has been produced.
- The operations of Wadjet and those assigned to Bune are not close enough to establish identity.
- “Buto” may name a city, a Greek rendering of a goddess, or an associated cult title; modern retellings sometimes conflate Wadjet, Buto, and Isis.
- Runyon himself presents the proposal tentatively, not as a demonstrated etymology.

It should therefore be recorded as a twentieth-century occult hypothesis, not an ancient identity recovered from the manuscripts.

#### Other lookalikes

**Disputed or unsupported.**

- The medieval and early modern *Bune* has not been shown to be connected with the Islamic occult author Aḥmad al-Būnī.
- *Buniel* in some Theurgia-Goetia material is a different name and office; resemblance does not establish identity.
- Modern spellings such as *Bene* are generally copying mistakes or loose transliterations rather than early witnesses.
- No reliable evidence derives *Bune* from English “boon,” French *bon*, Latin *bonus*, or German *Bühne*. These are folk etymologies based on surface resemblance.

## Number, order, and the passage from 69 to 72

### Bune’s movement through the catalogues

- *Livre des Esperitz*: no. 27.
- Weyer: no. 24.
- Scot: no. 24.
- *Ars Goetia*: no. 26.

**Scholarly reconstruction.** The *Goetia* catalogue depends especially closely on the English Weyer material printed in Scot, but radically rearranges it. Peterson remarks that the reshuffling sometimes resembles a stack of separately written leaves becoming disordered. That is a reconstruction rather than a proven event, but the manuscript practice of devoting individual leaves to spirits makes it plausible. [Peterson’s Weyer introduction](https://esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

### The 69/72 problem

Weyer’s printed *Pseudomonarchia* has 69 entries, although its Belial narrative refers to 72 kings imprisoned by Solomon.

The *Goetia* has 72 because it:

- Adds **Vassago** near the beginning, as no. 3.
- Adds **Seere**, **Dantalion**, and **Andromalius** as nos. 70–72.
- Omits Weyer’s no. 4, **Pruflas**, also called Bufas.

Thus the net change is plus three: 69 − 1 + 4 = 72.

**Documented transmission issue.** Pruflas is absent from Scot’s English catalogue, either because Scot or his translator accidentally omitted it or because the exemplar available to them already lacked it. The *Goetia* also omits Pruflas. This is one of the strongest signs that the *Goetia* compiler used the Scot transmission rather than independently returning to a complete Weyer print. [Peterson’s comparative discussion](https://esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm); [comparative spirit table](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia_spirits.pdf).

**Tradition and legend.** The number 72 was not obtained by historical census. It carries theological and magical prestige: 72 divine names, 72 angels, and the legend of 72 infernal rulers confined in Solomon’s vessel. The later Rudd system makes the numerical symmetry operational by setting one angel over each demon.

### 1. *Livre des Esperitz*, fifteenth century

**Documented text.** The complete content, rendered closely into modern English, is:

Bune is a great duke. He makes bodies go, come, and travel from one place to another. He makes a man rich and able to speak wisely before all people. He gives a true answer concerning whatever is asked of him. He has thirty-five legions.

The expression *les corps* means bodies. Whether these are corpses is strongly suggested by the later transmission, but the surviving French does not itself use *cadavres* or *morts*. The text is therefore compatible with, but not quite identical to, the later claim that he relocates “the dead.”

**Developmental significance.** Almost every fundamental office is already present: bodily relocation, riches, eloquence, truthful answers, ducal rank, and legions. The dragon and grave-demons are later elaborations or material absent from this abbreviated witness.

### 2. Weyer, *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*, 1577, §24

Weyer’s Latin opens:

> “Bune Dux magnus & fortis, apparet ut draco, tribus capitibus, tertium verò assimilatur homini.”

A close translation of the complete entry is:

Bune is a great and strong duke. He appears as a dragon with three heads, of which the third resembles a man. He speaks with a mute, inarticulate, or indistinct voice. He makes the dead change place and causes demons to gather above the sepulchres of the deceased. He enriches a person in every way and renders that person eloquent or talkative and wise. He answers questions truly. Thirty legions obey him.

Edition reference: *Pseudomonarchia daemonum*, §24; in the 1660 *Opera omnia* pagination represented in Peterson’s apparatus, the surrounding text falls on p. 920. The relevant number in the original 1577 catalogue is §24, not “page 920” of the 1577 printing. [Peterson’s Latin text and edition notes](https://esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

#### *Muta voce*

**Documented text with disputed interpretation.** Weyer prints *Muta loquitur voce*. Literally, *muta voce* is “with a mute voice.” Depending on context, it could indicate voiceless, dumb, inarticulate, subdued, or difficult-to-hear speech. It does not ordinarily mean divine.

This is probably the point at which a copying or translation error generated a new characteristic.

### 3. Reginald Scot, *The Discoverie of Witchcraft*, 1584

Scot begins:

> “Bune is a great and a strong Duke, he appeareth as a dragon with three heads…”

The remainder says that the third head is like a man; Bune speaks “with a divine voice”; makes the dead change their place; makes devils assemble on their sepulchres; greatly enriches a man; makes him eloquent and wise; answers all demands truly; and is obeyed by thirty legions.

Edition reference: Book XV, the demon catalogue traditionally printed as chapter 2. The 1584 pagination and later reprints differ substantially, so book, chapter, and entry are the stable references. [Cornell’s 1584 facsimile record](https://digital.library.cornell.edu/catalog/witchcraft081); [1886 Brinsley Nicholson reprint](https://archive.org/details/discoverieofwitc00scot); [Peterson’s aligned Weyer–Scot text](https://esotericarchives.com/solomon/weyer.htm).

#### From “mute” to “divine”

**Scholarly reconstruction.** No convincing semantic route takes Latin *muta* directly to English “divine.” The English probably reflects:

- a mistranscription of an abbreviation or damaged word;
- a translator’s misreading of the Latin exemplar;
- or corruption in an intermediate manuscript.

Peterson argues more generally that Scot’s translator may have worked from a careless or partially illegible manuscript copy rather than directly from a printed Weyer edition. The translation may have been prepared by Abraham Fleming, but that attribution is not certain.

“Divine voice” was not retained by the *Goetia*, which substituted “high and comely.” The resulting three-stage sequence is therefore:

1. Weyer: mute or indistinct.
2. Scot: divine.
3. *Goetia*: high and pleasing.

### 4. Sloane 3825, fol. 104r

The manuscript begins:

> “The 26th spirit is called Bune, he is a strong, great & mighty duke…”

Clause by clause, the manuscript says:

- The twenty-sixth spirit is Bune.
- He is a strong, great, mighty duke.
- He appears as a dragon with three heads.
- One head is like a dog.
- Another is like a griffin.
- The third is like a man.
- He speaks with a high and comely voice.
- He changes the places of the dead.
- He makes the spirits beneath him gather upon their sepulchres.
- He gives riches.
- He makes a man wise and eloquent.
- He gives true answers to demands.
- He governs thirty legions.
- His seal is one to which he owes obedience.
- A second seal or character is supplied.
- Either may be used, but Solomon is said to prefer the first.

This is the most important Buné witness for the usual modern description. [Peterson’s diplomatic transcription of Sloane 3825, fol. 104r](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

### 5. Sloane 2731 and 3648

**Documented variants.**

- Sloane 2731 has **Bune**, with a marginal note saying that another copy reads **Bime**.
- Sloane 3648 has **Bime**.
- The descriptive substance remains in the same *Goetia* family.

These readings demonstrate that *Bime* predates Mathers and Crowley. It is not their invention, although the accented *Bimé* is Mathers’s editorial presentation.

### 6. Harley 6483 and Rudd

Harley 6483, internally dated 1712–1713 and presented as copied from “the sheets of Dr Rudd,” reads **Bime**. It preserves the larger *Lemegeton* structure under the title *Liber Malorum Spirituum seu Goetia*. [Harley 6483 contents](https://www.alchemywebsite.com/lemeget.html); [Peterson’s manuscript survey](https://www2.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm).

**Attribution caveat.** Thomas Rudd died in 1656. Harley 6483 was copied decades later, apparently by Peter Smart, and its relationship to Rudd’s own work is indirect. “The Goetia of Dr Rudd” is therefore a convenient traditional designation, not proof that Rudd personally wrote the surviving manuscript.

### 7. Mathers–Crowley, *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King*, 1904

The printed entry begins:

> “The Twenty-sixth Spirit is Buné (or Bim). He is a Strong, Great and Mighty Duke.”

The remainder closely follows the Sloane text: three-headed dragon, dog/griffin/man heads, high and comely voice, changing the place of the dead, gathering subordinate spirits upon sepulchres, riches, wisdom, eloquence, true answers, thirty legions, and two seals.

Edition reference: Mathers and Crowley, 1904, printed p. 20; PDF image p. 33 in the linked facsimile. [1904 facsimile](https://biglevi.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/The-Book-Of-The-Goetia-Of-Solomon-The-King-A-Crowley-1904.pdf); [Harvard copy at Google Books](https://books.google.com/books?vid=Harvard%3A32044011342870).

#### The reversed seal preference

The manuscript says the **first** seal is best. The 1904 edition says the **last**, meaning the second form shown, is best.

This is a concrete editorial divergence. It may be a transcription mistake, typesetting confusion caused by the placement of the two figures, or a deliberate editorial preference; the book does not explain it. Modern readers often reproduce Mathers’s statement without knowing that Sloane 3825 says the opposite.

### 8. Peterson, 2001

Joseph H. Peterson’s *The Lesser Key of Solomon* was published by Weiser in 2001. Its *Goetia* text is based primarily on Sloane 3825, with manuscript variants from Sloane 2731, Sloane 3648, Harley 6483, and other witnesses. The stable reference is *Goetia* no. 26; Peterson’s online equivalent locates it at Sloane 3825, fol. 104r. [Publisher record](https://redwheelweiser.com/book/the-lesser-key-of-solomon-9781578632206/); [Folger catalogue](https://catalog.folger.edu/record/234358).

Peterson restores the manuscript’s preference for the first seal and explains the Bune/Bime variants rather than silently normalizing them.

## What Buné does—and does not do

### Offices actually attested

**Documented text:**

- Relocates bodies or the dead.
- Causes subordinate demons or spirits to assemble above or upon graves.
- Grants wealth.
- Grants wisdom and eloquence.
- Gives truthful answers.

### Offices not assigned to Buné in the early texts

Buné does **not** teach languages, all sciences, invisibility, transmutation, love, familiars, or treasure-finding in the principal source chain. Those are offices of other Goetic spirits.

Modern spirit profiles sometimes transfer generic Goetic capabilities to Buné. Unless separately identified as practitioner testimony, those additions are textual errors.

“Riches” does not necessarily mean locating buried treasure. The manuscripts use general language for enriching the petitioner; they do not describe Buné as a treasure-discoverer.

### The dead and “necromancy”

**Documented text.** Buné changes the location of the dead and gathers spirits at sepulchres.

**Interpretive question.** Whether this means physically moving corpses, moving souls, changing burial places, or rearranging the supernatural occupants of graves is not stated. The *Livre des Esperitz* refers to moving “bodies.” Weyer changes the object to “the dead.” The *Goetia* speaks of “places of the dead.”

**Modern reception.** Contemporary practitioners often classify Buné as a spirit of necromancy, ancestor work, funerary magic, or cemetery magic. That classification is a reasonable interpretation of the textual offices, but the elaborate modern claims that Buné reconciles restless ancestors, guides souls, or rules a specific underworld domain are not found in the early witnesses.

### “Your sepulchres”

The Sloane manuscript’s possessive construction—spirits gathering upon “their” or “your” sepulchres—has been normalized differently by editors. Weyer has demons gathering over the graves of the deceased. Scot has devils assembling upon the sepulchres of the dead. The safest meaning is that Buné collects subordinate spirits over graves, not necessarily over tombs personally owned by the conjurer.

## Rank, planet, metal, and time

### Duke: copper and Venus

The *Goetia*’s general observations prescribe:

- kings: gold/Sun;
- marquises: silver/Moon;
- dukes: copper/Venus;
- princes or “prelates”: tin/Jupiter;
- knights: lead/Saturn;
- presidents: mercury/Mercury;
- earls: copper and silver.

Thus Buné’s rank implies **copper and Venus**. This correspondence belongs to the *Goetia*’s material technology of seals. It is not stated in the earlier Bune description and should not be mistaken for an etymological or mythological identity with Venus. [The *Goetia*’s observations on metals](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm).

### Time of conjuration

The same observations say:

> Dukes may be bound from sunrise until noon in clear weather.

Accordingly, Buné’s traditional rank-based period is the morning, sunrise to noon, with clear weather specified.

The text also recommends working when the Moon is 2, 4, 6, 8, 10, 12, or 14 days old.

**Later esoteric practice.** Some modern magicians add Friday, the day of Venus, or a planetary hour of Venus. That is a coherent combination of planetary magic with the rank-metal table, but the Buné entry itself does not say “Friday” or “hour of Venus.”

## The seals

### Earliest evidence

- *Livre des Esperitz*: none.
- Weyer: none.
- Scot: none.
- Seventeenth-century *Goetia*: two.
- Mathers–Crowley: two, redrawn for print.

The Goetic seals therefore cannot be projected back automatically into the fifteenth-century Bune tradition. Peterson notes that medieval and sixteenth-century spirit catalogues either omit individual characters or supply figures unlike the later *Goetia* seals.

### Verbal description

Both Buné figures are abstract linear characters, not pictorial depictions of a dragon. They use straight and curved strokes, loops, crossings, and terminal hooks or circles. Exact identity depends on stroke order, orientation, and the relationship among small terminals; verbal summaries are inherently less precise than the manuscript images.

The important manuscript distinctions are:

1. **First character:** the figure identified as the first seal in Sloane 3825.
2. **Second character:** introduced as “another seal or character.”
3. Sloane 3825 permits either but calls the first best.
4. Mathers–Crowley prints the two figures but calls the last best.
5. Mathers incorporates or places **BUNE** conspicuously with his printed rendering; this lettering should not be presumed original to every manuscript seal.
6. Harley’s catalogue name is *Bime*, not Mathers’s accented *Bimé*.

For exact geometry, the manuscript-derived images beside item 26 in [Peterson’s edition](https://www.esotericarchives.com/solomon/goetia.htm) and the [1904 facsimile, printed p. 20](https://biglevi.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/The-Book-Of-The-Goetia-Of-Solomon-The-King-A-Crowley-1904.pdf) should be treated as the evidence. A modern vectorization is available at [Wikimedia Commons](https://commons.wikimedia.org/wiki/File:26-bune-sigilo-ars-goetia.svg), but vector files regularize the irregular hand-drawn strokes.

### The 72 names from Exodus

**Textual and traditional basis.** Exodus 14:19–21 consists, in the Masoretic Hebrew text, of three consecutive verses traditionally counted as 72 letters each. In the familiar construction:

1. The first verse is written in one direction.
2. The second is written in reverse.
3. The third is written in the first direction.
4. The letters are read in vertical groups of three.

This produces 72 three-letter combinations. No. 26 is **האא**.

Suffixing the triads with forms such as **־יה** (*-iah, -yah*) or **־אל** (*-el*) turns the combinations into pronounceable angelic names. Haaiah is one such expanded form.

**Historical distinction.** The biblical verses themselves do not name 72 angels. Nor does “Shem ha-Mephorash” always mean this 72-fold construction. In Jewish literature the expression “explicit” or “ineffable name” may designate the Tetragrammaton or other extended divine names, including 12-, 42-, and 72-letter forms. The fully individualized angel list belongs to a later magical and Christian-kabbalistic development.

### Sefer Raziel and Jewish transmission

**Tradition with manuscript history.** The *Sefer Raziel ha-Malakh* is a medieval compilation, not a book demonstrably delivered by an angel to the biblical Adam. Its constituent materials developed over time; portions may date from roughly the eleventh century onward, while the familiar printed Hebrew edition appeared at Amsterdam in 1701.

It transmits divine names, angelic names, talismanic material, and forms of the 72-fold name. The triads are older than Lenain’s individualized nineteenth-century guardian-angel profiles. [Joshua Trachtenberg’s discussion of divine names](https://sacred-texts.com/jud/jms/jms09.htm); [Trachtenberg’s appendices](https://sacred-texts.com/jud/jms/jms19.htm); [recent scholarly discussion of *Sefer Raziel*](https://www.degruyter.com/document/doi/10.1515/asch-2024-2017/html).

### Reuchlin, Christian Cabala, and Kircher

**Scholarly reconstruction.** Renaissance Christian cabalists transformed Jewish divine-name traditions into explicitly Christian angelological schemes.

Johannes Reuchlin’s *De arte cabalistica* (1517) is especially important in the Latin reception of the 72 angelic names. Subsequent writers combined them with celestial hierarchies, Psalms, astrology, magical seals, and Christian divine names. Ian Christie-Miller’s study *72 in His Name* examines this textual history. [JSTOR edition](https://www.jstor.org/stable/j.ctv1zjg3h9).

Athanasius Kircher’s *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652–1654) reproduced and elaborated cabalistic diagrams and lists in a vast Christian-Egyptological synthesis. Kircher is an important transmitter, but his Egyptian claims reflect seventeenth-century speculative Egyptology, not modern decipherment of Egyptian religion. [Kircher scan](https://www.esonet.org/wp-content/uploads/2010/06/Oedipus_Aegyptiacus02.pdf).

### Rudd: Haaiah constraining Bime/Buné

**Documented esoteric scheme.** In Harley 6483’s Rudd material, the 72 Shem angels are paired ordinally with the 72 Goetic spirits. Angel 26 Haaiah is therefore placed over demon 26 Bime/Buné.

The governing principle is numerical and ritual:

```text
Haaiah, angel 26
        ↓ divine constraint
Bime/Buné, spirit 26
```

The angelic name or seal is integrated with the demon’s character and with protective ritual equipment so that the spirit is approached under a corresponding divine authority. Skinner and Rankine describe the Rudd system as using the 72 angels to bind or constrain their 72 infernal counterparts, including angelic material associated with the breastplate and Solomon’s brass vessel.

**Tradition.** This does not mean that a biblical or ancient Jewish text says Haaiah defeated Buné. The opposition is an early eighteenth-century Christian magical construction attached to the *Goetia* catalogue.

Stephen Skinner and David Rankine published this recension as *The Goetia of Dr Rudd* in 2007. [Publisher/catalogue record](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Goetia_of_Dr_Rudd.html?id=AFx3ngEACAAJ); [Peterson’s Harley manuscript discussion](https://www2.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm).

### Lenain, 1823

Lazare Lenain’s entry identifies:

> “26e. Haaiah. Son attribut (Dieu caché).”

Lenain gives:

- Divine attribute: “Hidden God.”
- Divine names: *Agdy* and *Abdi*, described as Saracen-language forms.
- Arc: 126°–130° inclusive.
- Decade/genius: the thirteenth decade and *Aphruimis*.
- Five scattered annual days: April 14, June 25, September 5, November 16, January 27.
- Daily invocation time: 8:20–8:40 a.m.
- Psalm: Vulgate 118:145.
- Virtues: success in litigation, favorable judges, pursuit of truth, contemplation of divine matters, diplomacy, ambassadors, plenipotentiaries, treaties, commerce, couriers, dispatches, agents, and secret expeditions.
- Contrary qualities: traitors, destructive ambition, conspiracies, and conspirators.

[Original 1823 scan, pp. 62–63 in the book’s pagination](https://archive.org/details/BIUSante_ms05365); [Google Books record](https://books.google.com/books/about/La_science_cabalistique_ou_l_art_de_conn.html?id=BqzyzEEX3MAC).

#### Psalm verification

Lenain cites Vulgate Psalm 118:145:

> “Clamavi in toto corde meo, exaudi me Domine…”

The full sense is: “I cried with my whole heart; hear me, O Lord; I will seek/keep your justifications or statutes.”

Because the Greek and Latin Psalters number many psalms one lower than the Hebrew-derived modern system, this is **Psalm 119:145** in most present English Bibles.

### The quinance

Lenain’s 126°–130° should be interpreted carefully.

- 0° Aries begins the zodiacal circle.
- 120° is 0° Leo.
- 126°–130° is therefore the latter part of the 5°–10° Leo sector.
- Traditional tables often state this as the sixth through tenth degrees of Leo.
- Modern occult tables normally normalize it to **5°00′–9°59′ Leo**.

The approximate solar dates are July 28 through August 1 or 2, but the exact calendar endpoints vary with inclusive counting and the Sun’s actual ingress times.

Lenain’s five scattered days—April 14, June 25, September 5, November 16, and January 27—are a different system. They distribute each angel’s influence across the year; they are not the same as the continuous five-day Leo birth interval later popularized as a “guardian angel” period.

### Papus

Gérard Encausse, “Papus,” supplied a letter-preface for the 1909 reissue of Lenain and helped return the book to circulation through the French occult revival. [BnF catalogue record for the 1909 edition](https://catalogue.bnf.fr/ark:/12148/cb30789329z).

Papus transmitted and popularized the system; he was not the first source for Haaiah’s “Hidden God,” zodiacal arc, or Psalm. Those are already in Lenain.

### Golden Dawn

**Esoteric correspondence.** Golden Dawn teaching incorporated the 72 names into:

- five-degree zodiacal divisions or quinances;
- hierarchies and archangels;
- Psalm verses;
- Hebrew-letter analysis;
- sigils derived through Rose Cross or related techniques.

The Golden Dawn did not originate the Haaiah name, but it systematized and transmitted it in English-speaking ceremonial magic. Its material was assembled from Reuchlin, Agrippa, Kircher, French occultism, Mathers’s manuscripts, and cognate sources. [*The Complete Golden Dawn System of Magic* scan](https://logoilibrary.com/wp-content/uploads/2019/07/Hour_Of_The_Time_10042012-The_Complete_Golden_Dawn_System_of_Magic.pdf).

Published Golden Dawn-derived tables are not uniform. Some later organizations shift starting points, rulers, or calendar dates. A table should therefore be cited to a specific edition rather than treated as a universal “Kabbalistic” assignment.

### Ambelain

Robert Ambelain’s *La Kabbale pratique* (1951) reorganized the angels into an elaborate practical system. It places Haaiah among the Dominations, under Zadkiel and the sephirah Chesed, and expands the astrological, devotional, and talismanic material. [Ambelain scan](https://www.markfoster.net/rn/texts/practicalkabbalah-part1.pdf).

This is a twentieth-century synthesis. Its Uranian, bodily, psychological, and detailed biographical correspondences are not found in Rudd or Lenain.

### Haziel

The French-language author publishing as Haziel popularized the “72 guardian angels” through books, prayers, cards, birth periods, exhortations, colors, professions, and life-purpose interpretations. [Éditions Bussière](https://www.editionsbussiere.com/product_info-products_id-41.html); [Haziel angel cards](https://www.editions-tredaniel.com/haziel-les-cartes-des-anges-p-4053.html).

**Modern reception.** Haziel’s Haaiah is a devotional and psychological guardian angel of diplomacy, justice, discretion, and political service. This literature descends broadly from Lenain, Papus, and Ambelain. Its detailed personality readings and personal guardian-angel claims are not evidence for medieval Jewish belief.

### Why each pip card can carry two spirits

Crowley’s system in *Liber 777* divides the 72 Goetic spirits into:

- nos. 1–36 as daytime demons;
- nos. 37–72 as nighttime demons.

The zodiac has 36 ten-degree decans. Each decan therefore receives one day spirit and one night spirit.

For the second decan of Sagittarius:

- Buné/Bim, no. 26: day.
- Volac, no. 62: night.
- Tarot: Nine of Wands.
- Planetary decan ruler: Moon.
- Golden Dawn title: Lord of Great Strength.

[Primary *777* scan](https://www.bibliotecapleyades.net/crowley/liber/libers/liber777.pdf).

This is distinct from the 72 angelic quinances, which divide the zodiac into 72 sectors of five degrees. Number matching between the two lists does not make Crowley’s ten-degree decan identical to Haaiah’s five-degree Leo sector.

### Day or night

Buné is a **daytime spirit** in Crowley’s day/night decan table. Independently, the older *Goetia* timing for all dukes is sunrise to noon. The agreement is suggestive, but the systems are historically different: the rank rule predates Crowley’s *777* assignment.

### Planetary ambiguity

Buné has at least two planets in later tables:

- **Venus**, because Buné is a duke and a duke’s seal is copper.
- **Moon**, as ruler of the second decan of Sagittarius and therefore the Nine of Wands.

Haaiah may meanwhile be assigned Saturn through the first Leo decan, or Jupiter/Zadkiel through the choir of Dominations and Chesed. These are not necessarily “errors”; they arise from different correspondence grids.

## Enochian and other later overlays

### Harley 6483

The Rudd manuscript remarks generally that some spirits occur in “Enoch’s Tables.” That links the *Lemegeton* compilation with the Dee–Kelley angelic corpus as understood by its early eighteenth-century redactor. It does not prove that Buné individually is named in John Dee’s Great Table. [Harley manuscript summary](https://www.alchemywebsite.com/mss/mss479.htm).

### Crowley’s Enochian conjurations

The 1904 *Goetia* contains Enochian renderings or adaptations associated with the conjurations. These are Crowleyan additions to the early modern English *Goetia*, not part of Weyer’s Bune entry and not proof that Buné originated in Enochian magic. [Bibliographic description of the 1904 edition](https://www.100thmonkeypress.com/biblio/acrowley/books/goetia_1904/goetia_1904.htm).

Crowley also made the “Preliminary Invocation” or “Bornless Ritual” central to his presentation. Its divine names are Graeco-Egyptian, not Enochian, although Crowley’s system later placed both bodies of material in the same magical curriculum.

### Modern demonolatry

Modern traditions add:

- an “enn” or mantra;
- colors such as green, orange, gold, or black;
- incense and offerings;
- stones, metals, herbs, coins, oranges, or wine;
- a female or androgynous manifestation;
- titles such as “Duchess Buné”;
- claims of a direct, benevolent prosperity cult.

These are **modern reception and practitioner tradition**, not documented features of the *Livre des Esperitz*, Weyer, Scot, or Sloane manuscripts. Their importance to living practice need not be denied, but their date and evidence differ from the historical catalogue.

## Iconography

### Manuscript iconography

The earliest Bune witnesses provide no portrait. The *Goetia* manuscripts provide characters or seals, not representational drawings.

The three-headed dragon is therefore a verbal image before it is a visual one. The dog and griffin heads first become explicit in the *Goetia* family; Weyer only says that the third head resembles a man.

### Collin de Plancy

The searchable text of the 1818 first edition does not yield a Bune entry. The fully verified entry is in the illustrated sixth edition of 1863, p. 124.

De Plancy begins:

> “Bune, démon puissant, grand-duc aux enfers. Il a la forme d’un dragon avec trois têtes…”

He continues that only the third head is human; Buné speaks only by signs, moves corpses, haunts cemeteries, gathers demons over sepulchres, boasts of enriching and making eloquent those who serve him, and commands thirty legions.

De Plancy then adds a second paragraph about subordinate demons called **Bunis**, allegedly feared among “Tartars.” They are described as numerous, powerful, malevolent, dangerous to a guilty conscience, tamed by local sorcerers, and used for discovering the future. [De Plancy, 1863, p. 124](https://fr.wikisource.org/wiki/Dictionnaire_infernal/6e_%C3%A9d.%2C_1863/Bune).

#### Does Buné have a Louis Le Breton portrait?

**Finding: apparently no.** The 1863 entry has no demon portrait attached to it, and Buné is not among the securely identified Louis Le Breton plates in the searchable edition. This corrects a frequent modern assumption that every named Goetic demon in the dictionary received an engraving.

The famous composite-demon images circulating online as “Bune by Louis Le Breton” are often:

- modern illustrations;
- mislabeled pictures of another demon;
- recent composites based on the textual dog/griffin/man description;
- or reproductions of a Goetic seal rather than a portrait.

The sixth edition certainly contains numerous Le Breton illustrations, but it does not follow that every dictionary entry was illustrated. [Full 1863 scan](https://archive.org/details/dictionnaireinfe00coll_2); [Library of Congress record](https://www.loc.gov/item/63056292/).

### De Plancy’s changes

De Plancy is not simply translating Weyer:

- Weyer’s mute or indistinct speech becomes communication by signs.
- Cemetery-haunting is made explicit.
- “Enriches” becomes “boasts of enriching,” introducing skepticism.
- The Bunis and Tartar material appears without support in the cited Weyer entry.

**Legend/disputed ethnography.** The Bunis story has not been traced to a reliable Central Asian source. De Plancy footnotes Wierus for the main entry, but Weyer does not contain the Tartar paragraph. It should be treated as nineteenth-century demonological folklore until an earlier source is identified.

### Modern art

Modern Buné art generally follows one of three models:

1. A literal three-headed Western dragon with dog, griffin, and human heads.
2. A humanoid or feminized “Duchess Buné,” frequently associated with prosperity, jewels, coins, or orange and green colors.
3. An abstract sigil-based design derived from Mathers’s printed seal.

Only the first has a direct textual basis, and even there the early Weyer text specifies only the human third head. Feminine forms, Egyptian dress, pyramids, oranges, and Wadjet-like cobras largely arise from the Buto hypothesis and modern practitioner reception.

## People and works

### Johann Weyer

Weyer printed *Pseudomonarchia daemonum* as an appendix to the fifth edition of *De praestigiis daemonum* in 1577. He was an opponent of witch persecutions, but not a modern materialist who rejected all spiritual realities. He presented the demonic catalogue critically and said that he omitted material to make it unusable.

His cited source was a *Liber officiorum spirituum*, or Book of the Offices of Spirits. The title was already known to Trithemius in 1508. Bune’s earlier appearance in the *Livre des Esperitz* supports Weyer’s claim that he was drawing on an older catalogue tradition.

### Reginald Scot and Abraham Fleming

Scot published the English catalogue within his skeptical *Discoverie of Witchcraft* in 1584. His purpose was exposure and critique, not instruction in demon worship. Peterson tentatively attributes the translation to Abraham Fleming and argues that it reflects a corrupt or difficult intermediary.

Scot’s wording became operational source material despite his skeptical purpose. The *Goetia* compiler used it closely, and the 1665 printing may have been especially important because its distinctive mistakes and spellings recur downstream.

### The anonymous *Lemegeton* compiler

The *Lemegeton* is an English seventeenth-century compilation of:

1. *Ars Goetia*.
2. *Theurgia-Goetia*.
3. *Ars Paulina*.
4. *Ars Almadel*.
5. *Ars Notoria*.

The catalogue adapts the Weyer–Scot tradition, while its conjurations borrow from wider Solomonic and planetary magic, including material related to the *Heptameron*. It should not be dated as an intact book to Solomon, antiquity, or the Middle Ages. [Peterson’s manuscript introduction](https://www2.esotericarchives.com/solomon/lemegeton.htm).

### Thomas Rudd and Peter Smart

The surviving Rudd manuscripts are presented as copies from “Dr Rudd’s” sheets, apparently prepared by Peter Smart in 1712–1714. They combine the *Goetia* with angelic and cabalistic restraints. The precise amount attributable to Thomas Rudd personally remains disputed.

### Mathers and Crowley

Samuel Liddell MacGregor Mathers prepared the English manuscript transcription or translation underlying the 1904 publication. Aleister Crowley published it as *The Book of the Goetia of Solomon the King* and added:

- editorial framing;
- an “Initiated Interpretation of Ceremonial Magic”;
- the Preliminary Invocation;
- Enochian material;
- diagrams and practical adaptations.

Crowley’s psychological language sometimes treats demons as portions of the human mind or brain, but this did not replace his ritual treatment of them as operative intelligences.

### Frater Achad

**Negative finding.** No Buné-specific operation, doctrine, or historical contribution by Charles Stansfeld Jones/Frater Achad was located in the sources examined. His importance to Crowleyan qabalah does not justify listing him as a documented Buné practitioner.

### Poke Runyon

Runyon reproduces the conventional entry, pairs Haaiah with Buné, adopts the five-degree Leo sector, and proposes the speculative Buto origin. He also supplies practitioner testimony that the entity grants money but may do so unpleasantly. The latter is personal occult testimony, not historical evidence.

### Lon Milo DuQuette and Christopher Hyatt

*Aleister Crowley’s Illustrated Goetia*, associated with DuQuette and Hyatt, helped modernize Crowley’s presentation and popularize psychological and practical readings of the spirits. It repeats Buné’s principal offices and contributes to the late twentieth-century revival. No securely sourced account of a distinctive Buné operation by DuQuette was located. [Linked edition](https://libraryofrickandria.com/wp-content/uploads/2024/05/Aleister-Crowleys-Illustrated-Goetia-Aleister-Crowley-Christopher-S_-Hyatt-Lon-Milo-Duquette-Third-1992-2000-New-Falcon-Publications.pdf).

### Stephen Skinner and David Rankine

Their 2007 *Goetia of Dr Rudd* brought Harley 6483’s angel-demon system into modern circulation and emphasized the controlling Shem angels, ritual breastplate, and Solomonic vessel.

### Joseph H. Peterson

Peterson’s 2001 edition and online diplomatic texts are central because they separate manuscript readings from Mathers’s editorial changes. His collation establishes the exact Bune/Bime distribution and the first-seal preference in Sloane 3825.

### Jake Stratton-Kent

Stratton-Kent’s work situates goetia within longer traditions of spirit offices, necromantic practice, and Mediterranean magic. He is important to modern reinterpretation of the grimoires as more than a Christian catalogue of fallen angels. No unique Buné etymology attributable to him was verified here; claims that he “proved” Buné to be a specific pagan deity would exceed the evidence.

### Owen Davies

Davies treats grimoires as historically circulating books whose meanings changed through copying, print, trade, and colonial transmission. His work is especially useful against the assumption that the *Lesser Key* appeared as a fixed ancient text. [Owen Davies, *Grimoires: A History of Magic Books*](https://academic.oup.com/book/47570).

## Grimoire ancestors and analogues

### *Testament of Solomon*

The late-antique Greek *Testament of Solomon* presents Solomon questioning demons, learning their functions, and compelling them through angels. It is a major ancestor of the general Solomonic demon-binding legend.

**Negative finding:** Buné does not appear under that name. Haaiah is not given as Buné’s opponent. The structural idea of demon–angel countermeasures is ancient, but the particular Buné–Haaiah pair is Rudd’s much later construction. [English text](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Testament_of_Solomon).

### Munich handbook, Clm 849

The fifteenth-century Munich handbook contains a catalogue of spirits and operations for treasure, answers, love, travel, and other ends. Several names correspond closely with Weyer’s later catalogue—Barbatos, Curson, Eligor, Volac, Gomory, Cimeries, and Amy among them.

**Negative finding:** no Bune entry has been identified there. The handbook is evidence for the wider medieval catalogue genre, not presently for Bune himself. [Richard Kieckhefer, *Forbidden Rites*](https://books.google.com/books/about/Forbidden_Rites.html?id=V-rDEAAAQBAJ).

### *Livre des Esperitz*

This is Buné’s most important direct pre-Weyer witness. Its wording shows that moving bodies, wealth, wise speech, and truthful answers preceded the dragon iconography.

### *Liber Officiorum Spirituum*

Weyer names a Book of the Offices of Spirits as his source. Related manuscript traditions survive in the Folger collection and elsewhere. The exact exemplar Weyer used has not survived or has not been identified.

A possibly cognate name such as *Busyn* in another offices tradition is too uncertain to substitute for the directly attested *Bune*.

### *Book of Abramelin*

Abramelin’s demon lists belong to a related early modern ritual environment, but no Buné under the standard spellings was located in the principal Mathers/Peterson text. It should not be cited as a direct Bune source. [Peterson’s Abramelin materials](https://www.esotericarchives.com/abramelin/abramelin.htm).

### Dante and Milton

**Negative finding.**

- Buné is not a named demon in Dante’s *Divine Comedy*.
- Buné is not a named demon in Milton’s *Paradise Lost*.

General references to infernal dukes, wealth, dragons, or graveyards are not evidence of the specific figure.

No Buné character was verified in Jonathan Stroud’s *Bartimaeus* sequence. The novels use Solomonic themes—rings, pentacles, enslaved spirits, and Solomon’s court—but their named spirits form an independent fictional system. ### *Persona*

**Negative finding.** Although *Persona* belongs to the wider *Megami Tensei* family, a playable or named Bune was not verified in the principal *Persona* titles examined. It is safer to say “Megami Tensei franchise” than to claim a specific *Persona* appearance.

### Dungeons & Dragons

A Bune appears in early *Dungeons & Dragons* treatments of the Nine Hells, as an infernal subordinate with a hybrid dog/human/griffin conception derived loosely from the grimoire. [“The Nine Hells” scan](https://files.spawningpool.net/docs/tabletop-gaming-magazines/Dragon%2B/26/The%20Nine%20Hells%20%28Old%29.pdf).

### *Helluva Boss* and the Hellaverse

**Negative finding.** The Hellaverse uses several Ars Goetia names and the title “Ars Goetia,” but no canonical Buné character was verified. Fan characters and speculative family trees are not attestation.

### *Hellboy*

**Negative finding.** No canonical Buné appearance was found in the surveyed *Hellboy* character and demon indexes. The setting’s general use of demons and apocalyptic folklore is insufficient evidence.

### General caution

Online lists often transform “this franchise uses Goetic demons” into “every Goetic demon appears in this franchise.” Each individual appearance needs a title, episode, issue, game, or official character record. Buné has a secure *Megami Tensei* reception; the other requested franchises do not all yield secure attestations.

## Controversies and disputes

### 1. Was Buné invented by Weyer?

No.

**Documented correction:** the *Livre des Esperitz* attests Bune in the fifteenth century. Weyer is the first printed source in the standard chain, not the earliest surviving attestation.

### 2. Is Bune an ancient god?

Not demonstrably.

The Buto/Wadjet hypothesis is possible only in the very broad sense that many grimoire names are corrupt and some derive from older deities. No source chain or linguistic demonstration presently links Bune to Buto. The honest finding is “unknown.”

### 3. Bune or Bime?

**Textual finding:** Bune is earlier and better attested:

- fifteenth-century *Livre*: Bune;
- Weyer: Bune;
- Scot: Bune;
- Sloane 3825: Bune;
- Sloane 2731: Bune, with Bime as a variant;
- Sloane 3648 and Harley 6483: Bime.

This makes *Bime* a genuine early modern variant, but probably a secondary corruption. *Bimé* and *Buné* are Mathers’s accented editorial spellings.

### 4. “Mute,” “divine,” “high and comely,” or signs?

All four readings occur, but not at the same historical level.

- Weyer: *muta voce*.
- Scot: divine voice.
- *Goetia*: high and comely voice.
- De Plancy: communicates by signs.

The earliest dragon description supports “mute/indistinct.” “Divine” is probably a transmission error. The *Goetia* consciously or accidentally replaces both with a new description. De Plancy rationalizes muteness as signing.

### 5. Thirty or thirty-five legions?

- *Livre des Esperitz*: 35.
- Weyer onward: 30.

The reduction may reflect copying, abbreviation, or a different branch of the catalogue. No historical meaning can safely be assigned to the change.

### 6. Does Buné move bodies, corpses, souls, or graves?

The wording evolves:

- *Livre*: bodies move from one place to another.
- Weyer: the dead change place.
- Scot: the dead change their place.
- *Goetia*: the places of the dead are changed.
- De Plancy: cadavers are moved.

These are similar but not identical. Claims that Buné raises the dead, resurrects corpses, commands ghosts to answer, or performs ancestral healing go beyond the wording.

### 7. Are the two seals interchangeable?

Sloane 3825 says they are, while preferring the first. Mathers–Crowley prefers the last. Since the preference conflicts, historically minded editions should show both and identify the source of the choice.

### 8. Is Haaiah Buné’s eternal adversary?

**Tradition, not ancient fact.** Haaiah is Buné’s ordinal controller in the Rudd scheme. No earlier Bune source names Haaiah, and no biblical text establishes their opposition.

### 9. Is Buné Leo or Sagittarius?

Both appear in later esoteric literature because two systems are being overlaid:

- Haaiah no. 26: 5°–10° Leo.
- Crowley’s Buné no. 26: 10°–20° Sagittarius by day.

Calling one “the true sign” suppresses the history. Neither attribution is present in the medieval or Weyer descriptions.

### 10. Five or Nine of Wands?

- **Five of Wands** results from applying the tarot decan covering Haaiah’s 5°–10° Leo quinance.
- **Nine of Wands** is Crowley’s direct *777* Goetic attribution to Buné’s Sagittarius decan.

The requested “Crowley’s *777* tarot pip for spirit 26” is therefore the **Nine of Wands**, not the Five.

### 11. Is Buné female?

All early grammatical references are masculine: French *duc*, Latin masculine forms, and English “he.” The feminine “Duchess Buné” is a modern practitioner development. It may describe contemporary visionary experience, but it is not a manuscript reading.

### 12. Are the Bunis an ancient Tartar belief?

Unproven. The claim appears in de Plancy’s nineteenth-century entry but not in Weyer, despite Weyer being cited. No secure Central Asian source for Bunis under that name was located.

### 13. Does Buné have a de Plancy portrait?

Apparently not. The 1863 dictionary contains an entry but no securely attributable Le Breton plate for Buné. Modern pages frequently conflate “appears in the illustrated edition” with “has an illustration.”

### 14. Did a misprint become a demon name?

Not in the strongest sense. *Bime* appears in multiple manuscripts and so is more than a single modern typo. Nevertheless, the direction of the evidence suggests that it developed from the older *Bune*. Mathers then gave the variant an accented literary form, *Bimé*.

### 15. Is Buné two conflated spirits?

No source clearly describes two separate beings named Bune and Bime. The shared number, rank, offices, appearance, and legions show that they are textual variants of one catalogue entry. Modern attempts to distinguish Bune and Bime as different entities are theological or experiential inventions rather than results of manuscript criticism.

## Open questions

1. **Exact stemma:** What is the precise copying relationship among the *Livre des Esperitz*, Weyer's lost *Liber Officiorum* exemplar, Scot’s source, and the earliest *Goetia* prototype?

2. **Name etymology:** Does *Bune* descend from an unidentified medieval personal name, place, corrupted spirit name, or vernacular word? No proposal currently has adequate philological evidence.

3. **The Bime corruption:** Did *Bime* arise from minim confusion—misreading the vertical strokes of *un* or *im*—or from a genuinely different oral/name tradition? Examination of the actual scribal hands may help.

4. **The voice:** What reading stood in the exemplar used by Scot? The jump from *muta* to “divine” strongly implies corruption, but the missing intermediary prevents certainty.

5. **Legion number:** Was XXXV reduced to XXX through abbreviation, or did Weyer knowingly follow another recension?

6. **Moving the dead:** Did the earliest compiler mean transporting corpses, altering burials, moving spirits, or a broader necromantic operation?

7. **The two seals:** Where did they first enter the Bune tradition, and do their graphical differences reflect two lost source manuscripts?

8. **Seal preference:** Is Mathers’s “last is best” a compositor’s mistake, an inversion produced by page layout, or an intentional preference based on another manuscript?

9. **Rudd authorship:** How much of Harley 6483 derives from Thomas Rudd, and how much from Peter Smart or another late seventeenth-/early eighteenth-century redactor?

10. **The Bunis:** Can de Plancy’s Tartar story be traced to a travel narrative, missionary account, dictionary, or folklore collection predating 1863?

11. **First de Plancy appearance:** Since Bune is not located in the available 1818 transcription, in which intermediate edition did the article first appear?

12. **Modern reception:** Many contemporary Buné practices circulate through private orders, online communities, and practitioner books. Their precise first publications—especially the enn, orange offerings, feminized iconography, and prosperity cult—require a separate bibliographical study.
